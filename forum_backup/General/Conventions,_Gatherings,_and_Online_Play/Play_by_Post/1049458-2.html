                            The priests seem to have forgotten Strife's remarks about the blood almost as soon as they pass his lips. But allowing a fellow priest to rest and pray is completely reasonable, especially a friend of the enchanting Persian astrologer. The images of the gods in the innermost sanctum are off-limits, but they lead him into the temple proper where he can have some time alone to meditate on his Momentary Name and its ever-shifting relationship to Anpu, enigmatic Lord of the Afterlife.

                            He's seated in front of a relief of Set in battle with Apophis. The Hellenes think of Set as a malevolent god to be avoided, because they are a young empire, and they do not see the world as clearly as the Weret-Hekau. Set is Lord of the Red Land, of the desert, of storms, violence, disarray, and foreigners who bring all of them. But Set also guards the solar barge of Ra, spearing the true enemy of all-that-is: Apophis, the serpent of Chaos and bringer of what the Hellenes call Nemesis. It's a cosmic truth and a familiar scene, although for some reason, Set is depicted at Hibis with the head of a falcon (like Horus, his counterpart Lord of the Black Land) rather than the sha, his chosen beast.

                            Regain 3 Mana from Oblations. Also mark your Lethal damage on your Health header, replace the leftmost O with a bold L for resistant damage.


                            Khvarenah has a productive night of spellcraft and sorcery. With his righteous purpose assured by Vohu Manah, creative angel of guaranteed victory, he returns to the temple in the dark of night. Mitry and a cadre of other priests invite him inside the first and second archways into the inner courtyard. He has successfully persuaded the doubters that he is no spy or saboteur, but a devoted scholar, a passing star of fortune to be embraced rather than rejected. They will give him no further trouble.

                            The mood is more serious tonight. The air is cool, the sacred pond as still as glass. They lead him up an undignified ladder to the flat top of one of the granary buildings: the highest point for many parasang, apart from the roof of the central temple hall itself. They provide him with a plumb line and notched palm branch to shut out starlight, even though his blessed tablet will do most of the work. They're expecting this to take hours, only the beginning of an extended star-charting process that could last all week. Plenty of time to get to know this enchanting stranger who has passed into their lives like an eclipse.

                            Roll results: your 4 exceptional successes on spellcasting were all prep for future rolls that you already factored, and you've already got the usual Fate bonuses, so I'll resolve the x-sux all at once and give you 5 Mana total. Magic makes the soul take flight.

                            The hugely successful Astrology roll is a bit more special. Take the Illuminated Condition regarding the Mystery of the stellar convergence, which you can Scrutinize once it happens.

                            Illuminated
                            You've been exposed to a Mystery, and now you're hungry for its secrets. Take +2 on any Scrutiny and Revelation rolls for the Mystery in question.
                            Resolution: Fully reduce the Mystery's Opacity.

                            Khvarenah regains 1 Mana for pursuing his Obsession, and the pot gets +1 Arcane Beat. It's now at 5.


                            The astrologer has his answer in minutes.

                            The starlight and flickering torches illuminate his tablet in a certain way, showing him all the information he needs to match the night sky to the constellations in Ratri's dream-vision. This level of speed and precision for such a complex problem won't be available to Sleepers for millennia, but sometimes genius comes in a flash of inspiration. The convergence will take place on Night 11 of the rising of Aquarius.

                            It should have been harder to determine. When he studies the night sky, Khvarenah is often frustrated by the dead places, the missing stars. The knowledge that should be readily available, but always proves elusive in ways a common astronomer couldn't explain. The promise of something vengeful hiding behind the last star chart. But tonight, somehow, the dark stars weren't in his way. Not one of them crossed the paths he needed to trace. It's theoretically possible, but the chances against it are staggering. He got very lucky.

                            Very, very, very lucky. Like the whole game has been rigged.

                            Khvarenah's incredibly precise manipulation of Fate has not gone unnoticed. Take the Star-Crossed Persistent Condition.

                            Star-Crossed (persistent)
                            Someone up there loves you. At the beginning of each chapter, the Storyteller rolls a dice pool equal to your Fate dots. Successes accumulate over the course of the story. Once the Storyteller has accrued a number of successes equal to your current Wisdom, something very exciting will happen.
                            Beat: Once per scene, take a Beat to invite the attention of a supernatural entity with an interest in Fate.
                            Resolution: Let the Storyteller accrue successes, or uncover and counteract the Mystery on your own terms first.


                            As he sets his tools down in satisfaction, the gathered priests can't believe their eyes. They're not impressed, they're shocked, dumbstruck, baffled, afraid, angry. How the hell did he do that? Is this some kind of trick? Are the ancient and divine arts of astronomy being mocked by a foreigner? By a sorcerer?

                            He can feel his Fatebound strings unraveling. For all their wisdom, these priests are un-Wise, and they can't see the perfected works of the Amesha Spentas clearly. He's in a bit of a tight spot up here on the granary roof, surrounded by spooked and seething priests of a foreign religion.                             Slightly more upbeat news for everyone else as they muse on the news Unbound brought back from the camp.

                            Prism has nothing to add about the gods. But he has an insight about the Red Land. When the nomadic tribes talk about going "shallow" or "deep", he thinks about the deep places of the world where spirits hold court. The spirit of the poplar grove still lives in the poplar grove, but a deeper part of it that passers-by don't see. Any traveler would have to drink from an oasis to survive a desert journey, but maybe drinking from the right ones is the key to going "deeper" into the Red Land. But what makes the right ones?

                            Strife isn't sure he knows anything about bandits, but Fate pushes him to ask around the market when he picks up the myrrh, and things begin to solidify. The unusually vicious bandits are known to disguise themselves as ordinary traders to get close to other caravans in the wilderness. Beware of banners and saddlebags depicting a serpent encircling a jackal! The bandits are thought to have stolen their equipment from a trading family that uses that emblem.

                            As for the gods: Set is much more prominent, as Strife was contemplating back at the temple, but Set and Ha are both gods of the desert. Ha is specifically the desert west of the Nile, which is where you are now. Whereas the desert east of the Nile is celebrated for fruitful seedings and life, the western desert is associated with tombs and the Underworld and the journey through the afterlife. Death is called "going westward" for a reason. Ha is a desert god, but also a Duat god.

                            Khvarenah is in fact quite familiar with Babylon, and the name Sin is known to him. He's god, not goddess, with a beard of lapis lazuli and a great winged bull for a mount. His worship is falling out of fashion in the time of Zarathustra, but he was once called the highest of the gods, the father of wisdom, teacher of astrology in the ever-changing faces of the moon.

                            None of that explains why isolated tribesmen in the Egyptian desert would be paying homage to a fading Babylonian god. How would they even know about him? As Unbound related the story, it sounds like the tribesmen discuss Sin in more concrete terms than Set or Ha. The moon association is still there, but they describe Sin as feminine, and not at all a leader of other gods. No one claimed to have seen Sin, but it was discussed like a real possibility, and a terrible fate. They conceive of Sin as living "deep" in the Red Land specifically. She doesn't show her changing face much in the shallow parts.

More people sit down and tell stories and rumors, and a free-flowing conversation starts up.
