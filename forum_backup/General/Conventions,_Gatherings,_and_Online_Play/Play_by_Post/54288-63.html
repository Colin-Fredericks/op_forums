                        Jensven watches the exchange between Dog and Shadow very intently. If he is concerned for his canine companion he shows no signs. His arms are crossed and his stance wide. The only muscles he moves are the ones that are required for him to watch the training session. He faces forward, but it is apparent that he is addressing Solon when he finally speaks.

                        "I knew he was special when I got him. He was a pup, smallest of his brothers and sisters," The Dawn muses. "The breeder said he couldn't be used for the sleds. I saw that he had heart. He was smaller, but he fought to be the best. I made a deal with the breeder. I could have the dog for some work I did for him for free. I had to accept lower wages for a moon cycle to pay for the bronze I used. I was able to train him to pull a small sled to help me move metal. With his help I was able to move three times the goods at once."

                        There is only a slight change in his voice as he relates more of his tale, "When the Sun chose me, Shadow became even more special. He is able to talk with me more than ever. I understand him. We act as if we were born brothers and I the older. It is not that I am man and he is dog anymore."

                        Jensven fights the urge to shout advice at the furry animal. He knows that Shadow has the endurance of his breed, but the climate is warmer, which could wear him down faster. Also, there was no telling what sort of supernatural endurance one such as Dog could have been blessed with. He decides that he should only step in should the unlikely happen and Shadow be injured more than a scratch.

                                Yi left the Dawn enjoy himself. He knew what was coming, had read the reports and heard the stories

                                Noise, endless noise, of the beating of man-skin drums and the high, wailing drone of leaden flutes until all reason was drown in the mad revelry beneath an angry sun. Eternal, shadowless noon in the city well named Damnation, where the very world was at war with the fragile minds of men while the alien seductions of the Damned exalted every vice and put every dear-held value to the test. Mortals had thrown themselves to the Silent Wind for a moment of rapturous peace or surrendered their reason and joined the dance before Ligier's mocking gaze. It was no fancy of rhetoric that named it Hell.

                                As for himself, Yi had a promise yet unfulfilled. One oath, though broken in faith, hung over him so that each day brought a new shame. To deny his heart's accusation he kept his other promises all the more strongly. He would not leave another city while this task remained undone. As he had many times before he began to walk toward the poorer portion of the city. As he did he passed many who looked at him with an expectant awe. Some he beckoned to follow with a wordless nod. Others did the same unbidden. When they came upon a field full of exhausted clay pits and the stink of rancid oil from discarded pots.

                                "Whose is this place and for what purpose is it used?"

                                "It belongs to no one master, it is useless. The clay is poor for pots and bricks and the land is no good for building." The man had the look of an apprentice. He was young, his garb was much mended, and he was quick to answer with 'Master'. Dried clay was on his cracked hands and clothing, and he frequently glanced over his shoulder to an older man not his father.

                                Yi Zhinan was silent for a time. Then he said, "Let us see what might be done with the uselessness of man."

                                He walked as he spoke, searching the ground as though he had dropped something.

                                "Tell me, why should we keep our oaths?" He turned back to the Apprentice.

                                The question seemed to catch the boy off-guard. He spurted out, "Well... that is... people depend on you, on you doing what you say."

                                "Yes, that is important. Yet when circumstances beyond our control prevent us from doing as we said it is not held a moral failing.

                                Now he spoke to the man he took for a master potter. "What say you?"

                                "Why do you think oaths are held sacred?"

                                The man seemed to have expected the question and answered, "Because the gods and the powerful command it."

                                Yi smiled. "That is very true, yet we tend to do what we have sworn to even when we could get away with breaking them. There seems to be something else at work than fear of the mighty."

                                As he rooted around the broken shard he noticed a child, a girl literally clutching her mother’s apron strings. The look of utter trust brought a pang to his heart. Once more he asked, "Why do you think we should keep our promises?"

                                "cause then you would be a liar. And that's bad!"

                                Her mother was just about to speak up and apologize when Yi seemed find what he was looking for.

                                "Just so!" He said, as he reached down and pulled an egg from a muddy pool. He broke it open with one hand, scattering the pieces, and seemed entranced by whatever had been inside.

                                "An oath is written on the heart. To break it is to do a terrible wound to yourself. For at their heart, all promises depend on a more universal feature of our character. 'What I do is what shall be.' To break an oath then, is to deny your own right and power to change the world. It makes you an automaton of circumstances, a beast driven by passion of pragmatism. Mankind is meant to be more than that."

                                He brought up his hand to eye level, and those near him saw a tiny snake, no bigger than a worm, tangled around his fingers.

                                "Keeping our oaths does not make us weak, it gives us the authority to endure where we alone are insufficient. Now think for a moment, about your lives. Here in this unpleasant place, consider why you are thankful and why you suffer. Weigh the good and ill of your lives. Think about the choices you have made and how they have affected that good and that ill. Be ready, for this day we shall do a great thing, such as has not been done for an age. Observe, and know that what you behold is not beyond you, but of you. You are the part of the power that will move the earth."

                                Then he began to chant, holding his hands out as if in supplication or expectation. Though none had noticed while he spoke the snake had bitten him. Even as it drunk from the wound, a thin stream of solar ichor pooled in his hands before falling to the ground through his fingers.

The 'klaive, Raija's Tears stays put. This is a demonstration, a test of skill. That blade is only for those times when winning could mean the difference between putting innocents in danger or ensuring their safety. Cinder stays home as well. He will reserve this blade for the Fae. The simple, unadorned steel blade of his own forging is the one that he will use for this bout.

                                                    As he settles in to give his all to the task ahead of him, he decides that he will use the visions of the Dawn that was to guide his steps and his blade. The more reckless style of combat he learned at home could score a quick win, but his more analytical, logical nature lends itself better to the measured, practiced style he had learned more recently. Give ground only when necessary and push the opponent back. Strike only when an opening is sure. Defensive spacing and giving every effort to deflecting blows will ensure that he can remain standing longer than the ones aligned against him. With that the blade is raised and the match is joined.

                                                            As his will strove against the elemental Yi lost himself. Reason and time dissolved into a storm of emotion and sensation without context. He did not fall to the edge of total dissolution, some precipice of no return, for those concepts had no place in that sea of mind.

                                                            Hunger… Loss… Isolation… Pain… Loneliness… Fear… Longing…

                                                            At the center of it all rested a single shining point, a pearl of awareness on which to stand firm. Yi had never truly understood what it meant for something to be alien, yet this was that. The mark of humanity was on it, though ancient and scoured of all that was not eternal. About it his mind crystalized in the midst of madness. Only as his first, all-consuming, thought rise to consciousness did he recognize it as compassion.

                                                            Nothing deserves this.

                                                            Gently, with only the slightest hesitation, he laid first his hand and then his head against the creature.

                                                            “Rest a moment little-maker. You will have from me something better than leavings.”

                                                            Straightening he turned back to the shocked onlookers. Many were still picking themselves up from where they had fallen. He gave them a slight smile, and hopped it was reassuring. Or rather he would have, had the motions of “reassuring smile” not been ground deep into the memory of his muscles.

                                                            “From the abundance of the heart does the mouth speak. It is for that reason I asked you to meditate on thankfulness. Seeing your uncertainty, now it seems better to fill up your hearts further.”

                                                            He turned slowly, meeting every eye he found.

                                                            “Many of you are thinking, and there is no magic in the knowing, that you do not deserve to be here. There is fear that perhaps I am the demon you have been taught. There is doubt that I can deliver on the endless stream of promises I make. Most honestly, there is questioning that you are good enough to be part of these things.

                                                            Let me correct you on that last point at least. I swear before Heaven and its king that these words I am about to utter are true. I stand here in awe of each of you. In every union of your ancestors since the Creation one, and only one, has been chosen from among a multitude vaster than all who now live. Every moment has shaped these ones into a soul unique in all the circles of world and time. So it has been every generation until you, each of you, only you, are before me now. We are grown too cynical from familiarity, yet we are, each of us, a miracle. The finesse by which we were shaped is greater than all the works of the Chosen’s hands. Compared to the vast gulf of time that lie behind us, the odds that we, and only we, would be distilled from the endless chaos of life are so long that not every grain of sand on every shore in the world are sufficient as the weight against.

                                                            So…”

                                                            This smile was not practiced.

                                                            “… let me hear you.”

                                                            Turning away he began to dance. As he did so the ground shifted below him, taking on whirls and rises as he went about his motions. The front ranks of the crowd stepped after him only to see the same happen for them. With the sudden realization smiles came to their own faces and they threw themselves into the song that was even now rising from a hundred throats.

                                                            It began with clay and water, writhing like serpents to lift the deep places and level the high. Then a true writhing began as a feast of many hymns fell into the maw of the worm, sending shivers of simple pleasure through its body. With a great molting crack the bracken of earth and stone fell away, the flash of alabaster and green stone replacing them.

                                                            Above the chaunt of a thousand prayers Yi’s voice rose, spinning the dance around him like the piper at a festival.

                                                            “Great Esculapius, skill'd to heal mankind,
                                                            All-ruling Pæan, and physician kind;
                                                            Whose arts medic'nal, can alone assuage
                                                            Diseases dire, and stop their dreadful rage:
                                                            Strong lenient God, regard my suppliant pray'r,
                                                            Bring gentle Health, adorn'd with lovely hair;
                                                            Convey the means of mitigating pain,
                                                            And raging, deadly pestilence restrain.
                                                            O pow'r all-flourishing, abundant, bright,
                                                            Apollo's honor'd offspring, God of light;
                                                            Husband of blameless Health, the constant foe
                                                            Of dread Disease the minister of woe:
                                                            Come, blessed saviour, and my health defend,
                                                            And to my life afford a prosp'rous end.”

                                                            For hours they danced: Exalt, elemental, and ecstatics, in great windings about the pits of clay. Grassy hillocks rose behind them, encircling courtyards of mosaic paths and shaded by the branches of fragrant trees. Brightly windowed rooms, full of air and light, spiraled up them like the whirls of some ancient shell. Many tiered seating girded the amphitheater, pleasant lodging for the sick looked out on green and the music of fountains, storerooms and places of instruction waited for physician-priests, and the golden kindly-faced statue of Asclepius stood in the many-pillared sanctuary of the gleaming temple. Before the great open doors of bronze, in the eyes of the god, Yi Zhinan spoke a secret prayer to the earth and a jade python rose up, twinned with a staff of gold.

                                                            In the stunned silence that followed, as participants and onlookers alike marveling at Asclepeion and its splendors, the Zeneith, bright as noon at midnight, said with satisfaction. “That will do.”
