            If the game is set in India, I think I may have something. How would you feel about a Kheru priestess and journeyman to the Mesen-Nebu. In her mortal days she was raised in the temples complexes of Bubastis and served as a healer and pourer of the sacred libations to the gods. Her talents drew her to Irem, and in the course of eternity led her to the service of Tutuutef in the afterlife. The tale of the Eye of Ra, and the transformation of the bloodthirsty Sekhmet into the more sedate goddess Bast (among other associations) fell within the pervue of Tutuutef's prohibition of indulging appetites reactively, and his chosen animal was sacred in that part of the Nameless Empire (quick aside, he is surprisingly the only Judge that uses cats as his motiff rather than lions or leopards). In her own existence she has striven for some of this same duality, exploring the world with a calm professional detachment one day while occasionally indulging in a wild hedonism in measured doses. Her cult has migrated from Egypt to Greece (where it adapted into a Mystery Cult based around Dionysius and appropriating more ancient rituals into his bacchannalian rites. She was pushed from that region by Roman conquest and resettled in the heart of India (where her cult has enjoyed modest success by synchronizing itself with certain aspects of the goddess Kali. Her cult is not as prosperous as it once was (the mercurial nature of the Mughals and the British aligned more clearly with the ideals of Dedwen than the current government) but it remains strong. Within the Guilds she is well respected by fellow Mesen-Nebu but are shakier ground with other Arisen. She has a reputation as a Lingerer, for one, pursuing her summoned Purpose with dedication yet spending her remaining time on more frivolous and personal pursuits. She has also embraced stronger than others the philosophies of reincarnation. Unknown to most fellow Arisen, she has tried over the centuries to incorporate the teachings of the Reborn into her faith, hoping that loyal servants and chosen lovers might in this way not be lost to time but rediscovered again and again with each rising. This has had occasional success, but it has also drawn the attention of unexpected enemies. There is, in particular, a sect of Tibetan Buddhists who became hunters of the unnatural long ago, believing that any entity that walks the Earth untouched by time (be it ghosts, vampires, immortals, and most especially Mummies) is an abomination that is not only removed from the sacred cycles but in fact delays and poisons the ability of other seekers of enlightenment from transcending the suffering of the world. She has clashed with them many times, and lost too many mortal allies to count to their damnable hunts.

                        Really great stuff ! My character is perhaps not as fully fleshed out yet, but some things I've determined.

                        Mut-Em-Kheri-Min ("Death Now In Front of My Face") bears a name assigned to them far into the cycle of their rebirths - a sentence from a poem from the kingdom of Khem, The Dispute Between a Man and His Ba. Those closest to them sometimes receive the right to qualify them by an older pronunciation of the quote's first word, Maawat. But this is not their name. Mut-Em-Kheri-Min has no name.

                        The rough, irregular ring fastened around their neck during henet may inform a Deathless witness that Mut-Em-Kheri-Min once stood before the ore kilns of the House of Gold and Bronze. They failed, and thus fell into slavedom, a factotum to the Mesen-Nebu. Did their devotion to those Born of Gold earn the interest of another Guild then ? Or was such failure a mere ploy ? Regardless, it's through that recondite path that Maawat garnered the approval and initiation of the Maa-Kep, and delicate bone pendants and pearl ornaments now adorn the twisted ring of their failure.

                        Mut-Em-Kheri-Min is, indeed, not their name. They had many, so many. Each ploy and stratagem added an alias, an epithet. And that dim clamor followed them, when time collapsed on itself, whispers like asps coiling around their soul. Their Ren was tattered from all this noise, but that made it all the more useful to pass the gates and trials of Duat.

                        And that name was heard by Nefer-Tem, son of Sekhmet, Judge of the Never-Closing Blossom, whose scent rises to the nostrils of Ra as he sails the night sky, whose flower - the blue lotus of the Nile - addled the mind and entranced the senses of devotees and vagrants alike. He adjudicates fine and ambiguous distinctions, not least amongst which is mankind's entitlement (and enslavement) to its own dreams.

                        Dreams, whether nightly visions, daydreams about one's hopes, drug-induced visions, echoes of the Astral realm, or all of the above, reflect the divine intent of the Shani'atu. Thus, the law of the Judges condemn both those who pursue a hopeless dream, and those who don't dare to pursue a sincere one. Therein lies the point that some emissaries of Nefer-Tem (Mut-Em-Kheri-Min among them) must adjudicate. Through Sorcery and Relics, access to the Astral might be secured - but the Arisen are clearly not the fittest creatures to roam the Invisible Realm...

                        Maawat's cult did spread from the Near East to Europe to follow opium, and travelled the Silk Roads to grow bhang. Most of them are bygones, former somebodies consumed by the revelation they dreamed for something even bigger that what they received. Many of them become proficient drug-dealers and smugglers, and attach themselves to figures close enough to their ancient glory so they can vicariously live the dream of the life they could have been. The cult shows an intense respect for people lucidly embarking on bouts of self-destruction, and will use all of their resources to nurture and protect someone willing to live fast and die young.

                        Mut-Em-Kheri-Min and their cult have a very external grasp on Kamakhya. They quickly took hold over the British exploitation of Assamese tea, and used this to probe the locals for rituals and lore for decades. They understand the trance and the tremors of the possessed: the struggles of power between the clergy and the folk ritualists. But they remain foreign, remote. They're trying to change that.

                        Mut-Em-Kheri-Min has no name. Rumor goes, they once gave it away, when Irem still stood proud, to a handsome lissom dancer of the Akhem-Urtu - a scion of the Will, a student of the Word, a member of the Lost Guild whose restless stars have disappeared forever more.

                                            Some more info:

                                            Kamakhya’s benbenet
                                            The ‘child goddess’ worshipped at Kamakhya in mortal form is usually just that: a little girl people believe has divinity. No magic surrounds her, no divinity dwells within her. This makes her eminently useful to the Arisen, who may work Utterances on her behalf while claiming she is the one doing so.

                                            Sometimes...sometimes the claim to divinity is real, as this child becomes a benebenet for a Judge. The earth cracks, locusts swarm, mundane rats build primitive shrines offering screeching nothings as devotion, cattle are born with heads split down the middle, tumors gain faces and utter prophecies: a Judge has manifested. The sky turns red and divine mandates are either broken or upheld.

                                            The Arisen of Kamakhya fear such an event. They live in dread of what a god of Duat might want in an area this removed from the Nameless Empire. And they fear for good reason: more Indian benbenets have been this priestess’ position for many centuries. The child-goddesses seem to draw the Judges, though for what reason the Arisen may only guess fearfully and with great concern.

                                                Some more info:

                                                Kamakhya’s Astral reflection
                                                As one PC has a mild connection to the Astral Realms, I felt it best to describe a portion of cosmology not normally relevant to Mummy: The Curse.

                                                The crown of fire sits in a place beyond the Arisen, and it burns with the waxing and waning of the sun. The crown is divinity, the idea a mortal can ascend through the proclamation of the believers. She who wears the crown is a goddess, and the dreams of gods bow down to her. Should she somehow commune with Goetia, these alien things will do as she says. As few child-goddesses can do this, this power is almost never used.

                                                Surrounding the burning crown are deep, still waters, wherein swim abyssal things that have long ago interbred with terrified human slaves. These fish-gods consider the crown theirs, and the child-goddess their representative.

                                                The crown sits upon a cushion in a ruined temple in the sky, and prayer-spirits in the shapes of mandalas tend to it. These manasaputra care only for the traditions of Kamakhya, and will tolerate only the right dogma.

                                                But when the benbenet rises, the clouds turn to storms and a vast shadow stretches across the sky. Instead of divine assurance, fear rises to strangle everything. The fish-demons swim deep in fear, abandoning their self-imposed posts. The crown gutters and burns low. The child-goddess wears a                                                 different crown, then, a black, charred ruin that was once magnificent.

                                                            Neter-Kherter and Kamakhya
                                                            Neter-Khertet is not just in Egypt and North Africa; it covers the whole world, a place that is everywhere at once, a nonpoint in infinity. It covers Kamakhya as well, and it is no safer here than it is anywhere else, and indeed, has dangers unique to it.

                                                            Fiends unique to India’s Neter-Khertet may be found near Kamakhya, and take the forms of towering hags coated in gore, dragging huge iron pots behind them. They snatch up shades as they find and cook them in these pots, which they light with vomit made of boiling flesh and molten iron. These hags find mummies particularly appetizing, but hunger wracks them constantly, and the Law of Suffering says they can never be satiated.

                                                            Kamakhya’s shades appear daze and out of it, surprised and horrified by everything they find. Word of Shezmu quickly reaches them, and they learn the ‘true’ gods are nowhere near as benevolent as they’d been led to believe. No reincarnation awaits them, only stomping feet and the cruel jaws of devouring gods.

                                                            False Brahmins, fiends in disguise, wander to and fro, backwards-facing hands and feet and charcoal-black faces all that gives them away. They offer shades libations of blood; those foolish enough to drink from them are now cursed to remain aware even as Shezmu’s feet crush them, only reaching quiet oblivion as the indolent gods of Duat drink their wine.
