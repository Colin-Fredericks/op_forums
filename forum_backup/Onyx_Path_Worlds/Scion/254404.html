    A myth retold - Shamans of the Xingu

    09-11-2014, 02:02 PM
    I’d like to talk to you folks today about a place I love to learn about: The Amazon rainforest. I love the Amazon for its biodiversity and for the interesting cultures it houses, as well as some of the batshit insane mythology spawned from it. There are a number of issues trying to find mythology for Scion from this place. One of them is that thanks to the deliberate efforts of many priests and conquistadors, a number of those mythologies have been lost to us, and any preservation of those myths comes from sources who treated it with derision at best. Other issues include the fact that there are a number of sources with no English translation, which sucks for me since my high school Spanish classes have left me unable to translate academic texts, to say nothing of the numerous Portuguese sources, or the rare-but-present French and German ethnographies. And, of course, the Amazon is not a homogeneous culture, but a mix of cultures that can be so profoundly different that two tribes on the opposite sides of a river can have languages from two incredibly different families, like an Etruscan trying to talk to a Samoan. This means that there can be amazing stories found that are completely isolated, with incredibly important gods who are, in essence, pantheons of one. I’d like to talk about one of those. His name is Sinaa, he has a Scion called Uaica. And they are awesome.

    First some background. One of the largest and most important tributaries of the Amazon river is the Xingu, one of which is known to anthropologists as the Juruna, though like most Native American tribes, the name they call themselves is different, as they call themselves the Yudya. Their myths, along with those of rival Amazonian tribes, are chronicled in “Xingu: The Indians, Their Myths”, by Claudio and Orlando Villas-Boas. These stories include tales of the Juruna’s ancestral god Sinaa and his sons and students. There are other places to discover these myths, but most of them are cribbing from this book, as I am now.

    So, Sinaa is a jaguar god, shifting between human and jaguar form at will. His father was an enormous black jaguar named Duca, and his mother was a mortal woman. He created the world as we know it, populating it with animals for humans to eat, and taught his sons and people, the Juruna, the ways of agriculture, crafting, and medicine, then saved them when their shenanigans resulted in a global flood. He is capable of seeing everywhere from the eyes in the back of his head. He is married to a spider-woman who weaves dresses. Despite being very old, he can simply take a bath and tear off his old skin and thus become young again. Furthermore, by his home there is an enormous rock, on which there rests a huge forked stick. This stick holds up the sky, and when the last of the Juruna die out completely, he’ll remove the stick, causing the sky to fall onto the earth and destroy everything. Interesting stuff, but I mostly told you that so I could tell you this story…

    So, back in the day, along the Xingu River, there was a kid named Uaica, a boy whose parents were MIA, so he was being raised by his grandfather. He was kind and quick-thinking, but also very sickly and small, so he got picked on a lot by his peers. So, one day, after the bullies were especially cruel, he went into the forest, where he always felt more comfortable. Given that this was the Amazon rainforest, a place where pretty much anything that can’t eat you whole is poisonous, that’s actually pretty badass.

    Anyway, Uaica’s walking along when he stumbles upon this huge-normous tree, and around the tree, all sorts of animals were sleeping soundly. Jaguars, anacondas, and caimans lying next to tapirs, monkeys, and capybaras, all that jazz. The sleep is so sound that some of them have died and are in varying stages of rot. So, obviously, there’s some crazy shit going on here, and Uaica’s all “Wow, I really should get out of here, because I don’t know what’s going on, but anything this powerful isn’t safe.”

    (Incidentally, this is why I really like Uaica. He’s one of the few heroes who consistently makes sensible decisions. It’s a rare thing in mythology.)

    So, Uaica tries to run away, but all of a sudden, it’s like he downed an entire bottle of NyQuil (the old NyQuil, before they had to change the formula for methy reasons). So, he goes to sleep, and once he’s unconscious, he dreams a number of wonderful things, people singing, animals frolicking, basically, he’s in his happy place. All of a sudden, he encounters someone.

    “Sup?” the newcomer says, “I’m Sinaa”

    “Wow, I must be dreaming!” Uaica says, impressed and gawking at everything.

    “You are, but this is all real, let me show you around!” Sinaa says, showing the kid his home, his bathing pool, his wife, the stick that holds up the sky, before going, “Hey, wanna learn some magic?”

    “DO I?!” Uaica cries out, nd about this time he wakes up. All of a sudden, it’s dark outside and he’s not in the same place he was when he went to sleep. Normally, this would be cause for alarm, but Uaica takes this in stride and just heads straight home to his grandpa.

    But, the next day, he goes back to the tree, gets hit with severe drowsiness, and has dream-talks with Sinaa. The jaguar god takes a liking to the boy, and teaches him healing powers and suchnot. Problem is, he keeps doing this so long that he keeps skipping meals, and thus gets even more weak and sickly, until finally Sinaa’s like, “Look, kid, I appreciate the effort, but enough is enough. Don’t come back to the tree again. If you do, you won’t have the strength to leave. Go eat something.”

    Uaica is, predictably, bummed, but he obeys Sinaa. He remembers one trick, using the bark of a tree to craft a hallucinogenic tea that allows him to remember everything perfectly. At this point, his grandfather is understandably worried, so he asks what’s going on. Uaica tells him, and then shows grandpa the tree, keeping his distance from it. Grandad falls asleep, but he’s not on Uaica’s level, so he only gets a few fleeting glimpses of Sinaa. He wakes up, goes back to Uaica, and says, “All right, yeah, I believe you, but we shouldn’t mention this to anyone else.” Uaica agrees, they go back home.

    When they do, it turns out a kid is sick, really sick, and no one in the village can heal him. What’s more, this is the guy who’s been the biggest asshole to Uaica. But, Uaica’s hella mature and willing to put pettiness aside and heals the kid. At first, the villagers are in disbelief, but he keeps pulling White Mage duty and by the end of the day, everyone’s singing his praises, and the bully becomes his best friend.

    So then, once he falls asleep, despite not being under the tree, Sinaa appears. And he’s all, “Whoa, not only did you actually LISTEN to me, but you helped out that kid even though he was a dick to you! Seriously, you don’t know how rare that is! Listen, just take care of yourself, keep being generous, and I’ll keep teaching you stuff.”

    So, for a while, everything’s cool. Uaica and his granddad and his buddy are all working together, and Uaica’s healing everyone and bringing in successful hunts and making stuff for the tribe, pots, necklaces, masks, all sorts of stuff, and Sinaa keeps teaching him new things. But, whenever things go smoothly in myth, something comes along and fucks it up. And, like in most cultures, the blame falls on a woman.

    “Hey, Uaica,” the village says as a single entity to make things easier for storytelling, “You need to get married.”

    “Do I really?” Uaica responds.

    “Well, yeah. It’s what’s expected of you,” the village says. Now, in 21st century Western culture, when we look at a man perfectly happy to live with other men who doesn’t want to get married but is pressured to by society… Well, it’s entirely possible Uaica was gay, but it’s never explored, and probably never was thought of by most tellers of this tale. Frankly traditional Amazonian society tends not to deal well with homosexuality, or indeed, any behavior that defies traditional gender roles.

    But anyway, the problem here isn’t that Uaica takes a wife when he’d be happier not doing so, but that he takes the absolute wrong person to be said wife, one of the few bad decisions he makes. The wife isn’t named; the Amazon isn’t the place to go for progressive gender roles. Then again, no one except Uaica is named, but still, not very egalitarian here. She cheats on him, then conspires with her lover to kill Uaica and take his stuff.

    And, like most affairs, this gets found out when hubby arrives home early. Uaica returns home from fishing and sees his wife in bed with someone else. So, in his trademark craftiness, he simply goes back to the village and distributes the fish at his usual time. Then, afterwards, he asks his wife to get him some water so he can wash up. She does so, and her lover joins her, and she sits in his lap. Because apparently they can’t not bone anytime they have a minute alone.

    However, once she tried to remove herself from her lover’s lap, she got stuck there. And then, just as planned, the entire village wandered on by and saw this. Her mother was enraged and beat them both pretty heavily, since it’s hard to defend yourself in that position. In her last attempt to free herself from her lover’s lap, she ended up dunking the both of them in the river. At this point, Uaica decides enough is enough and turns them into river dolphins. Since those are basically the Amazonian symbols for casual sex, the two of them might actually be better off.

    Of course, Uaica’s father-in-law and brother-in-law wouldn’t agree with me there. They weren’t too thrilled at the girl being turned into a dolphin, no matter that she was cheating on him and plotting his murder. And, upon seeing that the last people to plot to kill Uaica got cursed, they immediately start plotting to kill Uaica. Fantastic. I can’t see this idea backfiring on them…

    So, they start to rile up the other villagers, essentially saying “Hey, you know the guy who keeps healing us and giving us free stuff out of the goodness of his heart? Let’s kill him and take his stuff!” And this apparently works on a bunch of them. So, one day, the villagers conspire to murder him. They bring a lot of fish to the center of the village and invite Uaica down for lunch, and give him a stool to sit on. And while he’s eating, the brother-in-law rushes behind him with a club, intending to bash his brains in. However, one of the gifts Sinaa gave Uaica was a pair of eyes in the back of his head, so Uaica saw it coming and disappeared into the ground along with his house and his companions and all his possessions. They moved further into the forest.

    So, time passed, and the Juruna eventually found Uaica and went, “Hey, you know that whole thing where you turned my sister into a dolphin and we tried to kill you for it? We’re cool now. We want to give you a feast, for reals this time.” Uaica is magnanimous enough to take them at their word, he doesn’t want to be a dick. But, of course, this is just another ploy. They hold the feast on a rock in the middle of a lake, thinking that Uaica can’t move through solid rock the way he could dirt (a leap of logic I can’t pretend to follow, but apparently they’re right), so this time will be successful! So, once again, the brother-in-law tries to sneak up and club Uaica to death, and Uaica sees it coming, and dodges effortlessly, and the club falls down and causes a big crack in the rock.

    Uaica then goes, “Seriously? This is how you repay me for all I’ve done for you? Well, fuck that and fuck you. Sinaa told me a lot more things to teach you people, but you lost out.” And then he jumped into the crevice, bringing his house and his granddad and best friend with him.

    Uaica never returned to his people, and they never learned to create anything new since then. There were rock formations that were said to be Uaica’s home, showing his hand outstretched, as if welcoming people in, but no one has found the way in (and tended to be too scared to do so).
    I enjoy this myth, largely because, as I said before, Uaica is one of the few heroes who know what the fuck they’re doing. Not to mention that he’s kind and selfless (to a point, but then again, when people try to kill you, it’s understandable you’d lose your patience with them).

    This is one of a number of just-so stories in the Amazon that explain why the various tribes missed out on the goods that the white people have. There’s others, often saying that God, or Tupa, or whichever creator deity you want, said at creation that the whites and the Indians would have a footrace to decide who got guns and who got arrows. In most, the whites just straight up win, but interestingly, not all of them. Some of those stories state that the whites cheated, or that the Indians won, but that they chose arrows, finding guns to be too heavy and cumbersome.

    In terms of Scion, it’s clear that Sinaa is a God and Uaica is his Scion. Given that Uaica is an orphan, it’s logical that Sinaa could be his real father, though he could be adopted or somesuch. The fact that Uaica has eyes in the back of his head like Sinaa and Duca did, the idea that they’re related makes sense. The eyes could be a special Relic of some sort.

    Uaica probably becomes a Demigod Scion by the time he leaves at the end of the tale. He’s not worshipped as a god like Sinaa was, but he’s still alive and demonstrates powers that are found in Scion 1e and require the Demigod tier of Legend (Earth Travel, Rainbow Bridge/Come Along, Transform Person).

    Sinaa is an important god to the Juruna, but isn’t found far outside the Xingu area (a nearby tribe has a god names Ña Mai, which means “Jaguar God” and does a lot of similar tasks, as told in “From the Enemy’s Point of View” by Eduardo Viveiros de Castro). However, the Juruna occupied an area that, were it European, would be considered a nation, and since Sinaa at least survived to present day, than I think he could be called a Legend 12 god without a pantheon.

    For Associated Powers, Sinaa definitely has Animal (Jaguar) for his connections to the beast, Epic Intelligence for his teaching and crafting, and Epic Perception for his ability to see all. I can also see him having Epic Stamina (for being ageless and clawing off his own skin periodically), Health (his sleep-inducing tree and emphasis on healing arts, as well as creating people), and maybe Illusion (he’s focused pretty heavily on appearing in and manipulating dreams, although RAW Illusion doesn’t really cover it). Abilities that fit as his favoreds are Academics (teaching), Awareness (sees everything), Craft (creator god). Medicine (healer), Occult (crazy magics), and Survival (prowess in and teaches hunting/tracking/farming).

    Sinaa’s Virtues are a bit trickier. He has a ton of Duty, since both he and Uaica give their people everything they can while expecting nothing in return. There’s definitely Endurance there, since the two work on lessons instead of sleeping and eating, until Sinaa has to order Uaica to eat something before he keels over. Intellect is another strong Virtue, since all of this is done for the purpose of passing down knowledge. Vengeance is a concept with strong connections to Amazonian tribes, and the Juruna definitely go in for it, as shown by the brother-in-law, but Uaica never takes vengeance when able, and is rewarded by Sinaa for rejecting it, so they probably lack that (though the dolphin-thing fits). Other stories in the Villas-Boas book and nearby Amazonian myths seem to reward a sense of ecological balance, not taking more from the forest than necessary lest the forest fight back in a bad way, so Harmony is probably a stronger candidate. So, Duty, Endurance, Harmony, Intellect for Sinaa? Sounds right to me...

    Uaica also shows off something I’ve found can come up from an OOC perspective: Uaica is clearly dump-statting the three Social Attributes and a number of conflicts in-story can be described in game terms as him screwing up Social rolls. He doesn’t make any effort to make the villagers like him beyond his selfless providing for the community, which is a good thing, but means that his wife apparently didn’t care for his standoffishness enough to drive her into the arms of another man and plot murder, and such that even with the seemingly ironclad argument of “she was cheating on me and plotting my murder”, the act of turning her into a dolphin is enough to turn the villagers against him. Of course, by that point, he’s got enough physical Epics, particularly Dexterity, that the attempts made by said mortals do basically fuck-all.

    So, there you are. Incidentally, finding resources about Sinaa on Google is nearly impossible, largely since it means something in Inuit, as well as Hebrew and other Semitic tongues, and also mathematics (sin aa). You have a lot of tares to separate from the wheat. But, still, here's a myth I really enjoy.
