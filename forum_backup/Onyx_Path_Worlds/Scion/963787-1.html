                    Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post



                    I can't say if it was a lie or not, as that's the story I learned from the history channel who has been talking about Hitler for like 30 something years straight. If it was, then Germany can at least save face by saying they didn't comprehend what was going on...

                    If they knew about it and supported it... that leads to some very uncomfortable questions!


                    Still, the other parts I pointed out fit well with a Scion of Hel. Especially the Baldur reference.
                    If I, as a historian (though still working towards my BA and not focussed on that era) and a German person, may weigh in here without derailing the thread too much, these questions are very complicated. I'll try to explain, but I should probably give some kind of trigger warning because there will, as always when discussing the Nazi, be some nasty insights into just how horrible human beings can be below. Or, as a TL;DR: Yes, the German people were, at least on a basic level, aware of the Holocaust/Shoah. The Nazis weren't two-faced, but pretty open about their hatred, although the specifics developed over time and were, in part, at least officially government secrets. The German people today are neither trying to save face nor are we avoiding uncomfortable questions, we're trying to make amends for this stuff on a pretty much daily basis. And making Hitler a Scion is an idea I don't like, for reasons.

                    Now, the NSDAP did indeed come to power in part by popular vote and in part by using some very obvious flaws in the then young Weimar democracy, then declared (more or less in accordance with Hitler's legal powers as chancellor and/or president) a dictatorship that also met with popular approval. Now, why would that be? The racial ideology, while prominent, wasn't even really at the forefront of things in the beginning; it was about economic policy, about ending the Treaty of Versailles, about regaining national honour in the face of the WWI defeat - it was about strong, assertive leadership. Anti-semitism was still rampant in all parts of the Western world, as was racist and racialist rhethoric, so the NSDAP didn't even stick out all that much for that in the beginning. Their violent lashing out against communists was a lot more prominent. So I wouldn't say that the Nazis were elected for their racist views, but whoever voted for them (still the majority of the people) knew of those views and tolerated them at the least. As mentioned by others, the basic recipe of the new world order as imagined by Hitler was readily available in the form of "Mein Kampf", the possession of which became mandatory in the course of the dictatorship - though I'd cast some doubt on whether anyone was really sure what was really going on in that book. From the excerpts I've read and heard in school and in TV documentaries, this book was nothing but a long, disorganised rant by what was at that time after all nothing but a badly educated prison inmate.

                    And although racist laws came quickly with the rise of the Nazi dictatorship, things weren't that simple either. The Nazis didn't start shipping people to Auschwitz on Day 1. The Kristallnacht, that big, obvious outburst of violence, only happened in late '38, Auschwitz opened in '40, the star of David was introduced only in '41. (Please note that I'm not trying to play down the atrocities by pointing this out, I'm just argueing that the descent into this madness was gradual, in order to examine the relative obviousness of it that is at question here). By then, the dictatorship was already more dependent on public apathy than on public support, and dissenters were punished harshly (for example, the members of the White Rose, a student organisation, all got executed for spreading anti-Nazi leaflets at what is now my uni; they deservedly got every nook and cranny here on campus named after them after the war). Of course, there had been discrimination before, Jews being pushed out of the workplace and such, but it got worse rather slowly. We also have to recognise that the extermination of the Jews and other unwanted people(s) wasn't always the plan; this horrible "final solution" was only decided on as the most practical at the Wannsee Conference in '42 - other options discussed were e.g. shipping them all to South America, but in the end it was decided, as sickening as this is, that genocide was the cheapest approach. And views on the implications of a pseudo-biological concept of race differed greatly even within the topmost ranks of the NSDAP - my favourite example is Ernst Röhm, who saw "inferior" races mostly as sex objects and servitors rather than as pests and vermin. These terms "pests" and "vermin", by the way, I take specifically from a document that's among those forced upon schoolkids in Germany the most: These words are, as such, not from any law or party propaganda, but from a "fan letter" written to Hitler by a little schoolgirl. I'm quoting from memory, but she basically writes: "We don't show mercy to other vermin, why should we show mercy to the Jews? They should be treated the same way." So, by now even my own stomach is turning from dealing with this shit, so let's finally come to the main question at hand: How much did the population know of the Holocaust/Shoah, and did they approve of it?

                    That little girl seems to at least point to the conclusion that a significant part of the population shared the sentiment that certain people (and although the focus was on the Jews and should stay on the Jews in any appraisal of this era, we shouldn't forget how hard other groups like Sinti and Roma, Slavs, homosexuals, the disabled, and plain political prisoners were hit) should just be gone. We also shouldn't forget, however, not only the many heroes who fought against the Nazis but also the fact of how quickly Germany turned into a functioning democracy after the war. So, what is the truth? Fanatics like that girl (or rather, at her age, the parents and teachers who indoctrinated her) aside, we must assume that most of the population was apathetic and intimidated rather than actively participating. We can also see this from the fact that the extermination camps were treated as a government secret - it was expected that most people would, after all, find this as going too far. But then, did people even know? I would, from the information available to me, say that if they didn't, they chose not to. Government secret or not, there were more than enough pointers for freedom fighters and dissenters to figure out what they were fighting for, so the rest of the population chose to ignore. Inmates of concentration and sometimes even extermination camps (there's a difference between those two types of camp that many don't realise: concentration camps were for forced labour, extermination camps for the active genocide with gas chambers and all; the extermination camps were mostly built on conquered territory, like Auschwitz in Poland, for the obvious and previously mentioned reason of keeping them out of the public eye) were lended out to companies as labourers (that's how Schindler got his chance to shine, while a great many of other factory owners, including the oh-so-great Wernher von Braun, chose to ignore the fact that those slaves being delivered to them were mostly on the brink of starvation) and the death marches often led through towns, not to mention all the train workers and such involved in the transport of prisoners. The horrified reactions of the German people to being confronted with the Holocaust/Shoah show, to me at least, that most hadn't fully realised the scope and actual grusesomeness of things, but the point stands that enough information to figure it all out was readily available. Shit, people could even see the smoke from the crematories at the camps. Let no one tell you the Germans were just blissfully unaware. You can't hide millions of dead and many millions more of those that barely survived, tortured, persecuted, crushed. Trying to explain it away like that is an enormous disservice to both the dead and the living.

                    Now, why am I telling you all of this, discrediting my own people by admitting their guilt, both by participation and by inaction? Why am I not, as Nyrufa put it, trying to save face? Because, for all of the horribleness we committed or let happened, the German people woke up in the decades following the war, and (thanks to the Denazification programmes of the Allies as well) resolved to face our guilt in order to make ammends and never let something like that happen again (I proclaim proudly as rightist parties grab for power once again, as they are doing in all of Europe, but that's a story for another day). We didn't tear the concentration camps down and eradicated them from memory, we turned them into museums and every pupil in this country will visit one of those during a schooltrip some time along the way (as well, if possible, as a historical synagogue or Jewish museum). We commit more time in history class to this topic than to any other. We have monuments at every corner, the "Platz der Opfer des Nationalsozialismus" ("square of the victims of national socialism", a wonderfully inclusive term I'm very proud of) here in Munich, a sprawling labyrinth of a monument right outside the Reichstag building in Berlin, and so many others I could spend days describing them all. Every dissenter and freedom fighter who fought against the dictatorship, as well as many prominent victims of the Nazis, have streets and buildings named after them. It's all not perfect, and it surely can't actually make up for what the Holocaust/Shoah did to the world as a whole, but unlike many other countries (for while I would never argue that the Holocaust/Shoah was not the most horrible crime against humanity you can think of, there are more than enough other, "lesser" ones that are treated with silence) we face those uncomfortable questions, and commit ourselves fully to giving back where we have taken.


                    To finally return to the actual topic of this thread (sorry, this got a lot longer than intended), I would note that I, personally, am quite uncomfortable with the idea of Hitler being treated as a Scion. I don't like any approach that connects Hitler with any kind of magic or un-human origin, because that always tastes of apologetics to me. Seeing Hitler as a demigod or a demon or a wizard or anything just leads to conclusions like "It wasn't the German people's fault after all, there were higher powers at work" or "Humanity isn't capable of such brutality after all, Hitler was not human". And those are the kind of excuses that are not only exactly what we are fighting against here, but that also create the danger of history repeating itself. If we forget that Hitler was human, we stop expecting that anyone, anyone could turn into the same kind of monster under the right circumstances. I think there are more than enough populist "assertive leaders" with some extreme and inhumane positions on the side running around right now all over the world for you to believe me that. No, Hitler was no madman, no unique monster, no demon, and no Scion. He was a ruthless, brutal dictator who had his best friend murdered for fear of him becoming a rival. He was an incredibly benign, even fatherly boss to his personal employees. He was a power-hungry warlord who lay waste to half the world. He was a gentle man to Eva Braun, who freely married him and followed him into death on the same night. He was the architect of the most terrible genocide in human history, who tried to "purify" the world by murdering not only "inferior" races, but even those "impurities" he found in his own chosen people. He was a vegetarian and an animal lover, who abhorred the hunt and cared lovingly for his dog. He was all those things, and that marks him as human. And that's the scary thing about him. We can't rest assured that he was something special or different that will never return. He was just a human who made certain experiences, made certain choices, got certain opportunities, and was thus formed in a certain way. And he was not alone, but stood on the backs of many like-minded men and women, who in their turn were supported by a climate of racial division and hatred that, in those times, held the whole world, not just Germany, in its grip.

                    If you're looking for supernatural elements in the Nazi leadership, I'd recommend Heinrich Himmler to you. Probably not a Scion either, but as we've heard that there'll be mortal magicians and such in 2E, that fits him pretty well. Himmler was so obsessed with Germanic paganism and magic and witchcraft Hitler was embarassed of it. He wanted to prove that the witches of the witch trials were the real deal, collected a whole castle full of historical documents looking for real spells. Himmler and his folks also promoted stuff like swastika Christmas decorations and wanted to reintroduce Germanic paganism as the "natural" religion of the German people. Nothing against Germanic neo-paganism in general, but I think we can agree on the fact that those guys were more than just a little bit nuts. Have fun with them.
