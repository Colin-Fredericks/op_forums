        You might be able to make that comparison with the TDD, but none of the other pantheons were created as intentional fiction and then later people started believing them. So no - the difference isn't just time.

        Both of those are shows more about specific characters' disfunction than any actual horror at cosmic uncaringness. That's more how the awful leads project and make excuses for themselves. But, to the extent they are about that - Bojack is primarily influenced by Pascal and Sartre. While Rick & Morty has less thought put into its philosophical viewpoint by comparison, it definitely isn't Lovecraftian, because there's no horror at how unknowable and alien the universe is. That's kid stuff to a mind like Rick's. You can't get more anti-Lovecraftian than a great mind kicking the universe's ass. There is absolutely nihilism underlying the show and an existentialist struggle against it, mostly via the idea that infinite realities make everything meaningless as there's always another universe where any given tragedy didn't happen, or where some tragedy you prevented did, etc etc - but again, that's not what Lovecraft was talking about.

That corralling of the subject matter and making clear it's distinct from the World, not just an optional insert, makes me a lot more comfortable with the premise of the book. But conflating the fiction of the Mythos with real religion is just... completely wrong.

                                        Also, just gonna add for the record - a big part of the horror of Lovecraft is the context of the time it was written in. When Lovecraft was writing about the impossible vastness of the cosmos to try to scare people, common wisdom was that science was pretty close to done. People were genuinely pretty sure that we’d learned pretty much everything there was to learn, and that we’d solve the last few remaining questions within a generation or so.

                                        Now, I teach fucking 4-year-olds about the incomprehensible size of the universe in a 30-minute Planetarium show on a daily basis. The idea that we’re tiny and insignificant was a big, scary deal in the 30s. It’s common knowledge now. Contrary to being on its way to becoming a religion, the Cthulhu mythos is a pop cultural touchstone at best, and a played-out meme at worst. Nihlism isn’t a revelation any more, it’s the subject of animated sit-coms. That’s as far from religious significance as you can get. Lovecraftian horror is not awe-inspiring any more, it’s not even scary any more, it’s a literal joke.
