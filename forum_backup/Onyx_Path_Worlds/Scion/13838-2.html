            Session XVII

            One Night in Bangkok Part II

            Ji-Woo’s Visions

            Bangkok

            Andy, Magnus and I are hanging out at a bar, talking. Rajani wandered off somewhere. I’ve been sipping absinthe and enjoying the colors and sounds of the club. After Pipa Jing left, Magnus started trying to cheer me up since I’ve been down since Berlin, but I don’t really feel mopey anymore. I’m excited about meeting Pipa Jing. She’s such an interesting figure from my pantheon’s legends and lore that it’s hard not to be a little star struck. Also, Bangkok is a great city. A little sordid, sure, but very exciting. It’s easy to forget about past disappointments here.

            Of course, I’m also feeling a little impatient. I can’t wait for Pipa to return with information. Really, though, I just want to see her again.

            Zane left for some reason. I guess she’s out getting to know the Bangkok street urchins or something. She left me a note with a fresh glass of absinthe before she disappeared, so I’m not worried.

            * * * * *

            Oh my god, I just had one of the most amazing nights of my life! Pipa Jing is an unbelievable lover. I’ve never had sex like THAT before. She was aggressive but responsive, and her kisses were incredible. The marks from her love bites lingered a bit longer than they probably should have, but I didn’t mind. I even flaunted it. If I wasn’t smitten when we first met last night, I am NOW.

            And it’s not just because she’s so good in bed. Like I said, she was aggressive but never demanding. She made me feel wild and happy and a bit naughty, like I was a co-conspirator in some devious scheme, which I suppose I am, what with the plot to steal Laughing Carp’s pearl. Stormy, enticing chaos lurks behind Pipa’s dark eyes, and I felt the same storm stir in me as we made love. It’s not the formless chaos of the titan Hundun. It’s disorder with a purpose. Pipa knows exactly what she wants, which is something I envy. And we seem to have similar convictions. My prophecy suggested that she is Balance. Maybe that’s why she brings disorder to the Celestial Bureaucracy. It’s bland and boring and restrictive. There’s no room for freedom. My pantheon NEEDS some chaos to shake things up.

            Anyway, back to last night. Pipa showed up again at the club shortly after Zane reappeared. She still looked ravishing in her club clothes even though they weren’t anything special. She joined us at a booth on one side of the club and gave us a hand-drawn map to a bad part of town where there is a temple that doubles as a martial arts school. The chaos creatures there are corrupting the monks by infusing them with chaos, and Pipa said the place was also home to several Chinese ogres. The ogres are strong and their skin color indicates what sort of mystical powers they have.

            Unfortunately, the temple is just a distraction from whatever is really going on. We don’t know what that is, but we hope to find clues at the temple. Pipa doesn’t have a lot of confidence in Laughing Carp. She thinks Laughing Carp would probably fall for the distraction and call it a day. I don’t know if Laughing Carp is quite so foolish, but she DOES seem pretty naive.

            After giving us the information, Pipa asked me to dance, and we made our way onto the dance floor. I’m a very graceful dancer, and I’m very good at moving in harmony with my partner. Pipa was super hot and SO sexy. Together we must have turned a few heads, but we didn’t stay on the dance floor long before Pipa invited me back to her place, where we had the amazing night I’ve already described.

            On the way to her townhouse (which was very modern and filled with beautiful pipas and pictures of women playing them), we talked a bit. I was incredibly curious about her, and asked all sorts of questions. She does still work with Da Ji. The two of them are still out to bring a bit of chaos to the oppressive order of the Bureaucracy, and they still enjoy proving the hubris of virtue. But thankfully they don’t resort to murder as much as they once did. It’s not really as appropriate in the modern world, and there are better ways to go about things, usually. I was certainly glad to hear THAT. I was also glad to learn that aside from that, they haven’t mellowed out over the years. They are such an interesting part of my pantheon’s legends; I’d hate to think they’d gone soft over the years.

            This morning, before I left her place to meet up with my Zane and the others, Pipa and I spent a long time talking. She told me that while she’s not looking for commitment, our little fling is far from over. I was thrilled to hear BOTH parts of that statement. She said that next time we’d have to get together at MY place. I think she likes me.

            In fact, as a token of her affection, she gave me a gorgeous Pipa. It’s made of a soft, whitish wood with elegant black trim and inlay depicting a crane on a stormy, cloudy sky. Two crescent moon symbols flank the strings above the inlay. The pipa is a relic imbued with some of Pipa’s divine power. If I strum the strings, I can send her messages, and she can always find me as long as I have the Pipa. She can also travel through it, but it takes a lot of her power to do that so it’s not something she’s willing to do lightly.

            Of course, Pipa’s also working an angle through me. She hinted that she wouldn’t mind if I seduced Laughing Carp after we’ve stolen the pearl. Pipa’s not going to do that herself, but I think she would like it if someone helped open Laughing Carp’s eyes to the lusty pleasures of the flesh. I don’t really want to do that, though. Stealing the pearl is one thing, but after that, I think Laughing Carp needs to find her own way into the arms of a lover. Besides, Pipa Jing’s the only person I want to seduce right now.

            * * * * *

            On My Relationship with Pipa Jing - Tsui (Gathering Together) transforming into Xie (Taking Apart)

            I consulted the I Ching for insight on my relationship with Pipa Jing. The oracle says that bringing us together through a common goal or feeling is the right way to handle things, so that’s encouraging. I think my fate might be bound to Pipa’s somehow. Our interests aren’t entirely the same, but we do share some similar feelings toward our pantheon and the world in general. The transforming lines of the hexagrams imply that the relationship will bring transformation and a fundamental change. That’s good. My purpose is yet unknown, but if I analyze and understand my own role as our relationship evolves, then I will be delivered from my current state of directionless wandering. Symbolically, the I Ching suggests that I don’t have a “place” to call my own right now, and this relationship could help me find a new “home.” I’ve been getting a lot of omens about an end to my cycle of aimless wandering lately, and this one, combined with the dream prophecy and corresponding divination I had in Berlin, convinces me that I’m in the process of finding a sort of direction. I hope so.

            * * * * *

            I met up with the others at the hotel about mid-morning. On the way, Rajani called me to let me know she was out taking care of something and would join back up with us when she could. She’s probably slaughtering chaos beasts or something.

            We’re heading off to the temple shortly. Jean came by to meet with us first, though, with some new information. First off, Laughing Carp is apparently pissed off about something and summoned Pipa Jing to have a conversation. Zane confessed that last night, when she briefly left the club, she went to talk to Laughing Carp about the pearl. She didn’t tell her anyone was trying to steal it, but she asked questions about it. Laughing Carp is more clever than Pipa gives her credit, and she figured out that Pipa was after the relic.

            For the first time ever, I was angry at Zane. I mean, not REALLY angry. I guess I was more frustrated than angry. Zane was just trying to do what she thought was right. Now it’s going to be harder to steal the pearl, and Pipa Jing’s probably in trouble. THAT’S what I’m really worried about. I feel like we’ve betrayed her trust. And I’m going to have to lie to her to keep her from finding out who sold her out because she’s NOT going to be happy about this, and I DON’T want her to be pissed off at my best friend.

            Oh well. What’s done is done. At least Zane had the good grace to promise to help me steal the pearl, but she told me that she would do so only for ME, and NOT for Pipa. I don’t think she likes Pipa very much.

            Jean suggested that he could try to intercede on Pipa’s behalf, but that may not be enough. Still, it was then I realized that I’d wrongly interpreted my prophecy. Jean isn’t Corruption, he’s Balance! Pipa is Corruption. It seems so obvious now that I think about it. Jean did seem a little disappointed that he wasn’t Corruption. He’s such a scoundrel. Meow.

            Of course this also means that Pipa is probably bad for me, but I think she’s bad for me in a good way. I’m worried about her, though, and I hope she’s not in too much trouble. After we’re done with the temple, I’ll send her a message with my new pipa to make sure she’s okay. It has to sound awful when I strum the strings since I don’t have any idea how to play the instrument. All the more reason for Pipa to teach me how to play it beautifully.

            Oh yes, Jean also told us that he had gotten information on what’s REALLY going on in Bangkok. A powerful chaos creature with an unpronounceable name is using his servants to import mystical artifacts into the city. We have no idea why, but Jean’s going to go look into it. It might be dangerous, though, so Zane offered to place her protective charm on him so that we would know if he’s in trouble.

            Once Jean left, Magnus and I tried to explain to Zane how stealing the pearl was a good thing since it would allow Laughing Carp to experience love and romance and would give her a choice in how she lived her life instead of locking her feelings away with a mystical chastity belt. That’s when we learned that Zane’s never had sex! Whoa! I was shocked, but then everything fell into place and I started to understand Zane’s weird views on romance. She thinks romances it all about the guy protecting the girl and giving her presents in exchange for sex. Also, she doesn’t see herself being the “woman” in a relationship since she can take care of herself and doesn’t need protection. That’s why she plans to wait until her divine powers let her turn into a man before she hooks up with a girl. She’d rather be the boyfriend than the girlfriend.

            Wow. I don’t really know how to handle that. But my lifestyle probably hasn’t done much to change her views on romance, even though I have slept with two ladies and only one guy since I’ve known her. Anyway, these are all issues for another day. I’m sure Zane will figure it out eventually, once she accepts that she’s into girls and that’s okay.

            * * * * *

            The fight at the temple was one of the craziest fights we’ve ever had. Also literally one of the most colorful.

            We decided on the direct approach, and just marched into the place even though Rajani hadn’t returned yet. Several shadowy monks with glowing eyes crouched in rows to either side of the temple’s vaulted gallery. A grotesque bronze statue of a Chinese ogre dominated the chamber, glaring down at us with three bulging eyes. Two big Chinese ogres, one red and one blue, stepped into the gallery from side chambers and glared at us while preparing their weapons. The stand-off lasted about one second before I shot the statue’s third eye, and then everything exploded into violence. Monks were everywhere, flipping around and striking at every opening with their potent kung fu. Individually, they weren’t really dangerous, but there were so many of them that their sheer numbers overwhelmed me and one of then landed a glancing blow. It didn’t hurt much, but I decided to fly up and perch on the statue to make it harder for them to get to me. Fortunately, it wasn’t hard to take out the monks after Zane threw them off balance with her tidal interference, and the two of us made short work of them. And I learned a thing or two about how avoid being overwhelmed by superior numbers.

            The real problem was the ogres. Magnus finished off the first two pretty handily, but then a whole rainbow of new ones showed up, each a different color with different powers. I knew enough about these things to predict what each could do, and I wasn’t really that worried until the purple and green ones showed up. I didn’t have any trouble dodging most of the ogre’s attacks, but the purple one had Zane’s trick, which would make it hard for me to dodge, and the green one could heal his allies or hit me with a debilitating illness, and I didn’t want to go through THAT again. So I shouted a warning to Magnus and the two of us made short work of those guys. With Andy’s help, of course. His blessings and curses are amazing, and I like the way the chi looks as it flows around his magic.

            Just when we started to gain the upper hand, more of the purple and green ogres showed up with more monks, and then the statue cracked and its bronze shell fell away, revealing a gigantic, multi-colored ogre beneath. I bet it was annoyed that I’d shot it in the face first thing in the fight.

                                                Edmond Cross, Scion of Dionysus, Dodekatheon

                                                The Wreck of Edmond Cross

                                                “Hello, you’re on the air.”
                                                The voice on the other end of the phone was courteous, with a practiced politeness. Edmund had no idea who the voice belonged to.
                                                “Um…what? What’s going on?” A muttered confusion was all Edmund could offer. He tried to remember why he was on the telephone or what he was supposed to be doing.
                                                “You’re on the air, sir,” the nice voice encouraged. “Go ahead and tell us what you think.”
                                                “Um…” the only recourse Edmund could fall back on was honesty. “I think…I think I feel like I’m going to throw up.”
                                                “Exactly my point,” another voice came in. This one wasn’t nice. It was loud and demanding. “The politics in this country makes us sick. It is a mess, and quite frankly it’s an insult to the average voter…”
                                                Not sure what to do, and afraid that if he hung up he’d wreck whatever it was he was supposed to be doing, Edmund carefully laid the receiver down on the scuffed top of an end table and slowly backed away from the phone.
                                                Edmund Cross was an artist, but usually that was just an excuse made to cover up how broken he was. There were often times, like this one, when Edmund would come to himself, the fog in his mind clearing like a light switch being thrown, and he would have no idea where he was or what he was doing.
                                                Living in a fugue state is only a problem when rational thought intrudes.
                                                He had read somewhere how concussion victims would describe returning to consciousness like waking from a dream, only to discover that they were in the midst of an unknown situation and would slowly have to reconstruct the sequence of events. Edmund often felt that way, but he didn’t blame head trauma so much as the massive amounts of drugs and alcohol he habitually consumed.
                                                The room he was in was chaotic. It held a couch which was torn in places, worn odds and ends of furniture, the phone, and a lamp without a lampshade. It was student squalor, the kind one might find around art college people. Edmund didn’t know if it was some sort of low-rent flat or an abandoned building where squatters had taken up residence.
                                                He found other people in the next room. The air was filled with a haze of smoke, some of it tobacco. Two men with guitars chatted quietly with each other while a third seemed to doze behind a massive set of drums. Another man stood in the center of the room, eyes closed and arms outstretched, spinning slowly in a beatific trance all his own. In the hand of the spinning man was an open bottle of Cabernet Sauvignon wine. The bottle was half-empty. Edmund was a pessimist. Microphones, wires and a variety of amplifiers littered the claustrophobic space.
                                                There was also a surprisingly attractive girl there. She wore a torn t-shirt which showed off her bare midriff and a pair of strikingly tight pants. Perched, as she was, on top of a battered bar stool, the hip-hugging waistband of those pants wasn’t up to the task of modesty. Edmund tried not to stare.
                                                When Edmund entered the wine-bottle-guy opened his eyes and asked, “Dude, did you order our pizzas?”
                                                “Um…” Edmund stammered, “I…I think we’re on the radio.” He gestured vaguely to where he had come from.
                                                “Rockin’!” one of the guitar men exclaimed. The drummer snapped back to life as the group of them proceeded to produce a raucous racket which might have been music. It was easily loud enough to be heard over the phone in the other room, but by that time the switchboard operator had cut them off.

                                                * * * * *

                                                Edmund never really fit in anywhere. He was often seen as weird and an outsider. It didn’t help that he always heard voices, and had done so ever since he was young. Sometimes he would see things that weren’t there, and the semi-reality of the world around him seemed normal. Some people said that he had a dark, brooding nature which lent an air of mystery to his art. Others merely called him psychotic. When he couldn’t be sure if the person talking to him was real or just in his head, it didn’t help convince people to think otherwise.
                                                Edmund came from a family full of artists and creative souls. He was raised on the Isle of Mann, the self-governed island between England and Ireland. Even though the Isle of Mann had its own government, it was still a protectorate of England, so most everyone thought of Edmund as British, even though he always introduced himself as being “Mann-ish.”
                                                He grew up surrounded by musicians, poets and painters who instilled in him the idea that truth was beauty and that details didn’t really matter. When he was very young, he was told that he had been named after a particular musical ballad. It wasn’t until years later that Edmund Fitzgerald Cross learned that the song he had been named for described a real life maritime disaster in America which had claimed the lives of twenty-nine men. His childhood was full of uncomfortable revelations like that.
                                                The family he belonged to was more of a sprawling, semi-nomadic commune. They saw themselves as “free-spirits,” whereas locals often called them “vagrants” and the police called them “tax-dodgers.”
                                                The most important person in his life growing up was his mother, Sylvia; often called “Silly Sylvie.” She was strongly bohemian, a believer in nature spirits and a self-professed psychic. Her first love was music, but she constantly embraced new crafts, latching onto pottery or wood block prints and the like. She was always making things and giving them away as gifts, be it a slightly slumped glazed vase or a knit square of yarn which couldn’t really serve as a blanket. Her creativity was only matched by her lack of focus.
                                                Edmund was also close to a half-brother named Mark. Mark was several years older than Edmund. At some point Mark disappeared mysteriously, only to reemerge in America, in some artist colony somewhere in New England.
                                                Then there were his aunts, his mother’s sisters, who also had a hand in shaping young Edmund. There was Auntie Mary, who was legitimately good at sculpture and was the only one with any money sense. There was Auntie Nelda who was a professional leech. She used her charm and the sufferance of friends and family to get by. And most notably there was Auntie Lori, Edmund’s favorite. Auntie Lori was unpredictable and gregarious. Her main occupation seemed to be travel. She would constantly disappear to some strange corner of the world, only to return with exotic gifts and fantastic stories. There was never a suitable explanation as to how Auntie Lori could afford her globe-trotting life-style, other than the fact that she seemed to live a blessed life. Good things always seemed to happen to her.
                                                When he was a teenager, Edmund discovered that Lori was, in fact, his real mother and Sylvia was the aunt who had raised him. Lori had met a dashing stranger on one of her many adventures and had returned home, after wine, roses and romance, with little Edmund growing inside her. She had made the decision to leave Edmund with her sisters in the hopes that a “more stable” upbringing would be better for him. Edmund was not embittered by this revelation. In fact, it helped explain quite a lot.
                                                As a young man, Edmund somehow managed to scrape together the funds to enter the Edinburgh College of Art at the University of Edinburgh. Living life at the University was a dream come true for him, while the main voice inside his head was telling Edmund to get out and see more of the world. When his studies took him to Spain, the birthplace of Picasso, Edmund simply never came back.
                                                Ever since then Edmund has wandered from country to country and continent to continent, more as a series of accidents than with any real agenda. Like with his mother—his real mother—life seemed to find a way to keep Edmund moving.

                                                * * * * *

                                                Edmund turned twenty in America. He was in Arizona, where the hot desert days faded to cold, windy nights. It was on just such a night that he found himself in a drive-in theater off of some barren stretch of road. The days of the drive-in had come to an end, but the dilapidated property had been bought by an art-house collective. Every year they hosted a film festival where they showed avant-garde and independent films for three days straight. People would come from miles around to camp out for the duration. Inevitably it turned into a bacchanalian carnival.
                                                Edmund had borrowed a convertible from a friend and had driven it down for the film festival. With a cooler, a sleeping bag and a pair of blankets, that car became his home for those three days.
                                                The last night of the festival was amazing. The sky was clear, the stars were intensely bright and a full moon slowly rose over the desert. They were playing a series of films from Latin America, with no subtitles. Edmund didn’t speak Spanish, but there was a vitality and a passion to the films that made the need for language secondary.
                                                He was so enraptured by the screen that he didn’t see the stranger until she leaned a hip against the side of the car door. She had the smoky, sultry mystery of a Mexican beauty. With trim, tan limbs, full, glossy black hair and dangerous eyes, it was as if a Latino goddess had stepped off the screen. She stood there in a halter top and cut-off jean shorts, leaning against the car as though she were in an advertisement. Edmund wondered whether or not she was cold, but felt too embarrassed to voice the question. Without a word this woman reached out an imploring hand to Edmund.
                                                Edmund didn’t know what he was supposed to do. Who was this woman? What was her expectation? After a moment of indecision he handed her the open wine bottle in his hand. He preferred wine to beer. It was more artsy, and generally there was more of it per bottle.
                                                The stranger not only took the wine, but what Edmund was smoking. She took a couple of puffs and then a long drink from the bottle. When she was done she gave a pleased sigh and threw both the wine and the joint dismissively behind her. Edmund made a small noise of dismay, but his complaint was cut off when the stranger climbed over the top of the door and into the car. Seeing as how she had entered from the driver’s side, she ended up straddling Edmund.
                                                Edmund was beginning to feel scared, now. She was so pretty Edmund found it hard to look at her. But given where she was, he didn’t have much of a choice. Her smoldering stare kept him pinned to the seat. Though the position they shared was very provocative, the glint in her eye was one of mischief more than lust.
                                                On the big screen the film continued unheeded.
                                                She spoke for a long moment in the rapid-fire, lyrical speech of American Spanish. Edmund looked left and right, but there was no help forthcoming.
                                                “Um…I don’t speak…that language,” he muttered.
                                                “You, I have been watching,” she switched to English, the hints of her accent as attractive as the rest of her. “You pass through life as the wind passes through the trees. You bask in the sun and soak up the dark; embracing the gifts of life without begrudging what it is you lack. I say to you, the beings of heaven look upon those like you and smile.”
                                                “Um…” Edmund had nothing to say to that. “Your…um…hair looks pretty in the moonlight.” The words just came out on their own.
                                                The stranger smiled.
                                                “What’s this?” she asked. Her slim fingered hand reached down to his jeans. She reached in and pulled out the film festival ticket-stub from his pocket.
                                                “Moonlight is the sister to darkness and candles,” she said. “It is inside intoxicating wine and elusive music; the setting of romance. But it is also the light of lunacy and the harbinger of madness. For your heart of openness and acceptance I bring you ecstasy and insanity; enlightenment beyond the corners of the rational world.”
                                                She spoke in a soft, subtle chant, as though the words belonged to some ancient song or were from some spell older than mortal magic. She kissed the ticket-stub, leaving lipstick marks over the printed words, “Moon-Lite Drive-In,” and slid the slip of paper back into his pocket. Then she started to unzip his jeans.
                                                That was the night Edmund lost his virginity. His recollection of the evening ended up confused but grateful.

                                                * * * * *

                                                Edmund slowly became aware of his surroundings. His ears were filled with the hum of florescent lights. He was in a brightly lit room. It was too bright. The light hurt his eyes and the shabby white acoustic tiles of the ceiling reflected the glare. Looking down, Edmund saw white again. He was holding a Styrofoam cup. The cup was partially filled with coffee which looked like it had been there for a while. It didn’t steam and it had stained the interior of the cup with brown rings. Its inky blackness seemed mysterious, as though its depths held secrets Edmund was better off not knowing. He took a sip and it was cold and bitter.
                                                There were voices in the room with him. Voices with American accents. They held the soft susurration of Southern accents, or perhaps were from the Mid-West. He would discover some time later that he was in the northern part of Texas, somewhere along the border of Oklahoma, and for days afterward the theme song from the Rodgers and Hammerstein musical Oklahoma! would be stuck in his head.
                                                Edmund was seated on a hard, metal folding chair, in a circle of other people in similar chairs. One person was on his feet—in cowboy boots—and he was talking in his Texan drawl. He had been talking for some time, Edmund realized.
                                                “And that’s why, because my family, I’ll never touch another drop again.”
                                                There was a smattering of applause from the group in response to this impassioned declaration. Edmund couldn’t both clap and hold his coffee, so he just cradled his cup in both hands. A dowdy woman wearing too much red lipstick hugged the speaker, murmuring, “Thank you, Bill. That was very moving.” Then they both sat down and the lipstick lady said, “Okay, who’s next?”
                                                There was a moment of brittle silence. Edmund sipped his coffee again; its taste instantly reminding him why he wasn’t drinking it, but the unintended sound of his slurp came out uncomfortably loud. Then another man stood up. This man was large, not too tall, but heavy-set. However he carried his heaviness well, with a natural grace that said he was exactly as big as he was supposed to be. He had dark hair and eyes which seemed distinctly Mediterranean, but Edmund couldn’t place his accent. He wasn’t American.
                                                “This…whole thing,” the man began uncertainly, spreading his hands to encompass the room, “it’s supposed to be anonymous, right?”
                                                “Yes,” the lipstick lady assured encouragingly, “it’s okay to tell us whatever you want. It won’t leave this building.”
                                                The big man nodded, scanning the people around him. He looked at each person’s face in turn with a scrutiny that said his next words were going to be important.
                                                “My name is Bacchus, the god of wine,” the man announced. “Some of you may know me as Dionysus. I’m not an alcoholic, but I love to drink.”
                                                “Sir,” the lipstick lady interrupted. Her caring demeanor was replaced with a waspish disapproval. “If you’re not going to take this meeting seriously, then we’re going to have to ask you to leave.”

He was like a preacher at his pulpit, delivering a sermon on the grace of grapes. In many ways it was quite conservative. He spoke of how one should “drink to live, not live to drink” and how drinking one’s self into oblivion was contrary to the purpose of spirits. But he also talked of the wonders of fermentation and how alcohol was a gift of the gods for men to live well and enjoy life; how it was meant to inspire, not destroy. It seemed so very contradictory, but at the time, the way he told it, it all seemed to make perfect sense.
                                                When he was done there was enthusiastic applause from the other members there. All except the lipstick lady, who was trying to constructively channel her anger and Edmund, who was still holding his coffee. The others were all up on their feet, hugging the big man, thanking him for his words. It dawned on Edmund that he was expected to join in, or worse, that he may be asked to speak next. With a sense of panic he hurriedly left the building.

                                                “I don’t understand,” Edmund said. He was just being honest.

                                                “Embrace the journey, Edmund,” the stranger said. “Even when you stumble you still make progress.”

It was actually a Yukata, a Japanese robe from an Onsen, a hot-spring resort somewhere in Hokkaido. How it came to be with him in Amsterdam Edmund couldn’t recall, but he often wore it when he couldn’t remember what had happened to the rest of his clothes.
                                                He stared intently at his coffee pot. It was an electric percolator from the 1950s. Soon enough the ancient appliance made the gurgling sounds that meant it was working. It sat on a hot plate on a table in the middle of his studio. The hot plate itself was defunct. Bad wiring kept Edmund from plugging it in for fear that it might start a fire, but it served as a suitable resting spot for his coffee pot, which itself might also have been a fire hazard. In Edmund’s art studio, with paint thinner, old rags, canvas and bits of wood, fire was a real concern. That didn’t stop him from smoking, though.
                                                “Oh, Edward, you’re in!” said a bright voice.
                                                “Ji-Woo?” Edmund asked. He knew that voice and he knew it meant trouble. But usually a good kind of trouble.
                                                A figure breezed into the room as though she had every right to be there. Ji-Woo was a young Korean woman, perfectly at ease in her own skin and completely beautiful. Secretly he felt envious. At her worst Ji-Woo was a beautiful disaster, while at his best Edmund was still a bit scruffy looking. She always called him “Edward.” Edmund suspected that she knew his real name, but she just didn’t care. And Ji-Woo always appeared as sudden as a storm. She blew in unannounced, tumbled things around like a tempest and then left without warning, leaving behind artful wreckage in her path. Edmund had often been a victim of her unpredictable enthusiasm.

                                                “I…um…well…”
                                                “Thanks, I knew you wouldn’t mind. Do you have anything to eat?”
                                                “Um…coffee.”

She pulled out a handful of coins and tossed them into the air with an experienced fluidity. The coins landed with nice metallic clinks. Ji-Woo studied the way the coins had landed sedulously, and then she gathered them up and tossed them again. And again. She was doing an I Cheng reading.

                                                “When was the last time you left the studio?” she asked him.
                                                “I…what month is it?” Edmund couldn’t honestly remember.
                                                “I got the ‘Already Fording’ reading,” Ji-Woo told him gravely. “It tells me that it’s time to ford the stream of events. It also tells me that it’s time to end the cycle of disarray.” She peered around the studio. “And you are surrounded by disarray. We need to find you a girlfriend.”
                                                Edmund failed to follow her logic.
                                                “I have friends…who are girls,” he defended.
                                                “That’s not what I mean.”
                                                “I have female sexual companions. It’s exactly what you mean.” In his experience lovers did nothing to lessen disarray, but rather added to the confusion of his life.

                                                “Um…” Edmund felt a sense of rising panic. Part of him was glad, the part of him that was delighted by the unexpected and the strange. Another part of him, however, the part which was afraid and just wanted to be left alone—the larger part—wanted to ask her to leave. But one did not ask Ji-Woo to leave. One only weathered the storm. It wasn’t unknown for Ji-Woo to sweep Edmund up in her torrent of activity only to forget about him later, abandoning him at some techno dance club where he had no friends, no idea where he was, and no way to get home. It was an apocalypse of paint, drop cloths, brushes and clutter; but the canvas stood on its easel blandly, mockingly blank. It was untouched and Edmund couldn’t find any artistic resources to fill its taunting emptiness. The voices in his head were quiet tonight. Edmund’s mood darkened. He turned a bottle of red wine over and over in his hands. He had lost his bottle opener.

The stranger was a large man, with a Mediterranean duskiness. They shared a moment of contemplative silence.

                                                “I can’t open it,” Edmund confessed.
                                                Bacchus turned to search through the jumble of tools, paints and brushes on a nearby work table. He came up with a scratched and battered claw hammer, its handle wrapped in ageing duct tape and its head darkened by rust.

There was a crash of glass, a splash of wine and a muffled voice from the back of the room which sleepily complained, “What the fuck?”

                                                “Um…yeah,” Edmund said. “That’s Sharon, Sandra…and that’s Alex.”

                                                Bacchus found an empty glass and poured paint thinner from another one. He filled them both with wine for himself and Edmund.

                                                “Um?” Edmund answered.
                                                “I’m also your father.” Bacchus poured more wine.
                                                “Oh,” Edmund said softly. “That…that explains a lot.” Then after a bit he continued, “You know, I always felt I might be...special.”

                                                “It’s not good news, I’m afraid,” he refilled their glasses again, the ruin of the bottle miraculously still held its wine. “I wish I could promise you a simple life, filled with wine, women and song. Believe me when I say that’s the best kind of life there is. But fate, I’m afraid, wants you to play a larger role. The blood of divinity runs through your veins, which means the threads of destiny bind more tightly around you than for others. It’s a terrible responsibility, but it can also be glorious. The tread of your footfalls will echo more profoundly down the halls of history.”

                                                “Don’t worry. You’ll figure it out.”

Win or lose, whatever may come, you will always have the gift of inspiration.”

He scrapped a clump of paint from his pallet onto the head of the hammer. He slung it at the canvas and a splatter of paint and wine landed just so. He spun the hammer around and scraped a line of color down the canvas with the claw end. Edmund tilted his head to one side to gauge the effect. He liked how it looked. With his free hand he spread more paint on the canvas and smashed it with the hammer. Before long Edmund was furiously painting, lost to the rest of the world.
                                                “That’s my boy,” Bacchus said, patting Edmund on the shoulder. “You’ll be fine.” Then the god of wine left, disappearing into the night as mysteriously as he arrived.

                                                “Hello, you’re on the air.”
                                                The voice on the other end of the phone was courteous, with a practiced politeness. Edmund had no idea who the voice belonged to.
                                                “Um…what? What’s going on?” A muttered confusion was all Edmund could offer. He tried to remember why he was on the telephone or what he was supposed to be doing.
                                                “You’re on the air, sir,” the nice voice encouraged. “Go ahead and tell us what you think.”
                                                “Um…” the only recourse Edmund could fall back on was honesty. “I think…I think I feel like I’m going to throw up.”
                                                “Exactly my point,” another voice came in. This one wasn’t nice. It was loud and demanding. “The politics in this country makes us sick. It is a mess, and quite frankly it’s an insult to the average voter…”
                                                Not sure what to do, and afraid that if he hung up he’d wreck whatever it was he was supposed to be doing, Edmund carefully laid the receiver down on the scuffed top of an end table and slowly backed away from the phone.
                                                Edmund Cross was an artist, but usually that was just an excuse made to cover up how broken he was. There were often times, like this one, when Edmund would come to himself, the fog in his mind clearing like a light switch being thrown, and he would have no idea where he was or what he was doing.
                                                Living in a fugue state is only a problem when rational thought intrudes.
                                                He had read somewhere how concussion victims would describe returning to consciousness like waking from a dream, only to discover that they were in the midst of an unknown situation and would slowly have to reconstruct the sequence of events. Edmund often felt that way, but he didn’t blame head trauma so much as the massive amounts of drugs and alcohol he habitually consumed.
                                                The room he was in was chaotic. It held a couch which was torn in places, worn odds and ends of furniture, the phone, and a lamp without a lampshade. It was student squalor, the kind one might find around art college people. Edmund didn’t know if it was some sort of low-rent flat or an abandoned building where squatters had taken up residence.
                                                He found other people in the next room. The air was filled with a haze of smoke, some of it tobacco. Two men with guitars chatted quietly with each other while a third seemed to doze behind a massive set of drums. Another man stood in the center of the room, eyes closed and arms outstretched, spinning slowly in a beatific trance all his own. In the hand of the spinning man was an open bottle of Cabernet Sauvignon wine. The bottle was half-empty. Edmund was a pessimist. Microphones, wires and a variety of amplifiers littered the claustrophobic space.
                                                There was also a surprisingly attractive girl there. She wore a torn t-shirt which showed off her bare midriff and a pair of strikingly tight pants. Perched, as she was, on top of a battered bar stool, the hip-hugging waistband of those pants wasn’t up to the task of modesty. Edmund tried not to stare.
                                                When Edmund entered the wine-bottle-guy opened his eyes and asked, “Dude, did you order our pizzas?”
                                                “Um…” Edmund stammered, “I…I think we’re on the radio.” He gestured vaguely to where he had come from.
                                                “Rockin’!” one of the guitar men exclaimed. The drummer snapped back to life as the group of them proceeded to produce a raucous racket which might have been music. It was easily loud enough to be heard over the phone in the other room, but by that time the switchboard operator had cut them off.

                                                * * * * *

                                                Edmund never really fit in anywhere. He was often seen as weird and an outsider. It didn’t help that he always heard voices, and had done so ever since he was young. Sometimes he would see things that weren’t there, and the semi-reality of the world around him seemed normal. Some people said that he had a dark, brooding nature which lent an air of mystery to his art. Others merely called him psychotic. When he couldn’t be sure if the person talking to him was real or just in his head, it didn’t help convince people to think otherwise.
                                                Edmund came from a family full of artists and creative souls. He was raised on the Isle of Mann, the self-governed island between England and Ireland. Even though the Isle of Mann had its own government, it was still a protectorate of England, so most everyone thought of Edmund as British, even though he always introduced himself as being “Mann-ish.”
                                                He grew up surrounded by musicians, poets and painters who instilled in him the idea that truth was beauty and that details didn’t really matter. When he was very young, he was told that he had been named after a particular musical ballad. It wasn’t until years later that Edmund Fitzgerald Cross learned that the song he had been named for described a real life maritime disaster in America which had claimed the lives of twenty-nine men. His childhood was full of uncomfortable revelations like that.
                                                The family he belonged to was more of a sprawling, semi-nomadic commune. They saw themselves as “free-spirits,” whereas locals often called them “vagrants” and the police called them “tax-dodgers.”
                                                The most important person in his life growing up was his mother, Sylvia; often called “Silly Sylvie.” She was strongly bohemian, a believer in nature spirits and a self-professed psychic. Her first love was music, but she constantly embraced new crafts, latching onto pottery or wood block prints and the like. She was always making things and giving them away as gifts, be it a slightly slumped glazed vase or a knit square of yarn which couldn’t really serve as a blanket. Her creativity was only matched by her lack of focus.
                                                Edmund was also close to a half-brother named Mark. Mark was several years older than Edmund. At some point Mark disappeared mysteriously, only to reemerge in America, in some artist colony somewhere in New England.
                                                Then there were his aunts, his mother’s sisters, who also had a hand in shaping young Edmund. There was Auntie Mary, who was legitimately good at sculpture and was the only one with any money sense. There was Auntie Nelda who was a professional leech. She used her charm and the sufferance of friends and family to get by. And most notably there was Auntie Lori, Edmund’s favorite. Auntie Lori was unpredictable and gregarious. Her main occupation seemed to be travel. She would constantly disappear to some strange corner of the world, only to return with exotic gifts and fantastic stories. There was never a suitable explanation as to how Auntie Lori could afford her globe-trotting life-style, other than the fact that she seemed to live a blessed life. Good things always seemed to happen to her.
                                                When he was a teenager, Edmund discovered that Lori was, in fact, his real mother and Sylvia was the aunt who had raised him. Lori had met a dashing stranger on one of her many adventures and had returned home, after wine, roses and romance, with little Edmund growing inside her. She had made the decision to leave Edmund with her sisters in the hopes that a “more stable” upbringing would be better for him. Edmund was not embittered by this revelation. In fact, it helped explain quite a lot.
                                                As a young man, Edmund somehow managed to scrape together the funds to enter the Edinburgh College of Art at the University of Edinburgh. Living life at the University was a dream come true for him, while the main voice inside his head was telling Edmund to get out and see more of the world. When his studies took him to Spain, the birthplace of Picasso, Edmund simply never came back.
                                                Ever since then Edmund has wandered from country to country and continent to continent, more as a series of accidents than with any real agenda. Like with his mother—his real mother—life seemed to find a way to keep Edmund moving.

                                                * * * * *

                                                Edmund turned twenty in America. He was in Arizona, where the hot desert days faded to cold, windy nights. It was on just such a night that he found himself in a drive-in theater off of some barren stretch of road. The days of the drive-in had come to an end, but the dilapidated property had been bought by an art-house collective. Every year they hosted a film festival where they showed avant-garde and independent films for three days straight. People would come from miles around to camp out for the duration. Inevitably it turned into a bacchanalian carnival.
                                                Edmund had borrowed a convertible from a friend and had driven it down for the film festival. With a cooler, a sleeping bag and a pair of blankets, that car became his home for those three days.
                                                The last night of the festival was amazing. The sky was clear, the stars were intensely bright and a full moon slowly rose over the desert. They were playing a series of films from Latin America, with no subtitles. Edmund didn’t speak Spanish, but there was a vitality and a passion to the films that made the need for language secondary.
                                                He was so enraptured by the screen that he didn’t see the stranger until she leaned a hip against the side of the car door. She had the smoky, sultry mystery of a Mexican beauty. With trim, tan limbs, full, glossy black hair and dangerous eyes, it was as if a Latino goddess had stepped off the screen. She stood there in a halter top and cut-off jean shorts, leaning against the car as though she were in an advertisement. Edmund wondered whether or not she was cold, but felt too embarrassed to voice the question. Without a word this woman reached out an imploring hand to Edmund.
                                                Edmund didn’t know what he was supposed to do. Who was this woman? What was her expectation? After a moment of indecision he handed her the open wine bottle in his hand. He preferred wine to beer. It was more artsy, and generally there was more of it per bottle.
                                                The stranger not only took the wine, but what Edmund was smoking. She took a couple of puffs and then a long drink from the bottle. When she was done she gave a pleased sigh and threw both the wine and the joint dismissively behind her. Edmund made a small noise of dismay, but his complaint was cut off when the stranger climbed over the top of the door and into the car. Seeing as how she had entered from the driver’s side, she ended up straddling Edmund.
                                                Edmund was beginning to feel scared, now. She was so pretty Edmund found it hard to look at her. But given where she was, he didn’t have much of a choice. Her smoldering stare kept him pinned to the seat. Though the position they shared was very provocative, the glint in her eye was one of mischief more than lust.
                                                On the big screen the film continued unheeded.
                                                She spoke for a long moment in the rapid-fire, lyrical speech of American Spanish. Edmund looked left and right, but there was no help forthcoming.
                                                “Um…I don’t speak…that language,” he muttered.
                                                “You, I have been watching,” she switched to English, the hints of her accent as attractive as the rest of her. “You pass through life as the wind passes through the trees. You bask in the sun and soak up the dark; embracing the gifts of life without begrudging what it is you lack. I say to you, the beings of heaven look upon those like you and smile.”
                                                “Um…” Edmund had nothing to say to that. “Your…um…hair looks pretty in the moonlight.” The words just came out on their own.
                                                The stranger smiled.
                                                “What’s this?” she asked. Her slim fingered hand reached down to his jeans. She reached in and pulled out the film festival ticket-stub from his pocket.
                                                “Moonlight is the sister to darkness and candles,” she said. “It is inside intoxicating wine and elusive music; the setting of romance. But it is also the light of lunacy and the harbinger of madness. For your heart of openness and acceptance I bring you ecstasy and insanity; enlightenment beyond the corners of the rational world.”
                                                She spoke in a soft, subtle chant, as though the words belonged to some ancient song or were from some spell older than mortal magic. She kissed the ticket-stub, leaving lipstick marks over the printed words, “Moon-Lite Drive-In,” and slid the slip of paper back into his pocket. Then she started to unzip his jeans.
                                                That was the night Edmund lost his virginity. His recollection of the evening ended up confused but grateful.

It made my heart soar like the boundless winds in the clear blue sky. I crawled onto the bed to kiss her . . . and we didn’t exactly make it to IHOP.
