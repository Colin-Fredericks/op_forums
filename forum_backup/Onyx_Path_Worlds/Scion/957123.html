    08-24-2016, 03:51 PM
    I think it's safe to assume that everyone here is a mythology nerd of one stripe or another, and while the field is inevitably broad there has to be one mythology that grabs you more than the rest. Which are you most excited for, going into the new edition of Scion?

    (For reference: the Greco-Roman, Egyptian, Norse, Hindu, Chinese, Japanese, Algonquian, Yoruba, Aztec, and Irish gods are confirmed for Hero, with the Persian, Vodoun, Mayan, Gaulish, Inuit, Polynesian, Mesopotamian, and Aboriginal Australian pantheons all mentioned in some degree for later products.)

              Hands down the Irish. Scion 1E got me into them back in Grade 10, and now I'm writing my Master's Application to go and do a Celtic Studies program in 2017. They are so great, they break molds, and defy things. The Pantheon never bothered to make the world! That is, apparently, not relevant! We never learn the ancient family lines of the Pantheon, not important! The story is not one of the Gods, it is the story of Ireland, and everyone clamoring to take, and hold the precious gem of a land.

              The Gods die! Not by each other, Mortals show up and kick everyone's teeth in. Sure, it's a hard fought battle and the Tuatha were already badly weakened from the usurpation of Bres, but the Mortals killed the divine. But, the Mortals, are they mortal? They can do things, strange, and powerful, entirely partitioned from Divinity. The Intoxication of the Ulstermen has droves, and droves of these Heroes. I'll leave you with my favorite passage.

              Originally posted by The Intoxication of the Ulstermen
              "I saw a young lad, almost a child, bound and fettered. Three chains round each leg and a chain round each arm; three chains round his neck and seven Men holding each chain, seventy-seven men in all. He turned strongly and powerfully and overthrew the sevenry-seven men, dealing with them as lightly and swiftly as he would have dealt with puffballs. When he perceived the smell of his enemies, when he strick the head of a man against a projecting clod, or against a rock of stone, then that man would say "it is not for valor or glory that this trick is preformed, but by reason of the food and drink in the fort." the lad blushed and fell silent and went about with them until the same wave of savagery overcame him.'

              'Destructive and interactable that description, indeed, said Medb. 'Destructive and interactable the one described,' said Cu Rui. 'He is the son of three champions of whom I spoke a short while ago: Uma son of Remanfissech, Errge Echbel, and Celtchair son of Uthechar. That many of the host are needed to guard him and to restrain his valor when he goes to the land of his enemies. Uanchend Arritech he, and he is only eleven years old, and never has consumed a portion of food that he did not offer to everyone in the house.'"
