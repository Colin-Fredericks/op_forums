                In our games, the mantle of Titan and God are morally neutral ones. There are Gods who are cruel, and brutal, and there are Titans who are benevolent in their own way. A being is given the label of Titan if they are distant from humanity, and are made to appear incredibly powerful. Being antagonistic towards the Pantheon of the culture they are from does tend to help one be labeled Titan over God, but that tends to be because the culture they hail from cast them as distant because of that.

                So, for example, The Morrigan is a Goddess. While she is powerful, cruel, and uncontrollable, she is also incredibly closely tied with the myths of the Tuatha. She is one of the most present, the most involved. And for that, she remains a Goddess. But, on the other hand, Danu is a Titan in our games. She is ancient, distant, and incredibly important to Irish mythology and the Tuatha, despite it being unlikely that she is malevolent. She is a Titan through her colossal importance tied with her incredible distance, from not just humanity, but her Tuath as well.

                There isn't as much an 'opposing team' in the games that have been run inside Scion 2.0 that I am aware of (Iry knows more about all the different games testing it out) in the form of the Titans. For example, in Aynie's game, Butterflies and Hurricanes, the Titans have not been playing an antagonistic role as much as the Pantheons have been playing the nemesis for each other. The Egyptians are on the brink of a civil war, the Teotl have taken over Mexico and immediately become incredibly insular, the Inuit and Aesir were about to march to war before the PCs derailed things, and so on. The Titans in Butterflies range from beings like Apep, desperately trying to consume the sun, to Coeus, who is observing with interest the fulfillment of The Prophecy of Zeus' son.

                        Oh, Prometheus didn't steal fire for the sake of humanity. That's a really common modern misconception of the myths. What has to be remembered through readings of Greek Mythology is that humanity are like gnats. Prometheus stole fire to give it to humanity not for any love of them, but specifically because Zeus said that no one was to do so. It's a rather tricky chronology of events, mostly because it appears bits and pieces were changed over time, but this is Prometheus' timeline.

                        1. Zeus and the Theoi defeat the Titans and capture Olympus, imprisoning Atlas, Prometheus' brother, Iapetus, Prometheus' father, and killing Menoetius, another brother.
                        2. Prometheus teaches humanity how to trick the Gods to give them the worst parts of sacrifices by wrapping the grizzle, and bones in the hide of the animals to spite Zeus for the mistreatment of his family.
                        3. Zeus punishes Prometheus and humanity for this transgression. Prometheus is bound to the mountain and ect. Humanity has fire taken away from them to punish them for attempting to cheat the Gods of their sacrifices.
                        4. Hercules sets Prometheus free some time in the future.
                        5. Prometheus sneaks an ember of The Olympian Hearth out of Olympus in a reed and gives it to humanity.
                        6. Zeus creates Pandora to punish Prometheus by proxy through his brother, and once again punishes humanity by giving them all the horrible things lurking in Pandora's vase.

                        Now, it is incredibly common that people see this act of Prometheus to be benevolent, but that tends to be from people reading modern values of religion into the situation. Gods who care about us are not common, especially to the ancient Greeks. The Illiad describes us as flies, living and dying in a blink. As far as we can tell, due to no worship, veneration, or thanks being given to Prometheus by the Greeks, we have to assume that they did not see Prometheus giving them fire as a act of benevolent kindness on his part. As best we can tell, Prometheus did it as one final act of spite against Zeus for the mistreatment of his family before vanishing from mythological record.

                        People can use Prometheus any way they would like in their games, but as we run things as close to accurate as we can manage, Prometheus didn't do anything for a love of humanity. Prometheus did it because he has a lot of Vengeance. But, a fun plot point could be Prometheus taking advantage of this good press that has materialised for him over the years and using it to continue his vengeful strikes against Zeus, and possibly free his brothers and fathers.

                        Just as a side note. Moloch's flames are probably not regarded as unclean by the Elohim (the Pantheon he is from) as Moloch plays an important part in the dance of life and death. Sure, from a modern standpoint, Holy Christ, he probably had children immolated for him. But, as with all things, we need to view them through the lense of the time. While we don't have exceptionally good records of the Elohim, we can assume that, while Moloch may be a colossal dick, he was doing something so important that people would let their children die in return for it. While whatever that was has been lost to time, and the whole thing may be Roman propaganda in the first place, we always have to remember to look past the base level of things and see the ancient world through their own lense rather than our own. No matter how horrific and, as we would see it, barbaric the practices may have been.

But, what has to be remembered about sacrifices in the ancient world is that they tended to be offering incredibly, incredibly important things. You would sacrifice a cow, an animal worth half a year's work for you, if you were a sustincance farmer for example. If you were a king, you would sacrifice one hundred. Just as any of those, things are important, so are the lives of humans.

                                The symbolism of killing a child, no matter how morbid, can be seen in a thousand different ways. The Romans would wall up a young man from Gaul, and a young woman from Greece inside the walls of special buildings.
