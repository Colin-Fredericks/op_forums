                Originally posted by glamourweaver View Post
                Is being irreparably dismembered and/or imprisoned in the lowest depths of the underworld (you know... where the dead go) substantive different than death? I mean, other than being an even worse experience for the god/Titan in question?
                This actually prompted me to check something out which is kinda interesting! Tartarus and the Grecian Underworld has been used as a prison longer than it was used as an afterlife interestingly! The first mortals, the mortals from the Golden Age of Cronus were ageless, deathless, suffered no sickness, and generally lived perfect lovely lives free from 'bad stuff.' Tartarus and underground had been used as a prison since the time of Ouranos where the Titans, Hundred Handed Ones, and the Three Cyclopses were imprisoned. Hades isn't used as an afterlife until after the reign of Zeus begins. So it's less like the Titans are imprisoned where the dead go, but the dead are kept in the front garden of a traditional super-prison.

                But, on an actual functional level, I don't think being trapped in the underworld is enormously different from being dead, it's essentially like being dead. However, importantly, the Titans don't have their memory wiped, and don't undergo the 'Fading' that the majority of mortal shades do. I suppose it also means they are able to just 'leave' if they can manage to get past the Hundred Handed Ones guarding the door. Where Hades is able to keep the dead in, I expect he alone might struggle to deal with the Titans if they managed to get through the bronze gates.

                The idea of the Theoi, Titans, and their ilk all being immortal is pretty important for Greek cosmology. "The Deathless Gods," and "The Deathless Ones," are terms used with exceptional frequency by the Greeks, they entirely believed their Deities to be beings who are beyond death. It's pretty neat, and I think pretty unique compared to a lot of Pantheons who tend to have their Deities entirely capable of dying given the right situations, mostly due to God-on-God violence.

                                Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post
                                If that's the case, then why the hell aren't mortals siding with the Titans against the Theoi?

                                The Greek Underworld is pretty frickin depressing. Only the best of the best get to go to any thing remotely similar to heaven (Elysian Fields) and you need to do that 3 consecutive times before you're allowed to retire on the Isles of the Blessed.

                                I used to be a huge fan of Greek mythology. But the more I learn about them, the more I realize they're a bunch of giant dicks!

                                The only one who was even remotely worthy of respect was Hades, and the only dickish thing he ever did was against a fellow deity. I mean, unless the mortal intentionally picked a fight with him...
                                That's a good question! I can't speak for the Ancient Greeks, them being dead and all, but I can sort of try to riddle things out on the subject!

                                So, the first thing, the topic of the Afterlife. Afterlives being nice is pretty uncommon, I think I've talked about it before, but the vast majority of them are really not fun places. The idea of paradises for humans to live in after death is a relatively uncommon notion, so the Greeks wouldn't think of it like, "Man our afterlife sucks," but more as "Life is pretty great, being dead sucks." They wouldn't blame the Gods for this necessarily, this is just the way it is. The Greek Underworld is pretty obviously influenced by the Mesopotamian Underworld, but comparatively, the Greek one is pretty awesome! For the Greeks, you have a chance for a nice afterlife. The Mesopotamians, not so much.

                                To build on this, there were ways for people who were really concerned about the afterlife to express this concern in a religious way, though we don't have awesome records on this. You touch on one of these notions in your post, the Mystery Cults. Be they the Eleusinian, Orphic, or some of the other less well attested ones. The details are lost to us, but participating in these cults, one was able to secure a spot in the Lethean Fields for themselves. The Isle of the Blessed was originally reserved for the Capital-H Heroes, but as you mentioned, later traditions which start playing with ideas of reincarnation kick off an idea where dying after gaining entrance to the Lethean Fields three times would stop your reincarnation cycle and let you hang out on the Isle of the Blessed.

                                However, these notions were not... unified, there was a lot of individual interpretation of these things, and individual understanding. Not all Greeks believed in the Orphic reincarnation system for example, some believed the Lethean Fields and the Isle of the Blessed were the same location. As it wasn't a centralized unified religious system, it's a bit all over the place.

                                But, right, anyways. On the death front, the reason mortals wouldn't despise the Gods for it has three major reasons. Reason #1: That's the way it is, it's like getting upset with Gravity. Reason #2: The Gods do offer you a way around it by participating in Mystery Cults (which you may or may not believe in), they are willing to show (?) mercy (?) to mortals or perhaps the ritual activities of the mysteries make a mortal capable of reaching those locations. It's not super clear exactly how it 'worked' like in a theological sense. Reason #3: They didn't necessarily have a concept of a paradise afterlife as a common everyday notion as we do in the modern period due to the influence of the Abrahamic Faiths on our everyday life, you can't long for what you don't believe in.

                                The second thing, the topic of the Theoi being dicks, is sort of more complicated. Think of it this way, myths are fundamentally stories, and stories need bad things to happen in order to be interesting. They need drama, excitement, and the like. The Gods, as the very embodiments of literally everything in existence, are often the catalyst for these stories. So, to us looking back, the Theoi appear to be giant dicks since our only window to them is their 'bad days' more or less. People in Ancient Greece experienced them differently. Every kindness that you were given when you were in need was because Zeus was the enforcer of such things, you knew people needed to follow the laws of Zenea (think like Hospitality) because Zeus was keeping an eye on that shit. Every time you recovered from an illness, hey, someone was looking out for you! When you gave birth and didn't die? It's all cool, you weren't Fated to die! Or maybe Hera or Artemis intervened. You woke up in the nick of time to notice one of your sheep was wondering off, Pan's got your back. You know when you die, you will be judged fairly by the judges of the Underworld. You know that spending all this time as part of the Eleusinian Mysteries means Demeter is making sure you have a good spot in the Underworld.

                                In some of the plays, such as Antigone, we get to see the kind side of the Gods when it comes to death. Her brother Polynices is denied a proper burial, will be denied ritual mourning, and anyone who tries to preform any of these death rituals is going to be killed for it. Antigone ignores the mortal law, and follows the Divine Law, and is punished by mortal hands for it. She is entombed with her dead brother, where she commits suicide before the mortal authorities decide that this actually has gone way too far. The Gods don't take action, but their presence is felt through this idea of the Divine superseding the mortal. The Gods want the dead disposed of properly, they don't want bodies disrespected (which is why the scene between Achilles and the corpse of Hector is a big deal) and their will of this proper treatment of the dead, even if that dead person was a criminal, is more important than whatever your local king thinks. You know that the Gods have your back, that at the end of the day, you're not going to be the one catching the ire of the divine.

                                Edit: I thought of another example of this. When a mortal breaks Divine Law, the Gods actually can sort it all out. When Agamemnon is murdered by his wife and her lover, his son, Orestes, kills his mother and her lover. This is totally in violation of Divine Law, no killing your parents, bad you. The Furies arrive on the scene, and then everything gets complicated. See, Apollo had ordered him to kill his mother for killing his father, so now everything just became a jurisdictional mess more or less. So, in the next play in the trilogy, we see this resolve through what's essentially a court case. Apollo and Athena set up a trial for Orestes is given with mortals voting as a jury. Apollo presents his case, the Furies present their case, the jury is tied, and Athena gives a tie-breaking vote to spare him. The furies are a bit miffed their authority just got overruled, but, the pair of them are favored children of Zeus. And then Athena essentially bribes them by giving them a new name, "The Kindly Ones," essentially going, "Oh you're so nice for sparing him, you are the Kindly Ones now, not the Furies," and they like the sound of that, so they go along with it. So, you can see from this the Theoi actually taking a hands on approach to things, and making sure stuff is fair. Or, well, fair for a Bronze Age society. Or any human society.

                                The Theoi are literally everything, they aren't people, we make them look like people. They're more like abstract notions that we mush into the shapes of people to understand them better, potentially why shapeshifting is such a big part of Greek mythology. They make sure the laws work, they make sure people are born, and that people die. (since when people stop dying in the myth of Thanatos' capture, things go funky) They make sure your animals give birth, that your crops grow, that you catch fish, that you come home safely from war, that you become wealthy, that you got married to your childhood sweetheart. They also will slap you for being a shit, offend them, don't venerate them, you know they'll be annoyed and will probably start making your life hard. However, you know that this is pretty much a fair system. You know that if you're a shit, if the King is shit, if a slave is shit, you're all going to get the ire of the Theoi on you. That's a pretty powerful idea, and one that people tend to like, that something is fair, especially when we live in a fundamentally unfair world.

                                And, lastly. Why wouldn't the Greeks side with the Titans. Well, as we have seen here, the Theoi are... not awesome, but more or less decent for the ancient Greeks. This is the way it's always been, it would be very similar to someone disliking Gravity, or other natural laws. Secondly, the Golden Age of Cronus is... a debated topic by the Greeks. They were never totally sure one way or another if it was a good thing. It was lawless, there were no cities, there was always food, but essentially there wasn't 'society' on a level we would really get. It was just people, wandering around in the nude, just... being. Zeus arriving, ending this, and giving humanity Law generally gets seen as a good move, people like laws, they're predictable, and they can 'get' them.

                                Secondly, the Greeks didn't necessarily side against the Titans, it's just how the world was. You want some good crops? Pray to Demeter, not Cronus. It'd be like filing a complaint with someone who was retired and hadn't worked at the company for a decade, it's pointless, they can't do anything. And you're not about to get them hired back somehow. There would be no point to siding with the Titans, you can't do anything about it, and they (mostly, with exceptions to those not locked up of course) don't have any influence in the world. The interactions between Gods and Humans was because through the Gods, Humans could hope to influence their world around them. They couldn't do that with the imprisoned Titans.

                                Thirdly, mortals probably didn't want the Titans breaking free the same reason I don't want people to start tossing Nukes around. These are beings who can hold up the sky, throw mountains, flood the planet, and they fought once. Here, where you live. And everyone died. So, maybe let's not have a gigantic war that kills us all and needs another generation of humans to be made up by whoever emerges the victor. The Age of Iron isn't great, but hey, at least it's not the Age of Lead. The Titans sure as hell aren't going to be nicer to you, they will also think you are annoying gnats. It would just be the same shit with a different set of names, with an apocalypse added in to kill you and everyone you love.

                                            Naw, the Mexica and the Norse had zero contact with each other. Cultures come up with the same ideas as each other all the time, Agriculture was invented several times all across the world. Bows, smithing, animal husbandry, people worked these things out by themselves all across the world.

                                            Just because two cultures share some concepts doesn't mean they had any contact with each other. The Mexica and the Norse do have ideas of a Warrior Afterlife, but they have pretty much zero similarities asides from that. The Mexica had a whole slew of afterlives, ones for children sacrificed to Tlaloc, one for women who die in childbirth (regarded as equal to men dying in battle), a whole slew of afterlives. The Mexica were becoming a powerhouse in the 14th century, which was several hundred years after the Norse had essentially stopped being Norse and had become Scandinavian, including conversion to Christianity several hundred years beforehand.

                                            For some other examples, the Ashanti and the Irish both have a term for "Long-Hand," as sort of a title for a person, but they don't have any cultural contact, they are separated by a thousand years. The Japanese, the Yoruba, the Ashanti, and the Haida all have notions of Divine Possession, but they're not at all connected culturally. (except the Yoruba and the Ashanti who are very physically and chronologically close to each other)

                                            Essentially, in order to be able to say that two cultural groups had a connection, you need really solid evidence. Back in the Bad Ol' Days of Western Academia, they loved going, "Oh, Cultural-Group-X was clearly influenced by the European-Cultural-Group because of the notion of XYZ," since they couldn't possibly conceive that Europeans weren't the tip-top best and everyone wanted to copy them. Lots of claiming that African religions were actually just practicing a misunderstood version of Christianity, Greek Religion, or Mesopotamian religion. Due to this, suggestions of cultural connections need to be really iron clad. The Mexica and the Norse don't have any evidence of contact, their timelines don't sync up, no archaeological or literary references, it's a solid bet to say that there was never any contact.

                                            Now, sometimes there are legitimate cultural connections we can see. The Greeks drew heavily from their surrounding neighbors, the Hittites, the Mesopotamians, and the Canninites. The Indo-European cultures have a thing about cattle and virgins. We have really, really strong evidence of these connections though, including linguistic links, chronological syncing, archaeological findings, all that sort of stuff supporting it.

                                            Good job seeing connections though! Desperate to make sure people learn from our own mistakes.
