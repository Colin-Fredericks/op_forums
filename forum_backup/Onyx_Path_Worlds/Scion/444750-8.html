                It's pretty well accepted that the various Indo-European werewolf mythologies descend from the memory of (Proto-Indo-European on) warbands that held the wolf as a totem and saw manifesting it as a mark of initiation. In some cultures, like the Hellenic peoples, this gradually became unacceptable as civilisation progressed (probably most famously remembered in the tale of Lycaon); in others, like Germanic cultures, it held on until Christianity took over and called it devilry (less than a thousand years ago, in some places; probably most famously remembered by its cousin, bear-shirting). In almost all cases, it continued to lurk in the psyche of the various peoples (which is why we have things like werewolf games). :P

                The wolf was so important to our (linguistic, for those of you who aren't Germanic) ancestors that we don't even call it by name..."wolf" is an example of taboo deformation, wherein something is so holy/respected/feared/adored that a euphemism is used to refer to it in common parlance instead of the actual term for the thing.

                            Hecate is a Greek Goddess, not a creation of Shakespeare.

                            More modern figures are pretty easy to explain as simply being re-interpretations (by mortals) of older figures. There may not actually be anyone anything like Santa Claus. Why would there be, when mythic creatures have been absent from the world for hundreds or thousands of years? Ditto Tooth Fairy, Jack Frost, etc. Now, it would be easy for a fae, elemental or nature spirit to slip into the role of Jack Frost, Johnny Appleseed or whatever, but it isn't necessarily that mortal perceptions caused that to happen. Instead it'd be an example of the vast power of human imagination to take something that once was real, and change the stories of it to suit the times. That power does not need to actually affect the mythological figures involved, though. Hades isn't the Greek Devil, no matter how amazing James Woods was in the Disney movie.

                            I'm stilly truly hoping that at it's core, Scion will remain a game about mythology instead of an urban fantasy kitchen sink like Dresden Files, Supernatural or Grimm. Those are all great series (ok, maybe not GREAT... Supernatural...) but they're not about mythology. Even American Gods is about modern mythology clashing with the old ones. It's a setting where mythological figures do get their power, their very existence, from the stories, prayers and beliefs of humanity. So is The Iron Druid Chronicles. That's a really cool idea... but it isn't how the ancients ever thought of anything. Zeus didn't exist to the Athenians because they believed in him. He existed because he existed. He was real. If they stopped worshipping him he would smite them, not lose power.

                            Truly and really, I appreciate the idea of wanting to use a system you enjoy to play every kind of folklore creature. But there are tons of systems that do that really, really well. There are not tons of systems that just do mythology. Scion 2E might actually do that. Is it limiting to say that Scion should just be about religions instead of fairytales and folklore? Yeaaah. But, then, it's limiting to say that Vampire: the Masquerade should be about playing vampires.

                            Now, I do want to point out that I went through the WW2 Pantheons and converted every single figure in them into something that actually fits into Scion. John Henry was a Scion of Hercules who died before reaching Legend 2. He's now a ghostly Guide to other Greek Scions. Betsy Ross was a woman who was granted immortality by a Colonial-era Scion using the Health Purivew, or a Relic. The Citizen is a very powerful ghost who acts a Guide to Russian Scions, possessing mortal soldiers to fulfill his duty to the Motherland. Paul Bunyan is a Jotun who broke free of the Titans' influence. Pecos Bill is a Demigod Scion. Rosie the Riveter is an Avatar of Bridget. Robin Hood is a Demigod. Madame Gullotine is an Avatar of Andarta. It's actually pretty easy to convert that sort of figure into a Scion-friendly form. They just won't be on the same level as Zeus or Odin. That's fine. They shouldn't be. Neither should Santa or the Tooth Fairy. They can still fit, but they should fit into the mythological framework. The framework shouldn't be warped to fit them.

                            I'm a professor! Why is no one listening to me?!

                                                    What I found kind of cool about Scion was the idea that, as Telgar said, Zeus exists because he exists, not because he was believed in. But, on the other hand, it's been a long time since he was a major power in the world what with the withdrawal of the gods (for the most part, and for their own reasons). A large part of Scion's coolness to me was that the gods were going to come back into the public consciousness, yes, but they would have to do so in a way that remained relevant. Because the rampages that many of them were famous for when they didn't get their way just isn't possible in modern day Scion. Back in the old days it was fine if Zeus wanted to smush Athens into putty. Odin and Horus would all be sitting back going 'Dude wants to blow up his own house what's it to me?'. Now, though, the world is so interspersed and intermixed. When Zeus wanst to throw a hissy fit to get (what he thinks of as) his rightful dues in downtown New York he can't just throw lightning all over the place without half a dozen other pantheons wanting to intervene because it's not just Greeks down there. Gods, in order to get the worship (or at least the respect) they want from a fairly jaded populace are going to have to adapt the way they do things to prevent being ignored by the people or checked by other gods in a mixed world.

                                                    The folklore vs mythology thing has a line down the middle. It's a hard thin line in some places and broad and fuzzy in other places. Certainly I would have to oppose the idea of, say, Pecos Bill existing because mortals believe in him. But a crafty supernatural entity taking on the role to feed off that belief for their own ends (glory, worship, attention, what ever) and pretending that it was them the whole time ("Well, son, I've always been here. Y'all just ain't needed my help for a while is all.") isn't that hard an idea to sell. Not saying that you need to assign every folklore hero a role (one of the things that always cheesed me off about oWoD is their notion that every major figure in the history of ever was secretly a supernatural creature of some kind) but it's certainly not inconcievable that it would happen. They don't need to be gods in their own right (and why would they be if gods have to be born, not believed into existance) or even Scions. Lots of creatures have a good reason to take on the role.

                                                    Of course for a giggle imagine a Scion of Odin staring slack jawed when he finds out that for fun and games Odin likes to play Santa Claus every so often and they catch dear ol' dour, serious, stuffy, dad in his jolly outfit sneaking toys to kids.
