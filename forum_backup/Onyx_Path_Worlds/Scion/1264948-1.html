                I’m not invested in what you do at your table, I’m discussing the arguments for what is consistently workable in the game.

                The Yazatas are also angelic servants of Ahura Mazda, the sole actual God in Zoroastrianism.

                Most of the Tuatha De Danann were never worshipped as gods. Nor was Loki. Trying to delineate which numinous high Legend being is and is not a “god” seems more a matter of theology than Myth, which is emphatically not the point of Scion.

                The Abrahamic traditions are very different from each other (particularly Christianity being as heavily Hellenized as it is compared to the more purely Semitic theologies of Judaism and Islam - thanks Paul). So are the diverse religions that Europeans collectively labeled “Hinduism”. The latter share a pantheon because of the shared mythic narrative of the Vedas*. Why would it be less accurate for the former to share a Pantheon because of the shared mythic narrative of Eden, Abraham, Exodus....


                *Note that despite a shared regional origin, Buddhism gets a separate Pantheon (even aside from individually Buddhist gods in other Pantheons like Guanshiyin and Hachiman) because it doesn't use the Vedas as its holy narrative, while that's the ultimate uniting thread for the diverse traditions that are still grouped together as "Hinduism".

                                        Originally posted by Resplendent Fire View Post

                                        This is not particularly a fight I want to get involved in, but I will lean in since Iceblade mentioned it and just to prevent any misunderstandings. While Túatha Dé does mean 'God Peoples' in Old Irish [though for this context I prefer túath as 'tribe'] we have zero evidence of any worship of any of the figures, and we can prove that several of them are made up well into the medieval period such as Ler. There is a very good book about this called Ireland's Immortals by Mark Williams, but I can give a quick breakdown.

                                        Essentially, when you think about it, it's nonsensical that all of Ireland would somehow have a unified Pantheon. A bunch of interwarring tribal factions semi-organized into small confederations constantly at each other's throats does not breed a unified religious system, same as what we see in Gaul. It appears (though our ability to say this is incredibly hazy) that each tribal group likely had its own collection of figures with maybe a small scant handful that crossed between tribal boundaries.

                                        Modern scholarship in Celtic Studies has abandoned the theory that the Irish Sagas are clearly representative of anything outside of the era they were being recorded in. The Túatha Dé Danann and their stories are a Christian era-recreation of a 'Pagan' past (keep in mind the earliest sagas we have are from 200 years post Christianization where the latest are nearly 1,000 years post) which was based on a desire for local stories. Some of these stories may have had a basis, though a particularly dodgey one, in pre-Christian belief, but the vast majority of them are solidly not.

                                        The Saga authors almost never reference the Túatha Dé as Gods even [Yes, Cormac's Glossary suggests it for one of the four Manannáns and that one description of The Dagda suggest it but that's a whole different kettle of fish], not even as Gods who shouldn't be worshiped. The more popular explanations for them at the time of the sagas are either 'Unfallen Humans' (don't ask how that could have happened but it was one of their ideas), and 'Half-Fallen Angels' (Angels who were neutral during The War in Heaven and thus fall but not as far as the rebels). The idea that the Túatha Dé might be worshiped never comes up in the sagas even, and the vast majority of their Cosmology is based on Biblical cosmology. The TDD can trace their ancestry back to Noah I believe using the geneology traditions in the Book of Invasions.

                                        So, Christian authors at the time never acknowledged that they were worshiped, and probably made about 90% of them up after the fact to make an 'appropriately pagan past' after it had mostly been obliterated. The word for 'God' being part of their name doesn't really suggest anything, CúChulainn isn't a dog even though he has 'dog' in his name, it's that sort of thing. We don't even have any archeological evidence for worship of the Túatha Dé.
