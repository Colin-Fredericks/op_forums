                            It certainly does create an interesting position, that being the nature of divinity itself. Do you need a 'special something' or can enough mortal worship do it? Is mortal worship of any use at all or is it crucial? Is someone like, say, Genghis Khan a god (or a demigod) based on his legend, or is it simply that people have convinced themselves that he must be even though in reality he isn't. What might that mean in either direction? Like, imagine running a campaign where the antagonists (or even the player characters!) are people who seek go obtain divinity and the means in which they go to get it. And, whether, in the end they end up with a bunch of gods going 'Nicely done. Welcome to the gang.' or whether they stand around laughing as the ones who have done so much find that it was all for naught.

                            Certainly it might well put the gods into a difficult position in this modern age, with its social media, where dozens, hundreds even, of men and women are being worshipped in such mighty numbers at a time as to suddenly flood the market with nascent deities. And what do the established gods do about it?

                                        Originally posted by Trippy View Post
                                        White Wolf games have been offending certain Christian groups since they were founded. Vampire’s lore is based on Christian myths. I doubt it will make any difference here, truth be told.
                                        And then there was a little game called Demon…

                                        I know that it can be done wrong, as illustrated in Scion 1e (where the Abrahamic faiths were either the minions of the implicitly malevolent Egyptian Titan or a mortal conspiracy to enslave gods).

                                        But I don't think that the driving concern is our should be “is there going to be bad publicity if we do this?” For better or worse, Onyx Path and its games aren't going to get the same cultural spotlight that Vampire: the Masquerade got in the 90s. Heck, even by the time that the aforementioned Demon: the Fallen came out in the early 00s, White Wolf's pop culture status was fading, and the supposed Christian backlash against the game existed more within its advertising campaign than in reality.

                                        No; in this case, “doing it wrong” has more to do with failing to respect the religion, in the form of insufficient authenticity — much like Scion 2e has made course corrections on several of the 1e Pantheons in an effort to get the myths right. That said, the same thing that I said in another thread applies here, too: it's entirely possible to be excessively respectful. Scion is a game, and compromises for the sake of playability if nothing else are too be expected.

                                        It helps that 2e has a better toolkit to work with, most notably Mantles, Primordials, and Chosen Scions. But there's also something about how Pantheons are formed in 2e, with a retconning of the World's history so that their own origin stories become true. This lets you write up each Pantheon on its own terms without having to point out that that's not what “really happened”: all myths are true, even opposing ones; though the latter tends to manifest as inter-Pantheon tensions rather than internal Pantheon inconsistencies (e.g., the Canaanites say one thing; the Abrahamics say another), or possibly through sub-Pantheons.

                                        Bottom line: I think it's doable, but I appreciate the desire to either do it right or not do it at all.
