                    Hmm...Lets see; We've got the horned serpent, Thunderbird, underwater panther, lets go further:

                    Cougar
                    In North America, mythological descriptions of the cougar have appeared in the stories of the Hocąk language ("Ho-Chunk" or "Winnebago") of Wisconsin and Illinois and the Cheyenne, amongst others. To the Apache and Walapai of Arizona, the wail of the cougar was a harbinger of death.The Algonquins and Ojibwe believe that the cougar lived in the underworld and was wicked, whereas it was a sacred animal among the Cherokee.                    .

                    Wolves
                    Some tribes, such as the Nunamiut of northern and northwestern Alaska and the Naskapi of Labrador respected the wolf's hunting skill and tried to emulate the wolf in order to hunt successfully. Others see the wolf as a guide. The Tanaina of Alaska believed that wolves were once men, and viewed them as brothers.
                    In the Cardinal directions of the Plains Indians, the wolf represented the west, while for the Pawnee, it represented the southeast.
                    The Netsilik Inuit and Takanaluk-arnaluk believed that the sea-woman Nuliayuk's home was guarded by wolves. The Navajo people feared witches in wolves' clothing called "yee naaldlooshii", literally "with it, he goes on all fours". Wolf in Navajo is mąʼiitsoh- lit. "large coyote". Wolves were feared by the Tsilhqot'in, who believed that contact with wolves would result in nervous illness or death.

                    Spider
                    North American cultures have traditionally depicted spiders. The Native American Lakota people's oral tradition also includes a spider-trickster figure, which is known by several names. The spider is also present as the deity Iktomi, which is occasionally depicted in this form.In Native American mythology, the spider is also seen in the legend about the birth of the constellation Ursa Major. The constellation was seen as seven men transformed into stars and climbing to paradise by unrolling a spider's web. The Hopi have the creation myth of Spider Grandmother. Spider Grandmother also plays an important role in the creation mythology of the Navajo, and there are stories relating to Spider Woman in the heritage of many Southwestern native cultures as a powerful helper and teacher                    .

                    Owls
                    In the culture of the Uto-Aztec tribe, the Hopi, taboos surround owls, which are associated with sorcery and other evils. The belief that owls are messengers and harbingers of the dark powers is also found among the Hočągara (Winnebago) of Wisconsin. When in earlier days the Hočągara committed the sin of killing enemies while they were within the sanctuary of the chief's lodge, an owl appeared and spoke to them in the voice of a human, saying, "From now on the Hočągara will have no luck." This marked the beginning of the decline of their tribe. An owl appeared to Glory of the Morning, the only female chief of the Hočąk nation, and uttered her name. Soon afterwards she died. People often allude to the reputation of owls as bearers of supernatural danger when they tell misbehaving children, "the owls will get you." Also, in the native Cherokee culture, as well as many other Native American cultures, owls are a very bad omen. It is said that if you are outside in the broad day light and an owl flies over your head a family member or loved one would die within the coming week.

                                                Yep, that's what I initially set out to try to do when I started the thread. In the previous edition, they seemed to have a pantheon for each different area of the World. The Greeks were part of Mediterranean Europe, the Aesir was Northern or Scandinavian Europe (with the Tuatha representing the island of Ireland), The Egyptians were Saharan Africa and the Loa were sub-Saharan Africa, with a touch of America and Caribbean. The Yazata and the Deva had their own spot within the Middle East, and the Bureaucracy and the Amatsukami ruling the Asian continent with China and Japan respectively. The Aztecs take most of Latin America and the entirety of South America. The only two places that were untouched, since the Atlanteans once ruled what is now Antarctica, are Australia, and North America, (even though there was a slight hint of a pantheon there with the Yankee Pantheon, I don't think that anyone would want to use it for reasons.)

                                                Now that Scion is more open, I thought that a respectful pantheon dealing with the Totemic spirits of Native American mythology. The difficulty that is faced here was the fact that I barely knew anything about the different pantheons in general (given that the info the general population knew was "flawed"), and yet I also wanted to use the familiar for those players who aren't Native American mythophiles like some of the more educated posters here on this forum. Face it. If you say the name of the fox god Whyoloo (?), the normal player would at first wonder if it's the name of a character from My Little Pony or a new type of Pokemon. That's why, with the outpouring of authenticity from the posters, I decided to use Kokopelli as a base for a possible pantheon. Just like Baron Samedi is one of the most recognized Loa by the general public, most people in America are familiar with the name and form of Kokopelli, due to his form being placed on souvenir pottery, shirts and mugs to represent anything Western.

                                                If they are willing to make a full-blown book on Native American pantheons, I wonder how many of the pantheons can fit into the book without it seemingly overstuffed? I mean, they would have to need room for the Pantheon Purview, the Overworld and Underworld, Axis Mundi, and the Titan (if there's just one) that is currently plaguing the gods of North America. Given how many tribes are represented, I'm worried that certain ones will be cut from the roster.
