    ​Well, assuming that the Bible in Scion is exactly like the Bible we know, then the Jews may still get the blame for the death of Jesus. An important fact that often gets overlooked is that the Theoi worshippers who actually nailed Jesus to the cross did so essentially at the bidding of the Jewish religious leadership. Pontius Pilate interrogated Jesus and found no real fault with him. He was never formally charged with any crime under Roman law.

    ​As was customary back then, Pilate gave the Jewish people an annual choice of convicted criminal to be released. He offered them the choice of Jesus: a man with whom he personally found no fault, and Barabas: a convicted murderer and rebel. The people chose Barabas and demanded that Jesus be executed. They didn't have the legal authority to condemn him to death themselves, so Pilate had the choice of carry out the execution himself, or face a full-scale riot from his subjects. Such riots broke out fairly frequently in Judea, and Pilate was under orders to keep things quiet. Hence? The decision to execute Jesus. Pilate, of course, washed his hands of the whole matter and refused to accept any responsibility for Jesus' death.

    ​The Theoi worshippers were merely the "tools" who carried out the will of the Jewish people, so I'm guessing the Jews would still get at least the majority of the blame. You could even argue that Jesus' declaration "Father, forgive them. They know not what they do." could've been Jesus personally absolving the men who killed him. Heck, technically, a Theoi worshipper (Pilate) was the first person to ever proclaim Jesus "The King of the Jews."

                        As I understand it, the key points in Christian development were:

                        · Jesus' ministry, a three-year period where he went around performing miracles and teaching his disciples, preparing them for what would come next
                        · the birth of the Church, which started with the events of Easter (from Palm Sunday through his crucifixion, Resurrection, and Ascension), and culminating in the day of Pentecost, when the Holy Spirit entered his followers, empowering them to perform the sorts of miracles that Jesus had been known for. This was followed by a period of persecution when the Jewish leadership attempted top stamp out what they considered a new heresy.
                        · The destruction of the Jewish Temple 40 years later. This was when the persecution of the Church shifted from being Jewish to being Roman. It's also when the divide between Judaism and Christianity really kicked in: before this event, Christianity was considered to be a branch of Judaism, and after this event, Christianity was seen more and more as its own thing.
                        · Constantine's conversion to Christianity, which ended the Roman persecution of the Church. Note that when Constantine legitimized Christianity, that didn't start the persecution by Christianity of other faiths; for a while there, Christianity coexisted more or less peacefully with other religions.
                        · If I recall correctly, the Council of Nicea was the point when Christianity started to develop an intolerance for other religions. It was intended to establish what the Christian orthodoxy was, so that it wouldn't become watered down and meaningless; but it had the side effect of not just purging heresies from the Church, but also viewing other religions as false and harmful and thus to be opposed (whereas previously, the other religions were merely viewed as wrong, and the extent of “hostility” from Christianity came from efforts to recruit non-Christians).

                        There's more, but I think the above covers the major points where other religions are concerned. For Scion, If be inclined to alter aspects of how the Council of Nicea played out. Meanwhile, in keeping with the “All Myths are True” aspect of Scion, I'd keep as much as possible as literal as possible, and say that the Biblical account is accurate as far as Judaism and Christianity go.
