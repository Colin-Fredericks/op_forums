    Originally posted by Xanthias View Post
    Hmm. Since discussing real-life religion is obviously not the topic of this thread, I'll go ahead and assume this is about storytelling.
    And in stories, what makes a divine being different from mortals is not always power (although it is an important attribute). Tales of gods failing at something, or having to work hard for their goals, are too widespread for that.
    I believe what we should be looking at is - the awe they inspire. The sheer metaphysical importance of these beings. Because, first and foremost, they are numinous. And they matter in the grand scheme of things.
    This does not mean they are unapproachable or alien. They still have personalities, strengths and weaknesses, they act - and it's not fake. But there is always more. There is always something transcendent hidden by the mist.
    When Inanna disappears, when Demeter misses her daughter, when Telipinu is in hiding - the world itself is diminished. When Indra killed Vritra - it was much more than a victory against a monster, or the end of a physical drought.
    The level on which gods operate is one we can touch only in moments of great inspiration. And, while a character in a story may forget, the storyteller should keep this in mind.

                                Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post

                                And there are lots of other pantheons such as the Greeks, Hindu's and Chinese that specifically talk about an elixer of immortality in some fashion or another (Ambrosia, golden apples, Amrita, est). It could be offered to mortals who prove themselves worthy enough to receive its blessing.
                                Amrita, similarly, technically grants Immortality, not necessarily Divinity. It's kinda vague as to what it does, since it only really appears in one myth, the Churning of the Ocean, in which the sage Durvasa is angered by Indra's disrespect and curses him and all his court of Vedic deities to lose their immortality. The Trimurti advise Churning the Primordial Ocean to retrieve Amrita which will restore Immortality to them, and after many shenanigans including teeming up with the Asura and Vishnu variously manifesting his Kurma (Turtle) and Mohini (Enchantress) Avatara, while also finding the time to get married to Lakshmi, succeed.

                                There are a few Indian folk deities that are deified mortals, but these are rare and/or extremely local. By and large any deities that are born mortal and became Gods later in life are considered Avatara of one God or another...whether these are later interpretations tacked on to once purely mortal origin deities, or if that was always the case is usually difficult or impossible to determine at this point.

                                Mortals DO become gods in the Vedas...the most famous of which are the Ribhus, a trio of brothers who achieved Divinity by their mastery of Craftsmanship...specifically, they are said to have created the ladles and other implements, out of the single existing implement of the God Tvastar, used during the Yagya (fire sacrifice) that is the cornerstone of Vedic religion. The Ribhus even have a few hymns dedicated to them in the Rig Veda. Ila, daughter of Manu is similarly deified for having laid down the orginal rules of sacrifice (this is probably the same Ila who in later Hinduism is reimagined as the genderfluid ancestress of the Lunar Dynasty).

                                With the rise of the Trimurti and Tridevi and the considerable lessening of importance of most of the Vedic deities, the idea of mortals becoming Gods gets mostly phased out...now mortals strive to become Sages in service to the two Triads (or whichever member of such or their extended family you personally prefer)...and Trimurti focused Hindu myths and scripture tend to regularly show off said Sages overpowering the Vedic era Gods (such as the time the Shiva worshiping Durvasa stripped the Gods of their immortality (see above), or the time the Sarasvati worshiping Vishvamitra created a new Svarga with it's own set of Gods because Indra annoyed him). Hell, whereas the Rig Veda only ever mentions three Worlds...Bhur, the Earth where mortals live, Svar, Heaven where Indra and the other Deva dwell, and Bhuvar, Space, which is basically the wind and firmament and empty space in between Heaven and Earth, later Puranic Cosmology adds three entire Realms on top of Svarga for Sages in service to the Trimurti and Tridevi to find a place in. Even better, at the end of every set of Four Yugas (a Manvantara), all the realms upto and including Svarga are destroyed...but not the ones above it, so while Indra and his court will die at the end of each Manvantara, the Sages in the higher realms will be fine and dandy.

                                Ok, that was supposed to be a post about mortals becoming deities in Hinduism and somehow meandered off tobecome a post about how thoroughly modern day Trimurti and Tridevi focussed Hinduism tends to love trashing the Vedic deities. My apologies.

                                Getting back on track...

                                Speaking from a game perspective, most Sages in Hinduism are clearly (IMO) just Scions of the Trimurti and Tridevi with the Chosen Origin who have achieved God level Power and just don't self-identify as Gods. Hell, their 'choosing of disciples' might just be how they refer to Choosing Scions of their own.

                                Tl;dr : Amrita makes you immortal, not necessarily Divine. Most exceptional Mortals in Hinduism are likely to get Chosen by the Gods as Scions instead of being deified outright. Do not piss off a Sage.
