    My working headcanon for Scion right now is the default state of the Underworld, as one vast dimension divided into different territories, is bleak and desolate - but some gods however create paradise pockets in them through death & rebirth cycles bringing life/light/etc to the Underworld. Osiris' death & resurrection, followed by underworld enthronement created A'aru. Persephone's annual trips gradually grew a garden touched by her experiences in the world above that became Elysium. Etc.

    That's why earlier and later Greek myth are so different with regard to the Underworld. It was originally utterly desolate, but Persephone gradually changed it over time. This song does a good job capturing the role Persephone plays with regard to her husband's subjects: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-7YKDgkVWvo

    (note: Valhalla is not an underworld paradise; the Valkyries are just bringing select souls to Asgard itself)


    That's kind of what the Over / Under Worlds are. They're all part of a vast, infinite space that the various pantheons found a place to set down roots and build their territories. When a new pantheon emerges, they locate an unoccupied spot in this expanse of infinity and build their realms from scratch.

    In the Scion cosmology, you can actually walk from Olympus to Asguard if you were so inclined. Might take you centuries to do so, but it is still physically possible, because they exist within the same plane of reality as each other. The original Tartatus was located in the center of each pantheon's Underworld, which were built around the outside of it like spokes on a wheel.

    When the Titans broke free, it scattered their Underworlds, so now they're isolated from each other.

                First, to directly address the topic this thread is about, in my 1E games, I regularly had the discovery and/or creation of God-slaying weapons. This is basically a magical alloy that reacts poorly to the presence of ichor, thus making it more brutal to those of higher Legend. I can't recall off the top of my head what I used as the ingredients for it, but I figured that the basic premise could still be useful. It was always rare, though, and usually created by Ixion. If obtained in large amounts, it could be quite dangerous in the hands of mortals. Of course, your average God won't just stand there and let you take a swing at them with it, so it wasn't exactly an "I win" button, but it definitely helped.

                Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post
                If that's the case, then why the hell aren't mortals siding with the Titans against the Theoi?
                Why would they automatically assume that things would be better with them? The Titans overthrew the previous generation, then tried to keep the next generation down. The Theoi weren't seen as these horrible usurpers that overthrew the noble Titans so that they could stomp all over humanity. The overthrow of the Titans was seen as a good thing in Greek mythology. Sure, Prometheus seemed to be on our side, but how many of the others favored humankind?

                Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post
                The Greek Underworld is pretty frickin depressing. Only the best of the best get to go to any thing remotely similar to heaven (Elysian Fields) and you need to do that 3 consecutive times before you're allowed to retire on the Isles of the Blessed.
                This assumes that A) the ancient Greeks would have had the thought that a heavenly afterlife for everyone was even possible and B) that all you had to do to get into it was start worshiping different Gods. I don't believe that heavenly afterlives were that common in the various mythologies of the world. Hell, I don't even think souls going to Heaven came into Christianity until a bit later on, though I'd need to track down my sources for that. I believe that early Judeo-Christian teachings said that we basically slumbered within our bodies until the end of the world, when we'd be bodily resurrected. I think even the doctrine of the soul was adopted from some of the Greek philosophers. People who assume that the afterlife works a certain way are unlikely to think otherwise just because they encounter someone who believes something different. If the ancient Greek hears someone saying, "Come worship my God! Forsake yours, and you will have an eternal paradise after you die," they wouldn't necessarily be able to just toss aside everything they grew up with and embrace this novel idea. They'd probably be more likely to think, "Yeah, sure. I can go and worship your God, and then when I die, my Gods will be mad at me for neglecting them on top of everything else. No thank you." Even today, afterlife situation is not necessarily the best way to go shopping for religion. People generally seem to believe that the afterlife follows the same rules for everyone, regardless of their belief system. I don't hear a lot of people saying, "Why would you want to reincarnate when you could come to Heaven with me instead?" I'm more likely to hear someone saying "burn in Hell for all eternity" than "reincarnate" in that sentence. Someone needs to believe in a given afterlife before they can begin worrying about how they're going to deal with it.

                The Gods did good things for people, too. If you go to www.Theoi.com, you'll see that they also gave blessings to many individuals. It wasn't all wrath, and even some of the wrath incidents could be seen as totally justified. Just look at Lycaon. I'm sure if we could look at it through the ancient Greek cultural lens, even more of the wrath would probably seem just.

                Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post
                The only one who was even remotely worthy of respect was Hades, and the only dickish thing he ever did was against a fellow deity. I mean, unless the mortal intentionally picked a fight with him...
                Really? Okay, maybe my memory's a bit foggy. What did Hestia do that rendered her unworthy of respect? Iris? Hebe? While I agree that Hades deserves a lot more respect than he's given in the modern day, I find it odd to say that he's the only one deserving of it. Of course, I respect them all, so that's to be expected.

                Originally posted by glamourweaver View Post
                Gods being dicks is pretty universal among ancient pantheons. It’s not the case with (for example) modern Hindu deities because said religion kept up with moral evolution of civilization, instead of us just looking at an ancient snapshot of cultural values as with a long dead belief system.
                This. Thank you. While I disagree about it being a long dead belief system, there definitely something to be said about the evolution of myth over time, especially in relation to the mortal culture around it. Even the Christian God was depicted as being pretty dickish in the early texts, but most of the believers in him today don't look at him that way. How different would these myths and the depictions of the Gods be today if they had remained at the center of major cultures for an extra 2000 years?
