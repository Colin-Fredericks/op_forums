    Originally posted by Neall View Post

    1e Virtues worked like they did in 2e Exalted - they were 4 arrangements (from a list of 16) objective values that a pantheon believed in. Courage, Piety, that sort of thing. The problem was that they failed to encompass a lot of what the pantheons actually valued and bringing them up in play was difficult.

    2e Virtues effectively describe a warring (if false) dichotomy in the pantheon, what two issues repeatedly bring it into conflict. For the Norse, it's Fatalism (our lives are woven by the Norns, and we are fated to our end) and Audacity (Fate can go fuck itself! Drengskapr ahoy!). For the Devas, it's Conscience and Duty, or two warring interpretations on "what is right" of individual judgment and proper action. The Tuatha, Honor and Prowess; the Manitou, Pride and Purpose.

    You may notice that the Virtues don't always conflict in a lot of situations, and that's intentional. A tuning fork has two tines, but they're part of the same fork, and they point in the same direction.
    Does acting according to a virtue helps you gain positive Fatebindings?

                        08-25-2016, 09:15 PM
                        Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
                        I would argue that the core dichotomy of the Netjer would be Order/Chaos, except none of the deities have anything to do with Chaos other than Set/Sutek (and even he bows to Maat when push comes to shove) Apep, and various demons. Anyone else have any thoughts?
                        A lot Pantheons have an Order / Chaos breakdown like the Netjer. The Theoi have the Rule of Zeus v. the Rule of Cronus, along with their City v. Rurality split both centered on Order-Chaos themes respectively. The Shen are very concerned with the will of Tian and the results of periods of confusion. The Bogovi bound themselves with Law and Legality and then struggle to live up to their own ideals. The Teotl put every last drop of their power and energy into fueling the Fifth World and protecting it from the beings who wish to destroy it.

                        However, it looks like from the examples Neall's given here, they're examining things in more detail. Additionally, in the five examples given here by Neall, the two Virtues are not a Good v. Bad thing (though, I know very little about the Algonquin, so I can't speak for the Manitou example given) but a complicated balance of two highly valued, but sometimes contradictory socital values. Since I know the Irish the best, I can give a breakdown.

                        Honor is key, your honor, be you warrior, bard, man, woman, king, or pig farmer, is you, more than you are you. Your honor is what everyone will know, even if they don't know you. Misdeeds stain you, and great acts elevate you. Prowess ties deeply into this, everyone must be great. If you read Irish mythology, you will lose track of how many, "Most skilled warriors," or "most beautiful men or women of Ireland" you read about. Being good isn't important, anyone can be good, you must be the best. This will tie in to your honor, these two things deeply entwined.

                        But, they can at times clash, with dangerous results. The story of Bricriu's Feast is a great example of this. The Heroes of Ulster are all at a feast (along with the rest of Ulster) and the Hero's Portion is great indeed. Whomsoever gains this Hero's Portion will surely gain all others for it is the greatest (Prowess again, for the host, which converts to honor) ever to be seen. So, three of the Heroes of Ulster claim it. Crowd favorite, Cu Chulainn, along with Conall Cernach, and Loegaire Buadach. The portion ought to go to the greatest of them (Prowess) so whoever doesn't get it will be recognized as lesser than the winner (Honor) which results in the three Heroes falling to outright battle during the feast attempting to kill each other. During this same story, these three Hero's wives face a similar struggle, as whomsoever enters the feasting hall first will surely be the first to enter all others and lead the women of Ulster for it is the grandest hall to be seen. So, they turn upon each other.

                        The conflict is only resolved (the Hero's conflict, Cu Chulain's wife Emer wins her contest pretty handily IIRC) after a long series of trials, running into Mabd, Anilil, The Dagda, with the heroes always arguing that the previous test proved nothing. Only when a stranger arrives daring the three to chop off his head and allow him to do the same the next day, Cu Chulain is shown to be the bravest and the strongest, being awarded the portion. (You will note that this is very Gawain and the Green Knight-y. This is probably a proto-version)

                        So, I'd guess less Order and Chaos, and more... not sure, I don't know the Netjer well enough. But, I don't know if Chaos would be one of theirs. It doesn't seem to be a good v. bad, but a matter of internal cultural value conflict.
