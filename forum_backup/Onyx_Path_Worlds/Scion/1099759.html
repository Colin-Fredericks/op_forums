    Mythological Disagreements

    06-03-2017, 01:35 AM
    If one attempts to study Greek and Egyptian Religious beliefs in a concerted way (as I am doing now in an attempt to better understand @atamajakki's point better regarding the Egyptian write up), one can not help but realize that the scholarship between the two often do not agree with each other. Some scholars say that they developed independently while others say that most of the Greek gods were derived from the Egyptians. And that's just the beginning as each side claims to be originators of different arts and sciences in the ancient world. The list goes on.

    Now this makes for cool story seeds of course, but it also explains why I was so disappointed in the Egyptian write up even though I didn't really understand why. It's like they were conceding defeat and agreeing with a viewpoint of the world that was thrust upon them when they should be fighting, in their own mysterious ways, in support of their own views and truths.

    If it is a given that all myths are true, then the Egyptians have plenty of advantages in their corner, from their history to their philosphy. So it seems that between the Greek and Egyptian Previews we have read, the Egyptians got the short end of the stick, as the saying goes, when it really should be the other way around.

    Or maybe I don't know what I am talking about because I know little about either. But hopefully I understand what many of you were saying about it, and why you believed it needed a revision.

    So, did I get the gist, or am I completely off.

    How can I know if what I claim I know to be true is rejecting the idea that there is something I might not know? How can I know if what I claim I don't know to be true is rejecting the idea that there is something I do know?
    -Zhuangzi

                              Originally posted by Ostarion View Post


                              What's that specifically?


                              And as I said, I am ultimately not interested in which side has made more contributions or which methods or models of historiography are more accurate since, ultimately, we have a very narrow view of what has occurred. Chinese History keeps getting rewritten all the time because we keep finding more and more texts even in recent years. Most recently the bamboo manuscripts of Guodian, the Tang Yu Zhi Dao, and the Tsinghua strips which I believe Sarah Allan translated recently in her book Buried Ideas if you are interested.

                              I was more interested in the overall dissatisfaction of the Egyptian gods and their modern interpretations by the community here and on the kickstarter. The conflating of the gods being a particular issue that was mentioned. I mentioned my own dissatisfaction regarding how the egyptian gods felt like westernized entities when they are clearly an African pantheon, but I didn't understand the other arguments, so I looked into it. I was just checking to see if I had the right idea of what people were saying. And yes Alpharius, you got what I was saying just fine. I would like for the Egyptians to have a line from their ancient roots to their current time. But nobody agrees on how that connection exists today, now I know why.
                              Yeah, new sources turning up is one of the most exciting, astounding, and annoying things in history, and it happens all the time. Not just with newly found sources, but also with someone finally giving some attention to a source everyone had ignored before - that's a favourite for historical B.A. theses where I live, interpreting an easily accessible source that no one had interpreted before. It can also mean new techniques, both in reading texts and in history's greatest ally, archaeology, which constantly finds new ways to gather data, such as DNA analysis, and using pollen to reconstruct trade networks, and who knows what else these wonderful, crazy people are up to. (I have only a mediocre understanding of my greatest ally, sorry ) All of this allows us to illuminate human history further and further, and yes, also to correct erronous assumptions we have had in the past. But it's unlikely for any of this wonderful new data to entirely overturn the history we know, though parts of it may be; mostly, the data just completes our picture. Of course, there are civilisations or periods within them where we know so little that each new scrap of information is a complete game changer, but as a whole, that's not the case for the Greeks and Egyptians. They are both well attested, and not just from questionable, translated, and manipulated sources. Sure, there are some dark points where info gets scarce on them (the Greek Dark Ages, for example), but especially in regard to their culture and religion, we have a pretty clear picture, at least as far as Ancient civilisations go.

                              With that said, I'm still not entirely sure what exactly you want to know or want to discuss, but I have an idea for why that could be - the more you discuss with the other people here, the more I feel that what you originally asked is quite big and complex, slowly falling apart into smaller questions that make up the big one. I surely can't answer all of those either, but I'll try my best to answer the ones I've seen, and maybe that will help to move this discussion on, and move closer to your core point. Questions I see are: How trustworthy are the Ancient Greek sources regarding Greek religion, culture, and tradition, and how were they preserved into modernity? How clear is Egyptian culture to us from both Greek and Egyptian sources? Is Egyptian heritage an African heritage, and which people today are descended from them?

                              In throwing up these questions, you have (if that was even what you meant; if not, I apologise for ranting) happened upon a number of controversies that are quite outdated by now, answered and forgotten. That's what usually happens to laypeople who start reading in a new academic field for the first time; that's something that happens to me every time I start reading up on a new pantheon, because, surprise, studying three years of history has not given me insight into the entirety of the world's past cultures. On the one hand, that's annoying, because it means struggling with stuff that doesn't need to be struggled with anymore, but on the other hand, that's good, because it means I can give you relatively simple answers to those questions. So let's examine the first one. The idea that all of Greek knowledge was lost during the European Middle Ages, and only preserved by the Byzantines and Muslim Empires in the East, to be returned to Europe by the refugees from the Ottoman Conquest to bring this foundation of modernity back, that is a popular idea. Many non-academics still believe it. That's because Renaissance authors want you to believe it. That whole idea of Antiquity as civilised and good, the Middle Ages as dark, primitive, and bad, and Modernitiy as reclaiming enlightened ancient learning was how Renaissance authors legitimised themselves, how the empires of Early Modern times constructed their self-image. But that doesn't mean it's realistic. Little had been lost, actually, over the Middle Ages, which weren't dark at all, but full of their own innovations (explaining why they seem so uncivilised then is a whole other debate I'll skip for now). And during these not-so-dark times, Ancient Greek authors continued to be preserved, read, and copied in the Christian monasteries, parallel to how Arab scholars kept their "half" of the texts. Aristotle, Plato, and even more so the Latin authors were quoted throughout the whole Middle Ages. What really happened when those Greek refugees came from the East was that they actually brought not that many texts that hadn't been known before, but that their presence alone reawakened a popular interest in them. Ancient Greece was chic again in Europe. What they did bring, and what the Arabs had possessed before, were texts not of Ancient Greek provenience, but of Byzantine origin, secrets of that glorious empire that had actually been kept purposely hidden from the West, which did far more to spur the inventive geniuses of Leonardo and his contemporaries than Aristotle.
                              The reason why I explain all of that at length is that we know where there are manipulations (or, more often, errors) in the texts are, because they haven't been preserved in one straight line from the Greeks and Romans via the Medieval Arabs to us. There are most often multiple preservations of the texts to compare, Medieval Latin ones, and Arabic ones, and lots of papyrus fragments from archaeological digs as well. Furthermore, it made little sense in the Middle Ages to manipulate Ancient Greek texts. As mentioned, the craze for these texts came only later, in a time, as I might add, when philology was born, and those ghastly scholars were proving all the papal and imperial charters the political map of Europe back then was based on to be fakes. And there's the answer: The Ancient Greek texts were read only by a small, interested, clerical circle, they had no power in the "real" world, so there was little to be gained by manipulating them; what this conterfeiting energy was instead directed to were the powerful legal documents issued by popes, kings, and emperors (I once wrote an essay on a 9th century empress who liberally enlarged her rights and possession by falsifying the privileges her deceased husband had granted her). Thus, I think it's pretty safe to treat the Ancient Greek texts as authentic products of their time - which, of course, doesn't mean they weren't manipulated in their own time, when they actually did hold power and meaning. But that's a science all to its own, and it doesn't pertain to your question, since in that case, even the manipulations give us information about their culture. Last, I'd like to mention that our understanding of the Ancient Greeks is, after all, not solely based on those Medievally-transmitted texts; the interpretation of art and architecture are huge, there are for example wonderful studies on how Greek art fills some gaps in the texts about Greek mythology, and the interpretation of public inscriptions and such is also constantly adding to our understanding. Ancient Greece is, in its own way, well-charted territory, and I think we can trust the generations of historians, archaeologists, and philologists who reconstructed it to what we know today.

                              So, we've cleared the name of the Ancient Greeks, but what about the Egyptians? At some points, you seemed worried that the Egyptian gods were too Hellenised, both in sources and especially in the Preview, and that they needed to be returned to a more natural, African state. Now, I'm usually trying to be circumspect in what I say, but let's be honest for a moment and admit it: Pretty much no one liked the Netjer Preview. That was exactly because it took great liberties (which, being circumspect again, I have to admit wasn't a wrong thing to do as such; it's just that many people in the community it was trying to cater to are very insistent on accuracy), including liberties regarding the gods' Egyptian identities. But that actually had little to do with Hellenisation, and more with projecting modern needs and ideas on them. Which, actually, is a pretty Scion-y thing to do. But I digress. Moving from that point forward, let's examine the Greek-ness of Egyptian mythology. And the starting point there is probably their names. Horus, Anubis, Osiris, ugh! Why can't we call them by the names the Egyptians themselves used? Many people in the popular discourse, and many people here on the forums, think that's exactly what should be done. And I totally get that position, I think it's a wonderful idea, but from an academic perspective, it doesn't work. There's a reason why professional Egyptologists still prefer the Greek names for the major deities, and it surely isn't popular accessibility (because if there's one thing folks like Jan Assmann aren't, it's popularly accessible). It's because the Ancient Egyptian names of the gods, as all of Ancient Egyptian, isn't accurately reconstructable today. I don't want to call into question that Egyptologists can read hieroglyphs or anything, because boy can they ever, but the problem in reconstructing spoken Egyptian when only having written Egyptian is the fact that Egyptian writing doesn't record most vowels. That means, when we read the Egyptian name of Anubis, transliterated as npw, it could be Anpu, or Inpu, or Inpew, or anything else along those lines. wsjr, is that Wesir, Usir, Usire? Osiris is about as close to it as the other variations, in any case. So just because someone uses the Greek name of an Egyptian deity doesn't mean that they're referring to a Greek influenced form of them, they're just using a recognisable shorthand to avoid the problem of there being no uniform way to spell their original name. And with that cleared up, there's actually not much of a problem of Egyptian culture and religion being "tainted" by Greek influence. Nobody listens to Herodotus when he talks about Egypt (although his weird details, like having to point out that the Egyptians use the left hand to masturbate where the Greeks use the right, are always entertaining). We read Plutarchus for the story of Isis and Osiris, because the Egyptian original is only preserved in fragments, but we know half of it is bull, and we try to understand where the Egyptian elements are in the story (like, recently, the whole deal with a mysterious queen of Ethiopia, Aso, helping Set murder Osiris in Plutarchus has began to clear up as papyrus fragments telling about a banished daughter of Osiris have turned up), but we don't take anything he says at face value. And otherwise, we have quite a lot of materials from Egyptian times themselves to tell us about the gods, again in text, art, and so on. Not as many as we would like to have, but Late texts and those from Greek and Roman rule are not necessarily bad. Professional scholars have enough material to figure out what changed when, and which elements from those later sources are genuine Egyptian traditions. So, restoring the Egyptian gods to what they actually were shouldn't be that hard; the changes the Preview made to their personalities can, if Development decides it wants that, be overturned, and a "truer" (for whatever measure of true) core beneath be revealed. The question that remains, however, is whether this "true", core, original Egyptian identity is "African" in a modern sense of the word.

                              This, the last of your questions that I could extract from the cluster that seems to bother you, is an extremely touchy one. The Egyptians were one of the most impressive and advanced civilisations in human history, so everyone wants to claim their heritage, and the valid question of "What ethnicity were the Egyptians? What skin colour?" is often drowned in the reflexive answer of "Whatever ethnicity and skin colour I am." And if white people can't have them, it's still better for them to have been aliens than to concede them to someone else. This question (the one about ethnicity, not about aliens) has in the past caused great stirs in the academic community as well, whole conferences being held on that topic alone. The racist idea of the 19th century that the Egyptians must have been white because how else could they have achieved greatness has been abandoned, but that gives us no clearer picture as such. The idea that the Egyptians were "black" in any sense of the word just because they lived on a continent defined by blackness today (in spite of the large populations of light-skinned, Semitic peoples living and often being forgotten there when Westerners talk about Africa) is equally problematic. The base line to answering that question is that the Ancient world didn't have concepts of race and ethnicity like we have, and that thanks to both those concepts changing, and due to migrations and ethnic mixes happening constantly, no modern ethnicity directly corresponds to any Ancient one. That doesn't go just for the Egyptians - it's completely wrong to assume that centuries of Slavic and Germanic invasions in Greece and Italy have left the populations there in an ethnic state untouched from their Hellenic and Roman days. Saying that frees us of the politically loaded question of what race or ethnicity the Egyptians were, but it doesn't answer the equally valid question of what they looked like. That question, in spite of all the material we have, is very hard to answer. We have Egyptian painting, that shows the men to be reddish brown and the women to be yellow, dinstinguished from both the stark white Near Eastern peoples and the pitch-black Nubians; people try to interpret the structure of the hair we can see in the paintings as typical African curls, but forget that the Egyptians mostly shaved their heads and wore wigs in all manner of styles. We have Egyptian statue, which is so idealised it doesn't look like much of anything (though, again, we happen upon the problem that we are trying to connect their features to those of modern ethnicities instead of taking them for what they are). We have chemical analyses that seem to show that some of the Egyptian mummies were red-headed in life, but those analyses could be wrong, sometimes finding nicotine in those same mummies as well (why this doesn't point to Ancient Egyptian-American connection is again a huge debate I have to skip here). We have Egyptian texts that do not speak in modern voices, that talk only about the singular Egyptians, never claiming ethnic relatedness to any other people. It's not just skin colour, hair colour, and so on, a lot of the physicals of Egyptian looks we can't reconstruct - we see paintings of women at parties with strange cones on their heads, for example. Some think that might have been an actual Egyptian practice, putting scented cones of wax on your head; some think it's only a symbol for joy, like the exclamation mark appearing above a video-game character's head when you talk to them. I told you before that we have a clear enough idea of Egyptian culture, but with the question of ethnicity, you have hit on one of those things I also mentioned, one of the gaps we'll probably never fill. A good suggestion I've heard is that they could have looked like the modern Berber peoples, browner than Europeans and Semitics, but far lighter than "black" Africans; but that's a guess, and no one will probably ever know for sure. We also shouldn't imagine that all Egyptians looked the same - it was a huge country, stretching from a North influenced by Semitic peoples to a South influenced by the Nubians. The Nubians are one of my favourite civilisations anyway, and a good point to examine perceived "blackness" from. Worshipping many of the same gods and imitating Egyptian architectural forms, the Nubians are often seen as a connection between Egypt and the rest of Africa, and the question I wonder about is - why? Because they use elephants and giraffes as architectural forms, which are more genuinely African than Egyptian fauna? Because they're depicted with what appears to modern Western eyes to be more stereotypical African features? That doesn't make them any more or less African than the Egyptians. We're again projecting modern ideas on Ancient civilisations there.
                              This also complicates the question of who the Egyptians' heirs are, and who should carry on their culture in modernity in a Scion setting. In the mid-20th century, as scholars stopped looking down on and began to get excited about sub-Saharan African peoples, it was a favourite claim to connect every single one of them, and especially their myths, to Egyptian migrations. The Yoruba/Orisha? They came from Egypt, don't you know? The Ashanti/Absoom? Egyptian. Most of that has been disproven by now, but people are also unhappy with accepting modern Islamic, Arabic Egypt as the legitimate heir to that grand Ancient civilisation. We're at the point again where everyone wants to claim them, and if one themselves can't have them, they sure as hell won't cede them to those dirty foreigners. And there, I think, it's up to everyone themselves to decide where to place the Egyptian legacy in a Scion setting; Scion is still fictional, we're allowed to make stuff up. It was far easier drawing modern connections in the Shen and Teotl Previews, because both religions and mythologies are still carried on in a myriad ways in the modern day. That isn't the case for the Egyptians. In contrast to the Greeks and Romans, the Egyptians really were completely forgotten in both Medieval Europe and the Muslim Arab Empires. Their monuments were buried in sand, and their writing became unintelligible.The modern Egyptian opinion seems divided between laying claim to that great legacy, and discarding it for their even greater Islamic legacy, and many poor people just wanting to go with whatever the tourists will pay for. There are modern revivals like Kemeticism, but try as hard as they may, that doesn't create a continuous line of tradition between them and Ramesses. That line is just lost, in a way the Greek, Chinese, or Mexican lines are not. That's why I, personally, with no claim to that idea being generally binding, prefer the approach of "Egypt is everywhere". Because not only since the 19th century, not only since we adore the Egyptian's greatness in museums, but for a long time, through all those cultures that have borrowed and copied from the great Egyptian legacy, Egyptian ideas have spread all over Europe, Africa, and the Near East. So that is something I could go with - the Chinese and Aztec gods being kept by what remains of their cults, while the Egyptian gods, whose proper cult is lost for good, being carried on in all of our minds. (That is, again, not to tear down Kemetics; I'm merely saying that whatever honest and pious devotions they pay to their gods, those are not equivalent to what the Netjer received in the old days; Egyptian religion was a state cult, and with that state gone, there can be substitute cults, but never again the "real deal".)

                              Now, that was one massive tour de force through some really big questions. I'm still pretty sure I haven't answered what you actually wanted to know, but I hope this has gone some steps in the right direction.

The idea that all of Greek knowledge was lost during the European Middle Ages, and only preserved by the Byzantines and Muslim Empires in the East, to be returned to Europe by the refugees from the Ottoman Conquest to bring this foundation of modernity back, that is a popular idea. Many non-academics still believe it. That's because Renaissance authors want you to believe it. That whole idea of Antiquity as civilised and good, the Middle Ages as dark, primitive, and bad, and Modernitiy as reclaiming enlightened ancient learning was how Renaissance authors legitimised themselves, how the empires of Early Modern times constructed their self-image. But that doesn't mean it's realistic. Little had been lost, actually, over the Middle Ages, which weren't dark at all, but full of their own innovations (explaining why they seem so uncivilised then is a whole other debate I'll skip for now). And during these not-so-dark times, Ancient Greek authors continued to be preserved, read, and copied in the Christian monasteries, parallel to how Arab scholars kept their "half" of the texts. Aristotle, Plato, and even more so the Latin authors were quoted throughout the whole Middle Ages. What really happened when those Greek refugees came from the East was that they actually brought not that many texts that hadn't been known before, but that their presence alone reawakened a popular interest in them. Ancient Greece was chic again in Europe. What they did bring, and what the Arabs had possessed before, were texts not of Ancient Greek provenience, but of Byzantine origin, secrets of that glorious empire that had actually been kept purposely hidden from the West, which did far more to spur the inventive geniuses of Leonardo and his contemporaries than Aristotle.
                                  The reason why I explain all of that at length is that we know where there are manipulations (or, more often, errors) in the texts are, because they haven't been preserved in one straight line from the Greeks and Romans via the Medieval Arabs to us. There are most often multiple preservations of the texts to compare, Medieval Latin ones, and Arabic ones, and lots of papyrus fragments from archaeological digs as well. Furthermore, it made little sense in the Middle Ages to manipulate Ancient Greek texts. As mentioned, the craze for these texts came only later, in a time, as I might add, when philology was born, and those ghastly scholars were proving all the papal and imperial charters the political map of Europe back then was based on to be fakes. And there's the answer: The Ancient Greek texts were read only by a small, interested, clerical circle, they had no power in the "real" world, so there was little to be gained by manipulating them; what this conterfeiting energy was instead directed to were the powerful legal documents issued by popes, kings, and emperors (I once wrote an essay on a 9th century empress who liberally enlarged her rights and possession by falsifying the privileges her deceased husband had granted her). Thus, I think it's pretty safe to treat the Ancient Greek texts as authentic products of their time - which, of course, doesn't mean they weren't manipulated in their own time, when they actually did hold power and meaning. But that's a science all to its own, and it doesn't pertain to your question, since in that case, even the manipulations give us information about their culture. Last, I'd like to mention that our understanding of the Ancient Greeks is, after all, not solely based on those Medievally-transmitted texts; the interpretation of art and architecture are huge, there are for example wonderful studies on how Greek art fills some gaps in the texts about Greek mythology, and the interpretation of public inscriptions and such is also constantly adding to our understanding. Ancient Greece is, in its own way, well-charted territory, and I think we can trust the generations of historians, archaeologists, and philologists who reconstructed it to what we know today.

                                  So, we've cleared the name of the Ancient Greeks, but what about the Egyptians? At some points, you seemed worried that the Egyptian gods were too Hellenised, both in sources and especially in the Preview, and that they needed to be returned to a more natural, African state. Now, I'm usually trying to be circumspect in what I say, but let's be honest for a moment and admit it: Pretty much no one liked the Netjer Preview. That was exactly because it took great liberties (which, being circumspect again, I have to admit wasn't a wrong thing to do as such; it's just that many people in the community it was trying to cater to are very insistent on accuracy), including liberties regarding the gods' Egyptian identities. But that actually had little to do with Hellenisation, and more with projecting modern needs and ideas on them. Which, actually, is a pretty Scion-y thing to do. But I digress. Moving from that point forward, let's examine the Greek-ness of Egyptian mythology. And the starting point there is probably their names. Horus, Anubis, Osiris, ugh! Why can't we call them by the names the Egyptians themselves used? Many people in the popular discourse, and many people here on the forums, think that's exactly what should be done. And I totally get that position, I think it's a wonderful idea, but from an academic perspective, it doesn't work. There's a reason why professional Egyptologists still prefer the Greek names for the major deities, and it surely isn't popular accessibility (because if there's one thing folks like Jan Assmann aren't, it's popularly accessible). It's because the Ancient Egyptian names of the gods, as all of Ancient Egyptian, isn't accurately reconstructable today. I don't want to call into question that Egyptologists can read hieroglyphs or anything, because boy can they ever, but the problem in reconstructing spoken Egyptian when only having written Egyptian is the fact that Egyptian writing doesn't record most vowels. That means, when we read the Egyptian name of Anubis, transliterated as npw, it could be Anpu, or Inpu, or Inpew, or anything else along those lines. wsjr, is that Wesir, Usir, Usire? Osiris is about as close to it as the other variations, in any case. So just because someone uses the Greek name of an Egyptian deity doesn't mean that they're referring to a Greek influenced form of them, they're just using a recognisable shorthand to avoid the problem of there being no uniform way to spell their original name. And with that cleared up, there's actually not much of a problem of Egyptian culture and religion being "tainted" by Greek influence. Nobody listens to Herodotus when he talks about Egypt (although his weird details, like having to point out that the Egyptians use the left hand to masturbate where the Greeks use the right, are always entertaining). We read Plutarchus for the story of Isis and Osiris, because the Egyptian original is only preserved in fragments, but we know half of it is bull, and we try to understand where the Egyptian elements are in the story (like, recently, the whole deal with a mysterious queen of Ethiopia, Aso, helping Set murder Osiris in Plutarchus has began to clear up as papyrus fragments telling about a banished daughter of Osiris have turned up), but we don't take anything he says at face value. And otherwise, we have quite a lot of materials from Egyptian times themselves to tell us about the gods, again in text, art, and so on. Not as many as we would like to have, but Late texts and those from Greek and Roman rule are not necessarily bad. Professional scholars have enough material to figure out what changed when, and which elements from those later sources are genuine Egyptian traditions. So, restoring the Egyptian gods to what they actually were shouldn't be that hard; the changes the Preview made to their personalities can, if Development decides it wants that, be overturned, and a "truer" (for whatever measure of true) core beneath be revealed. The question that remains, however, is whether this "true", core, original Egyptian identity is "African" in a modern sense of the word.

                                  This, the last of your questions that I could extract from the cluster that seems to bother you, is an extremely touchy one. The Egyptians were one of the most impressive and advanced civilisations in human history, so everyone wants to claim their heritage, and the valid question of "What ethnicity were the Egyptians? What skin colour?" is often drowned in the reflexive answer of "Whatever ethnicity and skin colour I am." And if white people can't have them, it's still better for them to have been aliens than to concede them to someone else. This question (the one about ethnicity, not about aliens) has in the past caused great stirs in the academic community as well, whole conferences being held on that topic alone. The racist idea of the 19th century that the Egyptians must have been white because how else could they have achieved greatness has been abandoned, but that gives us no clearer picture as such. The idea that the Egyptians were "black" in any sense of the word just because they lived on a continent defined by blackness today (in spite of the large populations of light-skinned, Semitic peoples living and often being forgotten there when Westerners talk about Africa) is equally problematic. The base line to answering that question is that the Ancient world didn't have concepts of race and ethnicity like we have, and that thanks to both those concepts changing, and due to migrations and ethnic mixes happening constantly, no modern ethnicity directly corresponds to any Ancient one. That doesn't go just for the Egyptians - it's completely wrong to assume that centuries of Slavic and Germanic invasions in Greece and Italy have left the populations there in an ethnic state untouched from their Hellenic and Roman days. Saying that frees us of the politically loaded question of what race or ethnicity the Egyptians were, but it doesn't answer the equally valid question of what they looked like. That question, in spite of all the material we have, is very hard to answer. We have Egyptian painting, that shows the men to be reddish brown and the women to be yellow, dinstinguished from both the stark white Near Eastern peoples and the pitch-black Nubians; people try to interpret the structure of the hair we can see in the paintings as typical African curls, but forget that the Egyptians mostly shaved their heads and wore wigs in all manner of styles. We have Egyptian statue, which is so idealised it doesn't look like much of anything (though, again, we happen upon the problem that we are trying to connect their features to those of modern ethnicities instead of taking them for what they are). We have chemical analyses that seem to show that some of the Egyptian mummies were red-headed in life, but those analyses could be wrong, sometimes finding nicotine in those same mummies as well (why this doesn't point to Ancient Egyptian-American connection is again a huge debate I have to skip here). We have Egyptian texts that do not speak in modern voices, that talk only about the singular Egyptians, never claiming ethnic relatedness to any other people. It's not just skin colour, hair colour, and so on, a lot of the physicals of Egyptian looks we can't reconstruct - we see paintings of women at parties with strange cones on their heads, for example. Some think that might have been an actual Egyptian practice, putting scented cones of wax on your head; some think it's only a symbol for joy, like the exclamation mark appearing above a video-game character's head when you talk to them. I told you before that we have a clear enough idea of Egyptian culture, but with the question of ethnicity, you have hit on one of those things I also mentioned, one of the gaps we'll probably never fill. A good suggestion I've heard is that they could have looked like the modern Berber peoples, browner than Europeans and Semitics, but far lighter than "black" Africans; but that's a guess, and no one will probably ever know for sure. We also shouldn't imagine that all Egyptians looked the same - it was a huge country, stretching from a North influenced by Semitic peoples to a South influenced by the Nubians. The Nubians are one of my favourite civilisations anyway, and a good point to examine perceived "blackness" from. Worshipping many of the same gods and imitating Egyptian architectural forms, the Nubians are often seen as a connection between Egypt and the rest of Africa, and the question I wonder about is - why? Because they use elephants and giraffes as architectural forms, which are more genuinely African than Egyptian fauna? Because they're depicted with what appears to modern Western eyes to be more stereotypical African features? That doesn't make them any more or less African than the Egyptians. We're again projecting modern ideas on Ancient civilisations there.
                                  This also complicates the question of who the Egyptians' heirs are, and who should carry on their culture in modernity in a Scion setting. In the mid-20th century, as scholars stopped looking down on and began to get excited about sub-Saharan African peoples, it was a favourite claim to connect every single one of them, and especially their myths, to Egyptian migrations. The Yoruba/Orisha? They came from Egypt, don't you know? The Ashanti/Absoom? Egyptian. Most of that has been disproven by now, but people are also unhappy with accepting modern Islamic, Arabic Egypt as the legitimate heir to that grand Ancient civilisation. We're at the point again where everyone wants to claim them, and if one themselves can't have them, they sure as hell won't cede them to those dirty foreigners. And there, I think, it's up to everyone themselves to decide where to place the Egyptian legacy in a Scion setting; Scion is still fictional, we're allowed to make stuff up. It was far easier drawing modern connections in the Shen and Teotl Previews, because both religions and mythologies are still carried on in a myriad ways in the modern day. That isn't the case for the Egyptians. In contrast to the Greeks and Romans, the Egyptians really were completely forgotten in both Medieval Europe and the Muslim Arab Empires. Their monuments were buried in sand, and their writing became unintelligible.The modern Egyptian opinion seems divided between laying claim to that great legacy, and discarding it for their even greater Islamic legacy, and many poor people just wanting to go with whatever the tourists will pay for. There are modern revivals like Kemeticism, but try as hard as they may, that doesn't create a continuous line of tradition between them and Ramesses. That line is just lost, in a way the Greek, Chinese, or Mexican lines are not. That's why I, personally, with no claim to that idea being generally binding, prefer the approach of "Egypt is everywhere". Because not only since the 19th century, not only since we adore the Egyptian's greatness in museums, but for a long time, through all those cultures that have borrowed and copied from the great Egyptian legacy, Egyptian ideas have spread all over Europe, Africa, and the Near East. So that is something I could go with - the Chinese and Aztec gods being kept by what remains of their cults, while the Egyptian gods, whose proper cult is lost for good, being carried on in all of our minds. (That is, again, not to tear down Kemetics; I'm merely saying that whatever honest and pious devotions they pay to their gods, those are not equivalent to what the Netjer received in the old days; Egyptian religion was a state cult, and with that state gone, there can be substitute cults, but never again the "real deal".)

                                  Now, that was one massive tour de force through some really big questions. I'm still pretty sure I haven't answered what you actually wanted to know, but I hope this has gone some steps in the right direction.

I have read far more from various people about most of this, some who might agree and some who might disagree with all the points you made. So many like to compare these two and their influence in the world today, and while I understand certain positions have more evidence to back them up, I find the conversation more interesting than the winner of an argument. This is also what I had decided to do when I studied under my father on our own culture, or when I had studied the Japanese and Indian systems of knowledge. I am just not interested in the outcome of what the truth is because it never can be absolute, I am interested in the conversation.
