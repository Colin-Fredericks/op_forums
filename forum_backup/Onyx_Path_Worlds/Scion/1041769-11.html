    Here's the thing, though. Would you run a modern game, but based on the worst pieces of morality of the Bible as it was 1800 years ago, because "that's what this book and stories tell me that [fill in your deity] thinks"? Because they do happen, and they are still happening in the real world, today. Realism is part of White Wolf's charm, at least to me personally. I'm not going to start throwing it around every corner and claim "This is how we do shit!" but I would make it crop up occasionally in the story, to remind players who / what they're dealing with.

        Also, there's a real difference between the perception of the deities of the most popular modern religions, and the Gods of mythology. The Greek Gods [and those of the Norse, Egyptians, Aztecs, and so on] were perceived as directly involved in the lives of humans, often interacting personally to have children with mortals, bless certain heroic types, curse the unlucky, and generally interfere in the affairs of humanity. I'm not aware of any stories that have the major Gods of modern faiths directly and tangibly involving themselves in the same way. Modern relations with the deities are a lot more static and distant [in that way] than the legendary ones were, and so I don't feel that you can say "[insert faith here] has the same rules now as it did back then, so the Greek/Mayan/Inue deities should be similarly unchanging". In the end, it really comes down to this: what's going to make a more engaging game? Heroic legends and larger-than-life deities that change the world, or static, restrictive, outdated morality and social roles? I know which one I would rather play...

                There's a difference between saying, "bad things exist in the world, and I want to include them in my chronicle/game for dramatic effect and realism", and saying "The Aztec Gods are completely intolerant of homosexuality and adultery, and Tlazolteotl will come and kill you if you do these things, so don't". One provides a dramatic framework for conflict and role-playing [if done well], while the other just provides for oppression of certain groups of people, or justifies discrimination and intolerance "because the Gods demand it". Yes, perhaps that really was the case, once upon a time, and it isn't far off the mark in some places even now. But I don't know about you, but I have no interest in playing a game that takes place in a world where the worst parts of religious intolerance and bigotry are central to the game - I can look at the real world for that. I game to escape, to play characters that can make a difference, can improve things, can change their world for the better - and be part of something better. And since Scion is a game specifically about the Gods and their Scions, there wouldn't be a tidy way to shuffle the bad stuff into the background if one assumes that the religions haven't changed their views on women, LGBTQ people, people from other faiths/cultures, rape, slavery, adultery, and so on. Saying that the Gods have changed leads to a better, more player-friendly world, and that's a good thing. Sticking to outdated dogma doesn't make it a better game, or a better, more "realistic" experience. There can still be drama and conflict; but it doesn't have to be based on the negative views of a presumably-unchanging Zeus.

One of the many variations on marriage is a man marrying a Cumal, not too sure if they had any guest rights or anything. I would assume not. I would suggest their slave system was focused on youth pretty heavily since the biggest detail of slaves in mythology is the Fomori taking a tithe of 1/3 of all children born to every household in Ireland under the reign of Bres.

                                                    If the Tuatha returned from being dead in a game, they'd probably snatch up a bunch of slaves. Most likely when they try to retake Ireland from the mortals. Their Scions might get given some, but I don't know if we know enough about the institution to really flesh it out much past that. It would be a sign of wealth, slaves or cows. I'd suggest to go with the cows, you essentially generate interest more quickly and with way less moral qualms. Sure your cow can't do the weaving for you, but as a Scion you probably don't have a huge need for labor.

                                                    To add to that, the Fomori shouldn't get a bad rap for that. The Fomori are just a foreign colonizing force that put pressure on Ireland, same as the Tuatha, and The Sons of Mil. All of them would happily impose tithes of slaves, and resources on anyone else. The Fomori just committed the crime of clashing with the Tuatha really, the Fomori and the Tuatha are pretty much the same as each other, no cackling evil or anything. I suspect they pick up their 'evil monster' tag later on, I would wager in the Victorian period, due to the Fomori having some sort of maiming thing going on with them.

                                                            The major factor in this question is that these are polytheistic religions, as opposed to monotheism. When you have multiple deities, you have multiple goals and opinions, and that results in internal conflict.; Some gods will probably change, especially if it supports their world view. But others will reject the change because it disturbs their comfortable life style. The most realistic way to portray that is to include both sides of the spectrum in the same game. Some gods "got better" over time, while others clung to the past.

                                                            In that sense, they're not much different from humans. There are lots of people who look at the way the world is changing, and disapprove of it, believing that things were better back in their time. (I, for example am nervously skeptical about the emphasis society is placing on cyber interaction, as opposed to dealing with people in person).
