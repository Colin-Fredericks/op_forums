                        Well, historically Palestine was bounced back and forth between Mesopotamia and Egypt like a provincial football, so it's not surprise that Hebrew culture shows strong influences from both. But at heart the Hebrews were originally Canaanites (i.e. Phoenicians), as far as culture and language goes. It's just that the cult of Yahweh became extremely powerful and eventually edged out everybody else.

                        For a bronze-age (i.e. pre-Hebrew and pre-Greek) look at Canaanite myth, see the writings we've recovered from Ugarit. The Sons of El would make a fine pantheon for Scion, albeit one with clear Mesopotamian influence (as all cultures in the Near East). For example, Baal Haddad is Addad in Babylon, although he's much more important to the Canaanites.

                        What seems to have happened in iron-age Hebrew culture is that Yahweh, patron god of Jerusalem, came to be identified with El and eventually absorbed or subordinated the rest of the family, whereas he's named as one of the sons of El in Ugarit. That sort of genealogical shenanigans is actually very common in the myths of the region, as each city attempted to show how their patron god was king of the others.

                            Originally posted by Telgar View Post
                            Dude's right, Assyrian, Akkadian, Babylonian and Sumerian are all super similar religions. Caananite is far more distinct, but the big four Mesopotamian cultures are all pretty close together.
                            They're all functionally the same religion, in the same sense that Greco-Roman religion existed on a single continuum. Individual cities had rites and traditions specific to them, as was the case in any ancient culture, but the basic forms and assumptions were shared in common throughout the region.

                            In Mesopotamia it's largely because Sumerian and Akkadian cultures (including the latter's Assyrian and Babylonian variants) existed side-by-side in the same land for many centuries. Each city was multi-ethnic, so it's not as if you have a Sumerian-only city or an Akkadian-only city by the historical period. It was basically just a question of which language you spoke at home.

                            The Sumerian culture was the oldest in the region, and the basic religious and mythic framework was originally Sumerian, but the Semitic-speaking Akkadians became Sumerianized pretty early on, to the point where they made the culture their own and added to it, eventually absorbing and overshadowing it altogether, to the point where Sumerian was primarily a ceremonial language. Trying to disentangle them and identify something as essentially one or the other is futile, just as trying to identify an "essentially Roman" culture apart from Greek influence is pointless (and characteristic of a rather nasty period in early 20th-century scholarship that was tied up with ethnic essentialism and race theory).

Remember the name, we'll encounter it again.

                                                Prior to the Babylonian exile, we know that the Israelites followed a wide variety of cults, most of which were polytheistic, and the cult of Yahweh was a relatively minor cult among the Israelites during this period that has been greatly revised over time. We know, for example, that the narrative of the Yahweh included a wife. Her name was Asherah, though in related cults she had different names, including Astarte and Istar. She was a powerful, important, even indispensible part of the cult, mostly related to fertility. We know that she figured prominently throughout the pre-Roman period, as she appears frequently in inscriptions and on figurines prior to the Assyrian expulsion and to a lesser extent afterwards. She remained a major figure in the Old Testament narratives, though as time went on, she was gradually edited out by subsequent copyists and editors, disappearing almost completely from the Old Testament narrative by about the 8th Century, though traces of this cult figure remain even today in the Old Testament. ...

                                                We now know this Mesopotamian god as "El-ohim," and our author "E," one of the earliest scriptorialists writing about this time, first has El introducing himself to Abraham as "El Shaddai" (El of the Mountain). He also appears as El Elyon, or El of Bethel in other, non-canonized scripture, and his name is also preserved in such Hebrew names as Isra-El and Ishma-El. The word Elohim was originally a plural of El. To the south, from Bethel to the Valley of Beersheba, a similar transformation is taking place. In this climatically and geologically harsher place, a place with a much smaller and less settled population with greater geographical isolation, the Canaanite god Yahweh is being transformed by a culturally similar people of the land of Judah. The unknown author known to scholars simply as "J" has his god being familiar with and comfortable with Abraham, and he casually appears to Abraham in Genesis 18, introducing himself as Yahweh. But "J's" contemporary, author "E" in the north can't have God being so casual, and first appears as a voice, commanding Abraham to leave his people in Mesopotamia and settle in Canaan. Yahweh, in his transformation from a pagan Canaanite god to the god of the Jews, becomes a cruel and vindictive god in the hands of author "J." He commands Abraham to sacrifice his first born son, an act which is not at all surprising given the nature of the pagan religions of the time. Many of these pagan religions (and remember that Yahweh got his start as a Canannite pagan god) considered the first-born to be the seed of a god. Because of this, they were often sacrificed to the god who presumably sired them. Yet Elohim in the north continues to be a much more subtle god, who directs the affairs of men by revelation of the voice, hidden from the view of mere mortals. There is a tension among these peoples, both of whom identify themselves as culturally descendants of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. One people, perhaps, but two gods.

Just bear in mind that Scion is a game about “myths made real”, not a documentary on “the truth behind the myths”: we don’t insist on a rigorous examination of, say, nordic history and what the realities were that gave rise to the myths about the Aesir; we take Norse mythology at face value as much as we can manage, compromising only to the extent needed to build a setting where the Aesir coexist with other Pantheons.
