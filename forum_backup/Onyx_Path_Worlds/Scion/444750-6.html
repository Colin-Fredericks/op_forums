                            Very slight corrections (I just like talking about history):

                            Originally posted by Úlfhéðnar View Post

                            I can say definitively No, The English are primarily Anglo-Saxons who are a Northern Germanic peoples.
                            Angles and Saxons (and Jutes, Frisians and Franks) are/were western Germanic, not northern Germanic (though at the time of the invasions, the linguistic differences would have been faaar less than they'd later become). This might be an important distinction, religiously-speaking, because:

                            They Worshiped Woden (Wotan/Odin) and the rest of the Aesir with little important changes from continental Germany and Scandinavia.
                            The three most striking differences (to me) between western Germanic and northern Germanic religion are:

                            1) The lack of Loki in western traditions, to the best of our knowledge.

                            2) The lack of a definite Ragnarok concept, though this one is a little less certain than the lack of Loki, especially given the theme's far-wider existence as a reflex in so many other Indo-European, not just Germanic, religious cosmologies. IMO, it existed; as support, I point to the continental references to the fires of Muspelhame scorching us all at the end of the world, and archeological material that almost certainly depicts the battles between Thunor and a serpent/dragon and the slaying of Fenris, two kinda important brawls that the Eddas describe as forseen at Ragnarok.

                            3) Most important to me, the lack of the Frigga/Freyja figures in lieu of a single goddess, Frige (whose name obviously looks like Frigga, but is pronounced like Freyja). This one causes the most friction between heathens, IME, to the extent that some Anglo-Saxon types have adopted the Scandinavian form (two separate goddesses), some Scandinavian types have accepted the argument that the division of the figure into two was a somewhat baroque innovation particular to northern cultures relatively late in the game, as displayed in the Eddas and similar material, and the rest of us sometimes just continue to argue about it. :P

                            As far as Scion goes, though, these are just academic distinctions; the northern Germanic forms of the mythology are both most familiar and most accessible to your average joe or jane, so they should obviously stay. At least Scion's Thunderer is a redhead.

                            England is the Kingdom created from the Germanic Invasions and populated by Anglo-Saxons
                            Technically, England the kingdom was established by the west Saxons after Danes had conquered most of the Anglian kingdoms, then been defeated in turn by the House of Wessex. In a way, the Vikings laid the ground-work for Saxon political ascendency (though that certainly wasn't their purpose).

                            Wales and Cornwall are the remnants of the ancient Brythonic peoples who were driven out of their original land by Saxons (Tensions still run high on occasion, it was rather brutal).
                            Eh. I think those accounts are a bit exaggerated. Germanic culture/language/political control certainly dominated in what's now England, but if you look at the genetics (particularly paternal markers, which should demonstrate at least to some degree the extent of expulsion of the Britons), it's still kinda half-Germanic and half-Celtic. I expect the real dividing point was how willing one was to acculturate; those who were unwilling went west, those who were willing stayed and (eventually) became English, if not Angles/Saxons/etc.

                            The south of Scotland is largely influenced by the Saxons and Viking invasion while the Highlands (Read north) of Scotland are descended from the Picts (Another Brythonic people).
                            In the south of Scotland, we're talking about Angles, not Saxons. They seem to have made up most of the invasion; the Saxons mostly settled in the south of England (which we call England because there were so many Angles...it's Angle-land).

                            In the north of Scotland (though this is a huge oversimplification), Gaels from Ireland subdued/assimilated the Picts, as typified by the folkloric attributes of An Ferbasach being the first king of Scotland through his royal Gaelic descent (paternal) and royal Pictish descent (maternal). But that's generally considered somewhat mythological at this point.

                                If your players are interested in actually accessing the myths relevant to their pantheon, they will get very very very confused if you conflate the Welsh and Irish myths. As previously stated, characters with very similar names do very different things. If you "statted them up" as potential parents it would be really difficult to pin down what Purviews, Epics and Favored Abilities they would have in common, so just shifting a few things around mechanically won't work out all that well. Lleu Llaw Gyffes and Lugh Samildanach aren't at all the same dude. Lleu's birth to the brother-sister pair of Gwydion & Arianrhod, being gestated in a magic box, training by his dad/uncle, tricking his mother into arming him, marriage to Blodeuwedd, death at the hands of Gronw after satisfying all the weird conditions necessary to killing him doesn't have much to do with Lugh's birth to the daugther of the Fomor king, fosterage and training with Mannanan, heading off to Tara and defeating everyone in everything, etc. etc. The two mythologies have a significantly different "feel" even where they track similar concepts - geasa vs tyngeddau, for example.

                                So, yes, this is not a case of pantheon bloat. These are mythologies from two very different groups of people, cultures, etc. Just because they get thrown together under the envelope of "Celtic" doesn't mean they have to have a lot in common. In fact, it's very likely they don't. The split between the two is likely to have been around 1200-800 BC. (Parallel to the time of the Trojan War, the New Kingdom in Egypt, the collapse of the Hittite Empire - emphatically the Bronze Age) So a millenium before the Romans starting messing around in the area. Or 2000+ years of divergence from the time the peoples and their languages separated and we get the first written versions of these myths (11th Century or so for Irish and 14th for Welsh). It's an accident of geography these two groups ended up next to each other. If things had sorted out differently, you might have have had the Irish in Ireland and the "Welsh" myths could have come from, say, Galatia in Turkey (though they would definitely have ended up different... lots of Greek influence, among other things). Which would probably make it easier for people to see how disparate the two actually are. Sure the mythologies influenced each other because of the proximity and I'm not denying that some crossover is valid, and very cool even, like having Manawydan, perhaps, being the younger version of Mannanan, for example.

                                And though there might be something to the idea that the Irish got to Ireland through Great Britain, linguistically and archaeologically, there isn't much evidence to support it. Welsh (Cornish/Breton and possibly Pictish) are more closely related to Gaulish (actually proto-Gaulish) from modern France (extending all the way over to Turkey) and Irish/Gaelic are more closely related to the Celtiberian languages of modern Spain. The archaeology and other aspects of culture (from the preserved myths) track this division. So it's more likely that Ireland was settled by Celts from Spain, not France via Great Britain. (And the mythology tracks this. The Milesians or modern Irish mortal folks are from Spain in the Book of Invasions. The Tuatha proper are from the Scythians (!?!?!) via Ireland via the Otherworld via Irelans)

                                                            On the topic of Roman Gods: Many people equate Mars to Ares without realizing that they're really not alike. Ares is a god of war. He's into bloodlust, conflict and shit-disturbing. Mars is an agricultural god, grandfather of the founders of Rome - War is attributed to him because he was viewed as a protector of the Roman people rather than a warrior.
