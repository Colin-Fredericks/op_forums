                                Originally posted by glamourweaver View Post
                                Only secondary sources, like here: https://books.google.com/books?id=gJ...20gods&f=false
                                Interesting, I wonder if the difference has to do with the fact it has an adjective attached to it.

                                Hittite is particularly challenging to translate since it doesn't have an alphabet, it used Sumerograms. So, you have to transliterate from cuneiform to the Latin alphabet, something which is really challenging especially since the Hittites were putting their own spin on things IIRC. I have a feeling it might just be differences in how different scholars are transliterating the term, you can see by the two terms the disagreement is actually internally consistent. In our transliteration, the "s" sound of GBN's transliteration is being elongated into a "sh" sound. (Siwannes = Shiunesh) Both transliterations have an i after the first s/sh, and precede the final s/sh with an e. (Siwannes = Shiunesh) Then the center of the word, "wann" and "un" is close to each other in sound.

                                I'll double check with Sacerdos since he read the German side of the literature for the project since Germany is the main producer of academic work on the Hittites, and we are lucky that he can read German. I think it's a transliteration difference. There are a few times it shows up with some of the Deity names, and then in some cases, we only have the Sumerogram for an entity which makes things confusing. For example, the Hittites use the Sumerogram for "Ea," for one of their own ancient primeval Gods, so we just have to call him Ea, even though he's not the same Ea of a few hundred kilometers to the east of their borders.

                                                        Originally posted by glamourweaver View Post

                                                        I'm finding Siwannes as a more common rendering than Shiunesh.
                                                        Both are different transliterations of the same thing. Leaving the š as s is academic custom, but it should still be spoken as "sh"-sound, so I prefer to transliterate it as sh right away. The u instead of wa is totally coming from me using, like Watcher mentioned, German scholarship, where this mode of transliteration is more usual; for example, we would also transliterate the Egyptian god you know as Wepwawet as Upuaut. Due to the importance of German scholarship in Hittite studies, I have seen many English-language works as well follow this mode of transliteration; frankly, the link you provided is the first time I saw "siwannes" used in an academic work. So, good to know this form of transliteration is used after all, but we'll stick with ours. I find that this mode better strikes the balance between accurate and readable, and it is what I'm used to from the uni courses I took on the Hittites. But in the end, your list is good with both.
