      Akan=Ashanti: Check out anything by R. S. Rattray, especially his Akan-Ashanti Folk-Tales. When we get our Ashanti pantheon out, you can look at things more closely there.
      Dine Bahane=Navajo: Actually lots of materials there. You can start here: http://www.sacred-texts.com/nam/nav/index.htm , but the best translation of their myth cycle, I think, is the one by Paul Zolbrod.
      Dravidian: Not a culture, but a huge language group in Southern India. Try stuff like Handbook of Hindu Gods, Goddesses and Saints Popular in Contemporary South India by Harry Daniel Smith and Tamil Temple Myths by David Shulman to get started.
      Hurrian: Not a lot left, better integrated into Hittite mythology like we did on the Hittite pantheon you'll get to see at ... some point. A complete translation of their myths is included in Hittite Myths by Harry A. Hoffner
      Micronesian: There are a lot of histories of Pohnpei in particular, I would poke at that. Otherwise, Summoning the Powers Beyond by Jay Dobbin sounds good.
      Miwok: This http://www.sacred-texts.com/nam/ca/mim/index.htm and this http://www.sacred-texts.com/nam/ca/dow/index.htm
      Seneca=Iroquois: I find Dean Snow's The Iroquois to be a conservative, but very good introduction to Iroquoian culture in general. He outlines important myths and gives pointers on where to find collections of them.
      Wyandot: Um, I'll ask Watcher, he should have a thing or two on them.

      various Polynesian cultures: I'm just copying what I have from a bibliography I once started on Polynesia, and never finished. Sorry, Samoa and Tonga are not yet included.

              Sacerdos is totally right that I can pass over some of the sources I have been looking at Wyandot mythology with. Helpfully, the bigger collections of their texts are digitized, and free for people to access. The older of the two, Huron and Wyandot Mythology, With an Appendix Containing Earlier Published Records was put together in 1915 by C. M. Barbeau as part of an anthropological series being done by the Canadian Department of Mines at the time. Since it is in public domain, it can be found on the Internet Archive right here. As always with a document this old, it is a product of its time, anthropology was undergoing some growing pains, so keep that in mind when reading it. The second free collection is actually hosted by the Wyandot Nation themselves, a transcribed collection named Indian Myths by a William Elsey Connelly, who was a member of the Wyandot tribe (by adoption). It can be found here.

              I don't know if you have access to these articles, but these are also rather interesting despite their age. If you want to dig into these ones, check out libraries around you, I bet someone has access to the articles since they're so old.
              Hale, Horatio. "Huron Folk-Lore. I. Cosmogonic Myths. The Good and Evil Minds." The Journal of American Folklore 1, no. 3 (October - December 1888): 177-83.
              Hale, Horatio. "Huron Folk-Lore. II. The Story of Tijaiha, the Sorcerer." The Journal of American Folklore 2, no. 7 (1889): 249-54.
              Hale, Horatio. "Huron Folk-Lore. III. The Legend of the Thunderers." The Journal of American Folklore 4, no. 15 (October - December 1891): 289-94.
              Barbeau, C. M. "Supernatural Beings of the Huron and Wyandot." American Anthropologist, New Series, 16, no. 2 (1914): 288-313.
              Sadly, for the rest of the cultures you are looking into, I can't be much help with directly. I can give you some suggestions for how to track stuff down if you want. If there are any Universities near where you are, totally just wander into their library and find one of their database computers. Most Universities will have programs so you can get a library card even if you are not a student, faculty, or alumni, so you can take books out no problem. Universities sort of just collect stuff over time, they will have something on the Pantheons you are looking for. Only thing to look out for his how old, and reputable the things you are reading are. If you can't do this, the other option is to find a Professor in the specific field you want to look at, and just drop them an email. Or see if you can find syllabuses for courses taught on these cultural groups floating around on line to mine for reading lists.

              Secondly, for the First Nations you want to look into, check out their websites. Just like the Wyandot I linked to up there, the Tribes will normally have their own websites, and the ones I have visited tend to have a section on cultural things where you could find mythology.

                  The Romani don't have a pantheon as far as I'm aware, they were originally Hindu and then were converted to Christianity and/or Islam, which lead them to have some distinct cultural views, but the vast majority as far as I'm aware are monotheistic. They have plenty of interesting folklore (like vampire watermelons) that could be incorporated and a patron saint (St. Sarah) who was probably originally the goddess Kali, so there's plenty to play around with, but no pantheon. Sacred-texts has a Roma section with a few Romani folktales if you wanna check them out.

                  The Lusitanians are in a similar boat to the Gauls, only they're even more obscure. All we have are inscriptions and the occasional statue, so there really isn't much to go on. Try looking into books about Celtic mythology in general. I haven't found any that focus specifically on the Lusitanians, at least not in English, but if you can read Portuguese or Spanish you might have better luck.

                  Also for the Native American pantheons, native-languages.org has a collection of different stories divided up by tribe, so you might find a few there.
