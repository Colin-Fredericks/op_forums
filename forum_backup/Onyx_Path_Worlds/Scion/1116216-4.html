                                                        I know I'm very late on this, but I want to agree with Starman that different individuals shouldn't be made into one, and Olokun definitely fits as a titan.

                                                        I also think Obatalá got a short stick here. This isn't really reflected in the purviews, but the write-up for him is just about how a king said he tricked Obatalá one time - even though Oduduwa's description does convey the notion that he might just be full of it. But Obatalá is known for having the greatest aché, which goes together for being the eldest, and for possessing the wisdom of both genders. He is the wisest, which is why he's the leader, and he's also the one in charge of diplomacy and peace, which for some reason wound up on Orisha Oko's description instead. I don't know if Yoruba in Africa no longer place importance on Obatalá, but at least in Cuban Lucumí he is arguably the most highly revered of the Orisha, and one of the five that every aborisha receives along with Oshún, Elleguá, Yemayá, and Shangó.

                                                        I also think that the emphasis on tolerance paints with a bit too broad a brush, to the extent that it gets perspectives on gender into the mix that aren't actually supposed to be there. Mostly related to complete disregard of the fact that it's an initiatory religion. This also comes up when it says (in Orunmila's description) that anyone can throw nuts or cowries - no, an aleyo definitely can't, and depending on lineage maybe an aborisha can't either, some say it's only to olorisha. But apparently Orunmila initiates women now? There is nothing intolerant about the orisha as a whole, but an individual orisha only crowns someone very similar to himself, which is why orunmila only crowns heterosexual men. An orisha who is gendered only crowns those of their own gender. Some others like Obatalá (who normally uses male pronouns by default, despite not being gendered) or Olokun (normally female pronouns) or Ibeji (who, unlike Obatalá, does use singular they) can take both genders, and Elleguá does too just because he's about communicating with difference. But gendered Orisha don't normally commune directly across lines, and Orunmila especially doesn't, he doesn't even commune with the faith in general very closely, Ifá is sort of a separate thing that just is compatible and shares cultural heritage.

                                                        I also felt like there was a flaw of the numbers - Ibeji is both two and one, which was dealt with okay. They detail of their scions was odd but I don't know if there's a better way of doing it. But Olodumare is three and one, which wasn't referenced at all except by listing all three facets as if they were just different names. I know that there's an aversion to monotheism in general, and Trinity is a Christian thing, but that doesn't change that it's also a part of the regla de Oshá.

                                                        I also thought it strange that exú was used as a name for Elleguá rather than a title, but the write-up went to such pains to use "camino" instead of the yoruba version but didn't do that for any other word. But it seems that he is sometimes referred to this way by some Brazilians. Although on the subject of Brazilians, it seems odd that there was all that talk of Candomblé but none of Umbanda, even though I know Quimbanda would have conflicted with the idea that this portrayal of the Orisha (like most) doesn't truck with human sacrifice, and they're apparently very different in how they worship anyway.
