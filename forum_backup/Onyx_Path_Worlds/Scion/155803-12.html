        Oh, that sounds quite neat Manannansun. I would be interested in seeing it whenever you find it in a presentable state.

        I can't remember where he was mentioned in Scion, I assume in Companion in the odd Underworld section they gave the Tuatha in RAW. Now, modern accounts I can't really say anything for. I work primarily with the medieval and early medieval literatures with only a dabbling in later folklore. Nothing that I would call modern, nothing from the last two centuries in the least. Straying away from what is written, getting into the realm of reconstruction, that is the point where my usefulness ends.

        Manannan and Manawydan are rather different figures in my readings of them. Sure, they may have at one point been a shared figure somewhere along the line, but they have been rather heavily changed by time, and by their recorders. Manawydan is wise, but not quite a magical trickster. He lacks the unending wealth of Manannan, and lacks any lands. In fact, that is likely the most important division of their characters. Manannan's connection to the oceanic otherworlds of Irish tradition is rather core to his character, while on the other hand, Manawydan lacks any connection to the ocean, and very specifically has no kingdom. He is spoken of in the texts of being this odd Landless Noble, a very strange concept. His lack of lands and wandering nature is important to him. But, nothing to do with the water, he just walks around Wales. Manawydan is more upstanding, lacking the Trickster angle to the degree that it defines Manannan, and is one of Arthur's many knights in the early lists.

        There might be a shared name, or possibly an ancient shared connection between the pair, but they are massively different. Their personalities are different, their positions within their respective literatures is different, their potential divine function is quite different. All they have is the name.

        For Rhiannon and Arwan, I don't think that she really has anything to do with him at all. A primary feature of Rhiannon in my reading of her character is that she has agency. By making it all part of some test by Arwan I think that agency that is very obviously being given to her by the author of the text is removed. We meet Rhiannon's father in fact, and he ins't Arwan. His name is Hyfaidd Hen (Hyfaidd the Old) and his lands are not otherworldly as Annwn is. Pwyll seems to know of Hyfaidd the Old since it forms a part of Rhiannon's introduction to him, there wouldn't be much of a point of her including it if Pwyll didn't know who she was talking about. I don't know where the implication she is from Annwn is, sure she's an otherworldly woman, but she's really just from around the block geographically. I don't think that there is any reason that Arwan would have for testing Pwyll at this point, Pwyll has already slain Arwan's one rival, ruled Annwn well, and did not sleep with Arwan's wife. In-character, I can see no reason Arwan would be testing Pwyll again, and the text never suggests it.

        Rhiannon is just a local titulary entity, she is a Soverignty figure in the text, that sort of thing. I don't think there is any relationship with Arwan past that they would meet each other one day since Arwan is so Pro-Pwyll and she has always loved Pwyll. Sure, they are both magical, otherworldly people, but there is never any suggestion that there is any connection between the two in text. There's also no suggestion that Bran, or Manawydan are related to either of them (before the third branch at least for Manawydan and Rhiannon). They are all magical and semi-Otherworldly, but they all seem to be operating in their own little areas.

        I think Rhiannon's agency is big though, she can't have been sent by Arwan without compromising the character she is written with. She has chosen to love Pwyll, and no one is allowed to marry her without her consent. Big stuff for the period. She even does the Niamh thing where she rolls up on her mortal boy-toy and sort of tells him that they're going to be lovers. Making her part of some test by Arwan undermines her character, and there doesn't seem to be any support in the text for such a thing.

                            Originally posted by Manannansun View Post
                            Does the artistry purview cover acting as well as film?
                            Artistry covers pretty much most forms of human artistic or technological creation. Acting and film both totally fall into the realm of the Purview. A Scion may devise a Relic monologue using the Artistry Purview for example. Actually preforming the play or movie in question might use Epic Manipulation though, similarly to how creating a magical bow uses Artistry, but to use it you need to use Epic Strength, Dexterity, or Perception.

                            Does that make sense? Inspiring others to be good actors, editors, or film makers is totally part of Artistry. Creating a script, or a musical score, or editing a draft of something is also Artistry. But, actually using the 'tool' that has been created starts to enter the realm of the Epic Attributes.

                                            Hi Watcher!
                                            I have a couple questions that you can probably answer. If a Irish Scion with Justice 4 Code of Heaven activates the boon in response to someone asking them to leave a place of business, that they were previously a valued client at does this work as an instance of Hospitality Breeching? It seems to me that it does since the owner welcomes a customer inside, offers them amenities, treats them like a honored guest, which is the same thing the Tuatha would do when welcoming a guest.                                             Secondly, when using Magic 8 The True World, does this also enact Justice 4 Code of Heaven? This would seem logical, especially if a deity with both purviews used it. Or is this incorrect?
                                            Sure, I'm more than happy to help answer your questions!

                                            So, for Hospitality. Having someone in your store wouldn't necessarily count as inviting hospitality to someone, the rules about it are both very complicated, and very simple. Depending on your social class, mostly detailed by your profession, you were given certain rights. One of these was the right to ask for hospitality from someone. Now, depending on your rank, and the rank of the person you are asking hospitality from, they may or may not have an obligation to provide hospitality on request.

                                            In the legal tracts, this is mostly a bunch of charts noting the rank of the asker, the rank of the askee, and then some extra notes such as the size of the retinue that needs to be supported. For example, a farmer would have to always give a King/Chieftian hospitality, giving them food, drink, and somewhere to sleep. The farmer was not expected to provide this for the entire retinue of their royal guest. Comparatively, if a Provincial King asked a Regional King/Chieftian for hospitality, the Regional Chieftian would be expected to provide for the King's retinue (up to a certain number, it's very detailed.)

                                            So, simply refusing to serve someone wouldn't be a violation of hospitality. Doing business with someone doesn't mean they are providing you hospitality, even if you are buying food, or shelter from them. However, you could ask for hospitality from them, and they might be expected to give it to you. If you were in a legal position to request hospitality, and they refused, that would count as a violation of Hospitality law and they could be compelled to pay you a fee based on their own rank, and your rank.

                                            If your character, and the store owner are both free individuals (not slaves) than they would be expected to provide hospitality for you. But, you must request it, and they must refuse before it would be a violation of hospitality. Depending on what profession your character is, the exact details would vary.

                                            Using An Eye for an Eye would likely have someone refuse them hospitality the next time they asked. It specifically has to be an echo of the broken law, so refused hospitality causes refused hospitality, theft causes theft, etc. So, if they refuse your character hospitality and you use the Boon, the next time they ask anyone for hospitality, it will be refused. Why? That will be up to the Storyteller. It might be that their friend who they just asked for hospitality from is out of town, but it couldn't cause their friend to dislike them, or cause a customer to stop using their services.

                                            Using The True World doesn't enact Code of Heaven, though I can see how it might sound like it does. The True World imposes one own's Pantheon's narrative concepts, and cosmology on the local area. It makes the natural laws of one's own Pantheon override everyone else's for a time. So, a Theoi Scion could use the Boon to bring Theoi cosmology as utterly dominant in the local Scene. Whichever of the Celestial Twins is active at the time would be able to see the area clearly from their chariot, Kami of objects might momentarily cease to be until the effect ends, Theoi Fate rules become active (inexorable, but enforced by the Gods), that sort of thing. It doesn't, however, make Greek or Roman laws or social customs become the norm.

                                            So, if you used it in Ireland, Dublin wouldn't become like downtown Athens culturally, but wherever you are would essentially become Hibernia. It would still be a far off, barbaric land, and outside the reach of Roman Law, but it would be a place defined by Roman cosmology. If a Tuatha Scion used the Boon in Greece, people wouldn't be compelled to follow Brehon Law, but the line between Mortal and God would become muddied like it is with the Irish, Professions might become semi-magical, bad kings would result in local fertility problems, etc.

                                            If a Deity who has both Boons uses both Boons, then they could totally work together. But, The True World doesn't natural cause the same effect as Code of Heaven by itself.

                                                    Originally posted by Manannansun View Post
                                                    Thanks Watcher! Could I also ask how certain social relationships work in accordance with Tuatha values, such as: romance, friendship, social media followers, etc. And how do certain factors such as declaring love, unfriending people, and such work in accordance with the Tuatha hierarchy? Lastly how would Tuatha relationships intermesh with those of modern society? I sincerely apology if these are nonsensical or stupid questions. I am just trying to figure out how the Tuatha and their scions would interact with modern society and how mortals would be expected to react. All of this is based on Scion 2e's premise that people just ignore what "shouldn't" be possible according to their personal beliefs and those of mainstream society.
                                                    Oh, don't worry about asking questions! There are no stupid questions, I am always happy to answer any that I can. I'll just break these down into sections so it's easier to read.

                                                    Social Relationships
                                                    Love is a very common theme in the medieval Irish literary texts. Modern ideas of love would mesh very well with the way the Tuatha see it, except with a dash of magic, and lethal sickness tied in. The texts have this theme of the Wasting Sickness, a really interesting concept. A Wasting Sickness strikes someone when they are in love, but, for some reason, can't physically access that whom they love. These people are bedridden, and literally waste away as they can't eat anything, slowly dying. CuChulain, Bobd Derg, and Aengus Og all fall ill through these sicknesses, and it takes the outside intervention of another to help snap them out of it. (Emer chases Fand off from CuChulain, Aengus finds Bobd Derg's lover, and Aengus is assisted in finding his beloved by The Dagda) So, in the love department, it fits in with modern ideas. Over the top and lethal, but that's par the course for the Pantheon.

                                                    Friendship is something that we don't see much in the Mythological Cycle, but we see it in the Ulster and Finnian Cycles. The one challenge we have is that, since these are stories, we don't ever see a 'normal day,' there has to be drama or there's no point in this being a story. So, the Friendships we see are normally ones under a lot of stress. CuChulain and his fellow Ulster Heroes are all thick as thieves, and in the Tain we see these Friendships put to the test as situations conspire to have CuChulain's friends set against him. Fergus mac Roich is a friend / father figure to CuChulain, and even though the pair are on opposite sides due to Fergus being incapable of making good life choices, they are still good friends who respect each other. There are several points where they both help one and other even to their own detriment. Conall Cernach is an older hero, but a ferocious friend of CuChulain who goes on the warpath when he is killed, slaughtering everyone who had anything to do with it. And then there's Fer Diad who CuChulain is very close friends with, hugging, kissing, sleeping in the same bed close. That's a whole other thing, but yeah.

                                                    So, Friendship is pretty much just like ours, except set in a narrative so constantly under threat due to the needs of a narrative for drama.

                                                    Now, Social Media is something something I hadn't considered before. The Tuatha would be those really overly dramatic people always up to shit more or less, but the idea of following, unfollowing, and that sort of thing, that would be a mess. That stuff would be taken dangerously seriously. Unfriending someone would be a really fierce social declaration that could really sour relationships and cause knock-on effects through a tribe. Friending someone could equally cause social friction. What would probably happen is everyone had very specific outwardly facing friend lists, friends with folks you didn't like, not friending friends of yours who would cause political messes. Unfriending someone who was above you in social rank would be a mess, I don't have any real textual examples to look to, but breaking up of friendships, or airing political dity laundry in the public like that's not something that would work well within the tight knit, incredibly dramatic social system we see in the texts.

                                                    Modern Society
                                                    Now, love the Tuatha as I do, their ideas of social relationships wouldn't work awesomely in modern society due to how their society was structured. Kings at the top, noble warriors below them, then trained professionals, everyone else, and then slaves. They were an incredibly classed society, complicated as shit. They were working with a class system that was, while theoretically mobile to a degree, so strict that your legal rights depended on what your rank was. Add to that the fact that the top of the social ladder is a Divine King, something modern society doesn't click much with, it would probably be rather tricky.

                                                    The biggest problem, however, is the exaltation of violence. The society the Tuatha are from / are an echo of / are a fabricated reference to (depending on what theory floats your boat) a society that exalted violence. Killing, raiding, taking slaves, all of this was a primary feature of society, and shaped the structuring of their society. Killing someone for wronging you is totally not cool modernly, but totally a legitimate (if not always legal) idea for the Tuatha. Add to that their Honor-Price system, and you get an even trickier class problem. The wealthier you are, the more you can inflict physical or social damage on other people by simply paying a fine. Implement this in our modern society with such a massive disparity between the wealthy and the not, and you would have the wealthy totally able to kill, maim, and worse to people with no real recourse for it. They would pay the fine, but it would be pocket change.

                                                    Divine and Mortal Interactions
                                                    Relationships between the Tuatha, their Scions, and Mortals would be a bit messy. For one, the Tuatha are a raiding, slaving, colonizing people who don't see anything wrong with that at all. Add to that the fact that they probably really want to take Ireland back from the Sons of Mil means that there would likely be conflict. The Tuatha are colonizers, Irish mythology is a story of mass colonization, and the Tuatha would be more than happy to just roll up on mortal Ireland and subjugate the mortal population. In my games, what I have generally keeping this at bay is all of the big names being dead. But, honestly, the danger to Ireland isn't the Fomori, it's the Tuatha. The Fomori want to tax people, and have client kingdoms. The Tuatha want Ireland, that's all they want, and they have slaughtered native populations (Fir Bolg) to do it before, and would be willing to do it again. The Tuatha are a dominating force, they want Ireland, and will accept no other options.

                                                    Now, ignoring all the problems with slavery, explotation of lower classes, strict social higherarchy, class systems, and general yucky social systems like that, there is one other big challenge with the relationship. The Tuatha, and their Scions, live these really dramatic, over the top lives. They are The Best and they are The Worst. There is no capacity of being in the middle ground, everything is Awful and Awesome. You fiercely defend your own supremacy, all the while trying to usurp others to nick their honor from them. It is a rather vicious social system, the focus on the best and greatest would create conflict everywhere. Every Tuatha Scion who doesn't win Best Actor would be up there drawing knives and walking on stage. The one who does win Best Actor is probably horrifically pissed they didn't also win Best Supporting Actor, Best Director, and Best Editor. The Olympics would suddenly be a very dangerous place to be. Any contest would be taken lethally seriously. From county fair, to the Nobel Peace Prize, the Tuatha need to be seen to be awesome and better than everyone else, including other members of the Tuath.

                                                    In Sum
                                                    Ignoring the Social System of the Tuatha, on an individual level they would fit into modern society fairly well. They would be behind the times with women, sexuality, and that sort of thing, but they wouldn't be too bad. The big challenges is that their Social System is something that most modern people would be very uncomfortable with, the fact that they are more than willing to purge Ireland, or enslave it's population, and the fact that they have no chill.

                                                    The Theoi are often described as a bunch of teenagers given Superpowers, the Tuatha are like if you removed someone's capacity to do anything not to the maximum passionate level, gave them super powers, and made them regard violence as a totally okay solution to most problems.

Conall Cernach is an older hero, but a ferocious friend of CuChulain who goes on the warpath when he is killed, slaughtering everyone who had anything to do with it. And then there's Fer Diad who CuChulain is very close friends with, hugging, kissing, sleeping in the same bed close. That's a whole other thing, but yeah.

                                                        So, Friendship is pretty much just like ours, except set in a narrative so constantly under threat due to the needs of a narrative for drama.

                                                        Now, Social Media is something something I hadn't considered before. The Tuatha would be those really overly dramatic people always up to shit more or less, but the idea of following, unfollowing, and that sort of thing, that would be a mess. That stuff would be taken dangerously seriously. Unfriending someone would be a really fierce social declaration that could really sour relationships and cause knock-on effects through a tribe. Friending someone could equally cause social friction. What would probably happen is everyone had very specific outwardly facing friend lists, friends with folks you didn't like, not friending friends of yours who would cause political messes. Unfriending someone who was above you in social rank would be a mess, I don't have any real textual examples to look to, but breaking up of friendships, or airing political dity laundry in the public like that's not something that would work well within the tight knit, incredibly dramatic social system we see in the texts.

                                                        Modern Society
                                                        Now, love the Tuatha as I do, their ideas of social relationships wouldn't work awesomely in modern society due to how their society was structured. Kings at the top, noble warriors below them, then trained professionals, everyone else, and then slaves. They were an incredibly classed society, complicated as shit. They were working with a class system that was, while theoretically mobile to a degree, so strict that your legal rights depended on what your rank was. Add to that the fact that the top of the social ladder is a Divine King, something modern society doesn't click much with, it would probably be rather tricky.

                                                        The biggest problem, however, is the exaltation of violence. The society the Tuatha are from / are an echo of / are a fabricated reference to (depending on what theory floats your boat) a society that exalted violence. Killing, raiding, taking slaves, all of this was a primary feature of society, and shaped the structuring of their society. Killing someone for wronging you is totally not cool modernly, but totally a legitimate (if not always legal) idea for the Tuatha. Add to that their Honor-Price system, and you get an even trickier class problem. The wealthier you are, the more you can inflict physical or social damage on other people by simply paying a fine. Implement this in our modern society with such a massive disparity between the wealthy and the not, and you would have the wealthy totally able to kill, maim, and worse to people with no real recourse for it. They would pay the fine, but it would be pocket change.

                                                        Divine and Mortal Interactions
                                                        Relationships between the Tuatha, their Scions, and Mortals would be a bit messy. For one, the Tuatha are a raiding, slaving, colonizing people who don't see anything wrong with that at all. Add to that the fact that they probably really want to take Ireland back from the Sons of Mil means that there would likely be conflict. The Tuatha are colonizers, Irish mythology is a story of mass colonization, and the Tuatha would be more than happy to just roll up on mortal Ireland and subjugate the mortal population. In my games, what I have generally keeping this at bay is all of the big names being dead. But, honestly, the danger to Ireland isn't the Fomori, it's the Tuatha. The Fomori want to tax people, and have client kingdoms. The Tuatha want Ireland, that's all they want, and they have slaughtered native populations (Fir Bolg) to do it before, and would be willing to do it again. The Tuatha are a dominating force, they want Ireland, and will accept no other options.

                                                        Now, ignoring all the problems with slavery, explotation of lower classes, strict social higherarchy, class systems, and general yucky social systems like that, there is one other big challenge with the relationship. The Tuatha, and their Scions, live these really dramatic, over the top lives. They are The Best and they are The Worst. There is no capacity of being in the middle ground, everything is Awful and Awesome. You fiercely defend your own supremacy, all the while trying to usurp others to nick their honor from them. It is a rather vicious social system, the focus on the best and greatest would create conflict everywhere. Every Tuatha Scion who doesn't win Best Actor would be up there drawing knives and walking on stage. The one who does win Best Actor is probably horrifically pissed they didn't also win Best Supporting Actor, Best Director, and Best Editor. The Olympics would suddenly be a very dangerous place to be. Any contest would be taken lethally seriously. From county fair, to the Nobel Peace Prize, the Tuatha need to be seen to be awesome and better than everyone else, including other members of the Tuath.

                                                        In Sum
                                                        Ignoring the Social System of the Tuatha, on an individual level they would fit into modern society fairly well. They would be behind the times with women, sexuality, and that sort of thing, but they wouldn't be too bad. The big challenges is that their Social System is something that most modern people would be very uncomfortable with, the fact that they are more than willing to purge Ireland, or enslave it's population, and the fact that they have no chill.

                                                        The Theoi are often described as a bunch of teenagers given Superpowers, the Tuatha are like if you removed someone's capacity to do anything not to the maximum passionate level, gave them super powers, and made them regard violence as a totally okay solution to most problems.

                                                            Watcher,Ive noticed the scion preview mentioned the possibility of the fey being older than the tuatha. But Ive also heard that the fey até the descendents of the gods. What do you think is true?
                                                            I think it's fair to interpret it either way, the Sidhe (Irish word for Fey, Fairy, or Elf. Sort of. It actually is just a word for the Hollow Hills these beings live in, but they get commonly called the Sidhe after their housing) as something older than the Tuatha, or possibly their descendants, or even the Tuatha themselves. It's sort of a tricky situation with them. The Sidhe are originally just the Otherworldly People who reside in the Sidhe Mounds, the Hollow Hills that dotted the Mythic Landscape of Ireland. The Tuatha are sometimes described as Sidhe, while other times, Sidhe are described as being something that is somehow different from the Deities. Manannan sends Sidhe Knights with Lugh when he rides out with his foster brothers to fight Balor, suggesting they are somehow different from the Tuatha. At the same time, in The Intoxication of the Ulstermen, you see The Dagda and some of the other Tuatha described as being Sidhe, unseen people capable of strange things.

                                                            Strictly, based on the Book of Invasions, the Sidhe could only be older than the Tuatha if they were something to do with the Fir Bolg. All of the other, elder peoples, are dead or dead enough that there's not enough people to remain a power. But, they are complicated enough a subject that it's totally up to the authors to decide how to best use them. They are particularly tricky, one of the most difficult Legendary Beings to acomodate in Scion.

                                                            One of the biggest challenges is honestly that the Sidhe everyone likes to talk about are from the medieval period, and totally non-mythic. Including them is a lot of fun, but they technically are not really part of Irish Mythology. The actual 'truth' about the Sidhe is probably that it's more or less a word that means 'Non-Human Human-Like Supernatural Beings Who Reside In The Sidhe Mounds,' including the Deities, 'Fairy Queens' like Fand and Niamh, and minor entities like the Dullahan. It's less of a species, and more of a really broad term.

                                                            However, that's literally an idea I just came up with right now inspired by how the Haida use a term roughly meaning 'Supernatural Beings' to describe everything from big Deities, to 'smaller' entities. Any interpretation of the Sidhe is okay more or less, they're a tricky thing to deal with. It's a term that has obviously changed meaning over the last thousand years. The way I tend to depict it in my games is that there is a species of Supernatural Beings who are the Sidhe, sort of like minor tribal groups with their own Monarchs, more or less subserviant to the Tuatha.
