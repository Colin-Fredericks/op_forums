                    Originally posted by Ryu View Post
                    China has quite a few beings who were elevated to immortality but are neither gods nor godborns in mythology.
                    Well, it's the term "god" that is the problem, really. Modern English-speakers bring a lot of Christianizing assumptions along with the word. But really all it means is "something that receives worship," and that's not an essential category. In ancient Greece, a theos could be anything from a powerful cosmic spirit to a local protective spirit to a dead ancestor to a living monarch. You could be born a mortal and become a theos if and when people in your region started treating you as a god (i.e. offering sacrifice and votive offerings in return for your aid). Chinese practice isn't significantly different, nor is Japanese, ancient Mesopotamian, or pretty much any other traditional culture whose concept of divinity hasn't been influenced by Christianity.

                    Scion adds an additional wrinkle in positing that gods are a particular species of being, distinct from powerful mortals, ghosts, heroes, et al., and mortals' attitude towards them is only part of the picture of how they got to be that way. So in Scion, the Theoi and the Shen are more specific than just any beings who receive divine worship; they're extended families whose status is hereditary and tied to the ichor in their veins. Of course, in ancient Greek (and quite possibly Chinese) myth, it's no problem, since everybody with a name has divine ancestry somewhere in their family tree. So it's easy to show how heroes like Heracles and Diomedes become gods. Does the same apply to Guan Yu? Not sure, but in any case every culture with gods has myths about mortals who were elevated to their ranks. Were they Scions? Maybe. Or maybe there are other means besides genetics by which a mortal can acquire a Legend rating.

                                        Izanagi-no-Mikoto and Izanami-no-Mikoto are brother and sister, and husband and wife. Amaterasu Omikami and Tsukiyomi no Mikoto are also husband and wife, sister and brother. They never divorced, but did have children over time (which is where the Imperial line comes from). And while Uke-Mochi was killed, there's some indication she simply reincarnated (as O-Inari) - or she may have been O-Inari's wife - the jury's still out on this.

                                        I'm doing historical research on O-Inari, which means I've had a good hard look at the Japanese pantheon. Susano-O caused a huge fuss with Amateras Omikami by throwing a horse's hide into her sewing room, causing her daughter / sister (jury's out on that one) to freak out and kill herself. Strangely, that individual is also considered an aspect of O-Inari. Then again, so is Izanagi no Mikoto and Izanami no Mikoto. And Amaterasu Omikami's grandson, who brought the three sacred treasures to the first Emperor of Japan. So, O-Inari is Amaterasu Omikami's parents, sister/daughter, and grandson. Oh, and three of the Seven Fortunes (Daikoku, Benten, and Benzaiten), and also the Ame no Uzume no Mikoto, who danced to draw Amaterasu Omikami out of her cave, and her husband Sarutahiko Okami.

                                        The thing is, Shinto is... interesting. Anything which inspires wonder or joy is a kami. And anything that is a kami can be revered. And if it is has a form, it is also a god. Going by the Shinto tradition, the Monkey King (while being a character from a known work of fiction) is an actual god, and respected as such. But this goes for any character as well. In fact, the Japanese have a word for 'gods of other lands', and these gods are respected and revered as kami. This is how they incorporated Hindu, Chinese, and Buddhist gods into the pantheon, and goes far beyond that. Jesus Christ? Yes, he's a kami. So are all the Archangels, named Angels, and Saints. They're all 'equivalent' as far as divine prestige is concerned.

                                        And it gets a bit mind-breaking as well - fictional characters are considered fair game (as per the Monkey King). This means Sherlock Holmes, Santa Claus, Harry Potter, and any other character who is inspirational can be accepted as a kami. You hear of Japanese men marrying their virtual idols? Well, that's because these virtual idols are kami, and as kami, they exist as beings. The Japanese pantheon has an endless supply of kami to call upon when needed... through the entertainment industry, they can produce hundreds of thousands of kami to throw onto the front lines, and the larger the fan-base, the more powerful the kami can probably become.

                                        The thing with O-Inari is that she's a composite goddess. The 'five gods who make one' thing isn't unique to her either, but in Shinto, it is understood that these individual gods are individual gods, but also O-Inari, and O-Inari is her own being, and also these individual gods. I can understand the concept, having taken years to wrap my head around Shinto before I joined the faith, but it falls neatly into the 'if it inspires, it exists'. O-Inari is the most famous god in Japan, with over a million shrines. When I asked my Sensei about this, whether O-Inari is an individual or a group, and whether it was right to revere her as an individual or as part of a whole, he answered 'yes'. To his mind, these are not contradictions.

                                        I'm probably going to stick with my Greek demigod, but I'm certain my sister's going to want to delve back into the Japanese pantheon, and she's going to want to try to adhere to the Shinto faith as close as she can - her character is a shrine maiden after all (and her mother, Ame-no-Uzume-no-Mikoto, was the first shrine maiden).

                                                Originally posted by Samudra View Post

                                                I think I understand the concept, since it is very similar to Hinduism's concept of Nirguna Brahman. The parallels between our religions is fascinating.

                                                Your explanation of the generation of new Kami makes Tsukumo-Gami sound a lot more interesting. It sounds not entirely unlike how many Hindu villages and various sacred groves have lesser immortal guardians or the nymphs of Greek myth, only far more widely applicable. Tell me, would you consider these spontaneously generated kami to be lesser immortals or minor Legend 9 Gods? I know Tsukumo-Gami does the former (and honestly, I prefer it that way), but I would like an actual Shinto perspective.
                                                It would really depend on the portfolio of the kami in question. If it is a kami of a lesser grove or something, it would probably be appropriate to say 'Demigod'. The other thing to consider is whether it is an embodied kami or a disembodied spirit. The latter are not venerated directly, though they're still powerful spirits. 'The spirit of the forest' is considered a being, but not a god, but the moment it takes a host and manifests an avatar, it is considered to be a god. Once it leaves again, it becomes 'the spirit'.

                                                The local museum had a Vodou exhibit, which was an incredible experience. One of the things which astonished me was that Vodou and Shinto are damn near sibling religions. They have kami, they have ancestors, they have the goal of "community over the self" - the happiness of those around you is worth more than your personal desires (and in turn, your community is expected to put your happiness over their desires as well). The idea of voodoo dolls is complete and utter bullcrap in the Vodou faith, and is not a thing.

                                                The entire thing was eye-opening, and I was able to understand the concepts presented for Vodou clearly, having spent so much time studying Shinto. It is a very ... clean.. religion, in that sense. The other thing that Shinto and Vodou have in common is the idea that "good" and "evil" are not spiritual concepts - neither religion has anything to do with the cosmic battle of Good vs Evil, because these things do not exist. "Evil" is defined by humanity and human laws. It is defined as 'what harms the community'. What Shinto, and Vodou, focus on is spiritual purity. That is why these faiths focus on ritual purification. They want your spirit and body to be unsullied by negativity and unclean things.

                                                And from the perspective of Shinto, all spirits are innately pure. They are not Good or Evil, they simply exist in accordance to their nature. A spirit, within its natural habitat, exists in harmony with that habitat. If it is moved outside of its habitat, then you get disharmony. The spirit acts purely in accordance with its nature, but the environment may not be meant for it. So the goal is to appease the spirit, bring it where it should be, and all will be well. Or, as an example: A demon in the Chinese Hells tortures sinners, cleansing them of their sins before the soul can reincarnate. That's well and good. The demon isn't evil, it is fulfilling its purpose. Summon the demon to Earth, and what do you get? A demon surrounded by people who are still filled with sin. So the demon tortures and wounds these people trying to purify them. Of course, these people still have fleshy bodies, so .. they die and suffer. The demon senses this suffering, and does more, trying to "correct" this as it only knows how. This is imbalanced, and so exorcism sends the demon back to Hell, where it can return to doing its job. The demon isn't evil.

                                                Of course, from a human perspective, the demon is evil, since it is torturing and killing. But that isn't the demon's fault - it is in the wrong place. So to deal with the demon, you should remain respectful and courteous, and then send it the hell on its way.

                                                    Hello, all. Just wanted to drop in with some slightly more solid information on Scion: Origins, though Neall has graciously touched base on some of this.

                                                    I've got several goals with this book. This stuff is from my outline for the book, as an aside.

                                                    Rules Book: First and foremost, I wanted a book where we can consolidate the majority of the system. Doing so gives us extra room in the Hero through God books for richer background material.

                                                    Pre-Visitation Scions: There's plenty of indication that Scions are just flat-out exceptional in many ways even before they receive that Visitation that brings them to awareness of their own semi-divine nature. For some folks, it's enough to just handwave and narrate away those sorts of stories, but if there is a motto I have as a developer, it is: "I like options." I love running prelude arcs, and know plenty of other folks do as well, so we'll be including some mechanics for these just-above-normal mortals, and give some guidelines for running Visitations and the like.

                                                    Followers: This is also where we're going to include the mechanics for presenting the various not-quite-divine sorts of characters that were often Birthrights in Scion1e: Amazons, satyrs, and that crew. These mechanics will provide stats for those characters to be used as NPCs or as player characters. We'll also be including some mechanics in Hero for granting these entities a full dot of Legend and "leveling them up" so that your Demigod can possibly have Hero-level cohorts and leave them in the dust once he transcends to demigodhood, or so players who want to play such a character in an otherwise Hero-level campaign may do so.

                                                    Mortals & the Fated: While the full rules for Fatebinding and the like are in Hero, Origins will be what you use to create those characters that become tied to your Scions. It's essentially a "mortals rulebook," with the options of adding some templates to them to turn them into supernatural creatures (though we aren't specifically using the "template" mechanic for this).

                                                    Sorcerers, Priests & Oracles: This book will also include rules for reflecting those mortals whose lives have been touched by Legend in some capacity. They might be occultists and spiritualists who are Getting It Right, they might be oracles and seers haunted by divine vision, they might be those who still render up worship in the Old Ways (or Current Ways for some!). These folks will also get some "level up" potential that follower/cohort-types do in Hero.

                                                    Antagonists: There were plenty of not-quite-Hero-scale antagonists in the original game, many of them supernatural gribblies: vampires, ghosts, werewolves. This is going to be where those get placed, as well - also not just as NPCs, and with the potential to "level up" to Hero-scale so you can have awesome ancient vampire lords, super-werewolves and whatnot.

                                                    Urban Fantasy: Because I don't like the idea of a core rulebook that just "serves" another sort of game entirely, we're also making this book a sort of "urban fantasy RPG," as it were. Inspired by stories as myriad as Butcher's Dresden to the works of Charles de Lint and everything inbetween (and beyond), we'd like this game to provide some inspiration for the sorts of stories you might tell about a world that has seen an upswing in Legend once more and how that affects normal folks.

                                                    So, yes. There you have it.
