                                suspension of disbelief is defined by the willingness of the audience to believe in the reality the story is telling. It is a must thing to enjoy every story, or else the crowd starts questioning the story they are watching. The writer defines the reality the story takes place in, and as long as the story doesn't contradict the laws of that universe, the crowd is willing to suspend their disbelief and enjoy the story. There is no story without a suspension of disbelief, for once people stop believing in what the story tells, they don't care about it anymore. This is one of the most important things for writing a good story.

                                Now, the law of reality defined in American Gods is that their universe is almost like our universe, with only one difference- gods are real. Gods are defined by the things people have faith in, and that they require that faith to exist. As long as people remember their gods, they should exist. Once forgotten, the gods die. As far as I understand (as I haven't read the books), that's the law for American Gods's universe. Now, killing a god is possible- gods do it all the time in mythology, but true death seem to have been defined through being forgotten. While Vulcan has been presented as gaining a great push at his worship through working with the New Gods and having a company at his name, we know who Vulcan is, and that even without the weapon company he should be at least in the same state as Odin in terms of being remembered or honored. We also know that Vulcan is a real place named after that god. As far as I understand the story until now, the events shown in it are not going to end with the change of the world as we know it- it is not some sort of apocalyptic story, or that it is about the future of the world. It is about the present, or maybe the past. The key for American Gods's universe is by being similar enough to our universe. If people would start forget about a god which we, as an audience, still remember, or talk about a collapse of a place which we, as an audience, know is still around, the suspension of disbelief starts to strain- not collapse, just strain. That's a dangerous thing to do. This is not a good story. That's how I get bored- and for stories, there is nothing more dangerous than getting bored of them.

                                At least, that's my world view, both as an audience and a writer.
