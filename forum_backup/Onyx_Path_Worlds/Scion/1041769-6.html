                                                That is a really good way to explain that, I just had to chime in here. The Chaos - Order situation is something we tend to have a very hard time grasping, really well exemplified by our modern conception of Chaos being all about randomness, such as the old 1e Chaos Purview was based around. To the ancient world, Chaos was more of a societal thing, or natural thing. The sun breaking it's pattern, that's chaos. A king who is unjust (for the Irish, many cultures have a king=law thing so the king can't be unjust) is chaos. Killing a king is chaos, etc. No one wanted to take the risk of their city or town falling into strife without any local authority.
                                                * Zeus and Fate: I guess one could say he can not completely get rid of the fate of being overthrown because that fate is part of his nature. Zeus is Zeus, and as long as he behaves in a zeus-like manner, as long as he stays true to himself, eventually his own actions will inevitably cause his downfall. Like a proper Greek tragedy. But, since Zeus, for all his faults, is a better ruler than his predecessors, he's not as thoroughly doomed as they were.

                                                * Chaos and order: thank you, this angle could really use more discussion. But I guess, considering the way tyrants were viewed by many in ancient Hellas, the fall of a ruler would actually represent the restoration of proper order to them. Even a benevolent "monarch" lives constantly aware of a sword hanging above his head. And in Ireland, the Sovereignty Goddess knows full well when a "king" (chieftain, rather) becomes unfit for his position, and easily dumps him for another. What is disruptive is actually a ruler who refuses to submit to this cycle. I would argue that the tension between a desire for safety and stability vs. a desire for self-expression and freedom is impossible to fully resolve on a cultural level, but different civilizations occasionally will value one over the other (temporarily). An unjust ruler is still better than no ruler/foreign ruler in Kemetic or Babylonian political culture, but not for a proud Norse bondi or for Athenian citizens in the age of Perikles.
