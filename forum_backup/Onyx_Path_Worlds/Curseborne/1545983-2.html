    "Obsolete" may not be the best word, here, and there is a definite reason I avoided the notion. Many pre-modern weapons do absolutely devastating things to the human body in comparison to a single bullet from modern military small arms (grenades are another matter, of course) - there are a lot of strict international treaties regarding what a military weapon can be manufactured to be capable of that absolutely did not exist before World War I. (For example: most real-life hunters use ammunition against animals that would absolutely be considered an instant, no-questions-asked, automatic conviction of war crime if used against a human target in a military context. Expanding bullets are a huge no no in war, for precisely the same reasons they're considered the most humane choice for a hunter: they do lots of internal damage and kill very quickly, leaving little to no chance for a medic to potentially rescue the wounded - or for an animal to limp off somewhere unseen only to suffer needlessly and die slowly.)

    ...it's just that modern military small arms put that damn many bullets in the air. The only thing actually "obsolete" about most pre-modern weapons is how slow their rate/volume of fire is by comparison - a detail that is somewhat less relevant in close quarters and isn't especially narratively useful to a game that wants werewolves tearing at things with claws and teeth while angels swing flamings swords around. Fundamentally, this is what I was thinking in choosing to restrict Heavy Weapon - and thus Power Advantage - to the full-sized machine gun. Curseborne already essentially gives everybody Chronicles' Quick Draw Merit for free, handling drawing or stowing weapons as reflexive actions, and I figure it reasonably follows that reloading is handled in like fashion - at least up until that moment where a Complication goes "click." While they aren't necessarily common the way the rules handle fights, I feel that Complications are a better way to bring in the limitations of pre-modern gear than penalizing functional tags that have nothing to do with slow rate of fire just because the weapon's design is "old."

    I'd also be careful about trying to split the tags themselves into formal groupings - you'll run into problems that way that will absolutely require frequent deviation from the formula you gave above. To use an example that comes immediately to mind, most "primitive" designs of one-handed swords would lose what you termed as the "form" tag (Light Weapon), not the "damage" tag (Wounding) - they're still vicious cutting edges, and quite large, but less finely balanced for ease of use. For the most part, I handled this from a mindset sitting between narrativist and simulationist - which two tags can I use to tell the story of this weapon best? No tags or just one tag were themselves an answer I tried to save for a specific purpose - mostly for things that are halfway to being improvised weapons (small knives and simple bludgeons) rather than being purpose-built to kill. (As for the blowgun, there's that obvious extra tag heavily associated with them that was deliberately left off on account of being applied through a certain set of substances that can also be applied temporarily to a large number of other weapons, too...)

    All that said, you do have a great point that in modern settings where Kevlar is a thing, flintlocks and other early firearms should probably be "demoted" and separated from the crossbows they otherwise share tags with by the loss of the Piercing tag. The loss of effectiveness against the class of armor they would and should defeat in a "Dark Eras" scenario (chain) where they still have that tag is certainly less important than the fact that they definitely shouldn't be effective against the modern equivalents of that same class of armor (ballistic fiber jackets and vests).

        Fair point that "obsolete" is a tricky word to use, although I thing when it comes to premodern firearms there's a lot more to it that just rate of fire. There's also accuracy, tendency to misfire, and so on. Although you're probably right that a single musket ball with the right charge of powder might do more to an unarmoured person than a modern .23 bullet I don't think I'd give the musket ball any tags to actually *represent* that because all of the other drawbacks of the weapon are so limiting.

        I do agree that formal categories are a bad idea, I was mostly thinking less in terms of formal categorisation and more in terms of keeping an eye to what tags *actually do*. Brutal is a good example here. Sure from a certain perspective a musket is a more "brutal" weapon than a handgun but is it actually, broad strokes, easier to kill somebody with? It's fiddly to use, if it's not already loaded and primed it's basically useless, it's just not a good weapon by modern standards. The reason I wanted to separate out tags was that the "damage" tags are the ones that actually make the weapon more *effective* relative to other weapons, as opposed to the "form" tags, which are more about just making the weapon work the way it's meant to.

        Give a musket Brutal and it's one of the best ranged weapons to use in a street fight (unless you're strict about reloading) and that seems like an odd call to me. Although as you say it depends on whether you're trying to "tell the story" of the weapon, or to think about the consequences the tags will have on in-game use.

        I also think the "Dark Eras" problem is best solved by straight up changing out the tags of historical weapons in their appropriate era. That's part of the reason I used the term "obsolete". I don't think a flintlock should have any tags at all in a modern day game because I just don't think it's a weapon that should be considered an effective choice relative to more modern options, but in a game set in its own era, I'd give it Versatile and Piercing.

                Y’all may disagree, but I would make Brutal the go to tag for most weapons that hack, slash, stab, tear, or penetrate - and I would make wounding and piercing more relatively rare in comparison.

                Why? For a couple of reasons. Brutal is very straightforward, it makes it easier to inflict more damage on an opponent. A weapon with a sharp cutting edge is more likely to inflict worse damage than one with it, a club will inflict less damage than if you turn the club into a mace with metal spikes.

                Piercing ignores most armor and there are few weapons that ignore most armor. Modern rifles, at least rounds with a full metal jacket, are a weapon that ignore most armor. When I was in BLET, at the range we shot soft kevlar body armor with rifles and the bullet passed through both sides like it was cloth. The same vest which stopped a 44 magnum round. That indicates to me that they should be piercing. To stop such a round, you need armor with ballistic plates, which means it’s own tag for that armor.

                I would make Wounding a bit rarer, even though any sharp weapon could be potentially wounding, in part because of book keeping. Having a bunch of characters in a combat suffering from bleeding and taking wounds or suffering a disadvantage seems tedious to me. For ranger weapons, I would give Wounding to shotguns (that cause massive tissue damage) and arrows (which stick into the body and continue causing problems).

                So, assuming we don’t need to give handguns the versatile tag, which is convenient, here is how I would handle some weapons:

                Pistol: concealable, brutal
                Shotgun: wounding, brutal
                Rifle (FMJ): long range, piercing
                Rifle (expanding round): brutal, long range
                Rapier (or small sword, epee du combat): light, brutal
                Sword (arming sword, saber, katana, etc.): Wounding, Brutal


                I’d honestly like to give rifles a 3rd tag, but I’m staying within the 2 tag limit.

                This isn’t to say that this should be definitive, I like other people’s suggestions too and think that they make sense, but I think this is how I might envision things.

                        Originally posted by Sergeant Brother View Post
                        When I was in BLET, at the range we shot soft kevlar body armor with rifles and the bullet passed through both sides like it was cloth. The same vest which stopped a 44 magnum round.
                        That's actually an incredibly helpful tidbit. While I've at least worked with a few pistols and hunting rifles myself, I don't have quite that much experience - and none at all with military gear. To me, this suggests it should really only be rifles (or muskets in a pre-modern setting) and full-size crossbows that get Piercing, not pistols or pistol crossbows. (Needle-swords like the estoc too, as their whole design paradigm was the exploitation of joints and moving parts on armored targets.) Would that sound more in line with your experiences?

                        As for "most things are Brutal," can't say I'm much a fan of that idea myself, but this is definitely a spot where it doesn't hurt to say to each his own! For me, at least, the situational benefits and drawbacks of the different tags are part of the charm, and the extra bookkeeping is worth the variability of scenarios they create - I'd hate to get stuck with a system like combat in NWoD 1e again. Just with blades versus bludgeons, for example: Brutal makes purchasing Critical easier, which is great for emphasizing that a well-placed strike with a bludgeon absolutely does not care if you're wearing armor or not, while Wounding only costs 1 hit, not 2 like a Brutal Critical, but can't be applied if you haven't worked your way past the enemy's armor and dealt an injury to them underneath it yet.

                        It wouldn't hurt to have two separate equipment lists floating around for people to use, though - one more bookkeeping-intense, and one less. At least it gives us one useful advantage from Curseborne's lack of example mundane equipment!

                                An interesting spanner to be sure... but one which has me reevaluating the Wrecking and Piercing tags, more than the Wounding tag.

                                The more that I look at it, the clearer it becomes that quite contrary to Chronicles, at least, mundane armor appears to be intentionally worth less than dog spit in Curseborne. Why would any sane, rational player waste 2 hits on a Brutal Critical that's just gonna get soaked by armor alongside the base injury, when Sunder (or Destroy Object for ranged weapons with Wrecking) also costs 2 hits... and shuts down all of the target's worn armor's traits leaving the target to count as if unarmored, no questions asked, for the remainder of the scene.

                                Yeah, the base injury before you buy Sunder (or a Wrecking-empowered Destroy Object) still gets soaked by the armor before it goes offline, but... that was going to happen regardless.

                                (Note that Sunder, the close combat Trick, doesn't even require Wrecking to do this. By default, Sunder, which is melee only, destroys gear, while Destroy Object, which is ranged only, destroys cover or unattended objects - what the Wrecking tag allows is for each Trick to accomplish what is normally restricted to the other. Looks like I should have put Wrecking on the axes, as it turns out...)

                                Meanwhile, "armor" generated by Impenetrable Skin (or Swift Defense with its Entanglement 2 upgrade) cannot be removed by Sunder or Destroy Object, even with Wrecking, and continues to provide any side benefits (few as they are) even once the bonus injury boxes are filled... potentially making them a near-complete immunity button to the Wounding tag (outside of Red Death, which is Ethereal and therefore requires armor with Magic Resistance).

                                Your thoughts here?

                                        I won't deny the fact that we've hit some "white room" stuff here (with all the attendant problems associated with that). The specifics of Critical versus Sunder end up being more about whether or not the other benefits of armor remain online after the bonus health boxes are exhausted. If yes, then you'll want Sunder even at +1 to get those pesky Impervious or Fitted tags out of the way ASAP; otherwise, it's six one way half a dozen the other at +2 and even at +3 only actually matters if the two extra hits needed for Sunder come up immediately on the first successful attack. But while those kinds of semantics might be relevant to a project like mine in trying to build a cohesive table of weapons and armor drafted to use the tags to play off of each other in narratively-appropriate ways, I won't deny it definitely misses the six million dollar question, one you've alluded to indirectly many times: Just exactly how many significant antagonists are actually going to use mundane gear?

                                        Even as much value as I see in drafting my list of mundane equipment, I know perfectly well what the answer to that question is: "Very few."

                                        Venators, mostly - and venators aren't supposed to be that common. Social threats might surround themselves with leg breakers wielding this kind of stuff. Maybe the odd rival Accursed who is almost as green as the crew of Entanglement 1 PCs are themselves, or a Phantasm terrorizing people with telekinesis and whatever happens to be lying about.

                                        It has been a really good opportunity for me to think on how different parts of the system fit together as I draft my first characters in it, though! The map may not be the territory, but used properly it's still a tool to navigate with!
