    A repost of a section of my review of the manuscript that was influenced by this:

    What do you do in the Game?

    This complicated question is answered across the entire book but comes in two parts. The first being, "Whatever you want." I once described the initial success of Vampire: The Masquerade as being the game about nothing. You can get up, feed, and go down to the Succubus Club to hang out with other vampires as what you do in the game. The game recognized being a vampire or other supernatural was an inherently interesting Curseborne hasn't released its equivalent of Chicago by Night but there's plenty of fun to be had with the idea of socializing as an Addams Family or Munsters group of people.

    The second, more traditional, RPG part is probably best defined as, "Defend your territory against the onrush of the weird." While only the Outsiders are 100% committed to fighting the Outside (Extra-Dimensional Space), the game makes it abundantly clear that Earth's veil to the Spirit World is swiss cheese rather than a brick wall. Earth is constantly being invaded by supernatural nasties that turn houses into the Overlook Hotel or the equivalent of Walmart-sized Mimics.

    The nature of the WOC is that as an Accursed, you are burdened with the ability to see all the monsters around you and ignoring them is not necessarily an option. Even if your characters are sociopaths, they're probably not going to happy if Chucky starts killing kids around them. You don't want to attract the wrong kind of attention after all. Much is made of holding territory as a form of status and power in the Accursed world. To hold territory means you have to defend it against the other, less pleasant supernaturals out there.

    Author of Cthulhu Armageddon, I was a Teenage Weredeer, Straight Outta Fangton, Lucifer's Star, and the Supervillainy Saga.

        I'm a bit concerned I'm going to come across as just playing both sides here or arguing for the sake of arguing, but I think this is actually a really strong example of why so many people are bouncing off of Curseborne (a game which, I'll reiterate, I'm actually enthusiastic about and am actively preparing to run).

        This in particular:

        The nature of the WOC is that as an Accursed, you are burdened with the ability to see all the monsters around you and ignoring them is not necessarily an option. Even if your characters are sociopaths, they're probably not going to happy if Chucky starts killing kids around them. You don't want to attract the wrong kind of attention after all. Much is made of holding territory as a form of status and power in the Accursed world. To hold territory means you have to defend it against the other, less pleasant supernaturals out there.
        seems to be exactly the kind of thing people are talking about when they don't see what the game is set up for beyond reactive, monster-of-the-week style gameplay. Certainly it doesn't appeal to me as an elevator pitch at all; if anything it reminds me of the absolute worst experiences I've had with Vampire the Masquerade, the games where despite all the lore and setting details, in practice what you have is a totally reactive game where some problem comes along and it's just kind of assumed that it's up to the PCs to solve it because they're PCs.

        What it comes down to for me is that I'll judge the success or failure of this game on whether playing a Hungry makes me feel like a vampire (which honestly VtM didn't always do well and VtR second did very well indeed), whether Sorcerers feel like sorcerers and the werewolves feel like werewolves.

        The pitch you're giving here makes it feel like no matter what Family I pick, I'm meant to be playing An Accursed (tm) which is a type of ordinary human who is cursed with the ability to see monsters and feels like they have an obligation to protect people from them. That might even be a fun thing for a game to be, but then it's wasting a ton of time and energy with all this stuff about being angels and demons and shapeshifters. Going back to Michael Brazier's point, it feels very strongly built around telling the kind of story that defaults to having mortal protagonists.

I think a lot of us are seeing the problem with the current set up almost being more appropriate for a "Mortals with psychic abilities fighting the liminal spaces and what they spit out", then a game where we play Supernatural monsters co existing and our different lineages and families actually *mean* something significant.

            I think I'm going to post my own version of the lineages soon, when I have a better handle on how the combat system works.

            Things I would do to make those lineages feel more impactful to one another.

            I think I'd make the Dead more like The Crow, unstoppable revenants that don't feel pain, recover easily from damage and are driven by passionate obsession.

            For Primals, a customizable Wild Form that gets more powerful with each level of entanglement.

            Outcasts all get the ability to make "Pacts" with Mortals, granting their wishes or imparting supernatural power. The pacted mortal then becomes a servant and source of curse energy with higher entanglement giving a greater amount of mortals that can be pacted.

            For the Hungry , they're pretty good with the undead might ability but I think also giving them natural weapons for eating based on family would help.

            And for Sorcerors? Honestly just have them start with more powers than everyone else and maybe have a slight xp discount.

                This is very much something I'm nervous about but am reserving judgement on.

                My plan for my upcoming game is to play it straight but to be very generous with spot rulings and non-mechanical stuff. Basically I think the game will work fine if every Family formally works like the Black Hearts, Ascetics and Vorare - they get a bunch of abilities that actually do mechanically affect the world, it's just that they're supposed to be the sort of thing the GM eyeballs and says "yeah of course that works, why wouldn't it" rather than something you have to pay points or have a system for.

                (For example, I won't houserule in customisable Wild Forms for Primals but I'll absolutely rule that they: get Enhancement on things their Wild Form would be unusually good at; have Natural Weapons if it seems reasonable that they'd have natural weapons; can use the special methods of locomotion their wild form would get, potentially including flight, and so on).

                        Oh yeah that's all valid. I'm not suggesting that you've misrepresented the game or didn't include the "kit" aspect, I just thought it was a good example of why some people are seeing the core pitch as really not appealing.

                        I do also agree that "big courts and politics" isn't a route I especially want to see the game go down.

                        I think for me, the issue here is that there's a certain amount of conflict between what the game pitches itself as and what it does out of the box. That in itself isn't a huge surprise because (as I've mentioned above) it kind of pitches itself as being all things to all people so it's bound to drop some balls here and there, but I think for me the key is more in realising that it's not particularly an "out of the box" game. I've lost count of the number of times I've used the phrase Curseborne would massively benefit from clear campaign frames in various fora, but, well, Curseborne would massively benefit from clear campaign frames.

                        Out of the box, Curseborne really does kind of only do what you're describing, the my-least-favourite-kind-of-vampire, there's a thing going on and for some reason you decide it's your job to fix it, low key monster of the week story. But out of the box, D&D is kind of like that too. And I don't mean that in a disparaging way, D&D has proven itself over the decades to be an extremely flexible game (not quite as flexible as WotC seems to want it to be, but that's another story) and the reason for that is that its core mechanics can actually be taken in a variety of directions.

                        Curseborne is similar, but in some ways more flexible. A while back I compared it to GURPS which I think some people took as disparaging, but I mostly meant it as a compliment. This is a game that gives you a whole lot of tools and one or two somewhat uncomfortable pieces of furniture. That's personally exactly what I want but I think a lot of people are really looking for a nice dining room set.
