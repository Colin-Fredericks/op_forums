                                                Character Attributes are not supposed to be measured against normal people. The Storyguide uses a completely different system for stats on characters they control for an important reason, and that's to divorce the idea that we are comparing our character's Attributes to real world physical measurements. We aren't doing that. The Attributes don't measure anything. They measure how likely it is that a character is going to succeed on an action using it. That's it.

                                                So, if you have Might 3, you have a decent chance of overcoming most strength related challenges. This isn't average. This is pretty extraordinary. Also, Attributes are not the primary driving factor of the fiction in Storypath games. Skills are. You must have a Skill to take an appropriate action. There is no "roll with Attribute even if you don't have an appropriate Skill" in Storypath. You first choose what Skill the action requires, then pair an Attribute. In a lot of cases, the choice of Attribute has nothing to do with the chosen Skill, and everything to do with how you're approaching the situation. Are you attacking someone's weak spot to maximize damage? Maybe make a case for using Cunning instead of Might. We normalized the attack Attributes in SPU because people were having a hard time wrapping their heads around this aspect of the game, but it's always been part of the design, and it shows up everywhere else.

                                                Antagonists have things they are good at, things they are just okay at, and things they suck at, which are represented by an entire dice pool for doing those things. They do not have Skills and Attributes the way characters do. Their pools are an abstraction of how good they are at those things, both because it's far easier for the SG to interpret and use, and to because they are almost always less bad-ass overall than characters, meaning we do not need to know every Attribute under every circumstance, we only need to know what they're best at, because otherwise they are probably kind of shit. This is important, because we want the characters players control to be good at a great many things, and have only a few weaknesses. Where NPCs are the other way around.

                                                Danielle Lauzon, Requiem Developer, Storypath Guru, freelance writer

                                                    But in some ways that makes the customisability issue with the current system more acute.

                                                    If your Attributes are essentially a measure of your character's preferred or most-likely-to-succeed approach to difficult situations, then it's even more important that players have a significant degree of control over what their characters' attributes are. Even if a character's Intellect, Cunning and Resolve scores don't actually translate to a real-world IQ rating or its equivalent, playing a mental-primary character does strongly imply that your character prefers to think their way around problems rather than, say, talk their way out or fight their way out.

                                                    As the system currently stands, there are multiple Families who have an Attribute group they can't take below 7 and several more who have one they can't take below 6 (I can't comment on maximums without knowing for sure how Role Paths work). This places real limits on the type of character you can play in those Families.

                                                    I appreciate you want PCs to have a lot of strengths and few weaknesses, but some characters are locked out of putting their few weaknesses where they might want to. Right now you *basically* can't play a Maven who is bad at taking a Social approach, or a Battleground Angel who is bad at taking a Physical approach even if you want to. I suppose you could intensely min-max within the category but even then you're mathematically incapable of having more than one stat below 3 (a level you say yourself qualifies as "extraordinary").

                                                    This might be an intentional feature of the system, but right now from my perspective as a player who is genuinely excited for this game, it doesn't feel like one. I'm honestly not suggesting much (admittedly we've bandied around a lot of ideas on this thread but that's kinda what this thread is *for*), I just think the numbers need to be tweaked to the point that every family can get every Attribute group down to 5 (still very good, but possible to build weaknesses in) and up to 7 (enough that it feels like a specialisation and a preferred approach within that specialisation).

                                                        On one hand, I see the point in such abstractions, but on the other hand imagining a character’s real world abilities is part of the fun of role playing. A game of pure abstraction would be craps or roulette. You roll the dice for craps, you win or you lose, but there is no meaning behind the dice roll.

                                                        It’s fun to imagine that the numbers we see on a character sheet mean something that has real world significance that could be compared to real people and events. Could my character win in a fist fight against a professional MMA fighter? How much can they lift? How fast can they run? What is my character’s IQ and their college GPA. Maybe not all of those questions are interesting to us, but real world comparison is the essence of role playing.

                                                        Now, abstraction can be useful if it allows for multiple real world interpretations for the same attribute. For example, is a human with a Might of 5 a huge power lifter strongman type of person? Could a person with a Might of 5 be much weaker than that but who is so ferocious that they hit as hard as the strongman? I imagine that there are plenty of professional boxers who hit as hard or harder than competitive power lifters even though they are a lot weaker. Or should that be determined by close combat skill? Could you say that your character has an IQ of 160 even with an Intellect of 2, because they have trouble focusing that intellect into things that are captured by the dice rolls?

                                                        From the high abstraction standpoint, it becomes even more desirable (as Nobody Special says) for flexibility within the system, since the themes associated with characters and families can potentially match with many of the interpretations of certain attributes.

                                                            Originally posted by Sergeant Brother View Post
                                                            On one hand, I see the point in such abstractions, but on the other hand imagining a character’s real world abilities is part of the fun of role playing. A game of pure abstraction would be craps or roulette. You roll the dice for craps, you win or you lose, but there is no meaning behind the dice roll.
                                                            I sympathise with this but I'm a *massive* stan for high abstraction systems in this context because they solve two of my biggest RPG bugbears: flavour text masquerading as rules, and false expectation.

                                                            Like you, I did really *enjoy* the little straplines in classic White Wolf games. Phrases like "you can juggle six knives blindfold" and "damn, I was aiming for his *left * nostril" are seared into my brain and have been for literal decades.

                                                            But the problem was that the descriptions didn't at all match what those dots did in the actual game. A Dex 5 character who tried to juggle six knives blindfold would be marginally more likely to succeed than a Dex 4 character, but if the ST said they needed five successes on difficulty nine, they'd probably both fail.

                                                            Plus the system just, well, *was abstract*. Slightly less so than SPU but we're talking about a system that had one skill covering *all of science* and another covering all other academic subjects. Everything from sprinting to darts was "athletics" and melee weapons covered sport fencing and knife fights in gutters.

                                                            Worse, people's desire to treat the system as (to use a phrase that was popular back in the day) "the physics of the gameworld" led to some weird arguments and some genuinely unhelpful taboos in the community. You got STs who would straight up refuse to let any player take more than 3 dots in any skill because if 5 was human maximum it stood to reason that you could only have it by being the literal best in the world and by the same logic to get a level 4 you'd need to be at least competitive at an international level. Never mind that these hypothetical world champions were never going to appear in your game or interact with your characters.

                                                            And the advantage of the abstract approach you've pretty much identified. You can more honestly reflect the range of things those stats can represent and, more importantly, what they will actually be used for in a game. You don't have to insist that every character with Might 5 be a circus strongman or Olympic weightlifter, or that every character with a high Intelligence have a "genius level IQ". That isn't actually helpful unless your game revolves heavily around lifting dumbells and doing IQ tests.

                                                            And it's not like the abstract system isn't grounded in any sense of game reality at all. In some ways it's actually *more* grounded in it because it doesn't claim to do things it doesn't. Besides, abstract doesn't mean arbitrary. Having high Might does still mean something concrete, it's just that what it means isn't "you can bench this amount", it's "you are good at achieving your goals using physical force".

                                                            Issy's character sheet from the Ashcan is a great example of how this approach can be good. The fact she has Might 3 Close Combat 5 isn't meaningless, it means she's very good in a fight. It just isn't also trying to mean that she truly has mastered every known armed and unarmed martial art because that's not the sort of thing the game is trying to simulate.
