    An interview with Matthew Dawkins regarding Curseborne

    11-21-2024, 08:47 PM
    An interview with Matthew Dawkins regarding Curseborne: The RPG
    https://beforewegoblog.com/an-interview-with-matthew-dawkins-regarding-curseborne-the-rpg/

    Curseborne is a new horror and urban fantasy RPG by Onyx Path Publishing. After an incredibly successful Kickstarter, it is now set for release sometime late next year. Thankfully, we’ve got Matthew Dawkins (They Came From Beneath the Sea, Beckett’s Jyhad Diary), to describe what the game is all about as well as what gamers can expect from it.

    1. For people who aren’t familiar, what is Curseborne?

    Curseborne is the new urban horror RPG by Onyx Path Publishing! We have a long and successful history in developing and writing horror games. It’s probably what we’re most known for, in fact. Curseborne is a natural successor to the work we’ve done previously, using everything we’ve learned to create a game of terrors, mysteries, fantasy, and intrigue.

    2. How did the idea for Curseborne come about?

    We’ve been planning Curseborne for several years. There have been plenty of ideas we’ve been unable to use for games we were working on under license for one reason or another, or ideas we’ve had that we wanted to reserve for our own games. Curseborne came about from our discussing on many occasions what we would like to see in a new horror game, separate and innovative from established properties. We then worked on it through an iterative process, recruiting multiple authors to pitch their best ideas, before refining what we had to bring together the central themes of what Curseborne now is.

    3. What’s the big differences between Curseborne and other urban fantasy/horror RPGs? You know the ones we’re thinking of.

    It’s okay, we can say World of Darkness and Chronicles of Darkness. It would be disingenuous for me to say we haven’t taken inspiration from those games, given we worked on them for over a decade and released critically acclaimed books for both, and we’re still very proud of them. But importantly, Curseborne is a different kind of beast. Yes, it’s a modern setting where you play monsters living among humans in a generally urban setting, but this is a game with new ideas, new playable options, new lore, new adversaries, new powers, and a whole new dynamic between its supernatural, playable options. Curseborne is fresh and vital, and your characters shouldn’t feel like throwbacks to previous games. Curseborne isn’t a game with an entrenched metaplot, there’s no grand Masquerade dividing monsters from humanity, there’s no innate hostility between Hungry and Primal (or indeed any other Accursed Lineages), and there’s no restrictions between playing mixed crews of Reeves, Heirs, Lykans, and Furies. Far from it. The default mode of play in Curseborne sees characters of multiple Accursed Lineages working together, and the system reinforces and rewards this style of player. So at face value, there are similarities. But scratch the surface and you realise about the only common feature is all three of these game worlds (WoD, CofD, and Curseborne) have archetypal monsters in them, which is typical of any modern horror media.

    4. Is Curseborne a more modern take on horror/urban fantasy than the very Nineties Gothic Punk? How would you describe its subgenre?

    It has some 1990s sensibilities, sure, but it’s more modern for the primary reason it was written by people in the 2020s and many of the inspirations have been drawn from modern media. Mike Flanagan’s work is a primary source, and while I often cite Stephen King and David Lynch, I think there’s a lot to recommend the horror of Jordan Peele, Prano Bailey-Bond, and movies like Smile, the Black Phone, and other, recent horror features. Its subgenre is urban horror or urban fantasy, depending on how you run the game.

    5. What are the kinds of media that people should think of Curseborne being like?

    A good place to go is to look for horror miniseries’ and shows like The Fall of the House of Usher, Midnight Mass, or even American Horror Story. But I like referring people to modern horror classics too, especially in their original written form: Needful Things, IT, The Shining, Rivers of London, and House of Leaves are all good sources.

    6. Why the Storypath system? How does it impact gameplay?


    The Storypath Ultra system is one we have a lot of faith in for its flexibility and robustness. Which sound like strange terms to describe a system, but we’ve demonstrated across multiple games now (At the Gates, The World Below, They Came From, and Curseborne) that the system can work for most genres of play and support both immersive roleplay and lengthy chronicles with character advancements. Storypath Ultra is a system designed to compliment a mix of system and narrative play. The core system is hardy and can withstand investigations, social influence challenges, and combats. The Complications, Enhancements, and Momentum aspects of the system facilitate great narrative play too.

    7. What do the Accursed “do” in Curseborne? Is it Seinfeldian What We Do in the Shadows or Blade or something in-between?

    There are a ton of story hooks in the book, demonstrating that there are countless things to “do” in Curseborne. Everything from trying to undo your curse or place a new one on someone else; to solving the mystery of the disappearing young men from your street or investigating the house where an old fellow shot his family and then disappeared; to engaging in Accursed Family politicking and influence demonstrations; to fighting against your Family’s whims and wishes or trying to gain status within them; to hunting monsters that have entered the world from the Outside to exploring the Outside itself by entering liminalities and alien realms… There’s a lot you can do with this game. It can never be accused of not giving players enough things to engage with, and that’s be design. I add story hooks and seeds to everything I write, because I’m a firm believer in telling roleplayers “you can do this!” I can’t stand games that read well and leave me thinking “but what do I do with it?”

    8. Do you have a favorite Lineage and Family?

    My opinion changes regularly, but right now I’m feeling very fond of the Primal and the Raptor Family. They have a lot of history and really blend fantasy with horror.


    9. How has the response already been?

    Tremendous. Our Kickstarter campaign exceeded 3000 backers, went over 600% of our funding target, and smashed through a huge number of stretch goals. The Onyx Path Discord (https://discord.gg/TcFut7Y8AF) has a regular buzz of activity in the Curseborne channel as people discuss their games and ways they like to play. The momentum behind this campaign has been huge, and we’re very happy with the fan response.

    10. What can we expect from the line in the future? What sort of plans does Onyx Path have for Curseborne?

    This line is truly important to us, to expect to see releases throughout next year, both big and small. New books on a monthly basis, for one thing, along with larger releases at various points throughout our production schedule. We have a lot of books planned because we want people exploring all aspects of Curseborne. As for what those books are? You won’t have to wait long to find out.

    You can preorder a copy of the manuscript and get a look at its materials on the Kickstarter Backerkit that is now available.

    Author of Cthulhu Armageddon, I was a Teenage Weredeer, Straight Outta Fangton, Lucifer's Star, and the Supervillainy Saga.

      Two major takeaways from this IMO.

      1) A book a month seems like a lot - I can't think of any non-D&D game that does that and I'm not convinced that the market's in a place where that Vampire-in-the-90s production schedule is sustainable. I'm also a bit confused about the distinction between "new books" and "major releases". Are some of the new books going to be minor releases and if so what will that actually look like?

      2) I still think they're dropping the ball on the But What Do You Actually Do question. I'm not a fan of applying "gameplay loop" as a concept to all RPGs because I think it's reductive in the tabletop format but I do think it's notable that the interviewer essentially asks "Is this more What We Do In the Shadows or more Blade" and that question doesn't really get answered. Obviously different people have different approaches to gaming but I feel like the Curseborne team in particular seem very hung up on the idea that plot hooks can carry the game by themselves, and I don't think that's true.

      I think the problem here (and it might just be my perspective) is that dev communications on this subject are misidentifying the question. Matthew almost makes it explicit when he says "it can never be accused of not giving players enough things to engage with". Because yes, that's true. But that's also not what people are saying when they say it's not clear what they'd do with the game.

      I think to me, this is a bit like if there was a game where the pitch was "you play ordinary people in the real world" and when people asked what you'd actually do in that game, the devs responded by saying "well for example, a duchess might have been murdered in a stately home, leaving the police baffled; or one of you might be running for president; or you could be lost in the middle of the Amazon rainforest with no food or supplies and have to survive on your wits alone". Those are, absolutely, all things that you can do with a game whose only premise is "ordinary people in the real world" but they're also such radically different concepts that just listing them one after the other doesn't really help.

      To be clear, I'm sufficiently enthusiastic about this game that I'm actively planning a campaign to run after the holidays and I have loads of ideas for things I want to do with it. But all of the things I want to do with the game include as their step one "sit down and work out how I can customise this game to fit the thing I want to do." Now, crucially, the game gives me plenty of tools to do that but I don't think the game has a clear answer to the "what do you do" question baked into the premise.

      If we're lucky, Point 1 will wind up solving Point 2. A really good use for the first twelve monthly Curseborne releases would be a set of short guides to running particular types of game: here's how you actually do a political game; here's how you actually do a low-key slice-of-life game; here's how you run a game that's all about exploring the Outside and so on.

          1) You'll find out as early as January.

          2) Yep, you've consistently said that. There is no single answer to what you do in Curseborne and it's our view as game designers that we'd be restricting gameplay by saying "this is a game where your exclusive focus is undermining your Family," as an example. That can be a focus, but you can also investigate mysterious occurrences, explore liminalities, hunt monsters, be hunted by monsters, and so forth. When I think of a game like D&D, to use a very archetypal example, I could say the point of the game is to "kill monsters, accumulate treasure, and level up," but the story for characters from Waterdeep is different than ones from Ghelspad and different than ones from the Crossroads Continent, not to mention what the players want out of their character journeys.

          In short, I've answered the question of what you do in Curseborne in that interview. It's "use the material in our game to inspire your stories."

          Matthew Dawkins
          In-House Developer for Onyx Path Publishing

              Originally posted by The Gentleman Gamer View Post
              In short, I've answered the question of what you do in Curseborne in that interview. It's "use the material in our game to inspire your stories."
              I think this is the heart of our miscommunication here.

              When I said I thought you dropped the ball on that question, I didn't mean that I thought the game needed a single clear focus (I've said repeatedly that I value it primarily as a kit game), I meant that in my personal opinion, people would be less confused about what they're meant to do with Curseborne if you communicated essentially the way you just have (explicitly stating how you don't think "core gameplay loops" are actually helpful and highlighting that the play group needs to decide what the focus is for themselves), rather than by examples of plot hooks. Hell I think "the first thing you do in Curseborne is decide what you want to do in Curseborne" is one of the ways I've personally tried to explain the game to people, I think on this very forum.

              "Use the material in our game to inspire your stories" is a fantastic mission statement for this kind of game. It highlights exactly how players are supposed to use the material and, perhaps most importantly, it makes clear that there isn't necessarily a default party structure or game premise, which is something that a list of plot hooks by itself kind of doesn't do.

              I've seen so many people saying that they don't think Curseborne can do things that I think you and I both agree it can do perfectly well (I could use this system to run a VtM style city politics game with my eyes closed) and I think a big part of that is that often when the material around this game has approached the "what do you do" question it's done it with sample plot hooks, which have inevitably created the impression that it's sort of a generic monster of the week game.
