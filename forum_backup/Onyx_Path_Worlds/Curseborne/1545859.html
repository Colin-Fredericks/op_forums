    Beyond "Teen Drama"

    11-14-2024, 01:20 PM
    Something that came out if the now-closed feedback thread is a sense that all Curseborne can do is "teen drama".

    I both do and don't get where this idea comes from.

    Stylistically, I completely get it. The book has very strong teenage energy. I don't especially mind that because it reminds me a lot of original Vampire so while I wouldn't call myself a fan I get a kind of nostalgic kick out of it.

    Mechanically, though, I have no idea how anybody could look at this game and not see near limitless flexibility (at least in terms of theme and content, if you want to be able to flip trucks or raise Zombies you're SOL).

    To me, Curseborne is basically a serviceable but generic resolution system (in what the Forge would once have called "Simulationist by Default" style) attached to a broad and flexible (if by no means perfect) system for playing a wide range of cool supernatural beings.

    That's it.

    Mechanically, ir no more supports or forces "teen drama" than it supports or forces "murder mystery" or "war story". People talk about how the default playstle involves hating your family but I really don't think that's how it'll work in practice. You're strongly mechanically incentivised to treat your family as a character archetype just like in any other splat based game.

    I'm going to actually get running the game pretty soon but currently I don't see any reason I'd have more difficulty running this in any given style than CofD or WoD. In don't says the blank slate makes things easier (I've shay's been a bit of a Dark Eras fan and I often like to run in historical settings where the default faction setup is a hindrance as much as anything).

    Maybe it'll turn out that I'm missing a bunch of tools I'll miss, but I really dig think so.

      Honestly, I don't get this as a game for teenagers and while I'm sure it is, it seems far too mature and far more written for adults.

      The game lists Buffy and Warrior Nun as inspirations, which is awesome.

      But the feeling I get is far more Twin Peaks, Silent Hill, and Stephen King.

      The essential feeling I get from Curseborne is dealing with the day to day grind of a dark supernatural world that is grinding you down as well as the fact that you have the push back. There's countless horrifying alien things that can and will destroy you as well as the humans around you. That's in addition to all the hidden horrible secrets that twist things into nightmarish reflections of your world.

      Basically, if I were designing a Curseborne game, I'd say that it's much more likely to have you play a middle aged writer struggling to pay the bills while investigating what is killing the local children with magical cancer than a perky Goth Girl in high school.

      But you can do both.

                                      An RPG setting lives or dies on its antagonists. The main antagonists in Curseborne so far are ... the liminalities, Shattered Spaces in particular. There are hints of others (the Fae, the Archons, inter-Family rivalries) but nothing definite enough to build a campaign on. Evil places are certainly an original idea for antagonists, but you can't really get more than one story out of each - one Shattered Space doesn't coordinate its plans with another. So how do you put together an overarching plot in this game?

                                      Also... Twin Peaks, Silent Hill, and Stephen King's books all have mundane protagonists. Indeed, their relative helplessness when confronting the supernatural threats is important to the tone in them all. Starting Accursed at 1 Entanglement aren't very powerful, compared to the antagonists in the book, so you do get something of that tone. But what do you do as Entanglement rises and the PCs get more potent?

                                      The game isn't broken, by any means, but resources for the Storyguide are kind of... thin, as it stands. We'll need supplements to support anything more than standalone episodes, unrelated to each other. Which is to say, the game isn't finished yet.

                                                          So I thought it might be worth responding to Orphan81's points specifically and addressing how I'm planning to approach each of them in my Golden Age of Piracy game, because if nothing else I think that'll highlight some of the places where these are clear preference differences, and places where maybe there's actually a ball the game is dropping.

                                                          Originally posted by Orphan81 View Post
                                                          1) We have very little information about how these families and lineages interact with one another, how any Accursed governmental structure exists.
                                                          I'm *super glad* it doesn't have this. Like don't get me wrong, an example or two wouldn't go amiss, but I'd want something like you got in 2nd Edition Vampire: the Requiem where it kind of went out of its way to show that (a) the default location isn't a "city" and (b) there fundamentally is no default "governmental structure". 2E Requiem has cities with a classic "Prince and Primogen" setup, cities where it's all Clan no Covenant, and at least one small town setting where the shots are called by a mysterious supernatural entity that nobody has ever seen but knows better than to cross.

                                                          For my forthcoming game my plan is very much to sit down and work out the political structure once my players have created their PCs, to make sure the game fits their interests. And because the game is probably going to be set in "the Spanish Main" rather than "Chicago" a default City structure doesn't really help me (not dumping on city-style games, just highlighting that as somebody who wants to use the game as a truly *flexible* horror/occult game, I get way more mileage out of pagecount devoted to flexible tools I can use in a variety of settings, rather than super-focused examples of the large-metropolis-in-modern-day setting).

                                                          2) Liminal Spaces are *not* Places of power. Particularly since the majority are innocuous or hostile.
                                                          I do agree that Liminal Spaces are a bit vaguely defined at the moment. A broad general issue I have with Curseborne as it currently stands (and this is a touch ironic for me to say as somebody who values it primarily as a kit game) is that it seems to be trying to be all things to all people. Currently "Liminality" is almost a term without a reference, the book is perilously close to saying that a Liminality can be literally anything and that's not super useful.

                                                          That said I *do* think Liminalities completely fit the Places of Power criterion. It's just (and I think this is the preferences thing kicking in again), they're places of power in a way that's mostly represented by in-world effects rather than game mechanical effects. This isn't the same as Werewolf Caerns/Loci or Mage Nodes in that they're places with a game mechanical rating that gives you mechanical resources. They're more like Verges in Awakening or for that matter Regiones in Ars Magica.

                                                          For the GAOP game I fully intend to have hidden seas, mysterious islands and probably cursed bits of Nassau, all of which are (a) Liminal Spaces and (b) genuinely useful to interact with. I think the book could include more examples of this sort of thing but to me the in-character advantage of controlling Liminalities jumps out very easily. For a start it's just straightforwardly handy to have a base of operations that mundane people literally can't go to (or can't go to easily), plus the impression I get is that Liminalities are supposed to contain a ton of ambient supernatural effects which are, again, likely to just be straight-up useful.

                                                          Having your home base on an island that can't be reached by anybody who hasn't already been there is useful. Having your home base on an island that can't be reached by anybody who hasn't already been there and also has the fountain of youth on it is a game changer.

                                                          3) Resources involve herds, servants, influence and magical items. Of which, no I highly disagree, there are far less magical items as opposed to wod with its fetishes, treasures, artifacts, relics and other such things.
                                                          This feels like a design difference between Storyteller/ing and Storypath. Herds, Influence and the like as game mechanical stats you can put points into don't seem to be part of the system, and even Wealth is decoupled from how much money you have, and has become more of a stat for how good you are at leveraging wealth you otherwise have as flavour text. This seems to be part of a broader design philosophy of game mechanical stats just representing what they do in-game rather than having a fixed in-universe meaning.

                                                          I like that approach, but YMMV.

                                                          I will say that I think Contacts and Bonds do a lot of the heavy lifting here, as do the rules for Access from Paths, so it's not like these things have no game mechanical representation, they're just filtered in a different way (a bit like CofD got rid of "Influence" and folded it into "Contacts" and "Allies").

                                                          For the game I'm currently planning, there's loads of space for people to have herds, influence, resources and the like. It's just that I'm expecting it all to be stuff that exists only insofar as it exists in-world. If you steal a pirate ship, now you've got a pirate ship. If you hire a crew, now you've got a crew.

                                                          4) There's no mystery over the origin of families. That's trying to find a mystery where there is none. Exploring liminalities can be a mystery but it's a very limited one in concept. As for breaking curses we're led to believe it's not possible. There's no ancient mysteries, no lost lineages or families, no former secret conspiracies that once existed, no real curseborne history to dive into.
                                                          To be fair to CTPhipps I do think there's supposed to be a mystery over the origin of Families (there's a Special Capitalised Term for this - "Provenance" - so I think we're supposed to care about it, but I agree it doesn't come across clearly).

                                                          Generally, though, I actively dislike canon mysteries. It just isn't useful to me to have a book that says "also there's this thing that used to exist but doesn't any more and nobody knows what happened to it" or "there's this big important cosmological thing but nobody knows what its deal is." My absolute 100% favourite thing an RPG can do is tell me clearly what stuff I'm supposed to make up for myself as a GM and having stuff hinted at with the implication that All Will Be Revealed if I just buy enough sourcebooks puts me off a game super fast.

                                                          So for my own table, the "mysteries" are the ones I make up myself for the players to investigate. What's the deal with the ghost ship? Did that gold really come from El Dorado? Who murdered your brother?

                                                          This one really is a taste issue. I'm not going to pretend that Curseborne as presented is filled with tantalising mysteries because I don't think it is. It's just that I actively prefer it that way.

                                                          5) No they're not. *SOME* Accursed are against Archons, mainly The Outcasts. The rest of the lineages can completely ignore Archons unless an St forces the issue. For that matter we have no great liminal spaces that have Archons that could potentially threaten everything given as examples and that's probably because they can't. We have no "Hell" for example with a powerful Demonic Archon that can threaten entire areas. As it is fighting Archons is a Outcast and specifically a Battlefield Angels sort of thing.
                                                          I think this one is slightly unfair, especially when you compare it to WoD games.

                                                          It's true that the Archons seem quite siloed off as a thing that only the Outcasts seem to give a crap about, but my strong suspicion is that it won't work out like this in practice. Everybody can go into and interact with Liminal Spaces and Shattered Spaces, and it does seem like Archons can at least send agents into the world to mess with stuff.

                                                          And when you compare Curseborne to Vampire: the Masquerade, while it's true that every clan has a founder, it's not like the Antediluvians are super relevant to most games. So I think it's reasonable to accept that the Archons are at least as relevant to CB as the Antes are to VtM. I agree they're not as present as, say, the Wyrm in Werewolf or the Technocracy in Mage but it *is* trying to be a different kind of game and I think that's okay.

                                                          For my own table, I'll want to fit the antagonists to the players' preferences, which is good because that's how I like to run games anyway. And the book really does give me a ton of options there. As I think I said on the "favourite antagonists" thread, what I most like about the game is that it has a simple templating system that means designing my own darned antagonists should be quick and easy rather than fiddly and time consuming (statting up elder vampires in VtM is a pain).

                                                          I'm absolutely not going to pretend that it does the thing you want it to do (although I'd argue that in this specific case it does it about as well as first or second edition VtM), but I do think it might simply not be trying to do that, and while it's valid to wish it had made a different creative choice, I'm kinda glad it didn't (ironically one of my biggest concerns about the game is that a few supplements down the line it'll be a whole lot more like the game you like and a whole lot less like the game I like).
