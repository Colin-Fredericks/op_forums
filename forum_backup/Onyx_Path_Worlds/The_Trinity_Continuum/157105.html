    Super-Science in Trinity Continuum

    06-02-2014, 08:19 PM
    What I’d like to see TC do with Super-Science:

    Make it available in all eras; but split it into different types, the same way characters are. In fact, have the super-science types mirror the character types:

    • Noetic Superscience emulates and enhances psychomorphic powers; it tends to fall into “subtypes” that individual Psion Orders specialize in; and so on. It isn’t exclusive to Psions; but Psions tend to have an edge in designing and using it. Incorporate elements of Trinity’s “bio-tech” into Noetic Science, such as the ability/need to bond Noetic gadgets to their owners.

    • Quantum Super-Science emulates and enhances Quantum Powers; it tends to be very individualistic and hard to replicate, and tends to have odd quirks. While it isn’t exclusive to Novas, it tends to be hard for anyone but its inventor to use, unless he has made a concerted effort to make it user-friendly for others. That said, it is not the exclusive domain of Novas: Talents and Psions can dabble in it, too; they just have difficulty doing so (psions moreso than Talents).

    • Where Quantum and Noetic Super-Science represent different kinds of what Adventure! called Innovations, Talents specialize in a form of Super-Science that features what Adventure! called Advancements: gadgets that don’t operate on any sort of exotic principles, but which perform better than they have any right to: a car that has unrealistically high acceleration and top speed, and/or which gets hundreds of miles per gallon; armor which withstands damage that it has no right to withstand; weapons with unbelievable accuracy and/or power; and so on. In short, “rubber science”.

    Thoughts?

    My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

          That’s a reasonable description of them, yes — with the caveat that they often go beyond the bounds of what regular science and technology considers possible, by being too good at what they do. The Rocketeer’s jet-pack doesn’t use Unobtainium Rocket Fuel, just a special blend of regular rocket fuel; but it manages to keep the Rocketeer aloft for impossibly long periods of time: realistically, a rocket pack using anything resembling normal rocket fuel would have exhausted its fuel tank before it could be used to do anything interesting. James Bond’s spy-tech is another example of too-good-to-be-true technology: again, no exotic principles are invoked; but the gadgets that Q supplies him with are smaller and more effective than is realistically possible.

          That’s what Talented Super-Science should focus on, IMHO.

                                                          Originally posted by Firanai View Post
                                                          wary businessmen putting them down to avoid competition
                                                          This happens sometimes in the real world, so it isn't far-fetched at all. If a powerful industry is threatened by a new invention, they'll pull out the stops to prevent "unfair" competition from challenging their business model, no matter how beneficial it could have been for society. Electric cars, file sharing models for movies, and renewable energy have all been delayed compared to their potential, possibly in some cases by decades, because powerful industries try to stop them, often via their large political influence.

                                                          Other inventions could have awful side effects (especially if folks like the REG developed them) that nobody really wants to put up with. Even one accident with a prototype could be enough to turn people off if it's a big, dangerous disaster that destroys a lot of property or kills a bunch of people. Imagine if the very first nuclear power plant had melted down and exploded in its first year of operation. Early setbacks and deaths have slowed down research in gene therapy, for example. Even fake awful side effects (such as the equivalent of the "vaccines cause autism" fraud) could have a similar effect if they get early and widespread publicity, regardless of what motivated the fraud itself.

                                                          A third option is that the infrastructure overhaul necessary for widespread use is more expensive or difficult to set up than anyone wants, or requires a large supply of some limited or dangerous resource.

                                                          Lastly, don't discount the possibility of religious or cultural movements in large enough numbers that simply resist or oppose new inventions for whatever reason.


                                                          On the other hand, none of the examples I can think of have been scuttled, only delayed. But on the third appendage, I'm not likely to be aware of technologies that were never adopted at all.
