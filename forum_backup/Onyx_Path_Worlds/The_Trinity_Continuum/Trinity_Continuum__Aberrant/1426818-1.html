                                Alright fine. Technology:

                                Automating processes has always been a mainstay of the industrial revolution, but what most people don't quite get is how expensive it can get. Most Capitalists required a pre-existing fortune to invest in, or a bank loan, or... you know, the various problems in a capitalist society which means that having wealth means you can more easily accumulate more wealth, and so on and so forth. This is because automation is frickin' hard. Most people don't quite get how complex the human brain, mind, hand to eye coordination, and flexible neurological program is. You get ordered to put in a screw in a particular component. You do that, pick it up, and then put it down. An automated system needs something that would automatically replace each screw when placed into the component, sensors and range finders to ensure that you actually move the screw to the right area, and of course an error checking mechanism that ensure that this won't gum up in like, after the first 600 screws. Maybe a modular set of machine tools, manipulators, sensors, and computers, not too dissimilar to lego or kinex, would let anyone, and I mean anyone, create any machine or assembly line they desire, powered by broadcasted power from super-efficient solar panels or other rot. And they will hold plans to produce anything they want.

                                At long last, the means of production will be given to *everyone*. It's not those nanoforges you see in things like Eclipse Phase, but those are things more suited to the year 3000.

                                Biotech has always been an interest to me. Biological cells and enzymes, capable of using massive scale step by step pathways that lets them produce and manipulate substances of mind-boggling complexity. All of them without the need for high temperatures, pressures, deadly acids, or precious metal catalysts. There are near to zero pathways involving the heavy metals, but that is as much lack of evolution as limits to biology. Synthetic microbes with a few added bonuses to extend their range of metabolism, along with either automated gene editing system or a machine-microbe interface that allows the onboard computer control the damned synthetic microbes in the nutrient bath, along with several taps that allow the pumping and control of any substance. Ranging from complex proteins, to antibiotics, paints, to nitroglycerin, to cleaning fluid, to gasoline, to coffee. As long as the bare atoms are there, it's all good. Chemistry first started from singular alchemists experimenting in lonely labs, to massive factories with huge reactors and humming of giant machines, to once again the lone singular chemist with a do-it-all chemistry set.

                                                Here is the problem with that: Humans make do, often adapting to any thing they go through, attempting to wring out every single last drop of power from their environment. This is especially true in the less prosperous parts of the world.

                                                You know what happens to most E-waste? It doesn't get recycled. It can't be. Too much mercury for incineration. Too heterogenous for any kind of sorting. It just gets sent into landfills. And by that I mean landfills in 3rd world countries. There is roughly a billion dollar's worth in those electronics, waiting to be recycled and rebuilt into new ones. Just that no one can, because they're locked in really complex alloys and bits.

                                                Most forms of mining is destructive to the environment, firstly because heavy metals contaminate the water, and secondly because of the introduction of acid into the water, creating what can be called an acid lake with heavy metal ions. Cleanup is hard, dangerous, and requires expensive equipment or reagants.... or it can be done a dozen times cheaper with phyto and bacterial remediation, using plants and microbes to absorb and render harmless the deadly products of mining. Perfect for places with weak governance and lack of infrastructure and tech.

                                                The thing here is that we already see people doing shit with tech that wouldn't normally be seen. In Africa people are using cell phone minutes as an odd form of credit, because landlines require infrastructure to build and their own currency is unstable, so they use mobile minutes to buy food, pay rent, and all else. And they'll do the same with this tech. Maybe some people would be conservative with this technology... but other people wouldn't, because those people won't have the luxury of having dedicated chemical plants for pumping out the chemicals they need to survive, like herbicides or fertilizer or those antibiotics you really need but can't produce. Cheaper microchips means that they can have more computing, automation, and all those little things like computer servers.

                                                You don't need a videophone call to survive. But for a state that's low on funds and capital, and sees a chance to create fertilizer and environmental cleanup on the cheap? To be able to attempt to raise his people's standards of living without having to spend precious capital and funds, already so difficult to obtain and needed in so many other places? You bet he'll use it.
