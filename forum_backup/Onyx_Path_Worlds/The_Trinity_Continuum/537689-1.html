                Alright, let's break this down.

                Basic Dice Mechanic

                As I understand the dice mechanics, they work thus: I form a pool of dice from Attribute + Ability. These are typically standard pools ("Charisma + Presence, Manipulation + Persuasion, Dex + Firearms") like in the Aeon games, and I presume they are made up of stats that are between 1 and 5, so we'll typically see pools of 1-10.

                Every die that comes up 8+ is a "success." 10s count double. I spend my successes "on the difficulty" or on "extra effects." If I do not have enough successes to match/exceed the difficulty, I fail. If I have enough successes to exceed the difficulty, I succeed and if I have any successes at all, I add any "enhancements" I have as successes. I can spend additional successes on effects, which include things like buying off complications or improving effect. If I fail and roll any 1s, I botch and something terrible happens. If I fail or botch, I gain momentum.

                Thoughts

                This is excellent! When I was tinkering with a "It's not world of darkness" system for a homebrew, I came up with a few similar concepts for similar reasons (I'm not accusing people of stealing ideas here, just noting that I came to similar conclusions, which makes me think I understand some of the reasoning). This system creates a bell-curve. I like bell-curves. Most of my favorite systems (GURPS, Fate, Exalted) have bell-curves.

                I like to use AnyDice for my calculations. I'm using the following for my calculations:

                function: sardonyx DICE tn TN double DOUBLE{
                COUNT: 0
                COUNT: COUNT + [count {TN..10} in DICEd10]
                COUNT: COUNT + [count {DOUBLE..10} in DICEd10]
                result: COUNT
                }
                output [sardonyx 10 tn 8 double 10]
                Just change the value after "sardonyx" for the number of dice, the TN to change the target number, and the double to change the value at which doubles begin to occur (so if it's 8 dice with a TN of 6 and the dice double at 7, you'd change the output to "output [sardonyx 8 tn 6 double 7]" It takes a little bit to calculate, be patient.

                So, what does 10 dice with TN 8 and double 10s look like? It looks like this:



                Anydice went to 20, but it hit 0% at that point. See that beautiful bell-curve? Here's why I think it matters: humans are irrational creatures, something gambling casinos often take advantage of. We expect the ordinary to happen more often than the extraordinary, regardless of what the actual odds are. You see this often in d20 games: People intuitively expect 10 to turn up more often than 20 or 1, but all three results have the same percentage result: 5%. We know, mathematically, that this is true (d20 percentages are super-easy to calculate, one reason why they are popular), but in our heart of hearts, we're often still surprised when this actually happens. A bell-curve eliminates this. It makes the average... average, and the extraordinary, extraordinary.

                That said, this particular system plays havoc with certain expectations. The more I look at it, the more I think it's a feature rather than a bug, and might actually be intentional.

                What are the chances for success on 2 dice? In my heart of hearts, I would guess "Well, about 50%, right?" It should be effectively the toss of the coin. The actual results are:





                The above assumes that each tier reduces the TN by one. At TN 8, our average number of successes is actually 0.8. At TN 7, we get the expected average of 1 success, and at TN 6, we actually get 1.2 successes. If we extrapolate this forward, we can see that the system roughly gives you 0.4 successes per die at TN 8, 0.5 at 7, and 0.6 at 6, which is hardly surprising.

                But players aren't machines. They don't roll them bones and then go "Aha, 0.4 successes per die plus or minus a small variance, quite right!" they say "Oh man, I hope I succeed. I should have about X chance..." At TN 8, the "low-level" tier, they're actually slightly wrong in their calculation and they're slightly more likely to fail than they think they are. The dice hate them a little. At the God-tier of TN 6, they're slightly wrong in their calculations and they're more likely to succeed than they think they are. The dice love them a little. The result is that for a mere mortal, the world keeps taking that unexpected little bit from you. There's always that little bit you didn't account for. You might still succeed, but it always costs you a little more than you expected. At the God-Tier, though, the world parts before you. Things are always a little cheaper for you, things just "go your way."

                If this is how it works, this pleases me. There's a little mathematical slight of hand that I think the average player will miss, but enjoy nonetheless. It's like a magic trick. It's neat.

                But how does it run?

                Dude, where's my dice?

                One thing that stands out to me from that design document is how often the writer says "Don't do this! Don't do that!" Some people don't like limitations, but I Iove them. By clearly defining boundaries, we can focus on what we want to matter, and this document does this well. The first problem I often run into a game is how it handles my mistakes. Here's a common mistake: See if it's ever happened to you:

                GM: "Okay! The door bursts in and gullets start flying everywhere as three goons bust in! What do you do?"
                Player: "Oh crap! I shoot back!"
                GM: "Roll Dex + Firearms!"
                Player: "Sweet, I got 3 successes!"
                Player 2: "Wait, didn't you just say it was dark?"
                GM: "Oh, that's right..."

                What do you do?

                In GURPS, I would say "Oh, right, add -3. Do you still succeed?" And the player might say "I have Night Vision 1" and I would say "Oh, then -2 instead of -3," and we'd move on.
                In Fate, I would say "Oh, sure. That's a scene Aspect." And I could give him a token to apply a -2 or a reroll, if I wanted. Or I could just ignore it.
                In new World of Darkness, though, and in many pool-based games, I have a problem. He needs to lose 2 dice from his pool. "Unroll 2 dice!" I would want to say, but I cannot. So he has to reroll the whole pool. Oh, but I forgot that he has night vision +1! So go ahead and add +1 die. Oh wait, but what about the fact that he's still wounded at -1? So reroll at -1 die... It's terrible. So terrible, in fact, that most nWoD games I've played simply ignore the dice penalties. The GM just says "Roll Dex + Firearms" and ignores all bonuses or penalties and just uses it as a sort of narrative guide.

                Here, we're back to the GURPS/Fate model, again: "Oh, right, add +1 difficulty." "Okay, then, 2 successes." And we're good.

                I find that difficulty is best when it's negotiable. If we can fuss back and forth with half-remembered rules or a GM's gut-feeling, then we have a pretty solid system. This allows that.

                Dude, that sucks!

                Failure happens. The point of a die-mechanic is to inject the unexpected into the game. There are lots of ways to play with that uncertainty, but the most common is a success/failure matrix: You attempt to do something, to make an impact on the world, and you either succeed or you fail. The problem with these mechanics is that the player conjures up an interesting set of imagery and then attempts to apply that, and the dice sometimes say "Nope!"

                Denied. No cool imagery for you. In fact, nothing. Nothing happened. You whiffed.

                It happens a lot in D&D and one of my players would throw up his hands and say "I hate it when I wasted my turn." Indeed, we all do. Other systems, like nWoD, make the results so bland that while you seldom fail, your success doesn't much matter ("Oh! This turn I do one damage instead of zero. If I'm lucky, next turn, I might do TWO damage! Woo!") They replace "whiff" with "whittle" which I would argue is even LESS interesting/dynamic than whiff.

                These mechanics have neither problem. Enhancements ensure that if you "hit," you can hit hard. Consolation ensures that if you fail, then at least something goes your way, and you didn't "waste your turn." And the existence of botches ensures that the injection of the unexpected remains.

                Say your character is SUPER-HOT, and you need to get access to a secure facility, and your approach of choice is to waggle your eyebrows at the facility's lonely secretary who's at a club tonight and see if you can "get close enough" to get her access pass. This might be a Manipulation + Seduction roll, but you get your Appearance of +2 as an enhancement: If you succeed, you'll really succeed. You can spend successes on all kinds of bonuses that might make the result of that action terribly interesting. If you fail, then you might be able to pass a little success on to your wingman who can suddenly show up and "rescue her" from your clumsy advances, or perhaps you can notice, when she's thrown that water in your face and stalked off, that she doesn't even have the access pass on her (she probably left it at the coat check. You could break in...). You might even botch ("Bouncer! BOUNCER! Throw this man out!") but consolation remains in place, so if you learn something vital about security concerns, your larger goal (to break into the installation) might advance a touch, even if you were humiliated in your attempt.

                This means that the game never has to shut down over a bad roll, that the player always has a sense of moving forward.

                Dude, check out my build!

                The next element that I find important in games are the ability to have interesting builds. A game, according to Sid Meier, is "A series of interesting choices." When it comes to RPGs, that often means a series of interesting decisions regarding how you're going to apply your mechanics to overcome a particular challenge. This needs to involve more decisions than just "How do I get as many dice as I can?" though that can have interesting implications (how Willpower interacts with Virtue and Vice in nWoD can create some interesting dynamics, though I seldom see them truly utilized to their full potential). Ideally, we'd have some funky mechanics that work a little differently, or even better, we'd have a system of funky mechanics that work to create a unique structure of mechanical themes that the player can explore.

                Everyone knows this, but the problem is that they're often badly executed. The developer throws a pile of ideas on the fire and says "Sweeeet! This'll be fun!" but doesn't think through the implications. This can create player traps. A good example of this was the alternate willpower mechanics in Hunter: the Vigil, where you could switch out the +3 for 9-again, only this was almost never a good idea (you needed something like 30 dice before it became more worthwhile). This set of mechanics doesn't seem to have that problem (though we have yet to see implementation).

                I've already touched on Enhancements. Let's look at another: Complications. Complications are bad things that can happen to you even if you succeed. Perhaps there's a security camera at an installation you're trying to break into, and a silent alarm, and a late night security guard. If you're breaking in, defeating the silent alarm is a minimum (it would mean a near-instant game-over, since the police would be there in but a few turns). The guard might be an additional complication: If he hears you, he might come to investigate and you'll have to deal with him. The camera won't alert anyone, but it'll mean they'll have your face on tape. If you roll Dex + Stealth and get 2 successes, then you can eliminate two of these three problems. Which do you pick? OOOO an INTERESTING CHOICE! Dun dun dun!

                We can even build this into our character strategies. We might have the ability, success or fail, to bypass one complication no matter what. We might apply automatic complications to our opponents, or we might apply complications to OURSELVES. Imagine a fighter build where, as long as you have any defense available, you apply a "Riposte" complication to any attack made against you, which sets up a counter attack if your opponent doesn't attack more carefully. A well-armored character might ignore it, while a dextrous character might go ahead and pick apart your riposte defense, but they have to deal with it somehow. Perhaps you have a Feint complication on your attacks: If your opponent doesn't eliminate that additional complication with his defense pool, you double your momentum against him. Perhaps you have an Overreach complication on your attack: you get a huge enhancement to your damage, but if you don't eliminate the complication (hit or miss!) you'll add an attack enhancement to any attacks made against you. Oh, you scored only one success on your overreach attack. Are you going to buy off the complication,or hit?

                Then we start to see builds emerge: A dextrous character who excels at ripostes and feints and powerful character who can inflict prodigious damage at a risk to himself, which he compensates for by being well-armored. The dextrous character's ripostes won't do much against that armor, but his feints can start to add up, so he'll make lots of little attacks not with the intent of hitting, but with the intent of building momentum that he can add up into a single, powerful strike that penetrates his opponent's armor and destroy him. The armored character is going to simply ignore most of this and barrel through the deft attacks, taking increasingly desperate risks in an effort to penetrate that defense and land ONE SINGLE HIT. Who will win?

                I don't know, but it'd be more interesting to watch that play out than the typical nWoD fight, or many D&D fights, thanks to the combination of complication, momentum and botches.

                The Dude Abides

                A die mechanic is not enough to judge an entire system. Implementation must be good. Exalted has a pretty good die-mechanic, but bad Charm design and some setting problems damaged the brand. I find the core D&D mechanic rather meh, but the implementation of powers and tricks in many iterations of it make up for some of those problems. But what I do see is this: A lot of careful thought was put into this mechanic. It is not over-engineered. It is simple and elegant and easy to implement. It addresses some fairly common problems. It thinks its way through some of its own implications (hence its many pieces of advice). If this level of thought is placed into implementation as well, we should have a pretty good system.

                I loved the Aeon games, and just the other day I found myself discussing it in fond terms. I also really wanted to like Scion, but the game was just not up to par. That this system is being applied to Scion and Aeon fills me with hope. It means a steady hand is at the till.
