                Originally posted by Malcolm View Post

                1) Why roll? Chases. There are environmental considerations. The formula also happens to plug into systems for vehicle speeds. The muddle order of operations is noted.
                We really need to see this chase and vehicle systems you are talking about to get a better clue about how things are supposed to work. That said, something occurs to me.

                First of all, the initiative roll doesn't really seem very important on the surface. It's mostly going to be determining if it's an NPC or a PC who goes very first in the turn, and that seems to be it. You aren't going to have situations of everyone dog-piling on the bad guy or all the bad guys getting the drop on the PC who rolled crap. That's probably good.

                What concerns me here is what it means for the director. Let's say I'm running a scene. I've got a big bad evil mastermind, along with one of his faithful lieutenants and a smattering of goons. I've also got both some helpful NPCs in the form of the cops trying to give the PCs some cover/support fire, and some hapless victims trying to get away. Now I could give everybody the same initiative, but that would be tricky across multiple traits, and it would mean the NPC turn would be really long and weirdly sandwiched - it just doesn't fit good timing. So I want to separate everyone out into five or so groups - the boss, the lieutenant, the goons, the cops, and the hostages. Now to do this, I'm going to have to calculate a fairly tricky dice-pool - I have to compare two different scores, then add, factor in environment/modifiers, then roll, then record and I have to do it five times. That is honestly kind of a drag. Now if it was something like say, 1d10+Cunning, what I could do is just roll five dice, assign them to each group, and be good to go.

                                                            06-21-2015, 01:12 PM
                                                            Considering that the engine seems to be putting a lot of emphasis (at least on the tactics of the game) on teamwork on cooperation, this style of initiative is often beneficial. The "everyone goes in order of fastest to slowest" is simple in an intuitive sense, but always a pain in the ass from the stand point of trying to encourage group tactical thinking. The classic style of fast-to-slow generally ends up forcing you to pick between tactics that only work based on that order, or force you to devalue fast characters by forcing them to wait until the NPCs go so that they can act with the slower PCs.

                                                            To pull an example from Marvel, if my team has Quicksilver (high init), Colossus (low init), and Wolverine (medium init), with Scarlet Witch getting the focus baton, Quicksilver goes first, sets up Colossus, who can then fastball special Wolverine, and the Scarlet Witch can use her Focus status to step in and Hex up the enemies for Wolverine to fly past the mooks and claw the big bad.

                                                            In a classic init style, Quicksilver basically adds nothing to this, or is basically there to try to pick off some mooks. Wolverine has to delay past Colossus, and Colossus past the Scarlet Witch, so she can cast her Hex, then Colossus can hurl Wolverine at the enemy. And all the speedier enemies got to go in the meantime.
