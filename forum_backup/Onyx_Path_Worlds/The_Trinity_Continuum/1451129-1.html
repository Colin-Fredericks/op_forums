            Originally posted by Dataweaver View Post
            Also, Dramatic Editing never causes something that wasn't there to suddenly appear; it merely causes something that might have been there already to definitely be there. It's a bit like Schrodinger's Cat: until someone checks to see if the door is there or not, it's in an indeterminate state where it may or may not be there, as far as the Talent is concerned: one might say that its existence is in Flux. And not only does the Talent get to treat it as being in Flux, he gets to determine (subconsciously) how that Flux will resolve.
            Yep, I got that, indeed, that was my first thought. The problem is: the construction workers that built - or not built - that door already know if it exists. The Talent doesn't, but a lot of in-universe people do. And these people "opened the box" at the moment they knew if the cat was dead or alive.

            TC Core says you can use Dramatic Edit to make an antagonist society of NPCs suddenly be revealed to be a good organization, just pretending to be evil for years as a long-term strategy. In this case, Schroedinger's box was opened years ago, since said characters knew they were undercover agents for years, pretending to be evil. So, this explanation can't really stand on its own: if this explanation is correct, whenever a Talent tried to use Dramatic Edit (let's call it DE), he almost never would succeed, because someone would "have opened the box" before. It's quite difficult to imagine a situation without even a single one prior observer... :/

            "-Oh, I use DE to find a gun in his drawer." -> couldn't do it; the gun owner knows if he already put the gun there or not.
            "-I use DE to find a manhole that probably wouldn't exist so I can escape". -> couldn't do it; the workers of said manhole (or street) already know if it exists or not.

            Unfortunately, Schroedinger's box (or superposition, to be mor precise), doesn't work for DE. :/

                For Dramatic Editing purposes, only those capable of Dramatic Editing count as observers. So the guys who did or didn't build the door are the cat in the box: they're in the Flux that the Inspired observer is resolving; and the way it resolves determines whether or not they built the door.

                Now, if your Inspired character happens to hear one of them talking about how they didn't build a door there, this is going to subconsciously bias him against the possibility that there's a door there, which will make it harder for him to resolve the Flux in favor of the door being there. Harder, but not impossible: it could be that the construction workers were lying for some reason. But it's entirely his observation that will determine whether or not the door is there; the construction workers, not being Inspired, have no say in it.

                The limitations on this are two-fold: first, the Talent has no conscious control over it, and his subconscious expectations will influence how things are resolved. Second, there's the matter of consistency: once something has been established one way or the other, you cannot contradict it. For instance, once you've resolved the existence of the door as being there, you can't then decide that it isn't there.


                Mind you, this only works on a meta-level so long as all of the players are playing characters capable of Dramatic Editing. Something like the issue you're describing becomes problematic in, say, an Aberrant-age game where one of the protagonists is a Talent and the rest are Novas, or an Æon-era game with one Talent, two baselines, and a couple of psions: saying that the rest of them are “cats in the box” doesn't work, because it takes agency away from other players. But then, that's why I don't like the notion that Dramatic Editing is an in-setting phenomenon that applies solely to Talents; I prefer the more meta notion that it's a genre convention that only applies to the protagonists and other comparatively significant characters like the major antagonists, whether or not they're Inspired.

                But even under that view of things, the construction workers are extras, and still don't count as observers.

                                            The point is that the changes that Talents deals with rarely rise to the level of any of those. They're things like “the bullet hits three inches to the left” — stuff that's unlikely to have such a sweeping impact barring the Butterfly Effect.

                                            And again, “parallels universes” gives the impression of a discrete set of universes; whereas Ian's “chance is a distortion of the timeline” explanation operates on a continuum — more like a spectrum of colors. Between any two possible timelines, there's always an infinitude of interim timelines where the differences are more marginal.
