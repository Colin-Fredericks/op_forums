                            Originally posted by nofather View Post
                            Whatever happened in Irem, the game takes place well afterward, and if someone wants to have their guilds experimenting with Amkhata and the ST is okay with that then no big deal. Presumably there will be some rules to hurdle but that's for the table to work out.
                            Nobody has been talking about what happened after Irem since the allusion to First Edition's briefcase nuke comparison.

                            I don't care what people do in their own games, but the Amkhata being Things The Arisen Notionally Stand Against is hard-coded into their cultural background and metaphysics and I've seen enough cases of runaway fanon in this franchise that I want to firmly state that "the Nameless Empire used Amkhata as weapons of war in a meaningful sense" is headcanon in the vein of alteration rather than reinterpretation.

                            This was a Bronze Age society with anachronisms of sorcerous origin, but what glimpses we've seen still mostly looked like a place where mundane people lived and the terrifying necromancy and god-magic had about the same place in the social landscape as it would in later eras. Their soldiers were primarily mortal folk fighting and killing other mortal folk by force of better arms and armor rather than ravening hordes of zombies and bestial mashups of divine mishap. If the armies of the Shan'iatu included Amkhata at any point, it was an aberrant circumstance rather than part of the standard battlefield practice. They're weapons of terror that you don't really need when your opposition hasn't figured out formations or alloys yet.

                                                            Originally posted by Pg. 183, The Lifeless, the Amkhata, Generation, from Mummy the Curse First Edition
                                                            Where do Amkhata come from? (Fortunately, it’s not, “Well son, when a jackal and a lion love one another very much…”) The lesser breed arises when Sekhem is wasted. Any time a relic with Sekhem is destroyed (not used up through use or drained for some other purpose, but physically ruined without first being emptied of Sekhem), there’s a chance of a lesser Amkhat generating spontaneously. Largely, this is a matter for Storyteller fiat, though if a set of guidelines is desirable, the Storyteller can roll one die for each point of Sekhem that was in a relic. Any success generates a monster.

                                                            Greater Amkhata are another matter entirely. Nothing that vile comes about by honest accident. Using a blasphemous rite passed down through ages, a mortal gifted with even the rudiments of necromancy can build a monster that would make Dr. Frankenstein throw up his hands and say, “I didn’t know this was a competition, but you just won.”
                                                            For clarity, this ritual involves an inverted limestone pyramid at least twenty feet deep, marked with hieroglyphics in human blood equaling to a pint per roll of work on a 50 success task (So assuming average rolls, 2-3 successes per roll, comes out to 25 pints-17 pints, the average human body containing 10 pints), assembling the pieces of animals who represent the sacred to the Nameless Empire (with the animal pieces ideally being as fresh as possible, which usually means the necromancer in question murdered them and probably tortured them), and then perform the ritual over the thing inserting the False Ka and Sekhem, and behold, now you have a monster that eats Sekhem instead of sending it to the Judges.

                                                            But yeah, tomb guardians and monster cats, and definitely not profanities against reality.
