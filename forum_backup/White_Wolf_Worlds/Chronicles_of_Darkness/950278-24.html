                            Authorial intent matters in a work, but how you discover that intent also matters.

                            In most works of quality, intent is largely discovered by reading the text. It should be self-evident.

                            Intent can also be discovered by context: John of Patmos was almost certainly writing about Rome, even though that's not transparently obvious to anyone who has nothing but the Book of Revelation at hand, and especially does not have a working knowledge of contemporary Rome, but many people believe that contemporary readers would have found it obvious. Shakespeare's historical plays have been interpreted as propaganda for the crown because of the likeliest interpretation audiences would have had to them. Reading the Letter from Birmingham Jail takes on an additional shade of meaning each time you learn a little more about the American Civil Rights Movement.

                            And of course, intent can be discovered by the author telling you. The trouble with this is that, any time an author speaks, that's actually a new text which can easily deserve its new analysis. If one day we discover a heretofore unknown letter from Shakespeare to a friend explaining that he had been chatted up by the Devil and that this is why he wrote Henry V, then we can't take that without a grain of salt, Word of Author or not. We'd have to analyze who he was writing to, the dates at which he wrote the letter, whether or not he believed it himself or expected his friend to believe him, and so on. If we find a new scroll written by John of Patmos laboriously explaining every metaphor and every symbol in his work, a lot of people will reject it as non-canonical and inadmissible because that's how their critical lens works.

                            We might not analyze the open development and the spoilers and post-development writer opinions to the same degree we analyze the text of a rulebook, but those things are themselves different texts subject to their own analyses and interpretations. Similarly reviews, explanations from fans, random forum comments, everything is an additional text. When Dan Simmons writes Shakespearean plays into a Gnostic universe justifying the existence of the human species in Muse of Fire, that's its own text with its own voice and meaning, not just a commentary on Shakespeare.

                            Even if, as Hayley describes, the author could stand over your shoulder and explain how you're supposed to read it? That explanation is a text. You get to decide whether or not you accept it. You get to interpret it as valid or not. That's the entire fucking point of reading it yourself.

                            I call the Integrity-analogue the "subjective stat".

                                        So I've been asked a few times now if I would review Princess the Hopeful in a similarly comprehensive fashion, and I've usually responded with "Look, it's not great, but I like it, and Sensitivity and Shadows are among my favorite mechanics" and felt okay with leaving it at that. However, when I was sitting at work, thinking about bias, I found myself asking some questions about Princess. Namely, I asked "Why do I have so little to say on that game?" and "How much do I actually like Princess?", and I found that the nature of Princess as I understood it versus how I viewed it made it an interesting question and case study on the subject of bias. So, let's talk about it.

                                        Stars in an Empty Sky-A Chibi Review of Princess The Hopeful

                                        *sets the music to Birthday Massacre, specifically Superstition*

                                        Now while this review is limited on the subject since I'm working off of memory and that may be mixing EarthScorpions's final project and Huitzil's efforts on it, I feel like the most adequate words to describe Princess are "competent but mediocre." It does enough to not suck, to be a complete game that anyone could actually understand and play. There are some points that really stand out, but for the most part, it's average.

                                        It's setting is about what you'd expect from a fusion of magical girls and the nature of Chronicles-they paint by the numbers the story of the World of Light before the current Chronicles world and how the darkness shattered it. It then goes on to explain how True Light, the essential nature of the World Before, manages to manifest in the world through the Nobility, how they cultivate it, strengthen it, and seed it around the world. They build a plausible global society that connects through the Dreamlands and rally around the visions set forth by the Queens, posited survivors of the World Before, as well as a variety of opponents who all default to extreme and different ideals, namely the Twilight Queens who wallow in despair, rage, and narcissism, as well as provide the result of broken Nobility in the Dethroned, magical heirs who surrendered to the Darkness.

                                        For the most part, it's nothing exciting, but it is coherent and connected to what it posits, and with pretty clear language and flow. You understand why Nobles would gather and form the society they did, they explain the metaphysics in terms that not only make sense but do evoke the icon they are setting forth. The Darkspawn are simple but easy to get, and more importantly, you understand the strife and, even more, the confusion that happens between the Radiant and Twilight Queens and their savior-warriors, and you also get a clear picture of the conflict the Nobility face when they confront the other monsters of the Chronicles world, and how they understand them in the view of their conflict of Light and Dark. They even manage to cast suspicion around at the defaults of their setting in a believable way, questioning the Queens, the seductive nature of the Dreamlands, and even whether or not the Nobility themselves are somehow a new way to snuff out the remaining Light by the Darkness. And it managed to avoid pissing me off by arbitrarily throwing in Japanese words(Except for Shikigami-*sigh*, and you were doing so well) even though that completely breaks with the Chronicles style as well as the feel they built up for Princess specifically. It's...There.

                                        This isn't to say I don't remember anything exceptional about it. The Dethroned were actually dramatically iconic as breaking a norm of the Chronicles world and being a Integrity 0 creature who could actually come back, but was given some cool mechanics and fluff to make that always feel desperate and climatic. Honestly, though, my favorite thing was Alhambra, the last city from the World before, the most complete thing left over. I know Mage has Ruins of the Time Before, but Alhambra and the shattered world around it just felt like a whole different order of exciting portrayal. It was a great contrast to the Dreamlands (Easily the better of the described other worlds in there) because the fact that it was this physical, heavy, textured place not only just made it more engaging, but it also greatly contrasted the ideal of hope with the sheer touch of reality. The constant rain and the guttering flames and the weird feeling of its' denizens embracing a long winter and, in some strange way, demonstrating this warped sort of hope through their despair-Alhambra kind of kicked ass. And, even better, Player characters often had a reason to enter it and sneak through, leading to a lot of tense scenes that ranged from sneaky and subversive to dramatic and bombastic. So, you know, it had some points where it kicked up.

                                        The same principle continues on into the Nobility themselves-really, I was not joking when I described this game as middle of the road. Transformation probably could have been better balanced to create a clear sense of when to be Radiant and when not to be, but you definitely had incentive to use it and to turn it off. There was a cool damage mitigation trick, or at least cool in the context of the archetype, and there were some solid relationship-based boons. Nothing really leaps out for being terrible or good. It worked to convey the Nobility as a kind of magical girl. The biggest annoyance is that it doesn't feel monster-y enough, but that might a debatable subject based on the shining gem of this game, Sensitivity and Shadows.

                                        I don't remember how it works in relationship to Belief, but the big draw of personal horror for the Nobility is how their powers were founded on empathy....and how that means a magical girl or guy can't just shrug it off when something terrible happens. Experiencing various kinds of traumatic, stressful, and depressing events that speak to the human experience causes the Nobility to roll, and if they fail that roll, they suffer an experience as a 'coping mechanism', as a way to reconcile the Darkness of it with their Light. The more they run into it, the more Shadows they pick up, which not only makes them more likely to triggers these Hauntings, but makes them more severe when triggered. Even when it's not a matter of Hauntings, Shadows linger and ruin a Princess'es life, greying and deadening the way life is, making food taste like ash and the world less noisy, as well as interfering with their ability to transform and do other magical girl things.

                                        While I do have a gripe that it follows the Kubler-Ross Model explicitly(Some of us deal with grief in different ways, you guys, stop clinging to this damn model), nevertheless....yeah, I love it. It's a unique and creative way to bring darkness into an archetype about the power of friendship, love and home-in fact, I love that it directly breaks the idealism of the genre and presses hard on the subject of what it's like to actually feel for people. The rest of the game fights to make that dream a reality, and it;s not bad, but this mounting despair, this listlessness-it's powerful and brutal, and may even create the moments of monstrosity others are looking for in a Chronicles games. As a firm fighter for the value of empathizing with others, for the power of understanding, kindness, and vulnerability, I like the fact that Princess didn't cheap out, but actually punched me in the god damn jaw, saying "No, you stupid shit-it hurts. It's not easy. In fact, it can destroy you as easily as others are destroyed by mere cruelty." It's a mechanic that builds stories when it triggers and chronicles as it gets worse. It's one of those mechanics that demand play, that does such a good job of making a story more dynamic and moving it along. The only thing annoying is, well, that reliance on the Kubler Ross Model, and even here, in the case of fiction, it works a little better than it does in other places. It is a mechanic I wish I had written.

                                        The relationship of Charms and Invocations is an interesting one, with Invocations being sort of add ons to Charms that create the main point of versatility, as well as it's main limitations, as Invocations are oftene needed to advance. It's a neat idea, even if the powers themselves never really impressed me and made me go "GOD, I NEED TO PLAY THIS GAME"-still, it was more than just lists of powers with no engagement. In fact, the big thing I liked here was how Invocations needed to be, you know, voiced. Loudly. Really, it's just delivering on the tendency of magical girls to use called attacks, but actually, as a way of directly pitting the splat's nature against the more secretive nature of the Chronicles world makes for a handy method of escalating gameplay. Sure, you can be sneaky and clever, but you're going to be weak. You can go full blaze, but everyone and their brother is going to notice. Between this and transformation, you have some knobs to turn and set the action tone for the scene, as well as providing puzzles in the form of "How can I get away using what I need to without breaking the veil of secrecy" that actually becomes a lot more dynamic with the Charms. Sure, the powers weren't interesting, but the way they were used and portrayed was pretty cool and evocative.

                                        And that's it. That's all I remember, beyond a vague sense of "Meh." I find I have to stand by what I said at the beginning. It's not great, but I like it. I would play it if I wasn't in a game and people I liked enough offered to run it, but if I wanted to really go play magical girls, I could go to Don't Rest Your Head, Magical Burst or Magical Fury, Sparks of Light, BESM(Hmm....), OVA(better), Chuubo's Magical Wish-Granting Engine(and I could also go play Leonardo de Montreal, who is the best fucking mad scientist ever, I WILL FIGHT YOU ON THIS), Magicians RPG, Black Rock Universe, and Nechronica, to name the easy ones that wouldn't require a little bit of mental jiggering. Hell, if I wanted to play magical girls in Chronicles of Darkness, I could still and would still rather go to Demon the Descent as a human character(I am not joking here), or Deviant the Renegades in the future or Changeling the Lost or Mage the Awakening(Your little girls are Guardians, folks) or Hunter the Vigil or Beast the Primordial(No, I am not joking here either). But would I never play it as it is? Nah. It's...adequate. Oh, and since I'm apparently a weeaboo, I guess I should say it's kawaii and sugo-desu and some other shit-look, I don't know how this shit works, I just play to type and make fun of it along the way.

                                        But speaking of weeaboos...

                                        The Vinegar Tasters-But What About Bias?

                                        In contrast to Genius, I didn't start this one out with any mentions of my biases, because I wanted you think about them without that coloring it. But, also, yeah, I think people know I like anime. It's not some shameful secret. Hell, I joke about the weeaboo thing, but it's not impossible I might be one-though, again, I personally have no clue, since where I align I feel more genuine about it and where I don't, I really do not fucking count(Japan is a country like all others, it rises, it sins, it saves, and one day it will fall, it's just a question of how and why on all of that). It is a hobby, probably the third or fourth one I am most into(RPG's and music are things I spend way more money on, even if I spend less time talking about them, except that first is a lie, and arguably Internet reviews and let's plays still get more attention). Going more specifically, I really like the Magical Girl genre-particularly, I like seeing people break it to pieces and then try to reconstruct it, but I enjoy it straight too.

                                        So is it really too shocking that, at the end of of the day, I liked Princess? Some might actually contend it's interesting how much I don't like it regardless. It's right up my alley. I am easy to please and probably just a little keen to overlook weaknesses because of what it is-magical girls in Chronicles of Darkness.

                                        But what if someone doesn't like anime? Nevermind going genre specific, what is Japanese cartoons are not their cup of tea? Does it hold up as much? Does it matter that the setting is coherent and established if they don't have an interest in that setting? Is "all right" a satisfactory hook for a character type to someone who's just looking to expand the Chronicles world? Is "neat" enough for how a power is done if the end result doesn't match? I don't think it's be unfair for someone who didn't like anime to say it was boring and lacking in proper monstrosity. There's nothing to really grab readers. It's setting is generic, it's heroes lack edges, it's powers aren't interesting. Even the parts I really like and think stand really well come across as muted-how interesting is Sensitivity without the context of magical girls, and how cool is Alhambra when this struggle doesn't mean as much to someone? Is it even still mediocre?

                                        And most importantly-is that opinion worth listening to?

                                        I would contend that, obviously, yes, it is. Niche of a niche it might be, but someone who talked about the game and exposed holes in it is worthwhile. it's not like the person is inclined to hate it, and so their arguments could probably be critically examined to figure out how to really make it a Chronicles splat, and in doing so make it as powerful or distinctive as Vampire or Werewolf. And I'll also be honest-I think someone who got the wrong rub of it could put out 50,000 words on the total project. It probably has at least a fair number of flaws really worth blasting.

                                        Sooooo what if we take it one step further? What is someone not only doesn't like anime, but hates it, thinks that it's the honest to god worst thing to have happened to civilization and that anyone who watches it is a brickshitting-weeaboo-dumbass? Princess is a god-damned abombination to that person, taking your animu and your fox girls and your blinded Nihon worship and sticking all that discordant bullshit in a dark, edgy, transgressive setting that's, you know, actually cool and interesting and stuff. How valuable is that opinion?

                                        Conventional wisdom says not a lot, that such a person just doesn't care about the buy-in and would be better served by a different game, possibly one including the ability to gain power ups for shooting anyone who utters a word of Japanese. But you know, I'm gonna take a second for devil's advocacy and say that while "It's terrible" is useless, it does raise some interesting questions around it. "How anime is Princess?" and "How ChroD-like is Princess?" is something to consider, after all, as the genre could be blended better and made in such a way where, if you didn't look at the inspirations, you might think it purely as ChroD Product. Is that even possible? Is that desirable? Who knows! What about changing things up even more? Like it or not, Demon the Descent is Onyx Path's most unique and definitive setting in the game because it manages to be utterly unique, with no one else coming close to that exact brand of techgnostic espionage. Maybe Princess could be an even more radically realized game in that same vein. Is that a good idea? These are questions I wouldn't have thought about if I hadn't asked questions about the relationship of anime expectations with the style of Chronicles of Darkness, which wouldn't have happened if I hadn't thought about what someone who would dismiss me as shit-for-brains would say about Princess. Does that make such a review worthwhile?

                                        Tougher question than it should be, idn't it? I mean, in the end, I would still feel safe ignoring such a review-But I can't deny that it has some interesting questions buried under it, questions that challenge my assumptions about Princess.

                                        And mind you, that's just anime. What about biases along the fundamental nature of man? About the highest and lowest traits of the species? What about occult models and aesthetics? What about Empathy? What about the importance of character over setting or visa versa? The importance and handling of metaphors?

                                        Just on one point, we go three radically different views of the game, and even that's not true because it's all my speculation. There's no objectivity there-there's just bias and whether or not you think the bias is worth the consideration.

                                        That doesn't feel like an ending, does it? Well, I had an ending, but it doesn't fit here. Really, it's not the time to talk about that. So, we end here, missing a question of "Why Genius and not Princess?"

                                        Next thingy should be Catalysts. No promises when it'll pop up.
