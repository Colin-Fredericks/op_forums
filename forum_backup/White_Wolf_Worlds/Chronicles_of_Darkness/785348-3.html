                        Originally posted by Aspel View Post
                        I worry what this means for Paradox.
                        It means they have basic business sense? As mentioned up thread aggressively enforcing copyright is (unfortunately) built into copyright jurisprudence. Once Paradox decided they got all the IP stuff sorted out, and the new WWP set up, going after major copyright violators is a top priority for protecting their new property.

                        It's also worth noting that a lot of studies on piracy have shown that piracy tends to help more often than not.
                        1) Please see the red text up thread regarding the rules of the forum.

                        2) And such studies are most likely irrelevant to the current RPG industry distribution models. There isn't an old status quo that's in desperate need of disruption to convince it to change. The RPG industry already did that when traditional distribution through physical stores stopped being financially viable for any RPG published not heavily invested in other physical retail products (miniatures, card games, etc.).

                        3) Onyx Path and By Night Studios (the two companies actually making books for the WW IPs) have both already moved towards a much greater percentage of core books being available in either free or extreme low cost (usually $1 minimum Kickstarter pledge which you can then cancel anyway) preview formats. Kickstarter based books esp. (the biggest exceptions are the 20th Anniversary core books due to their sheer size and effort) have free plain text versions for people to read; which are still up. You don't need to break laws to get exposure to product when companies move to facilitate reasonable previews (though we don't know what the new WWP will do, it's hard to imagine them not listening to OP and BNS on this).

                        Dracula's comments about cracking down on that kind of thing because so many people have been inspired by WoD calls to mind that time White Wolf sued Sony over their Vampire/Werewolf Romeo and Juliet action movie. Citing such things as "vampires drink blood".
                        This is a tad ridiculous. A company that doesn't even exist any more, sued another company over IP stuff.... which happens all the time. And while the suit did have some weak points in it (which is actually good lawyering since padding such suits with as many points of similarity as possible is part of IP law), keep in mind that WW didn't lose that lawsuit either. Sony felt the prudent course of action was to settle out of court (and it isn't like WW had enough money to force Sony into a settlement if Sony felt it couldn't lose in court).

                        People treat that lawsuit as some weird aberration (which it absolutely isn't), and a farce (which it wasn't because Sony paid WW off to make it go away), mostly because of Internet rumoring rather than facts.

                                                            Jakondite, that's kind of the thing about Fair-Use though: you don't need explicit consent. As Rich Stim points out on the Stanford Law blog, there are four tests for if a work is Fair Use:
                                                            • the purpose and character of your use
                                                            • the nature of the copyrighted work
                                                            • the amount and substantiality of the portion taken, and
                                                            • the effect of the use upon the potential market.
                                                            As we pass those four tests, we should qualify for fair use (informational, beneficial to the public, only titles copied and it increases sales, rather than taking them away). However, that really doesn't matter. If a company lawyer wants to press the issue, they have more time, resources and training to do so than I do as a hobbyist.

                                                            Put it another way: We are effectively hard-coded search results. Google Books already won a huge lawsuit brought by the Author's Guild on providing searches across books and actually showing large sections of the material, far beyond just the summary and page reference we show.

                                                            EDIT: Additional reading: In Lenz v Universal Music, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals stated "Because 17 U.S.C. § 107 created a type of non-infringing use, fair use is 'authorized by the law' and a copyright holder must consider the existence of fair use before sending a takedown notification under § 512(c).". Essentially, Fair Use is a right, not an excuse. The distinction is significant, if often overlooked by those of a more litigious bent.
