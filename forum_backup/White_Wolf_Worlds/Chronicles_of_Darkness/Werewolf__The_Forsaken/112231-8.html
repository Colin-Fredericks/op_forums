                Originally posted by Christian A View Post
                Re: the confusion about tribes and why starting level characters would join one of them - when considering the tribes, it is important to take into account the basic situation of a newly changed werewolf and of the setting at a larger scale.


                You've changed and you've probably killed someone. You already know what it's like to be hunted - strange shadowy beings stalking you, trying to kill you. Spirits and rats infesting your home and trying to get close to the people you care about. You've probably already fought for your life once or twice - and won, obviously, but it doesn't take much smarts to figure out that the next time could well be different.


                Your skin doesn't act the way it used to, and neither do your emotions, senses and appetites, even. You can't help looking at other people and, sometimes, thinking "food". You feel claustrophobic in your human skin, and when previously you felt like hitting somebody who pissed you off, now it's a challenge not to tear his throat out.

                This is where the tribes come in. This is what the tribes have to offer:


                As a werewolf, you'll have to fight for your life; probably on a regular basis. The Blood Talons show you how. They know how to protect yourself and those close to you; Fenris-Ur will teach you how to be an effective warrior. They also give you ways how to deal with the burning rage affecting all werewolves. The Blood Talons are the ones you approach when you don't want to wake up in the blood of your husband one day, and when you want to survive the next time the Predator Kings show up from nowhere and start biting huge chunks of flesh out of your flesh.


                This scary, hostile world of predator and prey you are now a part of? The Bone Shadows best understand how it works and how to make sense of it. They explain spirits. They tell you which places in the Shadow to avoid and where you'll be safe. Death Wolf provides you with the means of fighting your enemies with ways other than using your claws. And they also show you how to make sense of the death and pain and grief that always seems to follow the Forsaken around. They provide meaning in a world that doesn't appear to have any, at the first glance.


                There's a wolf in your heart, your bones and your flesh, and it'll never go away. The Hunters in Darkness know that wolf better than any other tribe. They know how to make sense of the rage, the new senses, instincts, demands of the wolf-heart. They also teach you how to hunt effectively, and how to survive when you're prey. Things like where to best hide, how to best track your prey, how to kill prey in as little time as possible are natural to the children of the Black Wolf.


                Werewolves are not human. The human skin doesn't change their basic nature; but despite the wolf-heart and spirit-soul there's still a human mind. After the traumatic experience of the First Change, which more often than not, ends with lots of blood, the Iron Masters are the ones who help a young werewolf to reintegrate into the human culture and world and to reconcile wolf-heart with human-mind. They're the ones who know how a wolf hides and hunts among sheep. Red Wolf knows the spirits that hunt in the cities and close to humanity, and his children will teach you how to hunt with cunning, how to adapt to situations that cannot otherwise be dealt with.


                The Storm Lords don't pick you up when you're weak. They won't comfort you in your time of need. Winter Wolf does not care about your weakness and failings. The Storm Lords cannot soothe any pain you feel from the First Change or from being what you are. The Winter Wolf will instead be here in your moments of strength and endurance. His children show you how to be your best, strongest self - how to stare this cold, dark, hostile world that wants you dead at every turn, in the face, and how to make it back down with an alpha's growl. They know how to fight. They know the spirit world, the worlds of wolf and men. All of these things are part of their responsibility.


                ... still three tribes left to cover!


                You've been beaten and bruised by this world. There's nothing that feels right. Friends? Jobs? Relationships? Family? Nothing has ever worked out for you. The world of men has let you down and failed you more often than you can count.

                No more. Something's got to give.

                Your old life is gone. You might have killed those you love during your first change. You're a monster and a hunter now. There's no way back.
                It's not your fault. It's THEM. This world isn't how it should be.
                There is hope. You've got the oldest, nastiest, toughest of all Wolf Gods on your side, giving you strength, rage, purpose and a mission - rebuild this world in your - in his - image. Make them all pay what they did to you. It's what you should do. It's what's natural to do.


                The First Change isn't the end of your old life, it's you reaching your destiny and becoming a true member of your family. It's when you realize your potential and the universe gives you what you're owed.
                The Ivory Claws teach you how people are not born equal. There are those whose blood and ancestry matters more. You're one of the lucky ones. You're special - the purest of the Pure. You're better than other people. It's quite simply genetics.
                This world belongs to its true rulers. The Chosen Ones, those selected by Silver Wolf to run this world, must take command.
                Go hunt those who would refuse to obey.


                Spirits stalk and try to kill you. The Hosts are at your heels, only waiting to take you down once you're weak. Werewolves hunt you as the competition you are.
                It's good, so very good, to have friends in this world. People who look out for you. Spirits who let you live as long as you abase yourself before them instead of attempting, vainly, to keep them in check. The Fire-Touched have more to offer than just protection from the spirits, strength in numbers and sympathetic ears for all your troubles and worries. They also have faith, insight and the certainty that their cause is the right one. And they're always, without exception, honest to you - Rabid Wolf sees lying as one of the worst of sins.
                Only a lunatic would refuse to take their offer and join them.
                I don't think the issue is Tribes is what they offer new Werewolves.

                I think the issue is that a lot of the stuff you highlighted in your above post is not really obvious when you read the core book (once again working for memory here and the observations of several other posts on this forum and my local player group). It had a tone that seemed to presume you were already part of one of these Tribes or the "Of course your in a tribe" sorta feel. Yes their were Ghost-Wolves and they were an option, but they were definitely the odd man out.

                To be fair to Forsaken on this point Requiem 1.0 kinda took the same tonal approach, with the Unaligned be a very marginalized group that was downplayed in pretty much all later line material. I think the key difference is that Requiem very much hung the "shiny stuff" Covenants got to its readers (blood magic, coils, devotions, oaths etc.) so people wanted to play them not only for the themes involved in each but because they got a unique mechanical thing as well.

                Blood and Smoke pretty much reversed this position. With each Covenant write-up straight out having reasons why you might join them from the point of view of a newly created vampire. Which, I thought was a great tonal shift and one I hope carries through into Idigam.

                To circle back around to the point I started with, the old version of the Tribes and Harmony kinda made this presumption that you'd be join the former and that you'd automatically/instinctually get the latter, with out either point really being expanded or explained upon in any great deal in the text of the initial core book.

                Hence some peoples disconnect with the idea of new Werewolves automatically jumping on the Tribe band-wagon or instantly being on Harmony rather then sorta slowly having their worldview and morals altered. As I mentioned previously, my issue with Tribes was always less the idea of joining them and more the idea of huge-multicultural secret subcultures of Werewolves that have existed longer then human history AND such groups exist as top-down (at least regional) hierarchies.

                Considering that the few spoilers we've had for the Idigam Chronicle pretty much fixes the above issues of Harmony (you naturally start off as "too human" and have to muddle your way to finding Harmony with your nature (which you will likely screw up on your initial try and have to keep working at for the rest of your life to not "fall off the wagon") and Tribe structure (now a loose affiliation of brotherhood of a common priority of prey that differs from region to region and individual to individual and is as such a bottom-to-top organisation with a Spirit overseer to preserve the tradition and all this is very secondary to Pack) in most of these regards, I don't think the new edition will suffer from the same problems. This is assuming it follows Blood and Smokes format of stressing the WHY a person might join a Tribe, of course, but considering I can't think of a reason they the writers would not do so, I hope that will not be an issue.

                "There is no God in the Badlands." - Sunny

                                            Forsaken was basically my first WoD game. I think I technically read Masquerade first, but Forsaken was the first one I fell in love with, and the first one I played. It probably helped that I found links to some Actual Plays on a wiki and read straight through an entire Matt McFarland chronicle right after I read the core book. For me the Shadow was a big part of what hooked me, and I always saw it as reactive and mixed in and out of the world. Thinking about how history and human society and geography and a half-dozen other things shaped the Shadow and spirit politics was fascinating. Reading and thinking about the Shadow was what got me interested in local politics and urban development policy, actually, but that's probably an unusual experience.

                                            The big hook that tied that setting and smorgasbord of spirits together with a character was Forsaken morality. It's easy to describe spirits and uratha as having a sort of blue and orange morality, but that would be boring. What draws me in is that the Forsaken experience an orange morality drenched in shades of gray. Their interests are never totally separate from those of the human experience, just divergent. That's what takes a generic story about 'the monster inside' and gives it originality and direction. The monster inside isn't just angry, it's angry because of instincts about alien things you're only starting to understand. You can learn to deal with them, but you've still got the old gray in you too, and spirit politics gets all the more treacherous when you're lonely or envious or have other human problems tripping you up.

                                            Purely human and near-human morality works for Vampire because your needs and purpose are all about existing inside humanity. A game about werewolves where they didn't have a purpose would just be Werewolf: the Anger Management.

                                            I think the other big thing I'm hoping to see highlighted in the Idigam Chronicles is how that moral picture quickly becomes complex. So much of the focus of the books is on what we now call tier one - antagonists are always being described from the perspective of first contact, and principles like the Oath of the Moon discussed only in highlights. There's mention of differing interpretations, but it's not really given consideration or weight. That was not at all helped by the almost 1:1 matchup between the default Forsaken morality and harmony breaking points. The game suffers when it's presented as a bunch of crusaders sticking to a singular moral vision and then suffering gruesome fates when they finally fail to live up to their impossible mission. That's a decent game of doomed heroes but it lacks the nuance and ambiguity of NWOD games. There are lots of different ways to take up the Forsaken mantle, and a lot more than one or two are valid, and that should be presented.

                                            Everything should have consequences, basically. There's a few tenets of the code of the moon (the herd shall not know and the people do not murder the people in particular) that could stand to have some observable impact and complexity to give arguments over them depth, but mostly this is just a matter of taking material that's already there and showing how it can fit together in unusual ways.

                                            And that should help show how you can have conflicts between packs that aren't just abstract philosophical differences (the split presented in Rockies never felt meaningful to me) or straightforward personal clashes over territory. When one pack's made an alliance with a brood of spirits that have a beneficial impact on their territory but start causing problems for a nearby pack with a very different territory, that's interesting. Or maybe they're so overwhelmed by a thin shadow and/or beshilu attacks that they've let Azlu broods spin unimpeded so they can turn a corner, but a pack from a much richer, safer, and thick-gauntlet-ed territory is losing patience fending off azlu coming from the other side of the border.
