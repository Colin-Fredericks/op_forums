            I'm not entirely qualified to talk here (Forsaken is one of my least favorite gamelines, despite what seems like nearly universal love from folks), but I want to see more horror in Werewolf.

            Forsaken suffers from a few similar gripes I have with Awakening; you're a normal person who one day sort of gets superpowers (this is a vast oversimplification, I know). Now you can shift into a wolf (or wolf-man, or man-wolf) and your job is to police some animistic spirits and fend off the Pure. The jarring peek behind the curtain (becoming a blood-drinking undead thing after an Embrace that you didn't ask for, being snatched away by horrifying 'aliens' and having your life replaced, outright dying and having to make a deal with a very insane monster in order to return, or even Hunter's "holy shit, the supernatural is real and it is a threat") is lacking in Werewolf (and Mage even moreso, but that hardly belongs here), as is a strong motivation to do what a Garou is expected to do.

            It seems much less like a game of personal horror and more a game about becoming a spirit-themed superhero. Maybe this is what people like, but the themes I associate with werewolves (loss of control, an inability to live in society well, a terrifying "this isn't me" sensation) are lost in favor of blindly accepting your new job as a magic, fuzzy cop, as dictated to you by a group of strangers. Most games are played as 'rookies,' and yet Forsaken are expected to just take on their role in the world after a traumatic First Change and a group of nutjobs track you down and give you a bunch of mythology that you've never heard of before.

            Again; I don't terribly enjoy W:tF, and I may well be ignorant of (or unreceptive to) what folks like about the game. I'm just pitching my two cents in.

                            I love Werewolves, but WtF has left me. . . cold, for some reason. Not that I think the most common complaint I've heard about it -- that it's solely about protecting your magic rock from the Monster of the Week -- is entirely valid, the game's focus seems a bit too narrow.

                            The fundamental unit of Werewolf is the Pack. The Pack is all-important, you need one to survive. Beyond Pack, you have Auspice, which is what you do (you can, perhaps uncharitably, boil it down into a "class" system: rogue, caster, leader, support, warrior), Tribe and Lodge are how you do it. Auspice is most useful at the Pack level. . . the gifts of your Auspice let you work well with other Pack members of different Auspices, letting the Pack tackle a wider array of challenges. A pack made up of all Rahu is very quickly going to fall into that old adage about only having a hammer, for example. However, Tribe and Lodge can, and should, represent vast differences in philosophy, some of which are simply not compatible with each other, but are forced to work together because, as a wise man once said, "The whole fuckin' world's against us, Silent Bob." Pack members of different Tribes will have vastly different philosophies on how to handle a given situation, and the Pack must come up with some way of reconciling those differences, at least on a case-by-case basis. In short, Auspice brings the Pack together, Tribe threatens to tear it apart, and that's the source of great interpersonal conflict and character development. You know what you call a group of people who always think the same way, always get along, and never disagree? Well, I was going to go with "boring," but another word that applies is "sheep." Werewovles are NOT sheep.

                            I agree somewhat on the idea that Werewolf isn't D&D, and you shouldn't need one of every Auspice to form a viable group. I do, however, like the concept of "Blessed Packs" that DO have one member of every Auspice, and how their versatility is more than the sum of its parts. That should be the fundamental function of a pack. . . being more than just the werewolves who comprise it. A pack of all Rahu is certainly going to be terrifying to fight in straight battle, but they are going to have certain deficiencies in other areas. How much that actually hampers them is up to the game the ST and players want to play. I'm sure there are groups out there who would get a kick out of a chronicle that is actually focused on the idea of four Rahu trying to be sneaky, subtle, and political.

                            The problem is, unless Auspice is fundamentally changed, it's always going to be likened to forming a D&D party, because at its most basic level Auspice IS your role in werewolf society, what kind of gifts (and Gifts) you have to offer a potential pack. And I really think any attempt to move away from that would be doomed to failure, since if Auspice doesn't highlight what abilities you have as a werewolf. . . well, then what DOES it do? Any other approach, in my mind at least, runs the risk of Auspices becoming 'samey' and ultimately irrelevant.

                                One thing I would like to see, perhaps related to Auspice and perhaps divorced from it, is the concept of Pack Rank. The whole Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Omega thing. Wolves and humans both crave rank and hierarchy, so why would a werewolf pack be any different? They'd want one wolf to be the leader, and to follow that leader (which certainly doesn't imply that others would give up aspirations of leadership, or always agree with, or even necessarily obey, said leader.) A structure something like:

                                Alpha: The leader. (Usually) only one in a given pack.
                                Beta: Just under the leader. Could be the leader's right-hand wolf, the next strongest wolf, or several wolves relatively-equal to each other. If there's only one Beta, she'll probably take over the pack if the Alpha dies. If there are several Betas, there's probably one who is clearly intended to be the Alpha's successor, "more-than-equal," you might say.
                                Gamma: Probably the bulk of any given pack. An Alpha, one or two Betas, and the rest likely Gammas.
                                Delta: The newest or weakest member of a pack.
                                Omega: The Lone Wolf, with no pack.

                                Exactly what Pack Rank would mean, or do, in play is something I don't have any ideas on at the moment. Just about anything I can think of to mechanically reinforce the concept either runs into enforcing player behavior (giving higher-ranking pack members abilities or bonuses related to abilities that let them control lower-ranking pack members), or overlaps with other aspects (giving Alphas abilities that let them co-ordinate the pack in combat, for instance.)

                                It should be noted that modern wolf researching is challenging the old idea that wolf packs have a strict dominance hierarchy based on physical challenge. The old model was that the wolf pack is run by the Alpha, the biggest, strongest, meanest wolf, who beats down any other wolf who challenges him, and is the only wolf allowed to get busy with the lady wolf. The other wolves sort themselves into order based on strength and power, challenging each other for position in the pack, a sort of sorting algorithm of furry badassery. More modern research suggests that dominance is tied to breeding, not the other way around. Wolves are dominant because they are the genetic parents of most of the pack, they don't get to breed because they are dominant. The pack structure above follows the old, perhaps flawed model, for two reasons. First, the human part of the Uratha will try and make sense of things, make rules to govern what is, in essence, instinct. Thus, they have adopted the human understanding of how wolf packs work to put into words what their instincts are telling them. Second, since Uratha should not mate with Uratha, basing pack dominance on mated pairs and genetic parentage just doesn't work. Perhaps there are groups of younger Uratha challenging the old pack structure, looking to base it on something more modern, both in terms of newer understanding of wolves and a more equality-based human society. . .

                                All of this is to put more focus on the game's pack level, which I believe is where it needs to be. There should be forces (Tribe, Lodge, even Rank, and especially basic personality conflict) that want to tear the pack apart, but when it really matters, and the pack bands together, they should be able to move mountains (perhaps literally.) Some of the most compelling stories ever told are about groups of people on the verge of hating each other, but when they DO come together they are unstoppable. Buffy's Slayerettes, the crew of Serenity, and the Avengers just to name three off the top of my head (and it's no mistake Joss Whedon was involved in all three. There are are worse people to learn the storytelling craft from.) As Tony Stark said of the Avengers, it takes them awhile to get any traction. But remember how amazing it was when the whole team finally coalesced, and what they were able to accomplish together as a group. They'll never see eye-to-eye on some things, and that's okay, because when it counts, all that matters is the goal in front of them.

                                Determining pack structure: Like I said, this could possibly be tied to Reknown, with relative Reknown ranks informing overall pack structure, or it could be determined solely within the pack itself by whatever means they deem fit. The classic example is that the werewolf who can beat the snot out of all the others is Alpha, but some werewolves realize that simple physical prowess tells you jack-all about a given Uratha's actual leadership ability.

                                Benefits: I had a thought about Pack Structure benefits, making use of Conditions/Tilts. Essentially, different Ranks within a pack can give each other Conditions/Tilts. For instance:

                                Followed
                                The Pack has rallied behind their leader, ready to follow.
                                Requirement: A Gamma can give this Condition/Tilt to an Alpha.
                                Effect: If the Alpha's next action is to declare a teamwork or hunting pattern action, all secondary actors in that action gain a +2 bonus to their rolls.

                                Basically, the Pack, by being willing to follow the Alpha, gives the Alpha a bonus to lead the Pack, but only if the Alpha actually leads the Pack.

                                Which brings me to another point. Something I miss from WtA is the concept of Pack Tactics. I remember tripping over those after running a Werewolf game for quite some time, and thinking "holy crap, factor in Crinos form, and these are EPIC!" While I don't think we need quite that level of awesome in this game, if the core of WtF is bringing the Pack together, they should have some means to actually work as a Pack. So, in a similar vein as Tactics from HtV, I submit that Idiagm Chronicles needs to bring back Pack Tactics. I choose to call them Hunting Patterns, both because of its focus on "the Wolf must Hunt" and in slight reference to one of my favorite Werewolf novels, Bitten by Kelly Armstrong. In it she mentions the various patterns wolf packs use to hunt being engraved in her brain through instinct, and how she and her pack can select a pattern and use it without actually talking to each other (since they can't talk in wolf form.) They use the pattern I example below to take down a deer. Later, they use the same pattern to take down a lone werewolf, and she notes that, while the pattern was in his instinctual brain just like it is in hers, because he's a lone wolf he's never used it, and thus fails to recognize it when it's employed against him.

                                    Running Down The Prey
                                    Prerequisites: Urshul or Urhan form, Stamina 3 (pursuit wolves), Stealth 3 (ambush wolves.)
                                    Requires: 1 "Ambush" wolf, 2 "Pursuit" wolves, each additional "pursuit" wolf adds +1.
                                    Dice Pool: "Ambush" wolf Wits + Stealth, "Pursuit" wolves Intelligence + Survival
                                    Action: Extended/Instant and Contested
                                    Description: At least two wolves pursue the prey, nipping at its heels and forcing it to flee. Very carefully, they control the direction of its flight, wearing it down and driving it into the waiting jaws of their comrade.
                                    The "ambush" wolf rolls Wits + Stealth, opposed by the prey's Wits + Composure (or Survival.) Success indicates that the ambush wolf may successfully declare a surprise attack on the prey when it comes within range. Multiple wolves may lie in ambush if desired, each making a separate roll. The "pursuit" wolves roll extended Intelligence + Survival rolls to keep the prey on course and running towards their waiting comrade.
                                    Potential Modifiers: Open terrain (+1 to pursuit wolves, -1 to ambush wolves), unfamiliar terrain (-1 to pursuit wolves), low visibility (+1 to ambush wolves, -1 to pursuit wolves), pursuit wolves have five or more speed than the prey (+2 to pursuit wolves)
                                    Roll Results:
                                    Dramatic Failure: The pursuit wolves chase the prey in the wrong direction, leading it away from the ambush wolf and potentially becoming lost themselves. The ambush wolf somehow alerts the prey to her presence, allowing the prey to avoid her attack.
                                    Failure: The prey breaks in an unexpected direction, and must be brought back on course. The ambush wolf fails to conceal herself, losing the benefit of a surprise attack (though she may still attempt a regular attack.)
                                    Success: The wolves chase the prey towards the ambush wolf. The ambush wolf conceals herself in preparation for a surprise attack.
                                    Dramatic Success: The pursuit wolves panic the prey, causing it to pour everything it has into an escape attempt, unaware that it is only driving closer to its doom. The prey takes a -2 penalty on its Wits + Composure (or Survival) check to spot the Ambush wolf. The Ambush wolf's extra successes are their own reward.

                                    Modification: Stalking the Prey
                                    Prerequisites: Hishu form, Intimidation 3 and Streetwise 3 (pursuit wolves), Stealth 3 (ambush wolf).
                                    Requires: 1 "Ambush" wolf, 2 "Pursuit" wolves.
                                    Dice Pool: "Ambush" wolf Wits + Stealth, "Pursuit" wolves Intelligence + Streetwise.
                                    Action: "Ambush" wolf Instant and Contested, "Pursuit" wolves Extended
                                    Description: The same pattern that allows a pack of wolves to run down, ambush, and kill a deer in the woods can be applied to stalking bipedal prey in the glass-and-steel forest. Rather than chase the prey down, however, the pursuit wolves simply drive the prey forward by appearing to be someone you'd rather cross the street to avoid. It is exactly this behavior that drives the prey towards the ambush wolf.
                                    As above, the Ambush wolf rolls Wits + Stealth, contested by the prey's Wits + Composure. Again, multiple wolves may lie in ambush, each making their own roll. Because this stalk takes place in Hishu form, the ambush wolves have a much greater range of ambush responses at their disposal, including guns and grapples, depending on their intent towards this prey. The Pursuit wolves roll Intelligence + Streetwise, since navigating the streets and alleyways and flushing their prey down the correct paths is more important to this exercise than looking dangerous, which is something werewolves tend to do by default.
                                    Potential Modifiers: Intimately familiar terrain (+2 to all wolves), Unfamiliar terrain (-2 to pursuit wolves), Pursuit wolf with Intimidation 4 (+1), Pursuit wolf with Intimidation 5 (+2), Pursuit Wolf with high Primal Urge (+1 per -1 Social penalty), Ambush wolf with ranged weapon (+1 per ten yards of range).
                                    Roll Results:
                                    Dramatic Failure: The prey, terrified at these creepy folks following him around, does something completely human: he finds other people. He walks into a public space, or worse, finds a cop or two. The ambush wolf alerts the prey to her presence, allowing him to avoid her trap.
                                    Failure: The pursuit wolves drive the prey off-course. The ambush wolf fails to conceal herself, losing the benefits of a surprise attack.
                                    Success: The pursuit wolves drive the prey towards the ambush wolf. The ambush wolf successfully conceals herself, gaining the benefits of a surprise attack.
                                    Dramatic Success: The pursuit wolves so successfully draw the prey's attention that he is heedless of the danger he's being driven towards. The prey takes a -2 penalty on his Wits + Composure roll to notice the ambush wolf. The ambush wolf's extra successes are their own reward.

                                    Sticky Widgets: This breaks a lot of the conventions about teamwork and Tactics from Hunter, since these rolls do not have Primary and Secondary actors. In essence, each wolf is acting on its own as part of the pack, rather than all helping each other as in a straight Teamwork roll. This has the "drawback" that, if one wolf fails, the whole thing could collapse. . . however, that is integral enough to the way packs actually work that I feel confident in declaring feature, not bug. Other Hunting Patterns may play the Teamwork rules more straight, but I feel that most would probably still focus on each wolf's individual contribution, rather than abstracting it out to roll bonuses for a Primary Actor. Roll timing would be another issue that could arise. The Ambush wolves make their rolls as the prey closes, at the end of the chase. The Pursuit wolves are making their rolls right up until the prey closes to within range of the ambush wolf. Again, this could lead to the ambush wolf screwing up at the last minute, making the whole thing fruitless (but again, this is something of truth in RPG.) It also means that the Ambush wolf has nothing to do but wait while the pursuit wolves chase/stalk the prey to them. Personally, I find the potential of scenes using this tactic engaging, but I could understand some players "relegated" to the role of Ambush Wolf being bored while their packmates are chasing the prey thither and yon. There's also the question of further modification. Personally, were Hunting Patterns or something similar to make it into Idiagm Chronicles, I'd like to see a section about creating and modifying them like Hunter Tactics. After all, there's nothing physically preventing a pack of wolves from chasing prey into, instead of the waiting jaws of their packmate, the waiting crosshairs of his sniper rifle. . .

                                            I always found it odd that despite being a combo of two highly social species (and yes, I know, half-spirit too), Werewolf ends up being the least social of all the games - it's just you and your pack, with occasional run ins with neighboring Uratha/Anshega. They're barely relevant when compared to, say, Vampire's Covenants. I highly doubt it'll happen, but I'd love it if each Tribe had a different take on the werewolf origin story, or a different story altogether. Give us options beyond 'descended from Father Spirit Cop,' let there be tensions between the Tribes, but have the Pure be so vile that they band together to take them down - oh, and make the Pure actually tempting to join.

                                                            Some posible ways of calculating Pack Rank:

                                                            1) Lowest individual Rank in the Pack, +1 per additional member of a higher Rank.

                                                            2) Highest Rank in the Pack, +1 per additional member of that same Rank.

                                                            3) Average Rank of the members, +1 per additional member of a higher Rank.

                                                            4) Highest Rank in the pack, +1 per as many Ranks among the rest of the Pack.

                                                            Example Pack: 5 members with individual Ranks: 1, 1, 2, 2, 3

                                                            1) Pack Rank would be: 1 + (1x3) = 4

                                                            2) Pack Rank would be: 3 + (1x0) = 3

                                                            3) Pack Rank would be: 1 (1.8) + (1x3) = 4 (if always rounding down) or 2 (1.8) + (1x1) = 3 (if rounding up)

                                                            4) Pack Rank would be: 3 + (1+1+2+2)/3 = 3 + 2 = 5
