    Werewolf Dragon ball Syndrome Discussion

    08-07-2016, 09:19 AM
    Every time I narrate or I play games in the nWoD/oWoD universe it appears that the game tends to scale for more powerful enemies, big challenges, big events from one campaign to another.
    I call that Dragon Ball Syndrome: First you start fighting bandits to catch the mysterious Magical Sphere now few episodes later you are saving the planet destroying the big bad alien.

    Have you ever notice that every campaign your game grows bigger with more content, more npcs and wayyy more harder to manage all that stuff? Since im here in the werewolf forum this is even more common to see since in this game you have two worlds to be aware at the same time.. so even more npcs even more content even more writing.

    GMing a game that borders on fantasy and reality is complex. Making too episodic seasons with no sense of continuity it becomes something that players do not feel immersion enough.
    A very continuous game becomes tiring and one day dies alone because players never have a sense of completion.

    How can I avoid the Dragon Ball Syndrome? How can I gm werewolf using stuff I already have on table but making compatible with the players skill level?

          It's storytelling. If you don't like 'Dragon Ball syndrome' don't base your games off of it and find another way to do it. But scaling threats is sort of how a huge amount of stories work. In Lord of the Rings they didn't destroy the armies of Mordor in the first book and spend the next two taking a peaceful walk through empty territories, interrupted only by an encounter with a spider, to get rid of the ring. Many stories are told with the same similar structure.



          But there are other ways to pull it off and things you can throw in to mix it up.

          That said, if your players are not feeling immersion enough or a sense of completion, it's not the games fault. How a storyteller presents things has a big impact.

              This video is primarily focused on video games, but it applies equally to traditional RPGs, and really any kind of game or story.

              https://m.youtube.com/watch?v=5LScL4CWe5E


              In brief, you see that graph nofather posted above? Your stories should follow that curve. The challenges the players face, whether they be physical threats, intellectual puzzles, or social interactions, they should start out fairly easy, get steadily more and more challenging until they reach the climactic battle (or whatever), and then level back off, to give the players a sense of accomplishment.

              And, the chronicle as a whole should follow that same curve as well, with the stakes for each story getting steadily higher until you reach the critical moment in the entire campaign, and then conclude with one last, slightly less tense story, to give the whole thing closure.

              And, each chapter within each story should follow that arc too, with the players starting out in their comfort zone, learning of a threat to that comfort zone, seeking it out, confronting it, and then finally returning to comfort.

              And, each scene within each chapter should follow that curve, with the action starting out slow, the tension rising as the challenge presents itself, rising further as the margin between success and failure gets narrower, and finally letting back down as the outcome (be it success or failure) becomes more and more assured and the scene gets resolved.

              And, each turn within a scene should follow that curve, with you describing the situation, the tension rising as the player assesses their options and tries to decide what action to take, climaxing when they throw those dice and wait eagerly for the result, and finally falling as they count successes and you describe the results.

              That's the basics of good narrative pacing, and it should permeate everything you do in a game or story, starting with low tension, building to a climax, and then leveling back off. A series of those curves, composed of several smaller versions of that same curve, that come together to form a larger version of that same curve.

                  It is hard to avoid the climb to save the world or else moments, so don't avoid them. Use those moments to illustrate how there is always something trying to end the world and that no one knows. Build up to the moment where the Vampires have to fight off VII, or else they'll awaken some blood starved ancient. Take a week or two and start your werewolf game. Build the story along the arc if you want, but never let the wolves know how close the world came to ending. Let the players see how dangerous and terrible the world is, and how the creatures of the night know nothing of each other's struggles.

                                  Besides the excellent advice of "keep it personal," there's also making sure to keep it lateral.

                                  As far as I can tell, the issue with "Dragonball Syndrome," is that Dragonball wrote itself into a corner (though not necessarily a bad thing for DB) because every problem is resolved by hitting it as hard as possible, which means new challenging problems have to come in the form of things harder to resolve just by hitting them harder but are still defeated by being hit really hard in the end.

                                  That is a vertical escalation of threat. Avoiding it is as simple as not tying yourself into that single vertical line of progression. Move threats laterally/horizontally so that new strengths/weaknesses/tactics/etc. are the most relevant. Not every climax has to be a big throw down with the final big bad. Throw in a survival plot where some big spiritual event is imminent and the players have to focus on their plans - and the plans of their rivals - to prepare and hunker down; as well as be ready for the aftermath. Do a more social or mental focused antagonist where wits and cunning are going to carry the day instead of force. Etc.

                                  If we're referencing an anime, I'd point to One Piece as an inspiration for this sort of thing. Sure, you've got a main cast that are badasses, and their skills at harming others is rarely overlooked as part of the plot, but there's plenty of arcs where opponents who can't take them in a straight fight come up with other ways to challenge them. As well, there are arcs where they're outmatched in raw fighting ability and they need to use other skills to win the day. Nami and Usopp are extremely limited in raw power, but both have repeatedly been essential to leveling the playing field through other means.

                                          As a little addition to the "keep it personal"-theme, I would also like to add, that there is nothing wrong with "keeping it local" even though characters progress in their strength. Even if your pack gets really strong, there is nothing wrong with stories still only developing inside their territory. On the same page it does make sense to make the territory more "valuable" as stories progress though. Let the pack discover/build a new locus or find other interesting stuff. As the campaign progresses the territory therefore gets more attractive for bigger threats, so you naturally get bigger challengers, but on the same turf.
                                          That way you can reuse old locations and NPCs and give the players a sense of belonging. And fully in line with the "keep it personal"-theme, there is nothing better than using NPCs the players come to love or hate over some stories, to develope a new one.

                                                  This is a weird suggestion, but I'd say this: go and find a copy of the Smallville RPG, which uses a system called Cortex+Drama. That part is irrelevant.

                                                  What's relevant is the character generation. Smallville has the players incrementally produce something called the Pathways Map. Basically, you go around and each step of the Pathway tells you to add various mechanical things, but more importantly, it tells you to add NPCs and locations, plus an explanation of what your character's relationship to that addition is. So you might add an NPC that's "your human sister, who has no idea you're Uratha" and say it's a strong friendship from birth. Or you might link an abandoned building, "former bar named Joe's", and explain that it's where your First, very bloody, Change happened. Everyone does this, and everyone is also encouraged to add links to others' things. So that human sister of yours? Might be recently divorced from your packmate, because he realized he couldn't hold down a marriage when he's a fucking werewolf.

                                                  It lets you wrap a world around your players' backstories in an incredibly intimate way, so when the city decides to renovate that old building, then the fact that there are old rage spirits and even older spirits of camaraderie supping at the small locus there? That becomes personal. It's not just about the pack: it's about you and your history. When you step into their midst, you feel recognition: spirits that are aping the behaviors of old, now-dead patrons in what feel like terribly-mocking ways. Your packmate, now a veteran and feeling well-in-control of himself, is thinking about reconsidering that lost relationship... which puts your cherished sister at risk if he loses control and you know he'll lose control. She ain't even Blooded; she's just a fragile human who'll likely trigger his Kuruth without having any idea what that even means. Do you support your packmate in his effort to make his life better, or do you defend the health and well-being of your touchstone sibling?

                                                  Which, yeah, means some inter-player friction is built in. Whether that's a good thing or not is up to your table, obviously: minimizing the interconnections between the players is a good way to reduce that friction while still holding onto the benefits of making the Map.

                                                  When you've got that Map, you can link those things to actual places on Google Maps or whatever you prefer to use. Your sister's house is here. The old building is there. The pack's den is here. This other pack claims that territory there. There's a burgeoning Host infection over there. There's a locus under here. And so on. That lets you start "accidentally" being in certain vicinities. Oh, we're just happening to drive by your sister's place on the way back from a fight with the Pure. Nevermind that the Pure might be sniffing down our trail; you gotta catch a glimpse of your old flame and thus endanger my sister.

                                                  In other words, in a story, places matter because they matter to the characters. My house isn't important because that's where we get the mission briefing. It's my goddamn house and I live here, dammit. Your characters aren't mercenaries who just rolled into town for some murder and mayhem; they live here and they have roots and memories and vulnerabilities. They've spent time and effort and money on improving their territory and shaping it into a place they want to live. This is their town. Exploit that.

                                                      There's a lot of GM advice about running local campaigns, usually through the tint of dungeon-crawling games, but the same applies to any setting.

                                                      Also look at your own life. Our day-to-day lives aren't filled with serial escalation of threats, but we're invested, there's drama, and emotional attachment. There's periods of intensity and periods of slow drifting; play through those slow periods. Play in the aftermath of the big events.

                                                      Great, your pack defeated the Big Bad Idigam but many other packs were killed in the process. What happens when the Pure move in to claim that old territory? They're not stronger than the Idigam but they're still a threat. Hell, I'd love to GM that pitch for a new campaign: the aftermath of something the characters did but the players didn't actually play, and then maybe show the Big Event in a flashback arc

                                                          I concur with the previous posts -- if your sole source of drama for the chronicle is fighting, then you have to ramp up the opposition to stay ahead of improving fighting skills. However, if you would like to do things different, then have a talk with the players. As a younger player I was locked in a spiral where I dumped all my experience into conflict-useful stats when what I really wanted to do was interact with various NPCs in a non-confrontational way. I was trying to get to a point where I was tough enough to not be in fear of every named Storyteller character that came along. The ST, on the other hand, didn't understand that, and only knew that he had to increase the power of his characters to provide a challenge.

                                                          To me, maintaining a territory is more than cowing the spirits. You're a beat cop and a gang member at the same time. You are a face of the neighborhood to the locals. You are like the Godfather -- dangerous and scary, but possibly very helpful as well. If you shop at the corner store, one day the store owner's problem might become your problem. The old farmer you've known for years is being forced to sell to a developer; are you going to do something about that, or just roll with the changes? Either choice can have interesting repercussions for the game.

                                                          I've run scores of sessions in the same solo game. The character is quite powerful compared to how she started. Some of the challenges have scaled up considerably, but others haven't changed from the early days. If you work it right, the players will spend their experience getting more tools in their toolbox, rather than just buying bigger hammers. But they have to know that you aren't going to penalize them by handing them nothing but nails.
