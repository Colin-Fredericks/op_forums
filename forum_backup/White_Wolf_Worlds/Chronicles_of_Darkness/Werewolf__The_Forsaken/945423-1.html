    That video about pacing is basically right, but I feel it can be worth it to free yourself from it occasionally and throw a curveball at players who expect that kind of progression. It's a movie, but Mad Maw Fury Road, is kind of like that. A few times, the movie hints that there is going to be a de-escalation of tension, only to throw another massive chase scene in your face. In the end, I kind of felt like the film was almost one large action scene with maybe one or two breaks in there. It also felt pretty cranked up from the get go and doesn't progress by a huge amount. It all makes for a pretty physically exhausting experience overall (especially if you see it in a cinema). Some people felt it was too much, but it's pretty unique in its own way. Again, this is still a blockbuster we're talking about, and so it mostly conforms to that pacing graph, but it's interesting to see how just a few changes or tweaks feel very different from the typical action movie structure.
    So while going up and down (but generally up) is a good advice to keep your players interested, I really believe it's worth it to defy what you'd normally be inclined to do. It's risky because if it fails, it can take the players out and kill the dynamic of the game, but if it's done well, it will be remembered. The important thing is to know what you're going for and how playing with the pacing will serve that.

    I give the example of a movie, but obviously, a gaming experience is much much longer and keeping a maximum tension for an entire campaign is a good way to ruin your game. My very best player experience was in a game (Trinités, nothing to do with anything white wolf) that had a billion things to do and at some point, the GM would just not stop throwing plot leads at us, essentially making us force to choose what to do and what to drop. It was kind of fun, but it also was kind of frustrating and we players agreed that we really needed the GM to give us some slack if he wanted us to remain invested in the game. The game stopped because the GM didn't feel up to the task of going for an endgame dynamic (it's a massive published game and campaign and the endgame hasn't been published and might not be for a few years still so he'd have had to come up with his own stuff and he was confident), so it didn't end up becoming a deal breaker, but I don't know how much more of that we could have taken.
    Still, a genius part of how he kept rising the tension is what he put us up against. We were almost at the peak of our power and he was complaining that there wasn't much he could threaten us with (honeslty, there were a few things, but he didn't want to use them for some reason). It's a fantastic realism game in contemporary setting (world is full of secrets but most people don't know about it). We're playing sort of highlanders with magic swords and a bunch of magic powers. The power levels are actually relatively manageable, we can't just take armies face to face, but a few commando squads are very manageable with a bit of strategy. Our worst enemies are basically dark highlanders who think they can take over the world in the end, but there are scores of secret societies with varying degrees of supernatural powers.
    Anyway, at some point, he came up with a campaign that put us against one of those secret societies, and probably the least supernatural of them. They have a few powers here and there (mostly some mind control), but most of their underlings are perfectly mundane. What they have, on the other hand, is insane economical and political powers. Basically, they're sorta illuminati and pull the strings of the US government and a few other countries. While we'd had to save the world from potential destruction once or twice before, all of a sudden, we had to take care of the Illuminati, not because of what it was trying to do, but pretty much because, by that point, they were openly gunning for us. They labelled us international terrorists, kidnapped the recently discovered twin brother of one of the players, which we then had to go rescu in Las Vegas. We managed but then they ordered a military strike on us and we barely escaped as the last two floors of the Excalibur Hotel were blown up (which they put the blame on us for). Then they captured another close one and we kind of stopped not very long after that (after a few revelations it turned out they allied themselves with some of our other enemies). It was really tense and hard and we managed to pull through because we planned well and acted relatively smart, and we weren't trying to save the world, just fight back against a mostly mundane but very connected and powerful secret society.

    This is one thing that is worth remembering when you're trying to come up with new things without wanting to go cosmic threat scenario. Antagonists can be a lot more threatening and difficult to fight when it's you they're after. If they're trying to accomplish something and you show up to stop them, it's one thing, if they stop messing around and decide to focus their efforts on killing you, shit gets real. A powerful werewolf can be the meanest thing in their block, city, state or even country, but if three slightly less mean werewolves get the jump on them, they're still going to get hurt. If somebody really wants to do away with them and they know how dangerous they are, they're going to try different methods : bombs, ambush, hit squads, poisoning. Worse is if they managed to turn other people against them. A werewolf is relatively known as a tough guy in the streets of a city, maybe their enemy will try to convince the local gangs that he's a threat/prize and he needs to go down right now. Maybe he slaughters the gang (they're just humans with guns), but another gang tries to do the same and when two gangs disappear, the police starts investigating seriously (maybe one of the gang members was an undercover cop and the police is seeing red) and the werewolf can't exactly keep killing everything that's trying to mess with him because all that's gonna happen is that eventually he's going to lose. So sure he may have taken a big part in taking down an idigam last year and is very respected, but some of his closest allies probably died back then and respect only goes so far. The other packs might be willing to help him get rid of the gangs, but if things keep escalating like that, they may start thinking that guy is starting to be more trouble than he's worth and that for the good of the city, maybe he should be left to his own devices, or maybe he should be driven away so he becomes someone else's problem, or maybe we might as well give him a honorable death before he gets us all in trouble. It's not cool, but sometimes you have to start being pragmatic.

    Which brings me to another way to raise the stakes without going cosmic : The "civil war" scenario. The last big campaign ended with complete victory : the common threat bit the dust and everything hugged it out like crazy and became buddies. Skip a few weeks and people are still cleaning up the mess, eventually, they'll start remembering why they weren't such great friends before the big bad started twirling their moustache. Some may have a very good reason to hate each other and some more reasons may appear. Who's going to take over the territory of that pack that got pretty much obliterated ? Is their last survivor going to become alpha of a new pack, is going to be composed of young werewolves (and so inexperienced and weak) or will some other leave their packs to balance things out (creating tensions).
    It's usually suited for vampire games which are stereotypically plotting fests, but a lot can go wrong with everyone. In my first big vampire game, I engineered such a scenario when a power vacuum started appearing in the city and no obvious choice was there for a new Prince. The players didn't really have a set side in this, so I started pouring some gasoline in there. One player turned out to have a legacy he wasn't aware of and the one who told him was the leader of a faction he hated so he started getting close to them until he officially joined, while the rest of the players were still very wary/hostile towards that faction. After some time, everyone had pretty different stakes in this situation and when things started catching fire, it got kind of ugly. The legacy player ended up captured by the other faction and another player served as his executioner.
    Of course, caution is advised in this kind of scenario. It can easily result in PC death. The plus is that it helps remind players that it is something that can happen. The minus is that it can result in some situation where PC death is pretty much guaranteed one way or another, which can feel unfair. If I had to redo that particular campaign, I might try to balance things out a bit, but the whole thing was very dramatic and sparked some nasty conflict so all in all, I don't regret it.

    Remember that it's easy to fight with everything you've got when all your friends are behind you and your enemy is some monstrous spirit thingie. It's far from that easy when you have to oppose said friends and you can't know for sure the side you're on is the right one.

            You don't always have to escalate the level of the individual opponents to raise the stakes. Even mere humans can pose problems. Your pack kills more people, perhaps starting as a turf war, maybe they're "just" killing Claimed or carriers of a Beshilu-born disease. Cops get involved. Cops get killed. Now all of a sudden new ordinances are making things tough. All stray or loose dogs are to be rounded up and tagged. A curfew is imposed on certain areas of the city, or they see regular police patrols. Maybe a new station is even built in that neighborhood to help curtail violent crime. Or maybe it becomes an effective "no man's land" devoid of much funding, quietly swept under the rug by city officials, and now crime is even more rampant. With that crime comes the spirits that feed on it, and Hosts who embed their nests in the chaos, and then the corrupt police chief whom you've long ago bought (or intimidated) is replaced with an idealistic DA and his appointed chief. The city becomes the focus of national awareness as one of the most violent cities in the country, and then soon you have big federal efforts to clean the place up, etc.

            Escalation is not always about raw power level. It's about consequences, and the position in which they put the characters. Is your pack just willing to keep on murdering every gang that calls "Felony Flats" home, despite the fact that more replace them? Do they want the negative emotion spirits? How much freedom to hunt is too much?

                Really good posts.

                I think Dragon Ball Syndrome just comes from series outliving their purpose and being dragged on to milk the cache cow. The writers never had a story that large in mind for their characters but continued it anyways. So plan on your Chronicle's ending, and bring them back after ending them if you only have a cool idea.

                My werewolf arc structure is based off of recombining three acts based around these themes.

                Animistic Myths - Gang Warfare - Nature Documentaries/Naturalism

                If you tell story in a different order it has a different feel. The first act usually sets up a threat or situation, and tricks the players into thinking they can deal with the problem using the themes of the first act. The second act reveals the true nature of the situation, and shows how the theme of the second act trumps the first. The third act then discovers a solution in the last theme, solving the problem (sort of). This triad to me is interesting because there is six different stories you can tell here, each offering a different kind story and focus.

                Example: There werewolves are just surviving and hunting. They run into a new prey, and make small successes against it. Their life is hard, but enjoyable in its own way and simple. They are defeated when other werewolves are turned out to be aligned with the new prey. They ignored politics and will suffer now. One or more of them die. They attempt to adapt, in order to survive, but because of their mistake they are enslaved. In the third act they players are find an old werewolf hunting alone in the woods, separate of the pack. He tells a story that is true, one of how Father Wolf dealt with a similar situation, or failed to do so. The players remember what they are, and use these insights gained to defeat the corrupt packs and their hated prey.

                Example: The pack is slowly building a pantheon of spirits and doing as they rightly should. The elder laud them for their spirituality and speak of the stories and myths which resemble them. The elders go missing. The spirits are helpful, kept in line by the renown of the pack, by the spirituality of the pack. It doesn't matter. More werewolves go missing. They look for answers and there are none. The spirits are no long listening and turn on them. The story of father wolf is repeated to them in the end. They must live without territory now, just surviving, living day to day. Many die, having forgotten that they should be surviving, not building religions. They in the end discover the mundane secrets among the enemy who took their people. They discover their allies. How weak they really were the whole time. They being to gather allies, and in the end, they slay them in their sleep with high tech machine guns and the help of a wizard.

                The first story was Naturalism - Gang Warfare - Animistic Myth, the second was Animistic Myth - Nature Documentary - Gang Warfare.

                    Keeping the game from expanding geographically and in threat level is difficult, especially as many players (with action and “rogue-hero” interests) can – without meaning to – build against your interest.
                    Look at it mechanically- what Skills and Merits are the characters buying? You might run into your Dragon Ball Syndrome due to your players making characters that have only “innate” Traits that can’t be taken away from them and usually assist in being better fighters/killers.
                    These characters might have little to interact with in the world, little connection to it beyond violence and combative skill.
                    The Chronicles of Darkness does it's damn best to work against this, usually with a boatload of dangling and stapled Carrots.
                    1. Touchstones are one of the better in-your-face examples. They show how the characters connect to the world, and give Carrots (Willpower and Harmony bonuses) to interact with them, and reasons to protect them, (losing said bonuses AND shifting Harmony).

                      1. Threatening these things makes the players more likely to “build defenses” of their Touchstones, which hopefully brings them to buy…
                    2. Social* Merits. And damn does the game try its hardest to entice the player to take these, especially in Second Edition. Sanctity of Merits was built to help take the sting out of the risk of taking External Traits, and many of these Merits (especially the ones with drawbacks like “actively participating in this group and being told to do something”) give mathematically “more” per XP than not having them.
                      1. Allies gives you free favors *and* the chance to roll for more, Status gives you pretty much everything your Organization should have and the ability to Requisition* for other advantages, Professional Training* gives you around 5 extra Experience of Traits without min-maxing, so on.
                      2. And Pack Merits and Lodge replace much of the Status hang-ons that Vampire and Mage have, giving a group to care about.


                    If your players and the game keep being a shonen anime, try to incentive or communicate how much Touchstones, Anchors, and ‘external’ traits are important to the game, helping cement the game’s scope to gaining these traits and *keeping, protecting, building-up* them.
                    If you need even more help, use a little Exalted trick- when the characters do grand enough deeds or put enough effort into some group or community, don’t be afraid to give them some free Social Merits like Allies, Contacts, etc….
                    Then after they appreciate these, endanger them some. They aren’t under the Sanctity of Merits, and Loss Aversion is a hell of a incentive. The game can turn into one where the pack is always putting out “fires” here and there, and there, and can get tiring, but it does help cement the Pack.

                    Tl;dr-
                    “Build an empire, and fight the rest of your life maintaining it.”

                    Hope that helps.

                    *And Professional Training, which isn’t in the Social category, but its pretty much “what my character does for a living”, so close enough.
                    **Mind, how Requisition works and the limits of it and Status are something to grok. It’s a post in its own right, as Status is pretty important for most games, but it's give-and-take could be expanded or explained some. Something I myself want to examine.

                            What an excellent thread!

                            In my current game, we're just wrapping up the third long-ish story arc.

                            I've kept the arcs in line by talking through with my players and telling them that's exactly what I'm doing. When the were-lioness wants to pursue weaponsweaponsweaponsweaponsweaponsweapons we talk about keeping to theme, and what the power escalation would do to the other characters (which she's motivated to protect mama bearishly) if she had an M2 HMG loaded with Bane Arrow ammo.

                            We started off local. I made sure to let the players know that's what I wanted to do and why: One main location (Indianapolis, IN), and three nearby groups of supernaturals. Plus one troubled soul appearing in the setting who was the seed for the first story and the antagonist who was after him. That's a good number of things that everybody can keep in mind at once while we were busy figuring out the rules and what our characters would be like.

                            Toward the end of the first arc, we had some hints that there was a larger world around (the Garou Nation, borrowed from cWoD) and that eyes were upon them. As a Pack of Changing Breeds who had the gumption to stand up and look the local werewolves in the eye, the Council had some interest in these new upstarts. Some were positive, some were curious, some were hostile. By this point, I knew that my players and their Pack were going to keep on being careful diplomats and not slouching on protecting themselves or calling in favors.

                            In the second arc we met the local Council (Gichigami Council, with the lead Pack near Chicago, in our setting) and found out about some of their relations with other Councils. We went to other areas, and played as strangers in somebody else's territory (New Orleans, which in our setting is beyond the control of any Council and firmly in the grip of jaded and cruel Kindred). We got to explore how the home-town Kindred in Indianapolis had to navigate their own politics, which is something that started happening on the fly in the first arc and grew organically.

                            In our setting, the Indianapolis Kindred are quite powerful, but not enough to stand up to either Chicago or New York, should either or both of those Princes decide to muscle them out. Our Pack helped them deal with a few issues, and in return got some help from them.

                            In the third arc we were well established and going more nationwide with the story. We went to New York to deal with the supernaturals there, helping with some more of that careful diplomacy. In so doing they've stumbled into the biggest plot reveal planned for the Chronicle: the Black Spiral Dancers are back, not nearly as extinct as they'd been thought. They've made a new pact, taking an Idigam as their Totem and pursuing a devil's bargain with some of the Strix secretly hiding among the Kindred. They're reaping as many particular souls as they can, for ends not yet revealed, but which will be the drive for the rest of the Chronicle.

                            That was a bit of a tangent, but the third arc is national and venturing deeper into the Umbra.

                            I've pretty much told them that this is where I want the power level to plateau, and why. Expanding horizontally is always great, as is improving basic stats. From here out, we might have a few international adventures, and we will definitely have things to do deep in the Umbral realms.



                            We've had a "rule of X" since the start of the Chronicle, which has really helped a lot. The way this works (for us) is that at character creation, four dots of anything is character defining, and five dots of anything is chronicle defining. That means that you only get one stat, skill, merit, or item at four dots - and that is the thing that really defines who you are and what you're best at as the story begins. Nobody can take five dot anything to start with, unless the players all agree, and we're making that the focus of at least our first story.

                            For example, somebody could take Resources (4) if they wanted, and be the rich kid. If the players all agreed, he could take Resources (5) and basically be Richie Rich - everybody else would be Richie's "and friends", bodyguards or whatever, that he's bankrolling. A 5-dot-anything would also be notorious, and other characters in the setting would react accordingly.

                            As the story arcs proceeded, I've slowly raised the rule of X, to the point that 5-dot is generally available. I'd allow a 6-dot if it was story defining, but it hasn't come up.



                            Making sure the players know the progression plan has also helped a lot. From the start, I let them know that I wanted to start local, expand to regional, and then to national levels of scope. In some ways that's a Dragonballing progression, but it's also let us incrementally add to the setting, and it's politics. We've also sunk a lot of effort and XP into being better able to get around rather than just RAWRSHRED - the Pack went in on five dots worth of seaplane with Fetish engines and a bound spirit for a stewardess, which could have been sunk into just-another-badass-Klaive otherwise.



                            Another thing that's helped a lot is allowing shared merits. From the beginning, it was clear that we needed a territory, locus, sanctuary, and totem that would help. Letting everybody sink XP and story sessions into those things lets us flesh out the Chronicle, and the power level doesn't get too Dragonbally from improving those things.

                                I believe firmly in the ability of limitations to improve things. Limitation is the knife that carves form out of possibility and without it all you have is an undefined lump.

                                This is a very unpopular idea currently with the relativist no-standard-is-objectively-better-than-any-other do-whatever-you-feel boundaries-are-bad weather pattern in the greater climate of the Western zeitgeist. I am looking forward to when it passes as the ability to define standards has been one of the greatest memetic technologies the human race has ever accomplished and should not be discarded to placate feelings. Our ancestors felt as deeply, and thought as deeply as well, but abandoning the (memetic) technologies upon which other technologies and civilization rely is potentially catastrophic, it would be like producing more combustion-engine cars while burning all the oil fields, there would be a gas supply-chain delay before the problem would cause the cars to be useless.With memes new ones can replace old ones, leaving little ability to rebuild what was replace unless referenced else where, without reference it requires reinvention, and some ideas took thousands of years to come up in the first place. Chasing new ideas, like relativism, without respect for the memetic technology they replace, like set standards, is ill-advised and it bothers me that so many people seem oblivious to what is being lost.

                                [^expands upon this but is ideological in nature and might be off topic^]

                                I, like the aforementioned DM, like to be upfront about expectation and am willing to define things as the above poster described in a "Heavy Handed" way. I spend time defining the setting and have the players collaborate, especially with the specific parts of the world their characters plug into, (the character's friends, family, rival, co-worker, or as I define them "Supports"). With defined setting and characters, setting expectations on advancement is easier to accept since consensus was gained through teamwork. storytelling instead of me bringing my setting, them bringing whatever characters they thought up independently and then mashing them together. If you look at my Homebrew, it strongly discourages violence as the primary tool to solve all problems because when used against Ensouled beings it is problematic in addition the normal sets of consequences which a dynamic reactive setting would produce. This has a nice side effect that those prone to violence need more non-combat scenes with Supports to avoid gaining Permanent Atrocity ans thus Personal Banes.

                                This affects pacing greatly, keeping it from being a block-buster action movie and more of a gritty noir. Players seem to take a more contemplative approach to my games, which I greatly appreciate.
