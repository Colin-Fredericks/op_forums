                    Collectively, coyotes, jackals and other, smaller-sized members of the dog family are known as sehker-nu'ur, "little not-dogs". That being said, they all have proper names in the First Tongue vernacular:

                    urwasa: coyote
                    urur: jackal
                    ursir: New Guinea singing dogs
                    urindabin: dingo
                    urzif: African painted wild dogs
                    iurhir: domesticated dogs (this one is canon)
                    urithim: feral dogs or pariah dogs
                    urthilibi: Indian dhole
                    urkanisik: short-eared dog
                    kaurpak: maned wolf (neither wolf nor fox, but still a canid)
                    kaurnuteh: Falklands wolf (actually a close relative of the maned wolf)
                    kaurat: bush dog

                    As for the others....

                    barzegh: fog, thick mist; has a connotation of heavier or worse fog than farsekh
                    wupa: hummingbird (lit. "flitting-wings")
                    thuskirlul: mockingbird (lit. "many-song liar")

                                    Since I think I got all the d-homonyms, it's time to move onto the e-homonyms. If you don't see all of the e-homonyms here, it's because their translations were covered in other words.

                                    e'e: barley
                                    kammu: chaff, fodder
                                    e: a vocative interjection, equivalent to, the "oh" in, "Oh God!"
                                    asku: to leave, to go out; to remove, to take away, to bring out
                                    a'e: to raise or bring up something; to rear something; to raise a child
                                    maku: raving; to rave
                                    eth: to pierce or scratch; to rip apart or demolish using claws
                                    ed: strong emotions; to have angry mood swings; to be angry because of a mood swing
                                    etha: a space between shelves that has things in it
                                    ethadi: a prebend or prebendary
                                    edhadhi: an offering of flour, whether as a gift, in trade, or as a sacrifice to the gods
                                    edendha: birds that live on flat plains or open ground
                                    ethen: plain, open country; a wide, open desert; hot steppe
                                    ehal: a palace, temple or other important building that has a spirit sealed inside of it; a building of great importance that has a slumbering building-spirit
                                    ehar: a wall or something repurposed as a wall, such as the side of a cliff that was built around
                                    ehirr: a bakery

                                                Originally posted by Sikla Alkis View Post
                                                Devangari can be used to write out First Tongue. All it's missing is a symbol to stand for the hyphen. If that isn't a plot hook, combined with links between Proto-Indo-European and FT, I don't know what is.
                                                Well... strictly speaking, there are other potential problems. There's no character for a glottal stop in Devanagari, and there are no characters for a long "e", though the character romanized as "ai" should be an acceptable substitute. Devanagari short "a" is usually pronounced as a schwa (like the "uh" in the first syllable of the word "number"), but that's not really a huge deal. Also, the Indo-Aryan languages don't generally have an alveolar "t", but rather two different, similar consonants that are pronounced more or less in front of (dental stops) and behind (retroflex stops) the English "t." I would recommend using Devanagari's dental consonant characters for th, t, d, and dh, rather than the retroflex ones.

                                                I would advise using the character for "ph" instead of "f"; Sanskrit and Hindi don't have f except in loanwords, and the character used in Devanagari is the character for "ph" with a diacritic. However, since there's no aspirated p in first tongue, you can just use the character with no diacritic. It's a bit more of a stretch, but you can probably use the character for "j" to represent First Tongue z, for similar reasons. Finally, you have to use the "v" character for First Tongue "w", but Hindi /v/ can be pronounced as [w], so it's not a big deal.

                                                Also worth noting, I've been using the virama, a diacritic that looks like this: ् , in first tongue words that end in a consonant. All consonant chracters in Devanagari have an implied short a following the vowel unless a different vowel or no vowel is indicated by a diacritic. However, the default vowel isn't generally pronounced at the end of modern Hindi words, so the virama may not be strictly necessary.

                                                        I figured I'd share some names I came up with for a couple San Francisco Bay Area-based spirit courts:

                                                        Anzaghar-De-Ulke ("Tower Piercing Heaven"): The spirit of the Transamerica pyramid ("de" is my own, intended as a verb meaning "to pierce")

                                                        Fela-Nuun ("The Defiled Prince"): Originally the spirit of a minor stream that was obliterated by the growing city, now the bitter and hateful lord of the city's sewer system ("fel" is meant to be a word meaning "to defile"; "fela" is a past-participle)

                                                        Hanzer Affathir ("Flame-colored Forest Elder"): General term for the spirits of redwoods, particularly old-growth redwoods

                                                        Kath Dhuruthfara ("Weaver of Golden Bonds/Ropes"): The spider-like, deranged spirit of the Golden Gate Bridge; possibly a Magath (The "dhur" part of "dhuruthfara" is mine, meant to mean "rope" or "bond")

                                                        Nir Hanzersakh ("Lord Blazingheart"): A particularly potent and hostile redwood spirit, who still holds a grudge for the logging of the Santa Cruz Mountains

                                                        Nir Zaghuldhim ("Lord of the Carnelian Pillar"): The spirit of 555 California Street, the second tallest building in the city, which is clad in carnelian granite ("zaghul" is mine, intended to mean "carnelian")
