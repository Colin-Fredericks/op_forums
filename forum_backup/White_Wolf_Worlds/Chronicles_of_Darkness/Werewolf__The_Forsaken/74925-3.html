    When I first picked up Werewolf: the Forsaken, I thought the experience was going to me much like the one I had with Vampire: the Requiem. I expected a lot of what was in Werewolf: the Apocalypse to be brought over, but stripped down to archetypes and not stereotypes, and focused on the individual player-character level struggles, not the world-spanning problems. I had very little interest in Apocalypse, but since I've almost spent more time as an ST than as a player, I made sure to always have the corebooks from all the lines in case I was asked to run one of them or introduce elements from that game. So, I bought Forsaken expecting it to collect dust more often than be read.

    What I got was many of the conventions of Apocalypse flipped on its head and instead of fuzzy superheroes, what I was given was Uratha, The People. People with a duty to their family, their tribe (or Tribes) and to their race as a whole. I got a family with one big promise expected of the children - keep up the family tradition. I got a proper respect of the heritage of shape-shifting (it comes from animism, folks, it's that damn old!) and all the old traditions that come with it. I got The People, who understand that the separations between flesh and spirit or animal and man are false and they know it because they can see it. Every day, all around them, right there in front of their eyes. I got the children of Father Wolf and Mother Luna, the Great Hunter and the Queen of Air and Darkness. Gods of the Shadow Realm giving birth to tiny, fleeting lives because they wanted to be part of the cycle of life. Those children being the products of passion and gaining the gifts (and Gifts) of such a heritage. The power of the Wolf, the flexibility of the Moon, the drive to Hunt, and all while being something that's fully human or animal or flesh or spirit, but a balancing force between all those things. Gone were the spirits to continually praised their "oh so worthy protectors" who tried to claim that everything that came after them was "unclean". Instead I got a Spirit Realm that placed werewolves firmly within the food chain, no better or worse than any other predators, as long as they could hold their own. Also gone were the wolf-men running around in full war form developing elaborate martial arts and complex rituals. The beast-man was now for one thing - kill everything in sight - and like all such weapons, it was dangerous and not something to be toyed with. There were no more Rage traits, just rage. There were no more Great Caerns, just "our territory". There were no more epic warriors, just The People. People who struggle every day with what they see around them and use what advantages they have to make their little corner of the world better.

    So, to answer the question, yes, Werewolf: the Forsaken is the game I want it to be, but that I never expected it to be.

    But that's just my opinion, I could be wrong.
