        The spirit chasing the pack, the Hewn, is quite a specific spirit - that of stone that has been carven into images of worship and reverence. It's one of an entire brood of such spirits that are ruling over a particular Fire-Touched sect of packs, each wrought from the frenzied emotions, human misery and religious ecstasy surrounding particular crafted works.

        As of two sessions later, the pack are still all alive, so that's something

        They've now actually made a big list of all the things they need to sort out, debts they need to repay, enemies they need to take out and tasks they have to accomplish soon, and are systematically working through it. They're currently sorting out the really immediate stuff - repaying some debts and hunting down certain foes - and are also working hard to gather some spiritual allies and prepare for the big decision - which of the two Forsaken Protectorates in Paris they should align themselves with...

                A tangential thought about stories in nWoD:

                So one of the structural elements of the nWoD system is the 'story', a particular story arc that has mechanical impact as a concept. For example, Auspice Abilities are useable once per story.

                Sometimes when running games, I keep to tight arcs that work well with this structure, a few sessions at most per story. With Werewolf, I'm more leaning towards the method I used for the last big Forsaken chronicle I ran, that had four 'stories' across 2 years of play, each encapsulating the events that the pack experienced in sequential seasons. This, though, doesn't work so great with the greater focus on stories as mechanical units. I've also found that I sometimes prefer the leaner approach of shorter story arcs rather than the grand, sprawling, messy and organic approach under my last Forsaken campaign. I like downtime time-skips that can be slotted in between story arcs.

                I'm trying an experiment with the City of Light campaign that mixes and matches a bit between the two approaches. I currently have in mind two particular events that will see the end of the first story; if one of these happens, then the story is complete and there'll probably be a short gap before Story 2 continues. I haven't told the players these story-triggers. Currently, the triggers are either of:

                a) Defeating the Smoke Drinker
                b) Joining the Lamplighter or Zero Point protectorates.

                These are both significant milestones for the pack, so seem natural places to end the story. Of course, if events unfold such that both are likely to occur close to one another, I won't arbitrarily end the story after whichever occurs first.

                I'm interested in seeing how long the first story arc goes before one of these gets hit, or of course my mad players manage to instigate something else major enough to be an obvious end-point for the story arc

                - Chris Allen, Aberrant Line Developer, Freelance Writer

                                                Originally posted by NiceBrian View Post
                                                I've a quick question. I've noticed in the pod-casts that the guys are always really excited to get an exceptional success because it gives them a beat, is this a house rule? I can't find it in the book.
                                                So it works like this. Getting an Exceptional Success gets you a Condition. Resolving a Condition gets you a Beat. However, a) remembering to add the Beat at the time the Condition gets resolved is a pain, b) on-the-fly Conditions with short-term bonuses can sometimes then not actually be of any use because the scene just doesn't play out that way, missing out on the Beat unless the character does some really tortured logic to justify doing whatever will resolve the Condition, and c) it can get pretty tough to come up with decent Conditions for every Exceptional Success that's happening, especially when sometimes it's easier to give a non-Condition benefit instead.

                                                Thus, in order to keep the Beat generation from Exceptional-Success-created Conditions simple and straightforward, and not to punish the players if I give them a Condition for their Exceptional Success that turns out to not actually be useful, I just give the Beat when the Exceptional Success is rolled; but if they do get a Condition and resolve it, they don't then get a Beat from that, as they've already had it.

                                                I should probably write up a big list of quick-reference Conditions for common exceptional success types as well, would make it easier to handle Exceptional Sucesses in general.

                                                - Chris Allen, Aberrant Line Developer, Freelance Writer
