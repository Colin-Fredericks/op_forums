                                        Originally posted by gebbethine View Post
                                        Thank you! How would you say, "grace under pressure" as a whole phrase, sticking as closely to its metaphorical meaning as possible?
                                        I think that would go something along the lines of "umalas-gi'endhu udhkalki'i gu'usu" — "grace when you are under pressure". Kal has been affixed to ki'i to mark a state of being, and udh (which means "when") marks the nature of that being. (I.e., it is what it is, it is who it is, it is when it is, etc.)

                                        Edit: Now that I think about it, I should probably put these down here for reference for myself and others later.

                                        udh: When, as in, "When does this happen?"
                                        eanam: What, as in, "What is this?"
                                        aggil: Where, as in, "Where are we?"
                                        abha: Who, as in, "Who are you?"
                                        anash: Why, as in, "Why would you do that?"

                                        Affixing these to the verb kal (to be) results in the following constructions:

                                        udhkal: "When one is.../When they are...."
                                        eanamkal: "When what this is.../When what these are...."
                                        aggilkal: "When where this is.../When where these are...."
                                        abhakal: "When one who is..../When ones who are...."
                                        anaskal: "When why there is..../When why there are...."

                                                    03-25-2015, 12:28 PM
                                                    The pronoun in First Tongue is like a spirit name: it is a summation of affixes and a stem that, in a nutshell, provides someone with what they're looking at while they address the subject. Pronouns in FT revolve mostly around "what is me" and "what is not me", with further descriptors to specify what you are and what you aren't.

                                                    ku: I, me, mine. The base of pretty much every pronoun that follows. Gender-neutral.
                                                    nuku: You, yours. Literally means "not-me". Gender-neutral, and may also be used for the ze/xe/xir/hir third-pronoun in English to denote genderless/third-gender individuals.
                                                    muninuku: He, him. Literally means "not-me male".
                                                    nidunuku: She, hers. Literally means "not-me female".

                                                    shakunuku: We, us, ours. Literally means "many-me-not-me". Gender-neutral. Place "muni" after the "sha" part to indicate "we the males", or "nidu" to indicate "we the females".
                                                    shanuku: They, theirs. Literally means "many-not-me". Gender-neutral. Place "muni" after the "sha" part to indicate "they the males", or "nidu" to indicate "they the females".

                                                    And three corrections:

                                                    With the release of 2e, farah as a term meaning "father" (and it's shortened form, fafa) is only used for very powerful divinities, such as Father Wolf. For spirits lower than Rank 4 and mundane creatures, we use the term aya, and its shortened form a'a. Idigam and totems do not follow this trend. Also, aske (which means "now") should be spelt ase, but either spelling can do based on your preference. I will be using ase from now on. The word zur, which meant the same as gathra (chiminage) in 1e now refers to religious rites and offerings in general, and not specifically the gifts, offers and favours used in social maneuvering with spirits. Gathra is bribing a fire spirit with a giant forest fire if it joins your side — zur is leaving money in the collection plate at a Catholic mass.
