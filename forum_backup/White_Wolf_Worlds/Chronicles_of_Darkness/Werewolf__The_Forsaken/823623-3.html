                                The advice to wait till you've felt out the system, gotten a taste of what the developers intended, before devising house rules is sound.

                                However, there is one little tweak that I would totally offer even players just starting out in WtF 2E. You know how Uratha get bans as they drop in Harmony? The lower they get, the more their spirit psychology asserts itself, resulting in bans. I like to add a thematically-appropriate reflection of this on the High Harmony side. At my table, as Harmony rises, they take the Open condition to certain kinds of spirits. The Uratha takes the condition as if they were a location; allowing spirits to manifest in their presence. When they break to Harmony 6, they take something very specific and thematically-appropriate [ie Open(Lust), Open(Revenge), Open(Cahalunim)]. At Harmony 8 the category becomes more general, so that different kinds of spirits can manifest around the Uratha. Finally at Harmony 10, the condition is simply Open, with no specific category. Any ephemeral entity may use the Uratha as a conduit.

                                The metaphysical fluff I came up for this is that the Uratha's spiritual side withers and their physical presence roots, allowing spirits to exploit that void and flow across it.

                                I've found this small tweak to be mechanically harmonious (no pun intended) with the effects on the reverse end of the Harmony scale. Low Harmony is personal and High Harmony is environmental.

                                            Not to, err, beat a dead horse, but in my CofD game (currently on indefinite hiatus), I award beats based on Rule of Drama/Rule of Cool/Rule of Funny. If the players have their characters do something exceptionally dramatic, cool, or funny (and it makes sense), I'll award a beat for it. I'll hand out a beat for exceptional roleplaying. I'll hand out beats if the player characters make headway in the plot, or just come up with a really interesting idea. I basically just reward them for being engaged in the game. This was partly because we started in 1E and were transitioning into 2E, so were still getting used to the Condition rules (and forgot about them entirely more often than not.) After three or four sessions with minimal beats being awarded, I decided to just start handing them out when I felt the players deserved them. Give the Condition rules a try (seriously, they're really good, if a pain to remember/look up a lot of the time), but don't be afraid to hand out beats "just 'cause." If the characters are advancing quickly, you can always bump up the stats of the bad guys they face to compensate. Or start requiring increasingly exceptional dramatic/cool/funny RP to keep the beats flowing, asking the players to step up their game in that arena.

                                            And my group used the group beats rule, partly so that everyone would advance at the same rate, and partly because, it being a crossover game, there would be sessions that focused more heavily on one player (the Mage dealing with Magey things, the Promethan dealing with Prometheany things, the Geist dealing with Geisty things, and everyone dealing with Vampirey things), so not everyone would necessarily have the same opportunities to shine in every session. I highly recommend Group Beats, because then the very act of gaining beats brings the table closer together. One great RPer earning beats left and right by being awesome in-character nets everyone beats, one "tanky" combat character taking all the damage (and bad Conditions) is not just keeping the "squishier" characters alive, he's generating beats for them. A lucky player's luck is benefitting the whole group, instead of just them. And so on. It highlights the "co-operative" aspect of "co-operative storytelling."

                                            The best piece of advice I would give, starting any game but especially a CofD game, is to talk to the players about what kind of game they want to play and what kind of game you want to run. If you plan to give them a wide-open sandbox where the only thing that moves the story forward is their motivations, and their motivation is to be pointed at an enemy they can slaughter, you're gonna have a bad time. If they want to play in a wide-open sandbox where their motivations are the only thing moving the story forward, and you want to run an episodic monster-of-the-week game, you're gonna have a bad time. CofD has so many tool and types of stories available that it can be rough getting everyone invested in the same kind of story. Being on the same page about what kind of game this will be before a dot is ever put on a character sheet is crucial. You don't want to get five sessions in and have the one player who's been twiddling their thumbs the whole time look up and ask "So when do meet the bad guy so I can kill him?" Or have the player who seems to have been merrily slaughtering everything alongside everyone else take you aside and ask "So when do I get to explore the tortured nature of my monstrous self?" From the very first page of this thread, the piece of advice about requiring the players to make characters who will engage with the troupe and the plot, is an excellent piece of advice.
