                    Just for the sake of clarity, I've worked up a phonemic inventory/pronunciation guide; let me know if I got anything wrong:

                    Nasal Consonants

                    m - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "map"
                    n - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "nap"


                    Stop Consonants

                    p - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "pit"
                    b - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "bit"
                    bh (IPA /bʱ/) - a "murmured b"; no direct English comparison, but like the initial sound in "Bhārat," a Hindi term for India

                    t - an un-aspirated t sound, as in the English "stone"
                    th (IPA /tʰ/) - an aspirated t, as in the English "tin"; may also be pronounced as a fricative, as in the English "thin"
                    d - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "din"
                    dh (IPA /dʱ/) - a "murmured d"; no direct English comparison, but like the initial sound in "dhárma," a Sanskrit religious term

                    k (sometimes written "c") - an un-aspirated k or hard c sound, as in the English "scone"
                    kh (IPA /kʰ/) - an aspirated k or hard c, as in the English "cut"
                    g - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "gut"
                    gh (IPA /gʱ/) - a "murmured g"; no direct English comparison, but like the initial sound in the "ghās," a Hindi word meaning "grass."

                    ' (IPA /ʔ/) - a glottal stop, like the "catch" between the two syllables of the exclamation "uh-oh!"


                    Fricative Consonants

                    f - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "fat"

                    s - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "sap"
                    z - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "zap"
                    sh (IPA /ʃ/) - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "shine"

                    h (probably IPA /x/) - If we're going by how Sumerian was probably pronounced and the sounds Grimm's Law actually produced, this is not pronounced like the English "h," but rather like the "ch" at the end of the German pronunciation of "Bach" or the "x" in most Mexican Spanish pronunciations of "Mexico." The difference can be hard for English speakers to detect, but this sound is made with the tongue in roughly the same position it would be if one were making a "k" sound, and produces a more audible hiss of air than is typical for the English "h."


                    Approximant Consonants

                    y (IPA /j/) - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "yes" (I've seen y appear as a vowel in at least one First Tongue word, but that my be a fluke and its probably safe to pronounce it the same as "i")

                    w - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "we"


                    Liquids

                    l - pronunciation is roughly the same as English, as in "left"
                    r - probably pronounced as either a flap or a trill; in other words, in more or less the same manner as a single "r" in Spanish. I think one of the special powers English-speaking werewolves get is the ability to roll their r's.


                    Vowels

                    Pronouncing the plain vowels in more or less the same way that they're pronounced in Spanish is probably a safe bet, at least for the long vowels:

                    a - like the vowel in "palm"; probably never like the vowel in "hat"
                    e - roughly like the vowel in "face"; possibly like the vowel in "dress" when short
                    i - like the vowel in "feed"; possibly like the vowel in "bit" when short
                    o - roughly like the vowel in "goat"; possibly like the vowel in "thought" when short
                    u - like the vowel in "goose"; possibly like the vowel in "foot" when short

                    Each of these vowels may be short or long. Based on the occurrence of doubled vowels in written First Tongue, vowels are probably always pronounced long in open syllables (syllables not ending in a consonant) and may be either short (written as a single vowel) or long (written as a double vowel) in syllables ending with a consonant. The difference between short and long vowels is mostly in how long the sound is "held," but they may be a qualitative difference in how they're pronounced as well.


                    There may be some distinction, particularly in terms of vowel length or quality, between stressed and unstressed syllables, but as stress is not indicated in written First Tongue it's probably safest to assume that syllable stress is not a major feature of the way the language is pronounced.

                                                    Alright, I went ahead and edited the guide to remove the diphthongs and describe th as an aspirated t. The term "murmur," FYI is usually used to describe a quality known as breathy voice, in which the vocal chords are allowed to vibrate as they do when producing a voiced consonant like d, but are held farther apart to allow more air to escape between them. Aspirated stops are also characterized by spread vocal chords, but in their case the chords are kept from vibrating, as in a voiceless consonant like t. So it doesn't make much sense to talk about a "murmured" t, unless you mean something other than breathy voice.
