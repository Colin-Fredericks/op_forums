    I'm pretty surprised that everyone is arguing the open game model lends itself to D&D but not CofD. From a purely game-balance standpoint I find that it's the opposite: in D&D you really need a certain number of players or you can't win a fight, and if you don't have your healer, skill monkey, damage dealer, etc. you can't get through a dungeon. Whereas, since CofD is more narrative, you can have people drop in and out and change characters freely.

        But the strength of the Open Table model is that it assumes players will have multiple characters (even making new ones that day) to ensure a stable of PCs to cover the essential party roles for a game like D&D. D&D is also really a stand in for any procedural focused game (treating dungeon crawling as the narrative equivalent to a procedural in the more traditional sense). Shadowrun or Leverage are also easy to apply the Open Table concept to (though Shadowrun can be hard if you're letting people make new characters during gaming time). Having major role-niches is important, but again that's why most players are assumed to have access to multiple characters so the party can be arranged as needed.

        The issue with games like the CofD is the games focus on introspective narratives over time. Bob the werewolf kills his wolf-blooded brother during a bout of Kuruth, and then Bob's player misses three sessions and misses out on the resolution of that dramatic plot point. Bob comes back into the story still in, "I just killed my brother!" space, but the story itself has moved on without him. This isn't an unsolvable issue. There are lots of ways to address it, but it's where the concern with the default CofD play style comes in. The focus on introspective narrative over procedural narrative makes breaks in cast a problem that needs solved.

                        Originally posted by Dusksage View Post
                        I think I might be a bit confused at what Open Table is, because I still see no reason why CofD wouldn't work for that.
                        It's not that you can do CoD using Open Table methodology, but that the approach changes the focus of the game when you do. As MagicSwordsman pointed out, it doesn't work as well for games that focus on personal or introspective horror.

                        One of the big reasons for this is the whole point of Open Table is to counter the, "we're older and everyone getting together at the same time to play is impossible" effect, The idea is to have a time when you run the game, and whoever shows shows, and you play with what you have. That's why there are techniques that go along with it like having multiple pre-made characters (both player based to make sure they have a character that plays well with the other characters there that day, and from the GM for new players to the game/group to have something to go with ASAP).

                        One of the big problems with CoD and Open Table, is that Open Table relies heavily on (not to be dismissive) interchangeable characters. For Open Table to work, I need to be able to say, "Oh, Sarah couldn't come today, so instead of my normal PC, I'm going to break out my social character to make up for hers not being in the group." In the CoD that assumption goes against a lot of how the games tend to treat player groups. A werewolves operate in packs that are too insular for that sort of swapping in and out of characters with the same specialty/niche/role. That's why D&D generally works for it. You can always set up the game as adventurers all part of the same guild that form impromptu teams based on the current jobs/expeditions/etc. available (aka the DM sets up stuff for the group that's there). The idea that one member isn't around and someone else is filling in isn't a big deal.

                        And in instances where personal horror comes into play there is nothing stopping the ST from taking that player aside and just doing a solo game to illustrate consequences, or even just working out a narrative without needing any rolls. That way the flow would hold and the characters would change and grow organically without removing the personal horror.
                        But, this is the sort of thing Open Table wants to avoid. The whole goal is to ensure that things don't get bogged down by non-game time issues. If you need to have special side session with individual players, you're getting bogged down.

                                I've had an idea that I've been noodling on: a kind of stupidly-massive epic where you use the Microscope RPG to define a sweep of history before getting into real CofD play. One thing I've been considering is when to dispense with history-making and to dive in and actually create full-blown characters with XP and stuff. Is it the first time we play a Scene, or should we go a few first? Do we try to hit an entire Period, with some foreknowledge of That Which Will Occur Later (suddenly, prophecies are easy!)? I'm not sure what would work and I haven't had a chance to get people to try it.

                                And it occurs to me that this can be adapted somewhat to Open Table CofD in that, for any given span of history, or even any given story, you just map it to some combination of players. Bob goes missing for three weeks? Fine. We'll play with what was happening fifty years earlier. New player? Well, let's mess with what's going on a decade later. The problem with this strategy is that it's probably not very fulfilling: if some combination of players never meets again, that story will never wrap up. But... that happens anyways and we figure out how to deal, so I don't think it's a huge problem. It's also probably very daunting for a new player, but as long as everyone is on board and willing to cooperate and figure out how to ease them into the mix gently, that might work.

                                What's fun about it to me is that it really messes with time. You might be fighting a Big Bad, but when you go fifty years previous, that might be a PC and he might make decisions about his character's motivations and ambitions that suddenly inform the present time of a diplomatic opportunity (yeeees, my character happened to find his journal! totally by chance), which might mean an apocalyptic event the group playing the decade-later timeline thought was caused by the Big Bad was actually just pinned on him, and there's a Bigger Bad somewhere out there they haven't discovered yet, or maybe it's a winners-write-history power play illustrating this other theme.

                                It might not work well for immortality shticks, but even Mummy would probably handle it fine because you'll have different combinations of insomniacs. (I don't actually know anything about Mummy.) It's certainly not as flexible as an open table of D&D megadungeoning, but well... what do you guys think?
