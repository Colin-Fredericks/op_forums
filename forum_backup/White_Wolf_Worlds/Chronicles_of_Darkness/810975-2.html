                                I agree, but that's not really the point. The point behind bringing up Challenge Rating, was that Dungeon's and Dragons had a preconceived notion of adventuring as a set of four challenges (or the equivalent) of approximately the Challenge Rating of your parties average ECL per in game day, This isn't some fan conception, this is straight from their DM advice in the encounter design chapter. Challenge Rating was built around the idea of a game of mechanical chance, simulating brave adventurers on heroic combat-oriented quests. The power level of your character in D&D was your ability to meet that specific challenge.

                                It's a lot harder to pin down what an "adventure" is in Chronicles of Darkness and thus it's harder to determine which characters are more able to complete one. It places Chronicles on a much more subjective assessment of power.

                                    Just because you don't like it doens't mean its not a concept baked into the game. There are other calculations, such as how many encounters a day one should run into, resource alotment, scaling of magic items, etc. You are ignoring information that does not agree with your view.

                                    Further, even in the fifth edition of the game, which has spent more time focusing on exploration and social interaction than many previous editions, the overwhelming majority of the game is still devoted to combat, and it is commonly accepted that you rate things based on combat performance. No matter your race/class/other combination, you are a character designed to engage in battles. Its a common point all PCs in D&D share. Even if you didn't have official attempts at ranking from WotC, its inevitable that unofficial rankings would grow, because we all operate under the same rules and assumptions in D&D. D&D was born of war games, and still bears a number of traits that its origin provided.

                                    The closest equivalent in CofD involves using all the subsystems together - we have social maneuvering, combat, tracking/chases, investigation/research, stealth and potentially social-group-fu. We also have crafting/gardening and dreams as potential areas of influence. That's a lot of different directions you can take a character, and they all bleed over into each other in a tangled mess. Even if you wanted to take one area here (say, investigation) and rate all the different supernaturals, there's still no basis of comparison. Vampires specialize in mind control, werewolves the hunt, demons on spying. Each of those three will approach investigation through the appropriate lens, and all will reach the end in roughly the same shape. If you completely eliminate any and all humans/ephemeral entities specifically to remove any advantage a vampire might have, you are screwing over the vamp. On the other hand, if you have an open room with nowhere to hide, the Irraka is out of luck, ironically making a white room stealth challenge impossible for her. Mages have trouble dealing with unexpected twists in ways vamps and wolves do not - lack of preparation is an issue.

                                    Everything is simply too fluid to make an objective determination here.
