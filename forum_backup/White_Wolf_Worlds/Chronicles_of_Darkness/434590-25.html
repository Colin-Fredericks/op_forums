                I have no idea if this project is still on-going, but I had some thoughts bubbling around in my head and I just had to get them out.

                Way, way back in this thread, back on page 14, I made a post about how the Leviathans represent the primordial darkness, the "bestial nature" of humanity that we try to tell ourselves we have conquered, but which may in turn conquer us.

                As was commented a page or two back, humanity is STILL, at heart, little more than an animal. We wear our clothes and use our tools and perform our rituals and tell ourselves that this makes us better than the beast. We shun the dark and foul impulses from our primeval self. But the Leviathans? They are US, stripped of all our trinkets and talismans. They are the beast within brought to hideous life, the living reminder of the bloody seas from which we dragged ourselves. They are our darkest impulses and most primeval natures garbed in hideous, ever-shifting flesh. We might try to deny them, we do so with great success... but we cannot eradicate them. And, perhaps, we never will.

                Leviathans are scary because they represent the darkness humanity was born in, and that it still hasn't finished clawing its way out of. Worse, they represent the possibility that we may - or even that we will - lose our grip and be swallowed by it once again.

                Their time has passed... and yet, their time may come again. That is not dead which can eternal lie. And with strange aeons, even death may die. What was once mundane is now unthinkable, and may yet, in time, become mundane again. The light may burn so brightly, but darkness is eternal; in the end, darkness will always swallow the light.
                The more I've thought about it, the more I've realised; this is actually very close to Lovecraft's own writings on the Mythos. See, the Mythos has never been about Good vs. Evil - August Derleth's interjections aside. Indeed, Lovecraft all but calls them out as purely human inventions. But Order vs. Chaos - the attempts by sentients to impose order on an ultimately chaotic and indifferent universe, that's another story.

                Moreover, Lovecraft's writings are significantly embedded in his own cultural zeitgeist, of which he was admittedly on the extreme side even for his time. The defining attributes of what makes many of his stories scary is a compound.

                The first element, is Xenophobia - Fear of the Other, fear of that which is different, alien, new, that's a huge part of what made Lovecraft's works scary at the time. Indeed, this is what Lovecraft himself had to say on it, proving the importance of xenophobia to his works:
                "The oldest and strongest emotion of mankind is fear, and the oldest and strongest kind of fear is fear of the unknown."
                The other element is... I'm not sure if I'm using the right terminology, but this feels right: Cultural Imperialism. Lovecraft grew up in a time in which WASP supremacy, the dominance of the world by WASP culture and its superiority to all other cultures were taken as given by its given. They were considered "natural laws", as inherent and as obvious as the sun rising in the morning. That's why Lovecraft's heroes tend to go mad; they are forcibly confronted with proof that this deeply held belief is wrong, and they can't take it that they aren't the pinnacle of life.

                Incidentally, this is also why some of Lovecraft's works... haven't aged terribly well. I can easily name "Facts Concerning the Late Arthur Jermyn and His Family" and "Medusa's Coils" as two that just don't hold up today, as we've progressed beyond the mentality to see them in as terrible a light as their author intended.

                So, what I'm getting at is this: Stagnation vs. Progress is good as a secondary element. The Cthulhu Mythos itself is built on the great cycle of birth, growth, stagnation, decay, death and rebirth; the Elder Things had their time, and now it's humanity's time, but humanity is also going to fall in time. But I think a stronger element to build this revamp on is the conflict between Order and Chaos. It's something that can be done both internally - the Leviathans' need to control versus their powerful, primeval instincts and urges - and externally. It gives a more ambiguous nature to the Leviathans, and also gives them a hope spot.

                As I touched upon in that earlier post I commented, a big problem with Leviathan is that currently, it has overtones that are too dark. There's a trope called "Audience Alienating Premise", and several of the canon World of Darkness games have fallen into it. If Leviathan is too bleak, it risks running into the exact same problems as Promethean and, closer still, Wraith before it; it becomes so dark a setting that nobody wants to play in it. And that's a certain death for a game. Prometheans at least have the New Dawn to look forward to, and we've received some hints that Promethean 2e is going to be tuning down some of the Hate Plague effect, for this simple reason. Leviathan needs some silver linings, or nobody's going to want anything to do with it.

                Also, while Marduk representing Order and Progress is fine, one needs to remember that the Chronicles of Darkness depict a Black and Gray Morality sort of world. Progress always comes with a cost, and that needs to be respected if Marduk and the other anti-Leviathan groups are going to actually enjoy respect. I mean, the cost/fearsomeness of progress was actually a part of Lovecraft's inspiration behind writing the Mythos in the first place:

                While most people of his time were entranced by the technological innovation produced by the Industrial Revolution, Lovecraft was deeply suspicious of modern technology and the poorly-understood powers it vested in mankind. All of his work resonates with the terror of the newly-discovered magnitude of the universe, which in the early period of his life was believed to consist entirely of the Milky Way. Einstein's theory of relativity opened a door into teleportation, time travel, and alien geometry, and radically altered peoples' notion of space-time itself, while the discovery of pre-Cambrian fossils and Wegener's then-new-and-controversial hypothesis of continental drift brought the notion that the Earth was far older than previously believed, and that even the shape of the continents was not set in stone. All of this was subtly addressed in Lovecraft's stories of alien horror, and of the remains of ancient civilizations lost to the abyss of geological deep time.

                The dizzying speed of progress of his time was compounded by an expansion of the unknown. Each new development, instead of reducing the number of questions as had been expected by pre-modern philosophers, instead compounded them exponentially. Leibniz had hoped that the entire world could be described by reason, and that this is the best of all possible worlds — a possibility utterly abolished during Lovecraft's writing period. Each new discovery only increased humanity's knowledge of its own ignorance and insignificance, encouraging a nihilistic atmosphere, and this is perhaps the central theme of Lovecraft's incisive fiction
                Finally... this is something I'm going to get a lot of hate for, but I think it needs to be said. Changes in culture aren't always a good thing. We've made a lot of progress in some ways since the Bronze Age, but in others, we've honestly gone backwards.

                Let us examine the case of Alan Turing. The man who saved Britain during WW2 with his codebreaking skills, the man who invented the Turing Test... the man who injected an apple with strychnine and ate it, committing suicide, after the same government he saved realised he was a homosexual, publicly prosecuted him as a criminal on crimes of immorality and indecency, and blacklisted him, until he couldn't live with a shame. Which culture would he consider superior? Britain of the 1950s? Or the Pagan Roman Empire, where his sexuality and his relationship with a younger man would have been seen as natural as the sun and moon?

                Much of the fearfulness in Lovecraft's work is born of the breaking of "irrefutable laws", of conventionally held wisdom as to what constitutes morality and decency. But, as the example shows, aren't some laws better off being broken? This adds some gray to the Leviathans; they are chaos, yes, but they also hold in them freedom from order's bonds, from the society that stifles and strangles people for being "different".

                I am not advocating that Leviathans should be some blatant GLBTIA metaphor. That's hamfisted, crude, and just generally offensive to everyone

                But Leviathans are the Outsiders, they are the Other. They are the shadow that terrifies those inside the circle, but which offers sanctuary, comfort and shelter to those who are similarly outcast. Leviathans do not merely force people into slavery, they actively draw other outsiders to them. It doesn't matter who you are; there may be no place for you in the System, but the Leviathans will take you. That, I think, could be an interesting angle to explore and exploit.

                                        Interesting question. I actually think that you could get something that looks a LOT like 40K and works perfectly.

                                        The Emperor of Man trapped on his throne. Simultaneously the single most critical piece of infrastructure and utterly impotent: Able to watch but not intervene as his empire decays and abandons his teachings. (He wanted an atheist empire and ended up with a repressive theocracy).

                                        This makes a great model for the Marduk Society; or at least the Society as it is in the time of the typical Leviathan setting - In the grimdark future anyone could be using the name. Marduk technology is the underpinnings of the human empire, but no one remembers how it works. Text books on critical thinking have been put to music and become holy chants, but no one understands the old languages of Earth.

                                        The Imperium of Man itself could be functionally similar, but with a different aesthetic. Instead of Gothic/catholic influences, bronze age Mesopotamian. Instead of corrupt bureaucratic governess, go for passionate warlords who think with their muscles. They build massive pleasure palaces or start pointless wars for glory even as the empire collapses.

                                        As mankind falls Leviathans fill in the gaps nicely. Every century mankind becomes less able to build spaceships and more reliant on Leviathans swimming through the Rift to other planets and defending them from Typhons. Freed from the limitations of a single world - ancient Typhons have grown to enormous size, along the lines of entire Tyrannid hive fleets.


                                        Basically. In a setting like 40K the idea of man as a being of reason is (practically) dead, and the Imperium is built upon it's corpse. So the Marduk Society would be long gone and it's embers slowly fading. As Mankind reverts to a more primordial way of thinking like the Imperium of Man's Leviathans become integrated into every day society - causing enormous damage through the Wake and their behaviour even as they prove vital to keep the Imperium running. Just like how the actual 40K Imperium's fanaticism proves vital to protecting it from Chaos even as it causes enormous damage that will probably destroy mankind in the end.
