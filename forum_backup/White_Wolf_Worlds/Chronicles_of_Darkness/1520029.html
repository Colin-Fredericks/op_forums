      I have two characters who I enjoyed so much I basically started including them in every game. I'll tell the tale of the first.
      -
      Namshiel wasn't really supposed to be a character in the Count The Cost chronicle of Hunter. The Vampire story in that game was essentially one low grade, wanna-be-slasher but also wanna-be-in-Kindred-society manipulating the player group into taking out two on-the-Prince's-shit-list vampires who had gotten an idea of what he was up to and were willing to rat him out to the Prince to get back into good graces and out of their exile-the monsters of the second story of the chronicle weren't supposed to be the most hyper competent of enemies for the group to deal with. The Mehket(I think his name was Mark) who moved this all into motion only brought up the Sheriff as a nebulous, terrible thing to come because it was his ticking clock and he needed his patsies to also having a ticking clock. If the players didn't really get the two vampires before the Sheriff made time to check out this claim of sussy behavior, it likely wouldn't have even bit the players in the ass directly-more an effect on the neighborhood.

      Exceeeeeppppppt.

      So one of the players was a former explosive ordinance disposal tech for the US military, and ported a lot of that over to his character because he was not very creative. Long story short, he blew up one of the two in-the-know vampires. While they were in a car. In a residential area.

      This, ladies and gentlemen, was the beginning of learning to pay attention to all of the consequences of a given action. Certainly I tried, but I was much greener then. I eventually did use it later on as the local government (and it's shadows) started getting really annoyed about this shit.

      But anyways, the immediate fallout was that the other hunter panicked, did some quick recon, and kidnapped one of the player group as insurance, and basically called in the Sheriff because he was scared out of his fucking mind, because who the fuck uses bombs in a residential area, holy shit. So the time table moved up, because there's a number of issues with that.

      Thanks guys, now I actually have to make this fucker.

      At all times in my career, I've been mindful of how in-range an opponent is to character's capacity to do something against them, but at this time, it was more in a mechanical/mathematical sense-DnD was still my major background, and I hadn't quite put two and two together from that time on why the CR building tools for encounters never seemed to be satisfying. In this game, I was having the players keep track of their EXP gains, and building characters within a range appropriate to how much they had. So originally, Namshiel (who I took the name from a Dresden File antagonist) had something of a different setup in terms of what he could actually do. This will come up. His basic concept remained about the same, though.

      So, Namshiel. Namshiel was a Malkovian Venture acolyte, a goth-pagan cowboy of a character. He himself wasn't particular powerful-on his own at the beginning, he was tough and astute, and his Dominate was frightening, but he wasn't usually a direct conflicter despite being more on the grounds than the usual Ventrue. For anything with violence, Namshiel relied on a Daeva draugr he had managed to subjugate and bodily equip thanks to some Ordo contacts called the Golden Hellhound, a feral and vicious physical antagonist-but the Hellhound was more an advertisement of Namshiel's ability to subjugate enemies, break them down, and control them (and also get away with walking the poor bastard out in public thanks to also putting some Obfuscate). I wanted the physical threat to be kind of clearly something fucked, and Namshiel to be more....the wrong sort of reasonable, if you catch my drift. I had a feeling there were a lot of Gangrels and Nosferatru in the Invictus as Sheriff's at the time, and I wanted to change it up some.

      So anyways, one of the players is held hostage, the other two basically gear up to deal with whatever they can-and then get the idea to go and get holy water, and since the players are all idiot Mormons of the time(I was one too, don't come at me), go to a Catholic church (standing amounts of holy water is not a thing the LDS faith, and no one thought to do a sacramental/blessing or anything of the like for the circumstance). I had them roll on the off chance, and they got a ten, so I said you're in luck, not only is there holy water, there's a cell of the Malleus Maleficarum there. So now they're cooking with gas and come up with a plan.

      The site of problems was a school rooftop with the meeting at midnight. Namshiel gets there, talks to the doofus with the player character, basically dresses them down for being sloppy as hell, gets told about the Mehket and gets around to be willing to investigate it for a while, and was going to go and wipe the player's memory (because they may be monsters but they're not the worst)...when the other two PC's and the Malleus Maleficarum show up.

      Older me might've taken advantage of the general layout advantages-Namshiel's not much of a fighter, but he's got a gun and is on a roof top that doesn't have a whole lot of ways to the top of it-an external ladder and a rooftop access with a lot of locked doors from the ground to the top, so there's plenty of room to call the cops and put pressure on everyone, take up defensive positioning, negotiated while manipulating the Hellhound into an advantageous position.

      Younger me just had Nam sic the Hellhound on them and start working out a retreat. And that might've been wise enough, if not for one thing.

      I was very lenient about the players saying they could get dragon breath rounds.

      I was new with Chronicles and urban settings, leave me alone.

      Soooo the Hellhound went to attack ,and got off a hard hit, before the players shot fire at the thing, and hey, here's a thing about draugr's, particularly those conditioned by a master of Dominate over so much damn time-they don't handle fear frenzy well with direction, and the main director was on the top of a building in the middle of a developing fog of war. So the Hellhound ran like hell, and Namshiel was already booking it. THe PC's and Witch's Hammer managed to run down the vampire with our other PC and kill him, but the PC's, not knowing what was going on in full, went after the Sheriff.

      After a bit of looking, they found him and tried to run him over. He took out their tires, but still got swiped, and two of the PC's very quickly and smartly went for his eyes, and managed to blind the man. And because I had built him to be approachable in terms of problems, he was now in a position where the players really could honestly kill him. Which leads to the scene that changed everything.

      Namshiel talked them out of it. He argued that he was here to take care of business, and if they just killed him for whatever reason (and it was whatever reason, he's only been understood as this vauge and nebulous threat for the most part who doesn't have investment in the area otherwise, then the ELysium was bound to investigate, and they would certainly find them and kill them or worse, where as if they let him go, he was happy to consider it a professional misunderstanding. And two out of the three party members ended up agreeing with that, and let him go.

      It was a mistake neither Namshiel or I let them forget.

      Namshiel ironically ended up being a more prominent background threat to the players than the main antagonist, a slasher called The Hood, was for a long time, because the players actively knew they were on his bad side, and he wasn't opposed to making it clear. Some of this was that around this time I started giving Vampire an actual chance, found I did have things to enjoy about it, and started developing my love for the Circle of the Crone, and of course I found out about Malkavia in the Venture Clan book. Some of this, though, was just the players being paranoid, particularly when they got confronted with the fact that as they were starting to learn more about the supernatural, Namshiel paradoxically presented an advantage by being someone who was the wrong sort of reasonable who knew more about this shit. During the Geist Story, when two of the players were suffering from a Curse that kept them from interacting with humanity and the Malleus Maleficarum proved to be of minimal held, it was a call to Namshiel that led to a meeting with the krewe of Club Melissa who could advise them, and in the Changeling Story, when the Winter Queen tasked one of the player with retreiving the fate-splitting axe, Namshiel in the spirit of the now-on-the-table alliance between the freehold and the Elysium that came about in relation to things happening agreed to be one of the two aids the player could take with him, and used the As One rite from First Edition in the Hedge to turn a privateer's Hollow against them (in what is still called one of my most viscerally disquieting scenes from my players). Of course, those times and other small interactions made it clear that the courtesy was because there was advantages for him for it, and he still did have a mind to pay them back-when the government started really getting annoyed, part of that was Namshiel taking advantages of having been a FBI agent (even VASCU) in life to create some pressure. He was the right mixture of cordially professional and psychopathically ruthless. Namshiel was bound to kill them at some point, but as a man whose job it was to keep a bunch of monsters in line, he did have common ground with the PCs, and ironically because of the players developing role as negotiators for the supernatural community, Namshiel ended up also fascilating a lot of new alliances that would lead to him doing more policing.

      So basically, any time I need a Sheriff, I bust out Namshiel. He's a lot more personally dangerous now, though he's at his most dangerous when he's not on the ground, as a proper Ventrue should be. Still, he's the ever dark, dealing-with-gods-and-demons, speaking madness into being, knows more than you and how to use it against you, gothic pagan cowboy he started out as-hell, he's more of that gothic-pagan cowboy now than when he started.

      Next time, We'll talk about a man who has always been one thing despite being many things-the ever cantankerous punk-rocker-turned-occultic-master Shane Shade.

          I think my favourite NPC in the books so far is probably the character "Mosspocket" from Changeling: The Lost 2nd Edition (Page 260), the Hobgoblin that got tricked out of his home and wants to get his home back by attempting to trade up for it.

          I think the reason why is because at first I got the impression that the Hedge would be this place where it's crazy dangerous and everything there is out to eat the PCs (or just devour their souls) or scam them into unfair agreements then weasel out via some exact wording or technicality, and it's either kill-or-be-killed, but I realized that if I made it like that, the player characters would stay the heck out of the Hedge and never willingly enter it under any circumstances or make any deals or interactions with any of the characters there.

          So I was very glad to see that there's an example character there that's just trying to make a living and is probably in just as much difficulty as the PCs are themselves and is also in a very sympathetic situation. Seeing that done there has given me a lot of incentive as a GM to see if I could figure out to produce similar characters or even societies that my player characters could come to see in a sympathetic light and potentially find as useful allies or draw positive connections to in character, rather than just make the Hedge full of monsters that want to do nothing more than scam the PCs out of selling their souls for a pack of cigarettes, or bake them into a pie.

          Editing again several hours later to add: as much of a grognard as I am (as in, the "plays military simulations" type of grognard, not the "whines that Xth Edition was the best" type of grognard), one of the things that I constantly try to tell myself is to not get too sucked into the hard factors of how much armour a vehicle has or how powerful a tank gun is, but instead to remember that "even the darkest shadows become invisible in total darkness". So a lot of my favourite NPCs in any RPG book tend to be characters that are highly accessible, and have realistic and sympathetic problems of their own that I can identify with, even if those problems tend to be cast in a fantastic light. In Mosspocket's case, losing a home and being unable to reclaim it due to someone else tricking him out of the deed and then that person dying with the deed in their name with no provisions to pass it onto another owner is something I can totally understand and I can totally sympathize with. Even if metaphysical world-binding magical forces don't apply here, legalistic technicalities come close enough. Another thing I'm big on is that Mosspocket is also a character that could be accessible to the players at quite a number of different levels and in quite a number of different contexts.

          I'm relatively new to Chronicles, but since a ton of the campaigns I run tend to be games set in war (often without any magical or occult elements), I think these types of NPCs serve a very practical purpose and that's to fill the world with as many realistically flawed and vulnerable characters to give my players the impression that not everyone is a fearsome Opposing Force Badass, but rather that people everywhere suffer very real consequences in war, and these people aren't just a number, but are people with their own dreams, desires, and vulnerabilities, just like them, who are trying to make the most out of the time they have on the planet, play the hand that they're given, and keep what hope they have alive even in the shittiest situations. This critical juxtaposition of light and dark elements I think makes the dark elements that much more meaningful.

          So I figured I'd apply what I know from running war games here, and when I saw Mosspocket, I knew I had an instant favourite.​

              My favourite NPC is a result of me asking 'can a Mage survive in the Underworld indefinitely?', Donn is a Master of Death and has an Obsession or two relating to the Underworld. As a result of prolonged exposure and lack of human contact, he is on the verge or becoming Rapt.

              I put him into my Chronicle as a background character who wasn't too important, but the players decided to visit the Underworld (despite not having a single dot of Death magic between them).

              He was busy summoning supernal entities from Stygian and building a ritual area, and all his research, so in exchange for what the players wanted (which included coming back to their University to testify against the Masterof Fate there, and/or teaching the Death Arcanum) they would have to search the Underworld for a Deep Dominion and record it's laws, bringing him back the knowledge.

              So far so reasonable, but one of the players was short on time so they decided to split the party, and after some considerable prompting from me, changed their mind and all returned to the material world, only to be confronted by some Sin-eaters protecting the Avernian Gate (i hadn't read about guardian geists yet). Some conflict resolution later they sit down with the Krewe and find out, Donn is very dangerous and even threatened to kill the Sin-eaters if they returned to the Underworld.

              Now the players have to decide will they help this Mage complete his research, or try to oppose him.

              NB: he is trying to 'fix' the Underworld, but hasn't really thought about ghosts, and whether he cares if they get wiped out in his little area... Drawing Stygia down is his primary concern not what the consequences would mean for the dead.

              I am hoping next session the players will tip him in one direction of an other. As I am perfectly happy with them devastating the Underworld or returning on Master to his teaching post with all the knowledge he has collected.
