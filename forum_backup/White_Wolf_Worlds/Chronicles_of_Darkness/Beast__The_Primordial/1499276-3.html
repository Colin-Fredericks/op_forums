        Originally posted by GibberingEloquence View Post
        3) Can Heroes change into other mythic archetypes?
        The thing that Heroes were before the signal-to-noise ratio went to shit was never about being a mythic archetype. Antagonist Heroes are built having crossed the threshold of "resemblance to the mythic/folkloric basis of your supernatural creature-type is overwhelmingly a failstate." Temenotic Dreamborn are bad news as actors on the worldly stage — even the people possessed by their own reputation don't get out of it in a good state of being. There's a reason the least-condemned "unite yourself with a supernatural force" outcome in the setting is the one that preserves your individuality by being a route to Ascension rather than literally anything trucking with ephemeral/invisible entities.

        Does a Hero's Journey have to end in either doom/death or giving up the sword to plant flowers, or can they find fulfilment and purpose by adopting other roles, perhaps by mixing and matching ideas and symbols from the Bright Dream, Primordial Dream, and other Temenotic Realms?
        There is a real extent to which this question is as flawed as "can a Ridden find fulfillment and purpose by assembling a different spirit to possess them, or are their options limited to dying in a conflict with werewolves or stopping being Ridden?"

        Even without accounting for the fact that the Oracular state Heroism derived from wasn't so far gone as to need a Life connecting them to mundane context, the Forsworn have committed to a cycle of violence wherein they seize Primordial power through the most overt form of power-claim there is. The Heroic cycle is literally ritualized murder to seize worldly power through your placement as a fulcrum for the unconscious power of collective humanity in reaction to the intrusion of the monstrous into that metaphorical space. The rest is decoration upon that line.

        I wholeheartedly believe there is genuine material to explore in the idea of people who are sick of pseudo-Heroism, and want to live up to the ideal version of that role.
        Chop wood, carry water. The ideal version of that role is "Become a good person. That is all."

        There is a reason the supernatural beings most associated with power constrained by morality and ideals are also notorious for adhering to codes of honor impossible for mortal souls to keep. This is a universe where Mummy's metaphysic presents one of the broadest applicable views around — the Law's a bastard, and humanity was create to trip over it often enough to feed the cosmic Devil in its service. You can be a good person who has magic powers, but that's generally "in spite of," not "thanks to."

        And perhaps for a chosen few, the Astral listens and becomes psycho-spiritually linked to these individuals. Not quite Comic Book Superheroes as we see in Marvel, DC or various animes/mangas, though. Something that is a mid-point between "glorious shining paragon of humanity fighting back the forces of darkness and inspiring others towards greatness" and "broken empty shell of an idealist who somehow has not locked themselves up and cried until they drowned in their own tears".
        That midpoint is "magic is not a toy, lay off the sauce, the real power is the friends you make along the way." The Self is a deep road with many turnings, and most of them are bad places to commit to.

        The general process of getting the Astral to listen to you entails Inheritance and/or knowing how to manipulate people and narrative, and both of those involve a fair bit of power (which, for Heroes, probably also entails murder, because there is no store-bought Primordial power short of getting entangled with Beasts in their own pursuits). Having the sway to convince thousands of people up is down is pretty much just normal fame and persuasion, with all the upkeep that entails if you're not bringing magical backing into the equation. This is not a route to power that doesn't look bastardous.

                                I'm writing at work so I must be brief, but I have two observations:

                                • I like the term "Priest" for Inguma Forsworn, but I have a hypothesis on why others can't connect with it. This monicker makes sense when you look at a Priest's relationship with their community. Someone who draws lines in the sand between acceptable an unacceptable, and advocates for them. That term becomes more confused if instead, you look at their relationship with their ideal. Perhaps, "Sacrosanct" really is a better term, since it implies a certain attitude these Heroes would cultivate in themselves and others. It gets into the Hero's head, rather that describing their public image.

                                • Due to developer's incompetence, the Beast Corebook had a tone of hipocrisy, when treated as a consistent whole. To avoid the same pitfall in the future, I am certain that Heroes and Beasts must be treated as equal opposites across an amoral line. If Beasts can try to learn about their blind spots and grow as people, trying to make the best of their condition, then Heroes must have the same option. The fact that Heroes who attempt this would play into their reputation as "good guys" would be incidental, but not the writing goal. The communication issue we're experiencing stems from the fact that "What about heroic Heroes? They should all be this way" and "What about heroic Heroes? Some should succeed in being that" are easy to mistake with one another.

                                                            So to properly address the general "If you value the moral equivalence of Beasts and Heroes, why Heroes written way they are?":

                                                            So I feel like it might be useful to return to the beginning of the Forsworn Heroes and the whys of it all to address this.

                                                            Back in 2019, when I was riding high on life before crashing and burning, and the idea that Cinder and I should try and claim Developer-hood over Beast was a very vibrant dream, we sat down and had a quick figuring of what were actual things that really needed retooling with Beast. The first and most obvious answer was "Something had to be done about Heroes", followed by "THe Astral/Dreaming needs to factor more into the gameplay and setting of Beast". In defining what had to be particularly addressed with Heroes, the priorities were to make a clear, understandable, and simple point to anchor Heroes on that would handily answer "What's with the beef between Beasts and Heroes?", and to find a way to empower them without neccesarily making them stronger than Beasts. Some ancilliary goals to that was to balance Beasts and Heroes on the same relative moral bar, and to find ways to increase Heroes viability as a crossover antagonist in the same way Beasts could function.

                                                            The idea of Forsworn, that Heroes were Children who Conquered their Horror rather than embracing it, was a handy answer to all of those concerns, and gave some added benefits. By framing Beasts as having embraced their fear while Heroes rejected them, we have a really simple opposition that informs action and stories that grow from that core point organically and makes sense. Since in many ways it is a very personal matter of approach to things, and is supported by both character types having the Same Hunger and General Drive to Build Their Lair, it also let us properly frame them both as sitting on a dark grey line of morality as a baseline with room to be so much (and "so much") better or worse from there, with Heroes having a benefit where the things they nominally represent being, on the surface, much more preferable in the broader social context to Beasts. The need to satitate Hunger seperate from building Lair also gives us a thing for Heroes to do when Beasts aren't around and a means to function as an antagonist in crossovers without losing their central importance to Beasts. The inclusion of Lair and Satiety and the metaphysical consequences that could be spun out from their rejection of fear provided a lot of design space for Hero's powers to built upon and shaped how we need them to be. Some of the other benefits included helping to draw Beast as a psychological horror game into focus, and rooting it's urban mythology genre elements into a symbol set and structure for personal conflicts and journey of discovery, healing , being overwhelmed by one's own mind, and with that making clear Beasts freedom to become active actors in that mythology while still permitting them to happily shit all over it, and allowing for the game to springboard that psychological discussion into broader sociological themes and concerns and then sew those back into the personal conflict, thus allowing us to root the game's overall criticism of heroism and monomythically-related issues as a concept into much more real and tangible terms for the audience to engage with.

                                                            Notice, though, that nowhere in the priorities or the spun out benefits, does it entail that being a Hero is a good thing, or that it's psychologically healthy to be.

                                                            Because it's not.

                                                            A Hero could be lucky and dedicated enough to have a long life of altruism and being legitimately better than every Beast they killed, and at the end of their life they would still be discontent, miserable, and rooted in hostility-and most Heroes are not going to be that lucky or that dedicated. Being a Hero is a problem.

                                                            Beast is a psychological horror game that uses mythology for the structure, iconography, and language for exploring and expressing it's horror, themes, and ideas, and it has always anchored Heroes as antagonists on the ground there is a quintessential need for violence against Beasts as the marker and maker of that Heroism. In this view, what Beasts do, whatever else is flawed and disturbing about them, is psychologically healthy-they embrace their fears and hungers and are accountable to and for them, they are self-permissive of being lesser, of being vulnerable, sensitive, uninfluential, limited, in over their heads, and even just being afraid, and they understand their peers in life first and foremost as people like unto them. Heroes feel compelled to violence this, regardless of what the person who starts from this place is otherwise doing.

                                                            The Forsworn model only essentially clarifies and solidifies the how and why it is by putting up a dichotomy of embracing fear as the starting point to a journey to a psychologically fuller and healthier position by contrasting it with a need to conquer one's fears, a position that is fundamentally impossible and toxic to try and maintain, no matter how heroic "conquering your fears" sounds-but that's the problem, that's almost quinessentially the heroic ideal. The reason Heroes kill Beasts is because Beasts represent everything that mindset rejects, and for whatever good emerges from that rejection (again, a subject we do want plain face, because we want work our way past the more boring and superficial conversations about morality and dig into the meat and bones of humanity and the hows and whys of people), Heroes still have a fundamental disconnect that encourages thinking of the world and others in ways that are constrained by a need to be strong, a need to always have the answer, a need to always be right, a need to be exemplary, to be the conqueror of fear, to be heroic, and if the world doesn't need heroes, then they'll remake the world so it does. They've traded bravery for fearlessness because, to them, to be afraid in the first place is to be lesser than what they want to be.

                                                            And, like, it's understandable. Being afraid is a scary place, it's not pleasant or fun. There's a lot of shame that is thrown on being afraid. there's an assumption of inactivity when you could, instead, find the enemies that are making you afraid and instead get mad at them. Uncertainty that emerges from fear can be unsatisfying, and many people will take an answer against that uncertainty over contemplation and acceptance of no easy answer. There's a lot of personally relatable reasons why one would go that route.

                                                            But at the end of the day, that's bullshit. That's always going to be bullshit. It doesn't matter how pretty the image that comes from that is, at it's core it's fundamentally flawed and rotten. But boy oh boy, doesn't it look nice.

                                                            So, with that in mind, I don't owe or have interest in providing a happy ending to Heroes for being Heroes. I do owe them the chance to heal-they can always go and pull the Anathema from the Horror, tend to it's wounds while bearing it's thrashing lashes and offer comfort to it's pain, and with time and healing come to embrace and devour them and be embraced and devoured by them to become Beasts.

                                                            But I don't owe Heroes peace or harmony or closure for being Heroes. It is a psychologically pained state that, in ignoring it's fundamental need, is going to encourage actions that control, hedge, and define, that will inevitably assume hostility. I don't promise that they'll do any good or amount to anything-though, like with Beasts, I do imply it, and more readily don't deny it (we're not Genius First Edition). They get that it's an understandable problem. They get that sometimes they'll reveal good things to consider and be-Beast is a game on the whole that points out that crooked sticks can still draw straight lines. But they don't get to have a good head space. They don't get to not be the opposition. They don't get to avoid their consequences.

                                                            They get to be heroic.
