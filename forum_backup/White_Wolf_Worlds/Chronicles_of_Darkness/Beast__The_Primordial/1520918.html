      Ultimately, the best antagonists are built towards the protagonists.

      You've also got a pretty big pile of examples that sound like their going to focus on trying to kill Begotten in combat scenarios: people trained in combat or wanted to be, and people that want personal revenge on a Beast for specific bad actions. Those types have their place, but in the myth and folklore the game draws from, those are the kind of people that end up as part of Medusa's garden of "statues" or added to a dragon's pile of intruder-bones. Successful Heroes are the last people to fight a Beast, and given the power disparities, that generally means they're not the first unless they can weaken a Beast a lot first.

      One of the reasons why Hunters that know about Heroes don't like Heroes, is that successful Heroes tend to get really good at things like whipping up mobs of normal people, getting younger and less experienced Heroes to lay an Anathema but die in the process, etc.

      This is why personalizing your antagonists is important; esp. with a splat as varied as the Begotten.

      But some more specific tips:

      1) Pay attention to Life, Legend, and feeding habits. You can get a lot of mileage out of Heroes that are pushovers in a direct confrontation if they're a threat to these core aspects of a Beast. Even if the fight is anticlimactic, the drama leading up to it can be the stuff of table talk for years if you come at it from a good angle.

      In my longest running Beast outing the most memorable Hero was really a one-scene NPC that set up a bunch of other things (with some added challenge because said Hero managed to get an Anathema in before losing badly). He was a kid that my PC had bullied, who grew up and started dating my PC's younger sister. As part of his Life, my PC was still part of his biological family, and they're important to him (and even kicked off a lot of his Legend for that matter), but he kept them at a distance so his crazy life with his found family of monsters wouldn't get them all killed. But his younger sister wanted to come up-state, hang out with her bro with her new boyfriend, and my PC has no reason to say no.

      Turned out, that bullying came back in a bad way. A completely unrelated supernatural thing was happening in my PC's old home town (like I said, plot introduction), and this kid was convinced my PC's whole family was responsible, but only he realized "how bad" they all were because of some of the things my PC had done to him (NB: don't skimp out on questionnaires even for splats without Integrity). But he interpreted the younger sister as rebelling against her family (not really, but she dressed different, did random things to her hair, etc instead of looking "too" straight laced like the rest) and he thought he could save her. He had to prove how evil the family was to her though, so he targeted my PC because he was isolated from the rest (and had no idea about Beasts, Broods, or Kinship with a pack of werewolves). So the young couple went upstate to visit her big bro, and hang out, and get a tattoo behind her dad's back because she was still living at home but was 18, and the Hero was secretly using some half-baked occult text to try some sort of ritual to "contain" the "evil" in my PC to rescue his sister. The Anathema placement did work, but my PC also KOed the poor kid in one shot.

      But the reason why that stuck around as effective, was how much it undermined my PC's whole idea of what he was doing as working out. It made him feel complacent and lazy instead of a badass ruling over his corner of the world with a supernatural fist. What he thought he was protecting he was putting in danger. All of this out of some punk that never stood a chance.

      2) And building on that point, when it comes to turning bland into memorable, the key things to Heroes is ruthless ego. Maybe they're born with it, maybe they had to embrace it to survive, but they have to believe they're the Hero. They have to believe everything they do is for the greater good, and that them winning is the greater good. My PC's sister didn't need saving from anyone but her Hero boyfriend, but he was convinced she needed saving because saving her would prove he was right and could beat back evil in the world, and show everyone that he could make things better if they'd just listen to him.

      It's not enough to just want revenge. Lots of people want revenge. Lots of people get revenge in some fashion, but most people don't turn revenge into their life. Figure out what sparked that drive to be willing to do anything, and anything at all, to win; however winning appears to the Hero's perspective. Being a police officer that's figured out that some local figures (who happen to be Begotten) are making crime worse doesn't make the cop a Hero. Where's the spark of ego? What makes him need to fight crime so hard that he's going to risk losing his badge to get "real" justice? What makes him think he's so right that he's going to take it into his own hands and break the law he says he's fighting for in the first place?

      The answers don't have to be complicated. They don't have to be simple. They can make the players feel sympathetic, disgusted, or conflicted about having to deal with the Hero in the way such conflicts tend to go. But you need answers because that's what separates a Hero from a Hunter without a Cell.

      I'm not sure how familiar you are with Thanos as a character in the comics, but comics!Thanos is.. kinda bland. He's a supoewered alien that conquers and kills, for some pretty strange reasons that don't really make sense for him to cling to no matter how many times he loses. Sure there's some interesting backstory, and he has motivations, but he rarely comes off as having conviction instead of just being petty and obsessed. MCU!Thanos from the movies (to mixed reception) has a plan that makes very little sense, but his pure unbridled conviction that he's right and that he's doing good, is surprisingly effective for setting up scenes that stick with the audience like how he gets the Soul Stone, or when the heroes come for him after he wins in the original time-line. There are a ton of great moments born out of understanding that Thanos is the most interesting not when he's got the best plans, or the most power, but when he's wiling to do anything to win and proves it for the audience.

      We're not replicating giant movie budgets with some of the best actors of our time in our TTRPG sessions, but we can take the storytelling lesson. Thanos wasn't so effective as a narrative device in the films because he won the first time, but because of everything he did to win. Thor could have beat him the first time, and pain and ruin Thanos spread in his wake still would have hit just as hard. It hit that hard even though we know there was another movie coming and the superheroe were going to get a second chance to make things right and win because that's how that genre works.

              OK. So, what matters is what the players see. It might be useful to us, as STs, to create a lot of details for NPCs because we have to bring them to life, but details that the players never see don't make the NPC shine. You can take super generic NPCs and make them fun by using them in a unique way.

              Sorry for going back to the movie references here, but in the first John Wick movie there's a character named Jimmy. Jimmy is a generic cops on the take. We only know his name and what he looks like. We don't know how much he gets paid to look the other way, he don't know why he agreed to it, or how it went down. We don't know anything about him. But he's perfect for his one scene, because we learn all we need to learn: we're told how far reaching the criminal influence is in the setting, we know that John's not just feared but well liked and respected because he treats people well and offers respect. While the action is the big draw for the John Wick movies, they're also a great demonstration of a minimalist approach to exposition and character exploration of everyone besides the core characters of each film.

              Generic characters aren't bad. Generic scenes are bad. A generic character in a well done scene is going to feel special anyway.

              What I'm trying to get at, is don't worry about trying to make less generic antagonists. Think about how the antagonist is going to enable good scenes. NPCs are plot devices first. When you combine the generic plot device with the specifics relevant to what the players are bringing to the table, you'll get characters out of the NPCs.

                  I am very fond of ArcaneArts' work on the Forsworn - her version of Heroes that are built around pathologically rejecting a specific fear. As every Beast belongs to a Family representing a specific fear, they all have a counterpart Forsworn who is naturally set against them. This approach works well because it gives Heroes something to do even when there are no Begotten around. Here's an example I posted before:

                  ​
                  Originally posted by Teatime
                  An Enlightened Forsworn has a rabid hatred for all who do their monstrous work in secrecy, for the affront it causes for people's right to know. Naturally, that covers the Eshmaki and miscellaneous monsters, but also mundane criminals who use information warfare to hide their tracks. However, that Hero can't find it in him to get angry at Beasts who are upfront about what they are. So, when facing with a Namtaru who manifests as a Horror to spread unpleasant revelations, the Hero finds himself approving.
                  There is also a flipside example. An Enlightened Forsworn who convinces themselves they're all-knowing in their area of influence. A logistics specialist who knows all the best routes, a video essayist with all the answers, a parent who already knows their children through and through. They don't fear the unknown, because all that matters is known to them. They can be quite helpful and useful to be around until the moment they're plainly shown to be wrong. That's when they fly off their handle and become a threat - be it physical or social.
                  ​
                  As Heavy Arms said earlier, the best antagonists are built towards the protagonists. Rather than starting with a Hero, start with a Beast. Figure out what effect they have on their surroundings, and only then figure out what Hero would be personally offended by that. You can create a varied collection of Heroes doing their own thing in the background, and once your PCs start making waves, most of them will start paying attention, but only one of them will get obsssesed.

                          So, first, a minor point on word choice: I really don't think you want to use consanguineous in this context. In English, for what it sounds like you're intending, you just mean "blood relatives." That are, people who he's related to by blood, rather than marriage, or people who are as close as family but are not blood or legal relatives. Consanguineous implies there's some in-breeding in the family tree that's relevant.

                          Second... more about who this man is as a Beast would be very useful. What are his Legend and Lair Traits? Does he have any current Aspirations relative towards his Beast side? What level of Satiety does he prefer? Does he have a Brood or significant Kinship bonds? Is there a local Apex skewing any part of the supernatural side of his life? What made him such a great candidate for being an Anakim Collector? It explains his choice in feeding habits just fine,but there's nothing about him that helps us understand why he's that instead of, say, an Eshmaki Nemesis that feeds by being a street vigilante in the gang-run part of town nearby.

                          It's difficult to suggest less generic things if half of the character is left too vague. That lack of definition forces ideas to be generic to make sure they work with all the stuff that isn't explained, or specific ideas are going to be giant guesses as to how relevant they'll really be.

                                  I can work with this. I think it will be most helpful if we treat your Anakim as thematically associated with poverty. Both him and his victims (and likely his victims' victims) had their lives shaped by it. It is also an unstoppable force, which further plays into him being an Anakim.

                                  Consider a Hero who pathologically rejects fear of poverty. I imagine someone defined by hustle culture, constantly taking on new jobs, both legal and illegal, convinced that fame and fortune are within reach. The Beast would represent all that he sees as wrong in the world, and he would be compelled to oppose him on principle. He may even be a burglar, which would make his enmity pragmatic in addition to emotional. He would pose a threat as an incredibly resourceful man with contacts in both the polite community and the local underworld.

                                  An opposite kind of Hero could refuse to admit that there is a problem. Likely affluent, she believes the poor are always at fault for their state and deserve to be destitute. The alternative so to acknowledge that they're all at mercy of circumstance, and that she herself could have turned out the same way. She would see the Beast both as moral corruption incarnate, but also resent him because of his attempts to uplift himself and those around him. These judgements are logically contradictory, but consistent in that they condemn him, and that's enough for her. She would pose a threat as an unfair and judgmental woman through her resources and having an ear of local politicians.

                                  So, here we have two Heroes. Please, notice how the paralell each other:

                                  One is a victim of cirscumstance, just like the Anakim. He opposes the Beast because of the latter's Hunger, and because he could have been one of Anakim's victims. He also gets down and dirty to personally accomplish his goals.
                                  Another reinforces the circumstances that shaped both the first Hero and the Anakim. She opposes him because of his higher nature, and could even be argued to be his oppressor. She uses resources and proxies to keep her hands clean.
                                  ​
                                  Whenever you invent a Hero, try to disassemble them into constituent concepts, and then replace them with an opposite. Another axes you can work with is having the Hero be Rational/Irrational, Physical/Mental/Social, Correctly/Wrongly assessing the Beast, and so on...​ You can even consider the Beast's friends and family and potential Heroes (like the sister or the niece).
