    Originally posted by nofather View Post
    Good thing the game doesn't, then.

    Because if you are starving, your Soul will go out and get a meal on its own, and its new Satiety will be determined by a die roll.
    Yes, which means by not eating, you are choosing let the monster that is inside of you ravage the dreamscape/personal souls of those humans a fund yu, amd also possibly create a hero bent on destroying you.

    Hunger does drive your psychology in real life. Human culture has entire portions of itself driven by it, from fasting and taboo foods in religions to just day to day social influence. Starvation and satiety both trigger neurological changes that shape the thought process of the person undergoing them.

    This game has the hunger be due to a primordial soul that is a manifestation of humans darkest fears. If that doesn't effect the psyche of a something who has human thought and emotion, especially when your hunger is for the emotions of fear, powerlessness, loss, vengence and hopelessness... then that thing is likely not very human anymore. Sure, these hungers could all be sated with non-physical or lasting means (turn in a their, don't kill him, as a means of punishment) but you are still inflicting psychological harm by targeting the most basic fears of humanity.

    The fact that you once considered yourself one of them, even if you never truly were one, means that you will still feel the tugging of your psyche saying what you do is wrong, along with (if you so choose) your efforts to prove you are more than a monster, means that you will face the effort of combating giving in to your hunger. At the same time, that growing hunger will put you in further conflict with that primordial soul, as it's hunger (and thus your own) grows. Eventually, you either choose to give in or you allow it to rampage and attack the Oneiros of the humans around you to sate it's hunger. And while that may sound preferable, rhe fact that you are letting a monster ravage the personal soul of everyone around you and possibly create someone whose soul purpose is to kill you... seems like it might not be a good call either.

    And if your players choose to ignore the influence of the hunger, that is their perogative. They can let their PC starve. But it simply means they arent really acknowledging the core concept, nor does it mean they are saints who don't allow harm to come to humanity (see the "Beasts ravaged the Astral" above). The fact that actual conditions exist at each stage to reflect your hunger means that players ignoring these means missing out on Beats and traits that exist within their PC.

    If your players decide instead to give up their humanity and not care about the harm they do, then that is all good. This game doesn't punish you for morality failings, at least internally. Beasts know that they are monsters, and their cruelty and dark acts are to them the same as a lion chasing down a gazelle: it is what happens, no matter how bad you feel for the gazelle. The punishment isnt internal, as they likely are more in line with their Soul, and I would argue that this is a far more accurate way to play a PC, based on the way this game is described (as an aside, for all those who hate the fact that this game makes the protagonists at best y grey and on average monsterous entities... I always got the feeling that was the point: play as the bad guy, a la Dungeon Keeper, or at least someone with horribly dark tendencies, a la V or Alan Moore's Batman). There are other dangers, such as the beast subsume ng any trace of your humanity, but this is much more in line then playing the game as the "Beast who never hungers".

    So yes, you can just let it hit zero, and roll. But that is ignoring both the in game consequences of the choice to do so and the fact that it means players are forgoing XP and playing their character.

                                            There's a lot of vitriol and snark to my two posts. I'll give people the benefit of the doubt and just assume it's due to a 190+ thread and the general back-and-forth from each side's inability to see the other's point (as is usual).

                                            As I said..many times...I don't have a problem with the dark nature of the game, and I generally enjoy playing horrible, dark characters. The root cause to all the general problems (other than mechanics and crossover issues) is the dissonance/conflicting themes in the book

                                            So, since I have seemed to fail getting my original thoughts across with the original posts, I'll just steal Rand Brittain's post from a different forum, since Rand is far more eloquence I could ever hope to be.

                                            If this is against the rules, or has already been posted, I'm sorry in advance.


                                            ------------------
                                            Rand Brittain
                                            Okay, I was linked to a movie review that helps me really express where Beast's problems stem from.

                                            The first two problems are ultimately intertwined. The first problem is that Beasts are incredibly unsympathetic. This exacerbates the second problem, which is that the text and subtext of the game are decoupled.

                                            I think the writers of Beast would agree that it's a darker game even than Requiem. Vampires prey on humans, but they're still ultimately human in psyche and a large part of their condition revolves around how they try to justify their new relation to humanity. Beasts don't come across as human. From the Introduction onward, Primordial revels in the inhuman nature of the Children, and each splat description gives loving attention to numerous examples of their sadism and lack of empathy. (There are positive examples, but they don't really stick out when they're sharing space with "Abusive Scorpion Principal" and "I Invented Some Crimes So I Could Drown You For Committing Them.")

                                            Beasts are just not likeable. They are nastier than Kindred, nastier than Seers, nastier than anything in the game line that I can think of. They even come across as worse than Slashers, because Slashers weren't in a book that took their pretensions seriously. Of course, it's not exactly wrong for a horror game's protagonists to be nasty, but this is the first time a game has tried to clear a hurdle so viscerally awful.

                                            (I think part of this is the result of how Beasts tend to commit literal acts of abuse where other games couch their monsters in metaphor: a vampire's Kiss can stand in for sexual, physical, or financial predation, but it doesn't actually require anybody to act out the sources of real-life trauma at the gaming table.)

                                            How did this happen? I think it derives from, while also exacerbating, the second problem: the game's text and subtext are decoupled.

                                            The relationship between text and subtext is complicated because a game's subtext tends to be layered and include multiple metaphors that sometimes contradict rather than a single straightforward allegory. Vampires, for instance, resonate with toxic relationships, addiction, and a number of other things.

                                            Requiem, of course, has a huge body of art and culture to draw on in establishing the subtext of what vampires can stand on. Primordial didn't have that kind of shoulders to stand on, and it suffers, because the subtext of the game doesn't grow organically from the text.

                                            On a text level, Beasts are children of nightmare who sate their hunger by tormenting humanity. On a subtext level, they stand for such things as outcasts and people who generally don't fit into society's prescribed roles and are looking for a home. There's a clear intended link between the Children and LBGT persons or those with nonbinary gender identity.

                                            This is where the book starts to fall apart, because while there is a link between the socially noncompliant and the monster, it's not strong enough to bridge the gap between "sympathetic outsider" and "sadistic beast." Some readers have been willing to suspend their disbelief and leap over the gap, but it's very clear that a large number have not been willing.

                                            This, I think, is key. The portion of the audience that has reacted negatively to Primordial hasn't failed to see the subtext in the book; it has rejected that subtext. The book is written under the assumption that readers will accept the sympathetic subtext and judge its protagonists in that light, but because the presentation of those protagonists is so jarring (honestly, reading the book makes me feel dirty), they are unwilling to do so. When viewed without that subtext, the Children are... well, they're pretty much just a bunch of gribbly serial killers with an overinflated sense of their own specialness.

                                            This leads to a related problem, the problem of Heroes. In the light of what I've already said, the problem seems pretty obvious. Heroes are, subtextually, the bigots and reactionaries who persecute the outcasts as represented by Beasts. They even have examples identifying Heroes with notable geek unlovelies, mens' rights activists.

                                            Unfortunately, on a textual level, Heroes and Beasts are nearly identical, except that Beasts are worse. Beasts torment, stalk, and kill humans; Heroes do the same thing, but only to Beasts, who they see as monsters responsible for their problems. (They generally aren't very far wrong.)

                                            When you view Heroes in light of the intended subtext, everything snaps into place. Readers who have rejected that subtext, will wonder why the book hates Heroes so much when they're undeniably "the good guys"?

                                            The book does hate Heroes. You can almost see the drops of spittle staining the page whenever it mentions them. The authorial voice is on a very high horse about the superiority of Beasts to small-minded Heroes. Since plenty of readers long ago arrived at the conclusion that chopping off a Beast's head is probably the best way to handle one, this attitude is jarring at best. As it stands, Heroes add very little value to the game.

                                            In Conclusion:

                                            How can Primordial's problems be fixed?

                                            Sadly, there's no easy patch. The first step would have to be a careful going-over of the text to increase its consistency and to sell the reader more effectively on the Children as protagonists.

                                            The book also needs to work on the link between the Children as monsters and the Children as outcasts. As it stands, Beasts are too powerful, too unconcerned with other peoples' feelings, and too socially connected to make the metaphor connect.

                                            Finally, to be blunt, Primordial needs to jettison its hate-boner for Heroes. A protagonist derives status from the power and allure of its enemies; Beasts derive no status at all from their implacable straw foes. If the writers aren't willing to ascribe depth to MRAs or high school bullies, then the connection between those things and Heroes needs to be dropped like a rock.

                                            (I would honestly be pretty okay with a game where villainous Beast protagonists work against noble Hero antagonists; certainly there are plenty of stories around where the antagonist is "the good guy" by normal standards.)

                                            Fixing these problems is going to require more work than Onyx Path is going to be happy about doing, but I think that doing so now (rather than waiting for Beast 2e in 2019) will help make the game into something that reflects well on the second phase of the nWoD.

                                            The responses we've seen from the readers who were able to find something to identify with the game show that there's definitely a gem in there somewhere, and it would be a shame if half the audience were driven away because nobody polished all the blood off.
