        Originally posted by Tymeaus Jalynsfein View Post
        No doubt... I agree that physics often gets in the way of RPG games (your examples are highly entertaining, Krobeles), due to a lot of various reasons. However, I have found that often people's experiences add to the enjoyment of a game. Equations notwithstanding, of course.

        From my experience in Mage (Ascension at least - never played Awakening) it often was the case that the person with the best underlying understanding of physics and science ended up being the best mage overall. Not because he argued for the most accurate understanding of Science (which we stomped on relentlessly and with utter and absolute prejudice), but because he UNDERSTOOD the rules which he was trying to break with Magic (or apply with Magic as was often the case).
        I've found this to be the case as well, and it's something I love about playing magic users in general and Mage in specific. (I am That Guy for my group, at least when I'm not the one running the game.) Understanding the mechanics of what you're attempting to do is always going to give you an edge, whether it's simple leverage or employing the Principle of Similarity and some runes.

        Originally posted by Tymeaus Jalynsfein View Post
        Fact is... "Shadow" and "Night" are no different, they are the absence of light due to the light being blocked (from one thing or another). Metaphysically you might argue they are different, but in reality, that is simply semantics. Wanna Jump all over the place in the Night (with line of sight)? Knock yourself out. It won't really make all that much difference, really. Definitely not as disruptive as many Mage abilities, in my opinion. *shrug*
        Shadow and night are no different in the sense of the physical mechanics of why there is no light, but the semantics matter here. My point wasn't mainly that there's some kind of metaphysical delineation between the two but that the person who actually wrote the power almost certainly did not intend for it to be interpreted in a way that treats night as a gigantic shadow because if they had it would SAY so. Not because there's some kind of magic logic involved, but because otherwise the first time someone tries to teleport to the moon with it you get this entire thread worth of arguing.

        What I was trying to illustrate (badly, it seems) with the vampire example was that you're very rarely going to see someone in the Requiem forums arguing that moonlight should be fatal to vampires in the RAW because it says indirect sunlight causes harm and moonlight is literally nothing indirect sunlight. The reason you don't see that argument popping up all the time is that it's understood that because the author didn't explicitly include moonlight as an example and it's treated as a separate thing in casual language it is it is meant to be treated as a separate thing. So it is with night vs. shadow. Physically they are 'the absence of light created by an opaque object obstructing its path' but you don't have people arguing that an overcast day and twilight are the same thing.

        If you want a metaphysical justification... heck, there's a lot of them. The simplest one is just that where Science is one way of labeling and analyzing the world, Magic is another and in the WoD when they conflict it's generally the magical answer that is the correct one for most purposes. In Mage sunlight and light are treated as such distinct things that it requires a substantially higher Forces rating to manipulate sunlight, which is patently absurd from a physics standpoint. (As is the fact that it is easier to convert a hamster into a machine gun than it is to turn lead into gold.) A vampire who would burn to ashes in seconds simply because they were on the far side of the room when someone opened the blinds can go for a moonlit stroll with no discomfort at all.


        For game purposes, my personal ruling for what constitutes a 'shadow' is going to be a patch of darkness that is visibly distinct from the surrounding light level on the relevant surface. That is, if someone who isn't being a pedant would look at it and consider it a shadow as opposed to just generally dark, it'll count.
