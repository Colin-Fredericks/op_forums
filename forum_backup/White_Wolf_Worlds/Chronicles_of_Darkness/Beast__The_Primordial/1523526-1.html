                        Well, since I'm in it now, let's address a few things.

                        Originally posted by Psicose View Post
                        but I think you know the bad reputation that BTP received, there are people who hate this title like hell, excume me the expression. .​
                        I am quite aware of it, thank you. And here's my response:

                        So?

                        Lots of people hated Chronicles when it was created, but it clearly didn't become what it is now by listening to those people. Lots of people thought Requiem was the worst thing to have ever been created, does that mean that Rose Bailey should have made Requiem to the request of those haters? Lots of people hated that Curse had their characters come from a fixed location and were basically ensured to be non-white characters, does that mean C.A. Sulieman should have instead defocused and watered down Curse just so people could have their Mayan mummies? Lots of people hated the tech in techgnostic espionage for Descent, does that mean that Rose Bailey should have weakened her unique take and laid out another fucking boring Christian-inspired take?

                        Here's the thing: people who hate things aren't equipped to provide good direction on how to improve a work by virtue of hating the thing. We've seen a lot of takes on how to "improve" Beast over the years, and to be quite frank, every single one that came in from someone who hated the game and had a cocksure answer for how to fix have quite frankly been worse ideas, from having terrible mechanical operations to being incredibly boring. These people, motivated by heightened emotion and arrogance, often just act instead of taking the time to really dig into and gain an understanding of what actually needs working, and instead just chase after fixes that are superficially shiny but substantially anemic.

                        And in the end, what does trying to appeal to people who hate the game do? Realistically, they're still gonna hate the game, just because it is. The nature of the argument might change, but honestly there will always be something they can grab as a reason to hate it.

                        Meanwhile, Beast has an audience, it has a fanbase. These are people who can see and appreciate the flaws and the way those problems work, but they can also hone in on what works about it to, and can reemphasize those point and build from them to strengthen the game all the better, and from there glean answers to solve those problems. Why the fuck would I want to appeal to haters when I can take greater insight from the people who actually care about the game and use that to create a strong base of evangelism for the game?

                        If it was possible to turn around opinion on Beast, it's not by catering to the whims and conceits of it's haters-it's simply by working to make Beast a better game, by making it the best version of itself.

                        All of this is moot, because with Paradox doing what they're doing, even if we released the most stellar stealth 1.5 version of Beast ever, the lack of promotion means that for most people, Beast is going to forever and always be that game that blows monkey chunks. So, again, why bother appealing to them when I can write for the fanbase that exists? Anyone who wants to give it a chance can easily find it.

                        So, yeah. I can gain insight from the haters, but design the game for them? That's just a waste of everybody's time.
                        Going on the subject of Hunger, the actual problems that stand with it is that it's easy to have normal Satiation be overshadowed by employment with Family Dinner. Part of this problem is that there's not enough demonstration on what can be done for Hungers (which also addresses the things people get squeamish about) and in being clear about high Satiation being the premise of story (-arcs). There's a couple of ways you can actually deal with the problem, mostly down presentation-having the Hunger sections actually get into what's going and can be done with them is a big one (I have a format floating on my drive that I reckon handily covers some of the differentiation, examining Hunger through the lens of To Take and Destroy [since the universal is to strip away the security devices, and the natural followup is to remove it forever], To Keep and Build [because Beasts have their own ambitions and ideals, their satiations acting as stepping stones for them], and To Return and Gift [because once the Satiation is done, a Beast doesn't have to keep their victims suffering from the loss-a decently connected Predator can even restore health and wholeness-and they can also take the subjects of their Satiations and bestow them to people who they find deserving of it), you can discuss Satiation in the storytelling section and provide examples of how to build stories around Hunger throughout the book, maybe even incorporate a subsystem if you feel insecure about it, and you can even build in reasons for intentionality to Satiation into mechanics-the Forswearing mechanic for my take on Heroes is rooted in providing incentives for Beasts to find ways to have their Satiation provide catharsis for the victims, as examples-you can re-emphasize that monsters can also be the subject of Satiation, and you could also just straight up have incentives built into Satiating Hunger (I made a pitch for something like that when the BPG was beginning development, and it was the only idea that didn't make it into the book, so there may be flaws with that idea, but still).

                        Chronicles very frequently has players commit to the sort of actions that make up Hungers. Beast's problem on the subject is not that Hungers are harsh as they are, or even as real as they are, it's that there wasn't enough focus on what can be done with it and about it.
                        ​
                        Originally posted by ShadowcatX View Post

                        I can play a Forsaken without being uncomfortable. I can play a Kindred without being uncomfortable. The players being uncomfortable playing their characters is a problem, characters in game being uncomfortable is different. I think this is rewritten for the first case.
                        See, this hits on something kind of important, because my instant reaction reading this first time was "I would probably chalk that up to either you screwing up or your Storyteller screwing up."

                        That's rude on my part, and more importantly limited (I'll get to that in a second), but on the anecdotal side of things, I have been uncomfortable playing a werewolf and a vampire (dear lord, have I been disturbed by playing a vampire). From one perspective, these characters are monsters, and what they do to get by is unnerving-that's the point of personal horror. I've made choices in regards to the execution of violence as an Uratha that made me stare in horror at the consequences. I've had characters strip human people to the bone of their free will and agency because it was convenient for my vampire, and it was deeply unsettling.

                        But, like, if I stuck to that as my response, I would also be a hypocrite (and very particularly, in a way I would actually have a problem with), because I've also had slice-of-life games of Requiem where the biggest threat was we lost the Prince's dog and had to scramble to find the little scamp before the night was up. I've had games of Mage where we've "infiltrated" a Mormon temple and had one player have a nightmare by getting caught up in doing their ritual of baptisms for the dead because they just plain sucked at social scenarios. I've had games of Hunter where an extended sequence was almost like something out of Scooby Doo as a paranoid player was getting jerked around by spooky seeming but harmless elements of the scene.

                        Players have a latitutde that game designers often don't, because you can play the game how you want, but game designers usually need to have and keep to a strong design focus to make their products snap. Players can play Chronicles as a horror game, an urban fantasy, a slice of life iyashikei, action-comedy, or what have you, and I've seen it be done.

                        But games don't always have that flexibility, and no game can be for all people. Going out of house for a second, Bluebeard's Bride is a pretty tightly designed game focused on feminine horror and doesn't have a lot of latitude for doing anything but that. There are a lot of people who don't like or can't play Bluebeard's Bride because it's horror hits really close to home for them. There are also a lot of people who love the game because it does exactly that. That it hits so close on intimately painful topics is not a reason to not buy Bluebeard's Bride, it just means that it's not for everyone.

                        Beast could have definitely benefited from dedicated sensitivity readers and having a developer who could actually pay attention when their writer's raise concerns about the material instead of juggling, like, four or five development projects on top of their day job, but suggesting that Tyrants feeding by popping someone's arm out of it's socket is too much when I've been unnerved by my Storyteller's making clear the pain and loss of power a NPC is feeling when my Cahalith popped their arm out of their socket is nonsensical. It IS important to understand that that makes it not a game that's going to be for everyone (I've frequently made the point that even a well-made Beast is probably a niche product), and it IS important to be mindful about how subjects are discussed-handling the mental health and wellbeing of readers and players is a paramount subject for any horror game-but it's also important to remember that some people pick up a game about personal horror expecting to experience some amount of personal horror, and while the execution of Hungers is very flawed, it's base idea sits soundly in that place.

                        Not everyone is going be comfortable with the fact that Beasts don't have a fictional sheen over their violence. A lot of people find it very par for the course with Chronicles of Darkness. Both are fine reactions to have.

                                        Originally posted by Psicose View Post
                                        This will seem strange, but what would you think if BTP's theme was about ambition? I mean, in this case the horrors are monsters that despite being nightmares are attracted by the ambition of their human part, whether this ambition is repressed or not, in this case they are very extreme desires, it would be in this game of balance between the morality of the human part or unbridled desire for horror. Does it seem too forced?
                                        I've said before, that in the reductive way to think of games way of analogizing them to singular aspects of human life, the same way some people will say Created is a game about childhood and Curse is a game about old age, that Beast would pretty clearly be the game about celebrity, that they are myth and folklore and creepy pasta given flesh, heart and mind, that it's a game about becoming memetic in that way that haunted houses and death turns and hurricanes and earthquakes do, monstrosity gone viral, and I have taken that read so far as to say that my personal theme for Beast on the whole is The Monster by Eminem, so no, I wouldn't say it's too forced.

                                        That said, Beast is, in a game about monsters, is a crossover template, and by way of being able to relate to any and all of the other splats, ended up in a place where it must reflect and contrast them, and has opted for the route of deconstruction, acting as a mirror that strips the circumstances that makes other monsters defensible (to be crass about it, strips the bullshit from the subject) and shows who they are and what they do in raw terms-a useful if uncomfortable role to play in pairing with any other sort of character.

                                        Part of the problem for Beasts that comes with that is that, by also scraping all the circumstance that makes other monsters defensible, not having all the reasons that it's not their fault they're like that, is that it means each and every Beast is personally accountable for what they do with their Hunger-it's on them for whatever they do. They don't get to blame the Curses or Luna or their Obsessions or Torment or the Strangers or their Geist or the Judges or the God-Machine or the Conspiracies, or even monstrosity itself, for what they do. And because of that, it's on them to put in the time, energy, effort, and thought in just what they are going to bend their Hunger toward, who they will be in the community and why that will be the case-and how they'll sustain it against extensive scrutiny.

                                        After all, for every haunted house left to stand, there are just as many that get bulldozed and paved over.

                                        Celebrity, ambition-these are words for social accountability, at the end of the day-these are words that describe character, in a game that has the other half of it's makeup in a form of storytelling notorious for it's maxim of "character-as-fate." To steal once again from XKCD, Beast is a game about how the thing standing in the way of your dreams is that the one having them is you. Beast is a game where you have made peace with the fact that you are a monster, and what you're going to do to defy the obvious thing we do to monsters.

                                        So, as it relates to the larger context of the conversation, why would anyone want to alleviate the demands Hunger places on their title characters? Deciding what you're going to do with your Hunger, if you're going to try and find a way to be productive and of benefit to your community-if you can dare to dream that's even possible-perhaps taking it too far in one way and creating a draconian slice of reality that "acts on their best behavior" under your violent present, or taking it the other way and abandoning concern for others and serving yourself up a healthy slice of whatever-the-fuck-you-want and relying on being able swat away any challenger-that struggle is already hella strong with Hunger as is. Weakening Hunger diminishes it-the weaker the Hunger is, the less you owe to yourself in defining who you are in relationship to your community.

                                        That there are some people who refuse to see anyway that Beasts can be a part of their community, healthy, supportive, and productive even-well, Heroes aren't the good guys for a reason.

                                        The most hilarious part of Beast is honestly the parallels between that text and the many people who think they can fix Beast with their hatred of it.

                                        EDIT: Also, while Beast is not so gauche as to outright say it, it's also funny how many people don't pick up that most Beasts aren't supposed to ultimately succeed. Things to be clear about in the storytelling chapter of the now absolutely-never-going-to-happen Second Edition of Beast, thanks Paradox.

                                                    My thoughts is maybe they're a psychic vampire version of the kindred. In order to feed they have to get a human to spend willpower. This could be done in a variety of methods, if the beast arranged something scary or terrible to happen to a mortal it might force the mortal to spend a willpower point, but it could also encourage them and motivate them to spend will power through positive actions, though I suspect that would be harder.

                                                    I'm not sure if it would work to keep the same 5 theme that runs through CofD, but I also don't think it would be the worst if it was reduced to 3 types of beasts, a physical, a social, and a mental, and maybe mortals spending a willpower on a skill of the beast's prefered tupe counted double. If you wanted to keep 5 families I suppose you could focus them on smaller groups of skills.

                                                    ​​​​​​Reducing the number of options available to beasts (ie. From 5 families and 5 hungers down to 1 of 3 skill sets) would probably make for a worse beast only game, I don't know that it would necessarily be a problem in a crossover campaign (or for Beast NPCs).
                                                    It kind of makes sense, it's really not a bad idea, but I'm already having trouble separating Beast from vampires in my head for various reasons, so I just think that in part they should have some identity of their own.

                                                        Arc is referencing Heroes, as mentioned in the line preceding "that text." Heroes as an antagonist category don't believe Beasts can or should exist as they do (even if the worst thing they do is be the focus of a recurring nightmare), often giving stated reasons for feeling this way that have nothing to do with the Begotten's activities or even reality in general, and most of the more prominent proposed "fixes" on this board have come from people with an avowed dislike of the game based on misunderstandings of the game's text and overall aims. She is describing a course of action that does not make sense in parallel with a character type whose actions do not have a consistent and coherent rationale behind it.

                                                        Given appropriate distancing from the inhuman nature of their abilities, to draw analogy to more common fantasy fare, Beasts' assigned cosmological role runs the gamut from bandit king to demon lord, and by the sensibilities of contemporary fiction we tend to look askance at heroes who think bandits should all die for living in the woods and stealing to eat.

                                                        To bring it back around to the topic, having been born under the banner of the Blue Manticore and needing to enact Ruin to at least some small degree to live a fulfilling life on one's own (or lend their aid to another fatefully compatible monster) is an unconscious decision the character makes at the Devouring because the Hunger of their Horror is important to them as much as the fear their Family relates to.

                                                        Horror-mystery setting that the Chronicles of Darkness is, the supernatural power derived from the fear of the unknown and revelatory shock is narratively relevant; feeding outside of Inflicting Nightmares doesn't drain Willpower or even necessarily inflict a breaking point, but the scale of a feeding roll is a combined function of dramatic investment and appropriateness to the Beast's nature and destiny. Hunger isn't about habits, it's about desire, and specifically interpersonal desires where something is gained at the expense of someone else.
