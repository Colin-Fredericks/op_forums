          Well, this seems like as good a place as any to really dig into some of the thoughts I have on Beasts and ethics. A lot of this has been on my mind since first reading the original draft, but recent topics have inspired me to try to articulate it for other people.

          Rather than try to talk for the splat as a whole I mostly want to explore this through the lens of one specific character, but let’s start with the one general thing I want to say. A Beast has two parts, Life and Legend, which is to say the Person and the Horror. The Person does NOT have to agree with the Horror. They don’t have to like the Horror, but they have to live with it. One place I think the writers really could have sold the premise better is in presenting more and stronger examples of people in conflict with their Horrors. I get why they didn’t, word count is limited and the Hunger examples need to be quick blurbs that demonstrate how visceral they are. It’s hard to fit resistance into that space. It did, unfortunately, lead to a lot of people coming to the conclusion that Beasts are universally unrepentant abusers. That’s not my reading and I don’t think that was the intent, but I’ll get to that.

          When I say I think Beasts can be good people, I should probably clarify what I mean. Horrors are without exception monsters that want to do harm, that is their nature. A Horror is at best easily placated. The Person, however, has choices. They can choose how they feed, and look for ways to do so as harmlessly as possible.

          Having said that, I want to talk about Drake Thorn.

          Drake hews much more closely to a Homecoming origin than a Devouring. The former always felt more more right to my personal metaphor. This isn’t something that happened to me, a specific occurrence I can point at and say “yeah that’s where it started” It’s something that was always there on some level. Accepting it gave it a name, and allowed me to start connecting a bunch of seemingly unrelated dots. It let me see that there was a community of people with similar struggles I could talk to for support and advice. I’m no longer fighting the monster that is me alone in the dark.

          That’s how it was in Drake’s nightmare. He was the knight in shining armor, and there was the Dragon. Every time they fought he would mortally wound it, only to suffer the same injuries and awake in a cold sweat. For years he refused to see the obvious connection, stuck between choosing life for himself and death for the monster, until one night it finally clicked and he realized it was all the same. The monster he fought was himself.

          Now here’s the thing: Drake has accepted that the Horror is an irrevocable part of him, but he doesn’t like it and he doesn’t agree with it most of the time. The Dragon is a bully, plain and simple. It demands everyone around it bow down in fear. Is it not mighty? Is it not terrifying? Given free reign it would find a village of mortals to terrorize into servitude. But it doesn’t have free reign. Drake is still in the driver’s seat, and the Dragon is going to have to settle for less. He likes helping people move, there’s plenty of opportunity for shock and awe when he singlehandedly lifts furniture too heavy for two normal people to easily move. It’s not a very filling meal for the Dragon at all, but it’s one Drake’s okay with.

          Drake doesn’t have total control either though, and if he balks too much the Dragon goes feeding on its own and attracts Heroes, so it wasn’t long before he had to cut ties with his family and take up a semi nomadic lifestyle. Of course, that makes it harder to find regular meals that won’t strain his ethics. He’s had to do things he’s not happy with to stay fed, but even when picking fights for a quick meal, Drake fights less to injure his opponent and more to demonstrate that they can’t hurt him. It’s in no way a good action but it’s one of the better of his limited options.

          Eventually Drake finds his way the Changeling Freehold of San Francisco, where he takes his current name and pick ups the surname Thorn. It’s an old tradition, started by the Spring Court, that anybody who can’t remember or no longer feels comfortable with the last name they once had can call themselves a Thorn. It’s a big family, and Drake finds his purpose in defending it from the Gentry and their minions. Whatever else it might be, the Dragon really is a powerful monster, and Drake can channel that power. Huntsmen, Hobgoblins slavers, Loyalists, there are any number of enemies looking for cracks in the Bulwark where they can drag one of the Lost back to Arcadia. Drake is strongly opposed to slavery. The Dragon wants to defeat strong enemies and show everyone how powerful it is and how much they should fear it. So when the Huntsmen come calling, Drake’s purpose and his Horror’s align. And that’s a significant point. The Horror is absolutely fighting for selfish, monstrous reasons. But those reasons don’t invalidate Drake’s decision to put his life at risk defending an adopted family, he would do that whether it fed him or not, and he’s never going to shy away from it in favor of an easier meal. The Gentry have hurt his friends, and will keep doing so, and he’s one of relatively few people who have anything resembling the power to prevent them. He will fight a Fae Lord if it comes to that, and despite the extreme danger there is at least a chance (under Equinox Road guidelines anyway) of pulling a “Puny God” moment, and wouldn’t that be a meal.

          Still, he spends a lot of time wondering if deep down he isn’t just as bad of a monster in the end. He wants to become more powerful to better protect his friends, but what happens when he reaches the top and there are no bigger monsters to risk himself against? He doesn’t want to knock the king bullies off their perch only to assume it himself, he wants to empty the perch for good and true, to break the cycle. And his darker moments, he wonders if it wouldn’t be better for everyone if he let himself be killed in the process. Build his friends up enough, and let them finish what he started, because he’s not up to the responsibility of managing the monster inside him and he never really will be.

          There’s two paths for him to take, and that’s the bad one. The good one is something along the lines of the Totem Beast Inheritance that was proposed some threads ago. Drake ascends to the position of something like a guardian spirt for the community and protects it from other supernatural threats. It has been mentioned that Hungers can change, and what he’s attempting is no less than shifting the Dragon to favor a more community friendly flavor of Power. It still demands respect, but that respect is earned and repaid with protection. If need be he can and will demonstrate his power against threats to his charges, but he can also learn to feed off symbolic offerings, given not out of fear but of respect for his power and trust that it will be used in defense of the community.

          No lessons, just a man trying his best to live by the principal that power should be wielded to help people, not hurt them, and that it’s his responsibility to make the best use of his situation. Beast can be a great game to explore what drives people, you just have to look for the disconnect between man and monster.

                  At the risk of being obvious - Punishment.

                  From the start, this approach allows for a lot of diversity. A Beast could find a way to work fully within constraints of the law, perhaps even as its officer. Further in, they can make plans worthy of Count Monte Cristo, as long as they shy away from committing murder. Such Beasts could easily come out to their human families and even be accepted. Of course, the Horror keeps gnawing at their resolve trying to push them further. At the far end are the more monstrous Beasts - those whose predations end with madness, mutilation or death. Such Beast have a mythological precedent in the Greek Furies. These Monster-Goddesses, while unrepentantly vicious, were highly respected. However, they did play by the rules: they punished acts ancient Greeks denounced as evil, and were one of few sources of justice in a lawless world.

                  Even now, depending on where you look, Beasts' brand of justice can be the only one there is. Things are more complicated in lawful areas. Setting aside the state of their psyches, such characters as Dexter Morgan or Frank Castle, even though their victims arguably had it coming, could have worked within the law, but didn't. Moral high ground is harder to reach now than it was in the Iron Age. Their cases also show the risks of trying to hold to one's Life, or risks of leaving it behind.

                  While playing such characters can be deeply cathartic, care must be taken to show the consequences of such feedings. Even punishing real crimes has consequences that the Beast would rather not have to face. This goes double for crimes that society turns a blind eye to - well meaning Beasts could create martyrs for the wrong causes or even provoke retaliation against victims. Even that assumes that Beasts choose objectively evil acts to punish. Even ordinary human beings have drastically different opinions about what acts are criminal. Becoming a Beast does not necessarily help grow past that.

                      The Social Darwinist attitude is the default of Horrors and Beasts, and it gets pretty boring. Rather like a vampire who's just vampiring it up, there comes a time after the hundredth or so time of feeding that a Beast is probably going to go "Fuck, is this it?". The Teaching Culture(and whatever else this thread comes up with) isn't just a moral fix, it's a way for Beasts to feel like what they're doing matters rather than just rutting around.

                      On Punishment, I gotta say this isn't new-most peoples ideas for lessons and proper eating basically begins and ends with "Do it to those who deserve it."

                      It's been brought up how Beasts have Lives and Legends. Worth noting is that the teaching culture provides an avenue that allows for a balancing of those two. Alternatives don't have to strive for balance, but accordance to one, the other, or both should be kept in mind.

                      The name of the game is "How do I take meaningless feeding and turn it into a meaningful contribution to X?", X being self, mortal society, supernatural society, etc., etc.

                      Let's see what y'all can come up with. This should be interesting.

                              Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                              On Punishment, I gotta say this isn't new-most peoples ideas for lessons and proper eating basically begins and ends with "Do it to those who deserve it."
                              You know, I think you're right. It's fair to say that Punishment is a subset of Lesson-teaching. However, that umbrella term is broad enough to cover very different attitudes - some more alien than the others. Whether we come up with a truly alternate philosophy or with a distinctive approach to Lesson-teaching, we accomplish the same goal: We give players an idea on what can they do, exactly. I think this might be the reason why some people are frustrated with the present approach. It doesn't explain how Lesson-teaching works in practice and how many forms it can take. We have a very general theory and many specific examples, but little on how Beasts connect the two and what motivates them to choose one approach over the other.

                              For example, targeting the deserving is readily understandable and most cases fit under the Lesson-teaching umbrella. However, if the target doesn't survive and the act is done in secret, there is nobody to have learned anything. On the other hand, a Beast may deliberately target innocents, trying to create martyrs. Considering how tragedy can bring people together and prompt changes for the better, a Beast may consider it a fair trade. The period after Kennedy's assassination was far more progressive than the one before it. This definitely fits under Lesson-teaching, while in some respects being the very opposite of Punishment.

                                  During the games games I played two distinct "philosophies" emerged amongst the players. The first one is to do as little harm as possible while feeding. Xhemal - an anakim tyrant - feeds by completely dominating his opponents, by defeating them in a way that shows that they never had a chance to begin with and could just as well forfeit. He is playing soccer and feeds every time he successfully seizes the ball (Cyclopean strength helps). Xhemal is aware that as he ages he will either lose his physical strength (preventing him from feeding this way) or keep it thanks to his supernatural origin (thus alerting anyone that he is supernatural) and won't be able to feed this He is studying law and for now feeds on the professors and students by solving the most difficult questions in the most detailed way possible that should be impossible for someone in the first semester (Thank you Mimir's Wisdom). Once he finishes his studies he'll become a lawyer (not a judge as a judge doesn't have to fight an opponent) and keep on feeding this way.

                                  Of course not everyone can feed in such a "vegetarian" way, and this was especially true for two other characters: David - an Eshmaki predator - feeds on stalking people from the shadows and hunting them down until they believe they're going to die. He doesn't have to kill them but all of them are seriously injured. Connor - an Ugallu ravager - feeds by destroying the very idea that anywhere would be safe. His homecoming happened in Afghanistan where he served as a sniper - and everyone was impressed by his ability to kill people who thought themselves perfectly safe behind cover. As a civilian this ability is much less appreciated. Connor does not have to kill people - a bullet passing over the table of a dining family and impacting in the wall behind them destroys the safety good enough - but he always has to traumatize his victims. Xhemal did try to get them to feed in a paint ball arena but both found the meals there bland and unsatisfying.

                                  Apart from the obvious conflicts within the brood this also gave rise to a second philosophy: Rather than trying to minimize the harm done during feeding they accepted it but they tried to do as much good with the unique abilities they had as beasts so the society as a whole would still be better off. Kind of like one accepts the (undeniably bad) side effects of medications because one is still better off than without. David became a fire-fighter and saves people from burning houses without any concern for his own safety - after all his relentless hunter atavism will take care of any injuries he sustains in no time. He still needs to hunt down and injure people to feed his horror but figures that the people he saves from dying in a fire make up for that.

                                                  Ok, so some ideas for other approaches:

                                                  The Dream Masters -

                                                  Most likely to develop in Beasts that are naturally fairly intellectually inclined, or those that spend a lot of time forming Kinship bonds with others of that bend (esp. mages and changelings), this approach goes beyond "teaching lessons." This philosophy assumes that as Horrors are born of the Primordial Dream, they are driven by Hungers because of the darkness of the world. Dreams are manifestations of the subconscious desires of humanity. They think the Lesson Teachers are simply shallowly responding to a desire from humanity for the world to "make sense" where evil is done unto evil to put humanity back on a brighter path. The Dream Masters want to be more proactive, and want to work towards reshaping the Primordial Dream into something "better" rather than accepting it as fact; feeling that this will eventually change Hungers into less negative expressions of humanity's desires.

                                                  In practice this is rarely a simple matter. The desire to focus on dreams - and Nightmare powers - requires a well fed Beast that means indulging the Hungers as-is. Dream Masters do their best to make their feeding more than just a lesson that humans have a reason to fear the darkness of the world, but to be "tough love" that is more guided towards a healthy respect for the state of the world that hopefully leads to humans wanting a brighter world, and thus the inertia of the Primordial Dream will shift to "better" Hungers.

                                                  The Hyenas

                                                  A more organized group, most Beats come to this approach by recruitment; though many were on their way there without the aid. First and foremost, the Hyenas are practical. Worrying about "why" and mortality is always second to survival, and keeping their Hungers in check. In the wild, hyenas are social animals that have to deal with an apex predator that's also a social animal as competition. No hyena can stand up to a lion, but a single lion can't stand up to a full clan, and even a pride gets nervous against such numbers. The Hyenas see Beasts as existing in the same state. The shadows are full of monsters, and the Apex dominates the local Dream that everyone else has to deal with. Beasts - and their Kin - are all children of the Dark Mother, should be creating families large enough to remind any Apex that they are never invincible.

                                                  Feeding beyond necessity is a matter of tactics; a form of political and social control over the local supernatural landscape with humans as the battleground. "Lessons" aren't the point compared to engineering social norms and/or changes. Every Apex has a pyramid beneath them, and the Hyenas see feeding as a tool to tear that pyramid out from under them if they don't treat the status of Apex as the Hyenas feel it should be, or work to support what they see as a "proper" Apex. If a Hyena manages to take on this mantle... things often get messy since the group is so self-associated with needing to be the masses that keep the powerful in check for their own good.

                                                  The Meal

                                                  To many Beasts, Meals are worse than those that simply aim to be amoral monsters in the shadows. They're fanatics with a dangerous faith, which is often true, though some are rather scary in how aware of what they're doing they really are. The core of this faith is simple: The Dark Mother is ravenous, and her children are not keeping her well fed. Eventually her hunger will drive her children and humanity insane, so the only option is to full sate her so she truly slumbers as a well fed Horror does. They see a simple pattern: their Horror drives them to Hunger, and unless they put their Horror to sleep with a good enough meal, Heroes are inevitable. They don't see Heroes as true enemies - as humans rising up to fight off predators - but as lesser children born to drive Beasts to greater power, so they can feed their Hungers more, which feeds the Dark Mother more.

                                                  The solution is simple. Just as fully sating a Hunger requires refined and thoughtful feeding, the Dark Mother requires more than the trickles of Satiety. She needs bigger meals. She needs monsters to step out of the shadows to terrify whole cities. She needs humanity to rip itself apart from the terror of the truth. This isn't a lesson, but an offering of the greatest levels. The Meal will save humanity by ending the cycle of Beasts and Heroes after an age of horrors that leaves the Dark Mother so full she'll never wake again.

                                                          One idea I had for an alternate goal for Beasts was that they essentially acted as the Monsters in Black.

                                                          What happens when humans interact with the supernatural? They run the risk of losing their minds, losing their souls, losing their lives.
