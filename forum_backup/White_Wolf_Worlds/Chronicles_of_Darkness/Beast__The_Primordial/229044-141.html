    Originally posted by nalak42 View Post

    Apparently there's a point where Beasts reach a point called inheritance. At this junction there are 3 ways a Beast can end up going. Sometimes the Beast ends up overwhelming the human portion and the two are merged into a shadowy nightmare figure haunting the primordial pathways. (The Unfettered) There's retreat which basically causes the Beast to take a chunk out of the person's soul ,since it was actually part of their soul when they were born, but leaves the person behind in a sort of hollowed out state. The third is a mixed state where the Beast and Human merge into a glorious hole more than either essentially the pinnacle of legend and one of the more dangerous beings around. I will point out though that the pdf seems to give the indication that these choices are things that happen at the point of inheritance,so it isn't like one of the begotten can just sever their Beast whenever they want.
    The Retreat and the Merger can be undertaken at any time (but honestly, why would you), while the Incarnation requires Lair 8 and destroying a Hero utterly without taking Lethal Damage or being struck by Anathema (tip: don't go for it with Satiety 5). It's easiest with a lot of planning and a Satiety below 3. It basically should play out like a war of assassins, until all he has left is his bare hands.

                                                        06-03-2015, 09:08 AM
                                                        That there's no easy or definite answer to the Hero-Beast dilemma is genuinely one of the most interesting things in the game, far as I'm concerned.

                                                        Heroes are undoubtedly victims. When confronted with the nightmare that is the Beast, their broken ego latches on to the notion that the Begotten is an absolute monster, and that they alone know the truth. They compose themselves through the idea that it's their responsibility to end the Beast. Why else would they know? Why else would their heart scream so that the Begotten are enemies of mankind? This is more than just responsibility - it's their role. They know because they are meant to destroy the monster. And that means they're the hero, with everything it entitles them to.

                                                        It's not a willful reaction. It's one born of desperation, an unconscious measure on the part of would-be-Heroes to not collapse into themselves. Their already shaken life sublimes into an enemy of Beasts. They are driven by these ideas, husks moved by their assumed righteousness against the one who made them this way. Against this imperative taken to support their existence after a traumatic experience, nothing else matters.

                                                        But that doesn't make them right by default. Every living being in the world takes from elsewhere in order to survive. Humans take not only from the world, but from each other, often not even for survival, but just because it pleases them; to satisfy their desires. Arguably, Beast take more meaningfully and deeply than many humans do, but they at least can say they do it to survive. Of course, this doesn't always make them right by principle either.

                                                        Heroes are irredeemably tragic figures despite their individual misgivings and their universal willingness to walk over everything to destroy the Beast, because they have no real escape from the cycle they are pulled into. But the bane of a Hero, what might antagonize even the noblest of Beasts against them, is that they don't even realize they're all puppets, tangled in strings. A Hero is as much of a slave as the Beast, if not more, to the perpetual narrative of the monster and the savior. But they do not, cannot recognize their position in this system; they make themselves believe it is about them, that they own this story. In the eyes of Beasts, they are ignorant - maybe offensively so, on a deep existential level that any person who truly lives should be able to understand. They accept the cycle and push it forward of their own vollition, in their weakness nothing more than prisoners seeking to drag everyone else down with them, unable to even struggle to be more.

                                                        You can't really fault Beasts for feeling that way. Of course, you can't really fault Heroes for not having a conscious choice in the matter either. But the bottom line is, being a Beast doesn't make one inherently wrong, and being a Hero doesn't make one inherently justified; ultimately, it's understandable that they are enemies. It's hardly a fair situation for anyone involved. And it is understandable the Children could think they have the worst side of the deal and hate their counterparts for their perspective on the Hero Cycle. There is some bliss to be found in ignorance, but self-awareness only makes the inevitable more painful.

                                                        And yet, they would rather know and curse their destiny than mindlessly go along with it.

                                                        Also see David's explanation, which is fantastic and I will second on being a very comprehensive rundown of the impression the book left on Heroes.
