            I don't really think it's a matter of morality. Morality implies agency. It implies free will. I wouldn't consider any of the sample Heroes to be evil before they became Heroes.                     Given this game's reliance on subverting the monomyth, I'm really surprised that the writers didn't emphasize that the difference between a regular person who might have a nuanced reaction to the depredations of the Beasts is whether the mortal in question accepts the "Call to Adventure", which is step two of the heroic cycle.

                    The Hero of the story is an earthly paragon. He’s the light that holds the dark at bay. He takes up the call to adventure, he learns wisdom and sacrifice, he dies and resurrects."

                    On the other hand, the book discredits this on the very next page "...                    the monster that made him a zealot in the first place. "

                        I think that's sort of the point. It's an enforced cycle, despite how much it convinces its prisoners otherwise. It's forced on those suitable to become Heroes, not by design but by accident and inevitability. And it all uwittingly begins with Beasts.

                                                    Heroes remind me of D&D in a way. You can be a murder hobo, going around killing things without a care because they're an "evil race" and still call yourself Good. Nevermind that those evil races have families, economy, mothers that have to raise their kids, very likely have agricultural or hunter/gatherer roots... Its kind of amusing in a way that the evil beings in D&D fall apart when you try to look at them in any depth.

                                                    Its that mentality, I think, that Heroes end up with. They slay the monsters. Why, because they're monsters, no matter who, what, when, where, how, and why the monster actually is. They don't bother looking closely, because why should they?
