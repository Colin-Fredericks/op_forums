    Arcane's Meditation on Heroes from Primordial

    06-11-2015, 05:11 PM
    I should be writing on the Hedge, but screw it, I want to meditate on heroes away from other contexts. Arcane Arts don't care, Arcane Arts do what he wants.

    So, with that said, moving away from the context of Beast the Primordial, what do you think of when you think of the word heroes?

    Well, if you're anything like me*, you probably think of superheroes like Captain America, Spiderman, Thor, Superman, etc., etc. I mean, there are other people you probably think of, but culture is kind of glutted on the subject right now, thanks to the American film industry cashing in on our youth and going somewhere with it. Lots of big epic fight scenes, tough moral choices, triumph and all that stuff.

    But for me, stuck in the corners of the subject, for me, I see some peculiar scenes. A lot of them are from Superman, who isn't my favorite superhero, and they're from the comics, which I pretty much explicitly don't read, and yet these kind of form a true 'heart' of the genre for me. One is where it's just Supes talking a kid down from a roof, convincing them to rethink things rather than committing suicide. Another is from a security guard saying "Oh, wow, guess the park doesn't get safer with you two around" to him and Nightwing, and Supes responds "You mean the three of us." It's one part gentle rebuke and one part supremely awesome inclusion, that Superman considers his contributions meaningful. A final one from Supes is just him getting a cat out of a tree for an old lady. Like, that's it. It's cliche, and yet he does it.

    Another moment for me that stands out is Andrew Garfield's Spiderman, who walks a kid home, just chatting to him. That was the first time, in a Spiderman movie, where I said to myself "I would want to be this character". Oh, and another one is just Captain America, comics wise again, just talking about the valor of the French resistance in World War 2 and explaining that he'll always love the people for that. That was a good moment that almost makes me want to read more comics. These sorts of moments and more from other works, like Madoka Kaname from Madoka Magica or Hikaru Shidou at the end of the Rayearth manga, hit this sort of notion that says "This-this is what is admirable about heroism."

    I imagine a lot of us have these sorts, and not just in superheroes. I think we all carry these sorts of things.

    ...But they come second to a whole lot of other things. You think heroes, and massive, crime fighting epics come in now. And something unsettling occurs. You question the heroes, and the heroism itself.

    There was an article somewhere, I think from the blogs on Star Power **, where one of the writers asks why it is that so many super heroes have something tragic kick off their heroic career, and that settled on me when I read it. It's not just superheroes, it's fiction in general. Spiderman would have been a poor sideshow wrestling icon at best and possibly a self-entitled douchebag villain if Uncle Ben hadn't died, his story seems to suggest. Luke Skywalker has a desire to fight and make a difference, but he rejects it when given the chance to care, and only goes once he's left with nowhere to go. Batman is born from the cooling corpse of Bruce Wayne's childhood, laid low by two falling bodies that used to be his parents applied directly to his memory. Superman apparently has to kill Zod in order to know that he'll never kill in the last movie. Captain America is an oddity worth evaluating, because he didn't have something tragic happen to make him Captain America-he just didn't like bullies. How many stories do we know of that start with the hero losing something, with something scarring them for life?

    Why can't a good guy...just be a good guy? Why can't a hero just be born from having the opportunity to make a difference, and taking that shot? They exist, but nowhere near as much as the tragedy-born heroes. Do I have to steal to know it's wrong and work to stop theives?*** Does someone have to shove their hand down the front of my pants and both of us suffer mental scars from violation before we can be a dynamic duo? It doesn't even have to be personal. Do I have to see a car crash to know that speeding will fuck a person up? Do I have to lose my mother before I have the conviction to do what is right? WHY IS THIS A THING?

    Part of this is that it creates flaws and holes and trigger points and such-it makes characters more human and makes the heroic struggle more dramatic, because there are torture points to be pushed and used for tension and effect. But are these things needed in and of themselves? Does Hercules story have to begin with him slaughtering his kids and doing impossible tasks out of penance? Does Gilgamesh really have to lose Enkidu and immortality before he stops being a colossal dick?

    The other thing that's sort of related is how divorced what I talked about at the beginning is the minimal. Our heroes are almost always violent, or some simulacrum of it. Even My Little Pony, a show for little girls(ostensibly) and spends most of it's time on slice of life comedic problems, invested heavily opening and closing episodes with the conflict being resolved by giant rainbow lasers of reprogramming. It took FIVE SEASONS for a major arc to happen that didn't resolve itself with a more violent answer. Hell, even the Care Bear Movies that had the original "TASTE THE RAINBOW MOTHERFUCKER" moments never resolved their huge ass problems with the Care Bear Stare-they legitimately solved it by, well, caring.

    Human vs Human is, for a lot of people, more dynamic and interesting that Human vs World, but how much of our idea of Hero versus Human wrapped up in "BEAT THE SHIT OUT OF THE GUY?" Is this part of the reason why we have tragic openings? SO that the audience and the heroes feel justified in beating the shit out of someone? Is that really the ideal we're selling? "You hurt me, and I'm moved by a sense of righteousness, so it's okay for me to beat you into a pulp"?

    I'm a fan of the recent Fate/Zero and Fate/Stay Night animes because it asks questions about these ideas. Noble Saber, King Arturia, pulled the sword out of the stone with the ambition of leading her nation into unity and happiness, and she's openly called a Heroic Spirit. But so is Gille De Rais, the child-murdering occultist from the Thirty Years War, and Gilgamesh, who seems to have lost the humbling from his original story and instead plays off like he does at the beginning. THe Hashashin, Medusa, Circe make for a dark cast, and Alexander the Great, Cuchulainn, Lancelot, and more of a greyer cast-a broad range of people are called Heroic Spirits right alongside the righteous King of Knights. Kiristugu, one of hte protagonists from Fate/Zero, has the same sort of tragic opening that makes a hero in most of fiction, and aspires to his heroic ideals....but never calls himself a hero. Shirou Emiya must confront Archer as the idealization and full commitment of his heroic ideals. With Shirou, they are, if misplaced, still something worthy of admiration and mimicry, but Archer is an ugly character in terms of his outlook.

    All of this leads to a question. Is the idea of heroism that we are sold so often, everyday, throughout the whole of human history, really the sort of Heroes we want to be? Or do we want to be a different kind of hero, one that aspires to make things better, ones that might use violence, but aren't defined by violence, and furthermore, violence solely justified by tragedy? Don't we want heroes who are heroes because that's the sort of people we want to be?

    It brings to mind a image I came across a while ago, where at the top, the headline and image from a newspaper clip was shown. A rape victim had went and found her rapist, and set him on fire. What followed was a lot of great comments from the internet, parodying the common shit thrown at raped women, like "Well, if he didn't want to catch fire he shouldn't have worn such flammable clothes". It was hilarious, and at the time I approved of it. But I think back now, and I wonder if the rapist died, and if he didn't, what was he doing with himself now, and what he might have done if he hadn't been set on fire. And I had to ask myself "While he deserves punishment, did he deserve that?" And I had to ask myself "How right is she to do that?"

    What is heroism? Is our ideal in fiction who we really want to be?

    I've never had much problem with Heroes in Beast: The Primordial. I understand why people do, and I have faltered at justifying it or explaining it several times. The responses are critical and keen. But I think there's just as much value is a piece of work criticizing this notion of heroism we have, the one where a person gets hurt, and so hurts the right people in kind, and is praised for it. I think we have a right to acknowledge that "Heroes" are as much the problem as "True Fae"****.

    Just because you got hurt, doesn't mean it's right to hurt others. Does it?

          The World of Darkness usually takes a skeptical view of the idea that the solution to a problem is violence, even when other solutions don't readily suggest themselves. Violent action is often the very thing that makes our headlining monsters monsters, and it's rare that the games are going to laud someone as a hero (small 'h') specifically because they stood up to beat the tar out of someone.

          Hunter, particularly, lives at the intersection of "killing people is questionable" and "I don't have any other way to help." Heroes (capital 'H') are a different side of that problem.

          None of the games presents a pat answer to these questions or an easy solution to these problems, though some are more fraught than others.

          (EDIT: Note that "I hurt people because I'm hungry," an issue in Vampire and Beast, is another angle on the same problems. No pat answers.)

                  When I think of the defining traits of a Hero, two key aspects leap to mind. Those being a larger than life motivation, and an unyielding drive to pursue it. Confidence is also common, as is self-righteousness, but not strictly essential.

                  Now what qualifies as larger than life varies from story to story. In The Illiad that Odysseus's motivation is largely to return home, not grand at all until you add the fact that he has to overcome Poseidon to do it. Conversely Beowulf appears to have little motivation beyond drinking and fighting, until you realize that his motivation is effectively, "never back down from a challenge and never be shown up" in which case it seems implausibly grand. A more recent example would be Wilson Fisk, the villain in Netflix's Daredevil. Though an antagonist Fisk has a driving, overwhelming motivation and a towering objective. In the end he fails, but not for lack of a heroic effort.

                  Deep down Heroes know what they want even if they cannot voice it, they are not the sort to be swayed or dissuaded. A Hero is her own person, not necessarily a nice person, or a good person, but profoundly their own person. And though they may be occasionally lead astray (generally somewhere in act 2) they always find their way back (usually right before the climax).

                      I just want to put out three things on this.

                      1) I woke up with this sort of rambling thought going n that was demanding to weave together into something, so this was more or less done to get this out of my head so I can focus on other things.

                      2) My thesis*, if I have one, is that the traditional structure of heroes throughout our fiction is problematic enough to warrant scrutiny in this sort of dialogue. I'm not justifying Beasts here(of the two, I think they are the more problematic of the central cycle of this game), just saying that this strange reliance on "Good Guys come from Tragic Backstories" and it's uncomfortable juxtaposition towards 'Heroic Violence' is an idea worth...if not attacking, then at least examining very closely. I would posit that there's room in a later supplement for "True Heroes" who make Beasts really sweat, partially because they're a little more unpredictable in their potential judgment and action, but I don't make those sorts of calls and I'm not quite sure it has room as a major thing in the World of Darkness.

                      3) Also, and this just occurred to me, I feel like the archetypalization of this really weird mix of tropes and their prominence in fiction lends towards some resolution on the "Heroes are bland" front for anyone who might read this. A skeleton is a thing you frame a person on, and that's what the books delivers.                                   I want to point out that there is a difference between a hero and a super hero. Heroes are humans that go above and beyond the call of duty for the good of people. With a super hero, the idea is that everything is magnified. Greater internal demons to fight, greater external villains to fight, greater embodiment of virtues*, greater temptations of vices*.

                                  The idea with a superhero is that everything is just bigger. We want to see them overcome the worst that life has to offer. That's why.

                                  The monomyth, the Hero's Journey, is a story about overcoming challenges. That's the root of every hero story. Heroes are defined by the trials and tests that they face. Personal tragedies are more trials that they have to face. And, remember, humans love tragedies as much as they do comedies or success stories.


                                  * Also, recall that different eras and different countries don't share modern day values. So, the virtues a hero would need to embody change with time and place, as would the temptations they would need to overcome. What we might find terrible now wouldn't necessarily be considered bad in the past.
