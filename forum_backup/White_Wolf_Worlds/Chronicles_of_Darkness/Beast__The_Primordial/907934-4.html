    Originally posted by tasti man LH View Post

    Not to derail the current thread of conversation, but this point stuck out to me.

    I see this particular complaint get thrown around, that Beasts are inherently "more evil" then the other CRoD supernaturals, both in the fluff and how the mechanics support and encourage Beasts to act more "evil", while the others do not.

    ...aside from, y'know, the whole blood drinking thing in Vampire, or the consuming a human's soul from Demon.
    I think it is the obligate abuse (mental, if not physical) that Beasts conduct. There really is no way out of it - any and every Beasts' existence means someone is getting hurt. Even if they don't "feed" directly, their Horror will on its own eventually. Even their "Family Dinner" means someone gets hurt by proxy (either their monster buddy's victim, or the monster themselves through encouraging self-destructive behavior).

    Vampires need blood, but they don't have to take it - it can be offered, and there are other methods for Vitae acquisition.
    Werewolves don't need to harm people for their Hunts.
    Mages really don't need to inflict harm to survive.
    Prometheans' harm is more incidental, and can be manage to the point they don't inflict major Disquiet or Wastelands.
    Changelings can farm Goblin Fruit, or low level harvest Glamor.
    Sin-Eaters don't need to cause any harm (depending on Archetype).
    Mummies can minimize the exposure others have to Sybaris, and refrain from causing harm at higher Memory.
    Demon's don't need to inflict harm either. (They don't need to "consume souls", and in fact some/many refrain from doing so)

    Beasts are about the only supernatural where they only exist through harming others, rather than it being a risk or byproduct. That they try to justify their abuse through a "teaching" metaphor that even they largely don't take seriously doesn't really help.

    Yossarian: "And lo, it was optimal."

                    One thing about the way that Hunger works is that if you try to sate it socially, I've found that a lot of the time you don't have to do things which are that far outside the norm for humans, especially Tyrants and at Low Satiety. Heck, for a Tyrant, earning a promotion over someone else, something that people do all the time, is a High Satiety meal.

The game explores (and explores well) themes of how, all to often, the monsters we denounce are all too close to us. Perhaps that's why the word 'monster' comes from the Latin for 'warn', because these monsters warn us that there is only a thin line separating us from them.

                                                The thing I find most appealing about Beast is the challenge of trying to reconcile the monster to a strong human code of ethics. It's difficult, and it will never be a perfect fit, but to me that's sort of the point and I'm actually glad there's no mechanical carrot for morality, and that it's left up to roleplay. 2e as a whole is much better about making sanity meters instead, but Beast in particular inverts it and encourages you to give into the monster. It demands that you make some concessions, and rewards you for making bigger ones. Being a good person is hard for Beasts.

                                                All the same, you can play Beasts who are decent people, as long as you're willing to spend most of your time at Low Satiety. You can find ways to feed that are minimally harmful, or that do harm in one way but offset it with good in another.

                                                The Apex in one of my games is an EMT with the aspect of the grim reaper. She makes sure people know how close they came to the brink and encourages them to be more careful and aware of how fragile life is. Nights when she's not working she joins the Autumn Scarecrows skulking around the bad parts of town, scaring the shit out of would be criminals. She's responsible for a lot of nightmares (bad dreams, but also the power of the same name), hence her position as Apex, but she's also responsible for lowering the city's mortality rate.

                                                    OK; so if I'm reading this right:

                                                    a Beast is a dragon, kraken, gorgon, or other mythical monster — but only in her dreams. When she's asleep, she becomes the Horror (which, in Mage terms, is basically a monstrous Daemon) and roams the Primordial Dream, invading peoples' dreams and giving them nightmares in order to sate her Hunger. When she's awake, the Horror lurks in its Lair (analogous to a mage's Oneiros) and she walks the material world as a mostly-normal human with a limited ability to manifest powers based on her Horror (Atavisms) and Lair (by inflicting appropriate Environmental Tilts) and an ability to mess with peoples' minds (Nightmares). In theory, the Beast does what it does to teach people caution, humiity, and other “school of hard knocks” lessons; in practice, Beasts often ignore that and feed solely to sate their Hunger.

                                                    The ability to physically manifest as her Horror is not something the Beast is capable of, but it is an ability available to the Incarnate Inheritance, the “Good Ending” state that a Beast can work toward, akin to a mage becoming an archmaster — in fact, it's the defining characteristic of the Incarnate Beast. (the other two Inheritances are “bad endings” where she becomes a feral entity, either by physically dying in a way that leaves the Horror and its Lair intact or by having the Horror abandon the Primordial Dream by, in effect, “Claiming” her physical form.)

                                                    The crossover aspect of the game exists in the Kinship mechanics which allow a Beast to identify, befriend, aid, vicariously feed through, and hide among other supernaturals. As well, Kinship lets the Beast develop additional Nightmares inspired by the other supernaturals that she hangs out with. This Kinship is part of the game's subversion of other games' themes, in that the Beast's view is that their monstrous natures are the best part of them while what's left of their human natures is an impediment. Complementing this is the Beast's relationship with deranged self-described Heroes who see Beasts as little more than creatures to kill in their quest for glory. Beast admits that there are Heroes who are sane and who serve a purpose that's complementary to the Beast's supposed “drill sergeant from hell” role — the carrot to the Beast's stick — but for the most part, it concentrates on those Heroes who slay Beasts.

                                                    Am I right so far?

                                                        Originally posted by Dataweaver View Post
                                                        a Beast is a dragon, kraken, gorgon, or other mythical monster — but only in her dreams.
                                                        I think this is the only part that's off. It's not entirely untrue, it's just that the perspective is a bit too clinical for the game. Using Mage terms is kind of telling here. Mages want to study, classify, and categorize things that most Beasts would not see a particular point to drawing lines between. A Beast cannot manifest as a giant dragon of yore in the physical world like they can as a Horror in dreams, but that Horror cannot truly be it's full self in just dreams and has to resort to squeezing into a mortal form and walking the physical world. Basically, Beasts generally are not two separate entities. They're one being that's being pulling in to two different worlds. As you noted the "endings" represent this tug-of-war, with the two "bad" ones being pulled in one direction too hard, and the "good" one managing to find the mid-point.
