    So on a bit more of an on topic metric. The easiest method to play as a Hero and you know the good guy would be playing the higher integrity Heroes. The lower ones are basically defined by their desire and need to kill Beasts. Essentially Heroes sense disturbances in the Primordial Dream and are drawn to fix it, with your lower integrity Hero it becomes find the monster ,because obviously this is the work of a monster, and kill it. Your higher integrity Hero has more to them than this, so they'd have more avenues open to them than that since they'd be interpreting the feeling as something being wrong and them needing to fix it. This is probably going to end up with a weird mix of stuff from books and just my ideas, so yeah.

    As previously noted you are technically playing a Hunter spliced with an action hero, take that as you will. Fun note this isn't without precedent in the actual Beast line.Conquering Heroes has an ex-Hunter referred to as the Silver Smith who pretty much plays this way. Now running your character as this you'll want to decide if they've already killed a Beast, since that'll determine whether or not they have access to Anathema, just treat it the same as buying an extra dot in the supernatural stat, like Lair or whatever.

    Now potential conflicts in this. 1) The Quest: your character has to deal with the fact that part of their mind, their soul, their being knows they have a calling, a purpose in this world. You feel a disturbance and part of you screams that you should go fix it, the bigger the ripple the more difficult it may be to resist the urge to go and fix this even if they have no clue how they could fix things or how it might impact their own life.

    2) The Beghotten and leveling up: When your lucky the Beast you've come into conflict with will make this easy. The one's that can't stand living with the gnawing hunger and the ones who've decided that its not a good meal unless everyone in a three block radius died screaming are probably going to be of minimal conflict to one's consciousness on killing and claiming the subsequent powerup. What if the problem isn't related to a Beast? Beast was local to the area and the disturbance isn't their doing, or they showed up because as a Ravager they feed on what's probably going to happen without lifting a finger when that hurricane hits. What do you do with the grey areas The Beghotten handling cleanup on Masquerade violation for a local kindred clan even if this occasionally means disposing of the odd normal human, the ones playing Dexter and making sure they only hunt bad people. If you end up teaming up with a Beast, do you kill them in the end? Hell if the Beast is about to die to something else do you kill steal that thing since you'll get a powerup for killing the Beast, and is this a thing you tell the Beghotten at the start of the teamup?

    3) Other Heroes: Pretty much what it sounds like. You'll come into conflict with them, so how do you handle it? What do you do with the low integrity Heroes? These are people who've basically thrown away everything for that purpose you feel, do they scare you, do you envy them, do you pity them? Do you try to help them find more than just killing the monsters, do you just go for a non-aggression pact, what's the line you'll allow them to cross?

                    For the OP.

                    I must agree with the others that the whole heroes hunting monsters thing is better done with Hunter or one of the many non Onyx Path games that focus on such action, like Monster of the Week. But it can be done with Beast if that is your wish and it doesnt even have to involve any changes to the game itself, you can even have your katanas and trenchcoats (an aesthetic I myself am rather fond of which is obvious in the way I dress amongst other things). With a certain build one can easily make a Beast version of Batman, vigilante hunting criminals and monsters that threathen common folk, feeding of the fears of the monsters.

                    As for the Homecoming Vs Devouring matter I have always been on the homecoming camp. And while I do agree, grudgingly, with Heavy Arms that the whole matter is about apples and oranges and the feelings we all attach to them and thus not really worth fighting over especially now 3, has it really been that long, years into the game I still cant help feeling kinda bummed out by the nomeclanture changes made into the final draft. I just felt that the Homecoming and Soul were much more fitting and appropriate than Devouring and Horror.

                    My problem with Devouring is that it emphasizes and implies that something comes to you and devours a part of you before replacing itself with the devoured part, I also disliked its emphasis on it being something other Beasts do to you, closing in on the whole embrace matter. Homecoming as a term sounds warm and welcoming, it in the first draft put the emphasis on how you always felt different and incomplete and now that you faced your Nightmares you realized you are those Nightmares and you have always been them, you just finally found your way back home. It also de-emphasised any outer party interference removing itself from embrace like matters where another monster makes you into a being like it.

                    Similarly after you become a Beast and it feels correct why would you call your inner self, your true being, a Horror? It does not horrify you after all it is you. Which is why I preferred the Soul term over the Horror term.

                    Similarly the first draft of BtP had no mentions of lessons which is what I personally preferred, and which is another matter talked about in the BPG. If Vampires have no excuse for their feeding and general bloodlust other than survival or the Uratha need no other reason to hunt other than the wolf must hunt why do the Begotten have excuses for their hunger? Beasts feed because they must, there is no need for a complicated excuse. But again this is a matter of taste and opinion and YMMV.

                    But in the end these are just matters of taste, easily handled at each table, and they do not really affect the game itself and are thus more or less meaningless things to argue about but they can and should be matters that can be civilly discussed despite the emotional attachments we might have regarding them.

                                        On the subject of Lessons? Personally, I feel like it was badly handled in the core book and I think this for several reasons, not just because it wasn't part of the draft manuscript presented in the Kickstarter.

                                        Lessons just don't really have any structure in the game to hook onto. There is absolutely zero interaction between the concept of Lessons and Beasts feeding their Satiety, which means there is no mechanical reasons for why Beasts would consider "Lessons" to be something they all consider and know of. Now, it can be pointed to the concept of Lessons being a social structure for Beasts, as in how you say "To give meaning to their feeding".

                                        But what society actually exists for Beasts? The only social structure really defined for Beasts in the core book is Broods. And they are very intimate close-knit groups.

                                        Beasts have no Covenants, no Orders or Legacies, no Courts, no Tribes, no Lodges.... All of these are present in the other games in order to provide societal structure of expectations of behavior or standards of interaction or anything like that.

                                        Beasts, as presented in the Core book, don't have anything like that. So, having some kind of creed of "Lessons" seems very.... tacked on. Disconnected. Because they don't really have any structure in place to share such teachings or expectations of behavior.

                                        (I think the game was designed to not have any kind of "Society Splat", but that creates issues that need to be addressed as what kind of society they do have, even if that society is "a lack of society". Now, the Players Guide comes out with four groups, but they're each kind of their own thing and further again, they still don't have any recognized structure that fits with Beasts as presented in the Core.)

                                        Ultimately, I don't think the concept of Lessons is bad. I think it's better as something that a noted faction of Beasts believe, espouse, and support, and would be better buttressed by showing other factions that reject the concept of Lessons, or have differing ideas of how their feeding has purpose. But we don't get that, and Lessons gets shoved really hard.

                                        Basically, I'm seeing two conflicting things here, which is something the Core really should've fixed before it was finalized.

                                        And I know the Players Guide is going to be pointed to again as having "fixed" this, but I don't see it that way. I'll quote a couple of things from the book. Page 130 under The Culture of Lessons: "Not all Beasts buy into the concept of lessons as deeply as others, but all Begotten hear about the concept of lessons shortly after their Devouring, and sometimes before."

                                        How?

                                        Page 131, under Lessons Learned: "Few Beasts come through the other side of a Devouring without any other family around. From the very beginning, other Children begin speaking of the lessons they teach. They tie Hungers to lessons and teach the new Beast to explore the lessons that satisfy her Horror the best. They don't tell each other how the Horror doesn't care about the lesson, how if left unsated it will feed from the nearest and weakest prey available, no lesson learned. Sure, the Children know these things are true, but if they talk about it, they make a lie out of their own lessons."

                                        Who is this constant "They"? Where is this social push to share everything about "lessons" coming from? Where did those Beasts come across it? Why is there such pressure here for "lessons" when Beasts somehow know that it's all lies?

                                        Same section: "Pinning a purpose on that pain, creating order within the madness of that fear, puts everything else into perspective. To prevent that kind of emotional breakdown in newly Devoured Beasts, other Children talk about lessons early and often. Even the most nihilistic of Beasts understands the concept of lessons, and understands why they are important to the Children, even if he doesn't consider them necessary for himself."

                                        Where is this impending "emotional breakdown" from new Beasts? Why do "even the most nihilistic of Beasts" somehow agree with this?

                                        Where are the arguments? Where are the challenges to their lore? Where are the disagreements? Where are the other opinions?

                                        You know... I think one of the biggest problems I have with how the Player's Guide approaches Lessons, is how it strives so hard to have "Lessons" somehow something universal to all Beasts, and yet wants to have Beasts to have their individuality too. This circle was not squared for me. I honestly got to say... Probably the best proof that the Devouring and Teaching Lessons is something that was mishandled in the Core book, is how much word count gets used in the Player's Guide hashing over the concepts again.

                                        I do not mean to be attacking the work of the writers who wrote this stuff with this. I view this as something the Developer really needed to tie together, to expand better, and provide the writers with better direction for the various sections to support each other better. The writers can only give their best with the direction they're given. And well, Beast: The Primordial isn't really different from other Chronicles of Darkness games in this manner. Mage: The Awakening, Werewolf: The Forsaken, Changeling: The Lost, each have really shined with a Second Edition. Each had some significant problems with their First Editions. I'm really hopeful that Beast: The Primordial gets it's Second Edition when that time approaches. I think it'll be something awesome to behold. ^.^

                                                            Page 131, under Lessons Learned: "Few Beasts come through the other side of a Devouring without any other family around. From the very beginning, other Children begin speaking of the lessons they teach. They tie Hungers to lessons and teach the new Beast to explore the lessons that satisfy her Horror the best. They don't tell each other how the Horror doesn't care about the lesson, how if left unsated it will feed from the nearest and weakest prey available, no lesson learned. Sure, the Children know these things are true, but if they talk about it, they make a lie out of their own lessons."

                                                            Who is this constant "They"?
                                                            Other Children. It's right there in the sentence preceding the switch to a generalized pronoun.

                                                            Where is this social push to share everything about "lessons" coming from?
                                                            Approximately the same place that Requiem's Traditions and Forsaken's Oath of the Moon come from: cultural grounding with historical inertia.

                                                            When the monsters who tend to stick around for a long time are the ones who mediate their activities and have a digestible rationale for doing like they do, it tends to develop some institutional memory, particularly when those monsters are part meme, tend to be better equipped to change monsters' minds through conversation, and associate with other monsters who live nearby.

                                                            Where did those Beasts come across it? Why is there such pressure here for "lessons" when Beasts somehow know that it's all lies?
                                                            They "somehow" know that the hungry monster that they're feeding isn't picky when it comes to what comes after the shock, which doesn't change the fact that a lot of people are still going to balk at the prospect of engaging in what is commonly understood as at least mildly cruel behavior on a regular basis right out of the gate.

                                                            "People are going to have a takeaway from what happens because of you, so think about the takeaway before you act on your Hunger" is a not-remotely-complex selective pressure to see emerge from a population whose carelessness can lead to angry mobs and home invasions.

                                                            Same section: "Pinning a purpose on that pain, creating order within the madness of that fear, puts everything else into perspective. To prevent that kind of emotional breakdown in newly Devoured Beasts, other Children talk about lessons early and often. Even the most nihilistic of Beasts understands the concept of lessons, and understands why they are important to the Children, even if he doesn't consider them necessary for himself."

                                                            Where is this impending "emotional breakdown" from new Beasts?
                                                            Empathy tends to make people a little uncomfortable engaging in behavior that harms others Just Because and leaning into that activity without a filter can go maladaptive very easily. Literally the sentence before the one you're opening this quote with points to the prospect of confronting "You cause pain not just by fulfilling your needs but to fulfill your needs" leading to an existential crisis — like the kind of things that directly contribute to Beasts pursuing the lesser Inheritances.

                                                            Why do "even the most nihilistic of Beasts" somehow agree with this?
                                                            The very first sample character we are shown in the book ends up a nonsapient monster attended to by the vampire he self-destructed with and her progeny. Most Beasts in a position to evince nihilism do so with the benefit of a human mind and understand well enough that the framework of lessons is a cultural method to keep the Begotten's human mind intact. They don't have to agree that a grounding framework is necessary to understand why other Beasts use it.

                                                            Where are the arguments? Where are the challenges to their lore? Where are the disagreements? Where are the other opinions?
                                                            There's no lore to challenge that actually exists at the heart of the concept, as is explicitly pointed out in that section: Lessons as a cultural framing are there to give their feeding a larger context with which to keep aware of the consequences of their actions.

                                                            The other opinions and disagreements and arguments are matters for individual characters and frameworks for the same reason 1e Promethean didn't spend a lot of wordcount on Refusers: You don't actually need to dedicate much attention to characters using their supernatural powers in ways that are counter to the high-minded cultural touchstone the game puts forth as common enough to be in the corebook, because those rationales are generally more individually focused and the practical outcome seldom looks very different unless the character is on the fast track to a sticky end.


                                                            Like, I get and agree that a Second Edition of Beast would doubtless refine the concept, but "Be smart about your serial villainy because the world is watching" is not nearly as out-there as some of its detractors have made it out to be.
