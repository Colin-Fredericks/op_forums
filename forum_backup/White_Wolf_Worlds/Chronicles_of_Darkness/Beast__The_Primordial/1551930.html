    How to Get The Point of It

    06-29-2025, 08:12 PM
    I'm finally reading Beast's core book, and I'm not really buying it. Not completely unexpected, I've heard before that the core book isn't great.

    Here's the thing: for now my take of it is that they're glorified human assholes with a tendency to stick their noses on other people's businesses. It isn't that they're not powerful, but their powers give me more of an impression of a super-powered delusion than them really not being human, their kinship mechanics feel really forceful, and Hunger frequently looks less like a nightmarish need and more like an excuse to be a bully.

    But then, as I said, I've heard the core book has problems (and I can't say the concept is easy to parse, so nothing against the authors). What am I missing, and which book would make it better to get it? Is my reading described above wrong, or at least incomplete? What does Beast bring of interesting as gameplay cycle, and what does it adds to other games if it isn't the main star of a crossover?

      The Player's Guide is essential reading, being the book made with the most solidified take on the material after the shaky start the corebook had.

      Beasts are in an odd place cosmologically, because they're one of four of Chronicles' eleven splats that doesn't have a proper soul of their own but they're specifically connected to a part of the Astral related to the collective human experience.

      Metaphysically, Horrors are shapeshifting Fear Goetia from the Anima Mundi that traveled through the Cave/Omphalos into the realm of art and rumor, transformed by the power of language, and settled in a place on the terrifying borderland of human experience where the heightened reality of traumatic contact with the supernatural lets them project themselves into the perceptions of people experiencing or dreaming of situations compatible with the hunting tools and hungers their transformation has given them.

      Horrors are inclined by the principle of least action and the knock-on effects of being memorable entities feeding on narrativized experiences to become recurring nightmares for the people they feed on, and when they feed deeply they cause the usual effects of a supernatural being killing someone in their dreams, but for the most part that just means needing to rest to recover one's ability to assert oneself. Beasts, however, dream deep.

      The people who become Begotten have dreams that touch the home of Horrors, and in their Devouring some part of them deep down identifies with the monster that ate them to the point of assuming its nature as a sort of totemic core, inheriting the central Chamber of its Lair and the ability to expand its power with this dark act of self-acceptance. This means you're generally not going to get the peripheral beats of Vampire and Werewolf where a fresh specimen is horrified at their newfound hungers, because getting over that was Step One of becoming a Beast, but while some of them revel in it, the generally fatalistic bent of Begotten society is such that it's not hard to read that as a "you have to laugh" response.

      Unfortunately, as we can tell from various parts of the Chronicles seen by other monsters, while it would be nice if Jules Winnfield was the shepherd, it is nevertheless kind of true that the world is evil and selfish. More broadly, bad things are bound to happen and, in much the same way that organized crime is preferable to utter chaos, the Begotten being Bad News attached to personlike actors with incentives to engage with their surroundings to allow them to continue to feed their Hunger or otherwise gain Satiety means their active presence can be better for the neighborhood than some alternatives. The balancing act Satiety Conditions bring into play means that oftentimes their relationships with the only people who understand them are strained by impulsive or depressive behavior, and human avatars of the same force that shaped their Horrors at their rebirth obsessively search for them and periodically have the opportunity to impose cultural quirks onto their nature as a prelude to ritualized murder for Primordial power without any real regard for whether they've done anything to deserve it.

      The Begotten are rare enough that they can emerge without the kind of support network that keeps them from shucking off all remnants of their human social mores to live in the sewers and eat people until an especially bad year leads to their Horror breaking containment and mutating them into its physical image, but much as the survivorship bias of hunters on the Vigil is coldly paranoid, Beasts who have been around for long enough know how to keep the lights dimmed in their Lairs by some combination of feeding their Hunger in lots of petty little bites, pursuing periodic grand gestures of villainy to shoot for a big score (possibly murdering someone in the process), and/or lending their open or covert support to another type of hungry monster or three to build bridges over the void in their hearts. They're people like anybody else, but they have dark appetites that need to be managed, not ignored, and balancing that with all the rest of their emotional interiority — especially the need for companionship — is part of why at least part of Beast's narrative space is slice-of-life.

      You deal with the fact that the things that you want are at least partially connected to the monstrous desires literally-metaphorically represented by a dream-cryptid, that the people who best understand where you're coming from can only be intimidated or bribed into continued association with you after you nigh-inevitably screw up the good impression your occult charisma affords you, and that the whole song and dance is never going to stop. If you are lucky, resilient, or particularly driven, you can keep the despair from setting in and prioritize pursuits less dire than figuring out where your next meal is coming from or how to keep your family together, but that's probably going to be a risky venture even if you're not trying to free yourself from the grim bloody fable with an unhappy end by innovating in the field of creepypasta.

      Because even if you're not after the Incarnate Inheritance, Beast characters benefit from working to control the narrative they inhabit, and oftentimes that means managing the supernatural needs of themselves and their kin so that the monsters can take a day off from being monsters to get coffee or enrich their lives with learning an artform. Their second Anchor Trait after the one that represents their memorable monstrous quality is the one that acts as their redeeming feature that lets them pretend the would-be Heracles has simply mistaken them for a Titanspawn. The list of people who identify with a monster who does bad things is biased towards people who enjoy doing bad things, but even feeling the Horror's emotions 24/7 doesn't turn one of the Begotten into a two-dimensional caricature of a crime-loving supervillain.

      Beast is a game built on approaching monsters as people without separating the monster from the person, particularly through a social lens (incentivized as the Begotten are to think about what makes a monster frightening to people), and the splat's conceptual connection to monsters as a whole gives them a foot in the door for most encounters with supernatural beings and magical real-estate in much the same way that Mage's protagonists all have an inbuilt Supernatural Phenomenon Alert; where the Awakened have reason to search the depths of the universe for magical curiosities to heighten their understanding, Beasts tend to focus on how nearby occult entities affect the people around them and are affected by them in turn to build familiarity (if you'll pardon the borderline pun).

          If Mage the Awakening proves anything, it's super powered delusions can end being really substantive things, but I'll admit that's me.

          Anyways, trying to address the questions.

          What are you missing? Probably and in short, amplification through simplification and critique by comparison. Beast doesn't really do anything more or less assholish than any other gameline with it's characters, but where other gamelines provide context that can obfuscate and excuse the actions they take, Beast instead gets to the heart of playing games of human monsters in a horror mystery setting and asks their characters to create their own accountability and/or justification for it, and viscerally translates the consequences of their choices in doing that into their social networks(usually other monsters, which is where the critique can play really hard in fun ways), with opposition that thrives on Beast's isolation or tainted sociality.

          There's also a lot of really cool psychological mythology stuff that Beast could have really rocked with if we could've gotten a storyteller's guide or second edition, but that won't happen so I won't do my usual shit in that direction.

          Which book would make it better to get it? Given the issue feels more rooted in the Beasts than in their slice of the world, Beast's Players Guide is the signature best one to start with. Building A Legend is also not bad given it's price, but it's not directly approaching your issues as presented.

          Is your reading wrong or incomplete? A lot of this depends on perspective, because I could pretty well find a way to say that about literally any other gamelines, but contextualization can tweak things one way or another. If I were going to accuse your read of anything, it's more that it feels like a superficial analysis-not inaccurate, but it feels to me more like a skim. That's not a slam on you either, because Beast's core is something a shallow mishmash of writing directions, and there's a reason why people who are fans of this series tend to swear very heavily by the supplements moreso than the corebook. That core is functional for the mechanics and some of the gist, but later books definitely have a better grasp on the depths and the heart of the game, and a lot of fan's takes have gotten credit for contextualizing the game in cleaner and more coherent senses. The homebrewers that like this game(Like, ACTUALLY like it) do a lot for it.

          What does Beast do that interesting in terms of it's gameplay cycle? There's a lot of things we can talk about in terms of streamlining the usual Chronicles methods, from the basics of economy action (Atavisms and Nightmares basically always being available does a lot to encourage action) to set dressing, and more than that, but we want the most interesting thing about the gameplay cycle. That would undoubtedly be that it takes the core gameplay scene and sequel cadence of goal-enacting and recovery and marries it to it's larger ambitions in an extremely elegant and marvelously realized way.

          Beasts are creatures of legend, of reputation, of folklore, creepypasta, rumors, and the like, literally. Lair as a Power stat is literally just how much of the pyschospiritual real estate of human awareness the Beast occupies, and the actions they take, the people they associate with, and the places they claim all bleed into it. Bringing it back to the gameplay cycle, Chronicles tends to have a cycle where, on the upbeat, you take the energy sources, Willpower, and other resources you have and try and achieve some kind of goal-topple the Prince, recover the artifact from the interstitial temple, dive into the Hedge and escape out, what have you-and then on the downbeat go into the very human interactions where you often have to predate upon people weaker than you, feed your better or worse selves, and ultimately reflect on your position in life as you do the things that regenerate your overall resources.

          Other games have tried to make the feeding of the monster more prominent, more of a direct thing you actually play out, taking that downbeat action and making more of the upbeat. Beast's brilliance is that it actually succeeds in doing that-the swing to High Satiety always requires something big and substantial, likely the result of an action that is the centerpiece of a Story, and what a Beast does and why definitevely says something about that Beast, confronting their humanity with the legend they are building about themselves and what those consequences are. Their feeding then also feeds into that idea of the game's larger points, because the fulfillment or faulures of these actions than become what it is that fills the psychospiritual real estate of human awareness with, it Defines their Legends as it Adds To It, and they are accountable directly to the choices of who they choose to be and what they are and stand for. Pursuing Your Hunger, Defining Your Kinships, Taking On Your Chambers, Defeating your Heroes, all of these Build Your Lair, Build Your Legend. It takes what Chronicles is about and finds a way to cram all of it into the main action. It is Chronicles in a nutshell.

          What Does It Add to Other Games? The second half of this question can be read a couple of ways, but I'm opting to go the route of "How do they work as NPC's in non-Beast games?"

          If that is the case, then the answer comes down to two particular points. First is that you can stick Beasts into just about any particular kind of context you want to play them in and do fine and dandy with it. Beast can neatly adhere into other political structures, but at the same time Beast Broods can make for interesting and Quirky Third Party options for your usual political affair, and in the latter case you have pretty open leeway for anyone in or under the brood to display very odd and interesting tool sets. They work easily well as allies, adversaries, or foils, basicallty always having a handy excuse to break out a curveball answer to whatever scenario you care to bring up.

          The second leans into that Foil consideration and brings up the earlier point of Critique by comparison. It's sister game, Hunter the Vigil, takes monsters to task for their consequences, for the damages their monstrosity inflicts, intentions and contexts asides. Beast instead foils by showing other monsters who they are without their surrounding bullshit, showing them at the heart of who they are and what they do. It can be easy for a changeling to lose the forest for the trees in the middle of explaining their durance and the issues of clarity, but a Beast who toys with emotions and goes to extremes for security in like fashion can be a particularly revealing way to lay out who changeling has chosen to be, and what they might choose to not be moving forward. They make existential foils that force other monsters to consider their usual reasons and justifications are, in fact, conveinences that they use to not confront themselves.

          And now a question you didn't ask but I will answer: What is your ultimate takeaway of Beast and why?

          TO which my answer is that Beast is a bit of a heartbreaker game at this point. There ARE a lot of really cool and interesting story ideas, mechanics, and themes to play with, and arguably all the pieces are there to make a really solid game, or at the very least a game that doesn't cause the bile to rise for so many people-but mismanagement of the property has ended up hiding a lot of what the game is and, more importantly, could have been, and the closure of Chronicles by Paradox now means that, no matter how much is done for it with Storyteller's Vault after the fact, Beast can't really ever have the chance to be what it could have been.

          The fact that Beast speaks to a fair number of people, and does so in a very personal way for a lot of them, is a testament to that Could've Been, but as is, the gameline just can't, and that makes it one of those classically defined heartbreaker games. So if it ends up not being to your taste, I can't blame you.

          But man oh man, if I could have another go at baking the pie.

              Following up Arc is always hard because as usually she's kicked it out of the park.

              A small bit of add from the "big picture" perspective, is that one of the oft-overlooked aspects of the evils that all beings within the Chronicles of Darkness do are fundamentally human ones. The CofD is a setting about Cosmic horror where the characters are puny specks in the eyes of grand forces beyond comprehension. The horrors and evils of the setting are compelling precisely because they are human horrors. Humans exploit other humans in countless ways, they resort to violence over positive methods of obtaining their goals, they lash out in pain despite it just causing others pain, they obey authorities they know are more corrupt than just flawed but justify it anyway, and so on.

              Something that I think falls between taste and "you kinda need to play it to get it" is that Beasts feed in exceedingly mundane ways. Every Begotten can sate their supernatural hungers by doing things countless more humans (not even getting into the foil-to-other-splats stuff) are also doing at any moment: steal, bully, be unpleasant in countless different expressions, hurt, kill, lie, cheat, intimidate, betray... you don't need to be a supernatural monster to do that. All humans are guilty of hurting someone in someway. Even people we perceive as broadly morally/ethically good have done things they regret... or we'd like to think they regret and don't frequently consider they might not at all... and Beast asks us to look at that aspect of human social structures through the eyes of creatures that see it as a buffet we've laid out for them instead of something they have to come to terms with. It's our class structures that make it so easy for Tyrants to bully others, our materialist lifestyles that give objects perceived value that a Collector can exploit in their taking, it's our desire to make things the endure in a universe that doesn't allow for it that makes in possible for Ruin to set in, and our failures at justice enables the disproportionate vengeance Beasts will dole out instead.

              Something I tend to say about the game, is one of the most potent things you can find even in the core book... is that a Beast can easily live a life that - despite being a bully, or an asshole, or whatever - that leads to social rewards in society because we've created societies were Beasts would have it pretty easy if they didn't have problems besides humans to deal with. The ease at which Beats can reject the pretensions other splats use to excuse their actions is the same ease a Beast can turn the mirror back on humanity. If what they're doing is so wrong and repugnant, why are humans so ready to give the money, social acclaim, influence, and praise for what they do (at least as long as they do it to the right targets)? How do we, as the audience, refine our moral thinking so we can condemn Beasts without condemning humanity as a whole? Can we?

              -----------

              Also, to be a bit less abstract in all of this, I tend to be the one around here that actually talks about my time playing a Beast character.

              I think just sitting down and making a character can cut through a lot of the problems in the core book. A lot of "so what do you do?" comes down to your characters in any CofD game, and Beast is no different. Different Beasts (esp. when you scale that up to a Brood of PCs) are going to get up to different things with the tools provided. Once I actually made my first Beast PC sooo much got easier to figure out because a bunch of things just weren't important to my character (he was an Atavism focused character, so high Satiety feeding strategies were a minor thing to consider at first, I could basically ignore Nightmares and deep exploration of the Primordial Dream... he was very focused on his life in reality with the people he cared about).

              I had to figure out what his personal ideals were: what lines he wouldn't cross, what lines he'd cross under duress, What mattered to him the most, and how that looked. The other players were surprised by how much he valued family (both his living mortal family, and others he slotted into the concept) when he sold himself as this loner monster living in the woods (which was also true in many ways). This could get very ugly... he ripped someone's arm off for messing with his pet turtle and murdered a kid for cheating on his little sister. He bit the head off of a Fire-Touched Pure for messing with his Uratha girlfriend... and forced said Pure's wolf-blooded packmates to eat the body. Was that absurdly cruel? Yes. But going to said extremes in the name of protecting what he considered his family was part of his Legend. Nobody - not packs of werewolves, Hunter cells, Sin-Eaters curious about all the ghosts he was creating - were allowed to disrespect his inner circle, or he'd come down with barbarity born of something incredibly basic: speed. He wanted "problems" gone and to get back to good things fast. And, well, negotiating is slow. Killing your enemies isn't. Dealing with him meant coming to grips with the fact that he had no attachment to humanity as a whole as a good. He was caught up in tribalism thinking: to his tribe he was loving, caring, protective... "good." To those outside his tribe, there were neutral people he'd ignore, and bad people he'd eliminate.

              Was he a good person? A bad person? How do you feel about someone that built a house with his bare-hands as a surprise gift for a friend that just needed something to go right in their life... but also blew up a VASCU agent's house with innocents inside of it because the agent was about to catch the same friend? Can you consider him a simple violent asshole with no depth? Or is that a complex character that brings up real moral questions when his motivations are understandable but his methods are extreme?

              After playing him for a few years... I still don't really know myself. But no other game lets you dive so deep by removing so many obstacles in terms of "stuff" for PCs to deal with that's unique to those games.

                      That's because it's not supposed to be. As a Beast, YOU are the nightmare. Whatever remains of your human soul was torn to shreds, devoured, and assimilated with your Horror.
