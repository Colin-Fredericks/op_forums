        Demonic Jehovah's Witnesses, you say? This comes to mind:


        That aside, I prefer stories where the traditional view of family, as being predicated upon the members having blood relations (or a suitable metaphysical equivalent) are challenged. The first draft did this fine, but lacked a catalyst for dropping out of a traditional family arrangement into this newer, self-selected model. The new introduction, in contrast, uses the Devouring to create the anomie necessary to make that leap. Part of that comes from knowing that staying with unchanged humans would be almost guaranteed to devolve into a vicious cycle of victimization and not entirely unjustified fratricide, while the other part comes from knowing that your fellow Begotten, and to a lesser extent, your Supernatural Kin, are hardly susceptible to the depredations of the thing that ate your soul the way your old friends would be, and more to the point, they get what you're going through in a way your old family never could. That acceptance is what makes that new family far superior to the traditional one, as is the lack of forced deference that arises in conventional families.

            Okay, being born a beast was a big selling point for me. Creepy kids whose friends and family instinctively feel there's something off about, who are troubled by strange desires and unwholesome urges and terrible nightmares? Kids who always feel uncomfortable and out of place among other people, until one day the realization cones to them that it's because they're NOT people, they never were, they were always something ELSE, something dark and terrible and ancient, something that suddenly explains all the weirdness growing up?

            That is a powerful story. It's powerful because it's a mythic chosen one narrative common to our culture inverted (what if your parents are not your real parents? What if you're really royalty?). It's powerful because it's a story many players will feel resonance with- beasts aren't queer kids (well, some of 'me might be), but I can see some similarities, especially in the whole "my family is the other freaks who know who and what I really am and accept me warts and fangs and dripping toxic ichor and all thing.

            A the new story is less powerful. It's not a bad story, but it's roots aren't as deep.

                                                    I will note that the text of the original draft does specifically site the "stories of marginalized people" as an inspirational source to draw on, and how Beasts are tied to "the other". And there's A TON of treaded ground on using "monsters" to serve that purpose in fiction. So people weren't seeing it out of nowhere. It just wasn't ever supposed to be the central theme.

                                                    I'll quote Rose over on the Kickstarter...

                                                    At 14, I had a terrible secret. I knew I was a monster, and there was no one I could tell. Vampire: The Masquerade told me there was a world of people like me. It was tremendously liberating.
                                                    But that's a double-edged identification. Because in the World of Darkness, vampires actually are monsters. They hurt and they kill and they break hearts and they're mostly not held to account for it. So at what point is the metaphor liberating and at what point is it shaming, stigmatizing? At what point is it saying that queer 14-year-old Rose really _is_ a monster?
                                                    Each World of Darkness game has to walk that line for itself. Sometimes we have to meander from side to side a little before we find the line.
                                                    This is how art works. It's messy and it's dangerous and your message isn't always what you thought it was.
