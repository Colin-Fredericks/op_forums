            All right, accurately describing a problem is a necessary step to resolving it, so I'm taking another crack at this.

            As stated the issue is the 'abusive things you have to do', but that's clearly not accurate because you don't have to do those things. The game offers viable alternatives. We're still hung up on it though, so there's definitely some problem here, and I have a guess as to what it is.

            Is your problem that the game incentivizes, encourages, or rewards those behaviors? Because yes, it does do that, and I can see that being a deal breaker for lots of people.

            It's also one of the central appeals of the game. What I love about Beast is the lack of guardrails on the slippery slope of morality and the Horror looking at you with a toothy grin and a pair of skis. And the mechanically unrewarded, unincentivized freedom to say And Yet I Choose To Climb. When falling is so easy and so tempting choosing to take the higher, harder path means so much more. The greatest test of character isn't adversity, it's power and lack of consequences.

            And you can't have that without acknowledging that a fair subset are going to be horrifically abusive because it's just easier to not care.

                                    Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                    But the ancilliary reasons have to do with people overblowing certain elements, and with that a general commentary on the failure of proper reading habits among the masses.
                                    Hey, don't do this kind of shit. The entire CofD line routinely blends flavor text meant to create an impressionistic feel with literal facts of the setting. It routinely mixes narrative abstraction mechanics that aren't meant to be used to draw fiction inferences with highly simulationist mechanics that /are/ meant to be used for fiction inferences. Flavor text and mechanics step all over each other like a junior high prom. It's composed by multiple authors with varying levels of kludge tolerance, system mastery, writing style, varying expectations/experiences for how actual play is conducted, varying awareness of other author's decisions in other books, and frequently has intentional textual ambiguities in service of an uncertain horror cosmology. The books are extremely jargon heavy and often dense with digression and throat-clearing. There are entire sections in some books devoted to explaining a single unimportant topic and single sentences that are load-bearing for absolutely core elements. The books often comment on each other, make iterative changes in response to criticism without doing so directly, and frequently require referential knowledge of other books, sometimes in other game lines. And historically, though improvements have been made, a somewhat laissez-faire attitude towards indexing, organization, editing, concision, ambiguity, error-correction, uniform terminology...

                                    "Proper reading habits" only make the problem worse. These books do not reward close reading. If anything, anytime I re-read one of these books I end up /more confused/ as to what the game expects. Beast is a game that was discussed in fragments before it was kickstarted, got a broad rewrite, has very important clarifying and expanding material in it's player's guide, and is a horror game that walks right into some very uncomfortable territory. Are you surprised there's some controversy about it?

                                    Now, I suppose we could blame the schools, but come on.
