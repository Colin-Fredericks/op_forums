    I unequivocally reject the notion that people don't fear the Other. It's enough to look at xenophobia, racism and LGBT-phobia rampant in the world. Even these phenomena are merely malignant expressions of a healthier fear of the stranger - a person whose trustworthiness is yet uncertain. At its core, that fear is meant to protect you from human threats - predators, competitors, or people whose influence would change your life for the worse. Inguma can also represent strangers in your midst, hiding their true intentions for malicious ends. They can also represent the anxiety of being the stranger - finding yourself among people whose language you don't understand, and whose mores you can't follow, even if you were raised by them.

    Inguma are stated to be the youngest Family, one born of societal pressures. They're subconscious carricatures of how the in-group sees the out-group. Ideally, once we acknowledge that fear, we can focus on understanding the other and assessing if they're a threat or a potential ally. The fear can keep us from being hurt, but also from botching the first contact through unquestioned assumptions. A well adjusted person will accept that they can't understand or vet everyone - there's never enough time or information. They know they have to do their best despite that.

    So what would it look like for a Forsworn to reject that fear? I have several ideas:
    • Replace fear with aggression. The stranger should not be feared - they should be confronted and either defeated or corrected. Once everyone is in line or subservient, there will be no strangers to fear. At their best, these Heroes will root out predators in their social circles, or keep vigil against con artists. They will represent the best of their community and keep watch over their safety within the wider world. At their worst, these Heroes become conquerors and oppressors, pushing laws that redline anyone who doesn't live up to an impossible standard of conformity, or condemning family for what they do in their bedroom.
    • Replace fear with uncritical acceptance. Nobody is really a stranger, and nobody should be othered, no matter what their beliefs. Once we stop judging each other, everything else will fall in place. At their best, these heroes will act as diplomats and charity workers, eager to understand foreigners and outliers to banish fear and help the needy and the troubled to find a place of their own. At their worst, these Heroes reject all judgement for sake of social cohesion. They will expect victims to sit at the family table with their abusers without complaint, or paint over malfeasance in their church to maintain a facade of respectability.

                    I am very much opposed to naming the Inguma-Forsworn anything like Priests. This is a biased and superficial reading of both religion and the fear of the other. It paints a needlessly specific picture that goes against the broadness of the previous Forsworn. I know this may come across as a bit of a surprise, but this notion that religion is one big excuse to establish guidelines and permissions for social exclusion is in itself a form of prejudice.

                    I don't expect to be taken seriously when I say this, but not all religious groups are interchangeable with, let's say, the Westboro Baptist Church or Scientology. Besides, I'm quite sure the anti-religion sentiment is not even applied equally across the board. We all have our favorites, and it's all too common to assume shit about religions of other cultures in the naive hope that they'll be inherently more ethical than the religions of one's culture, in all aspects.

                    This is an untenable position once you understand the ubiquity of historical cruelty and division based on the smallest disagreements. To me, it is more narratively productive to explore what happens when someone realizes there is no such thing as unconditional acceptance in any group, because all groups have rules, whether helpful or harmful. And let's not kid ourselves, there is no such thing as a Perfect Member Of [X], because that's not how people work.

                    There will always be at least one point of internal friction, whether it is about exegeses of the Bible, the Communist Manifesto or the newest edition of a TTRPG. I named my take on the Inguma-Forsworn "The Similar" precisely to avoid this prejudiced and small-sampling-induced outlook. Everybody wants to belong somewhere, and belonging has a price. And it is always the same price, especially when the person doing the sales-pitch claims it is totally different.

                        As someone who, back in 2019, identified the most consistent element of what's unnerving about the other in circumstances that range from it's most violent forms to it's most benign as the other presenting a way to be that is counter to your own that may shake your understanding of who you are and who you could be, and who understood the wisdom the Inguma communicate is learning strip one's self down and examine who they actually want to be instead of who they thought they had to be, and allowing themselves to explore and experiment with that, the Hero I arrived to as it's opposite is one who feels they know themselves without needing to question how or why they are themselves.                         And no, I don't think any criticism of religion that comes up with taking that angle is either misplaced or overtly malicious. I don't worry about Westboro or Scientology, I take issue with the well meaning Christian, because I was raised Christian and I know the good that can come from that, and I also know how it can lead to justifying modern forms of slavery, criminalizing and abusing minorities, and meaningfully inhibit a person's abiltiy to be themselves. It's not prejudice to admit to flaws in a subject that Beast is adjacent to in a game that takes a critical eye to society and civilization and asks questions about the intents and actions that made it the way it is, particularly where it's hostile. By similar token, Beast is also a good game to have that examination in because it sets it's baseline at "everyone is a bastard, but it's possible to make something good and contributing out of that bastardry', and in that way it still has a vested interest in showing how religion does do good. Hell, the idea of the Priest itself feeds into that initial point about the Inguma-for as much as Priest box people up, the nature of the particular boxes they use could easily provide words and definitions to being a person hadn't considered yet, and for as limiting as they can be, they also provide direction and, in their own way, safe spaces to explore that direction.

                        So, no, I'm not inclined to change an epithet just because it inherently invites religion for a critical examination that will invariably point out flaws with active religions today. If anything, I think that makes it all the more productive to immediately put that on the table. If that's a problem for you, well, I get where you're coming from, but I'm not making the Forsworn for you. I'm making them for Beast, and from where I'm sitting, that decision does the most good for it's space in the game until I'm otherwise persuaded.

                        EDIT: At the core of the Inguma and the Fear of the Other is the issue of Self-Alienation. The Inguma handle it by exploring it until they come to a place of self-acceptance and personal comfort with changing themselves as they discover new vectors to be. Priests don't see it as a problem with themselves, they see it as a problem with the world. In case anyone wanted a TL;DR on the Inguma/Priest psychological logic.
