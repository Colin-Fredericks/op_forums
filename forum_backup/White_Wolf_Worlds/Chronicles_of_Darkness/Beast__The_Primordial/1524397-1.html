                            The gist of it, I think, is in the starting points.

                            So way back in 2017 when the previews for Hunter 2e were coming out there was a discussion about the Code where I made a point that I can definitely word better after having read through that game's First Edition in full: While it would be a lie to say that Hunter is not a game for which The True Monster Is Man is a recurring sub-theme, the Vigil's generally maladaptive capacity to respond to that gradual revelation is a bit less front-loaded.

                            Yes, 1e included in its Antagonist section extremely clear messy-enough-to-include-other-stuff categories that directly corresponded to the protagonists of the preceding five gamelines, had the order of its main supplements focused on monster types come out with basically the two most human monster-types first, and made no secret of the fact that a prominent facet of expected information-gathering from monsters is likely to involve torture, to say nothing of the multiple neutral/exploitative/power-preservation-oriented lenses on the Vigil introduced throughout the line, but the foundational archetypes of splat are primed as reactively/protectively motivated in origin despite the protagonists' ongoing decision to crack their bones against the wall of magic and power and conspiracy until they're numb to the concrete suffering of individuals in service to a drive that is frequently violent and jealous and paranoid and reactionary — people are a lot more prepared to play the hotheaded altruists whose family got eaten by werewolves from the jump than to deal with the splat's survivorship bias being coldly paranoid and willing to let people get hurt to draw the monsters further out into the open.

                            Whatever your Hunger and typical level of fullness, the Devouring explicitly involves accepting that you and the monster are the same being, taking and intimidating because that's a key part of what you are, and the rest of your character arc from that starting point involves figuring out what you want to do with that and what else about you matters. "Welcome to Hunter, you're not the Good Guys" is an eventual realization for the casual reader, whereas "Just because you are Bad Guy doesn't mean you are bad guy" is a thing the Beast corebook makes somewhat difficult to get to thanks to the intensity of the language it uses to describe the generalities of Begotten activities — reading the books, Beasts in general are operating at the universal standard when placed in context with their fellow monsters (to say nothing of the fact that Family fellow-feeling doesn't undo the very solid reason to want an Apex who isn't a Beast in your town), but when words like "legendary monster" and "incarnate nightmare" are being used at all levels of the scale it tends to obfuscate the fact that the Begotten are powered by Significant Actions at a lower level than Godzilla Activities.

                            To be extremely reductive, it's part of why Changeling keeps getting people asking for Good Guy True Fae — Hunter can more easily check the escapist power fantasy boxes of having a traumatic origin story and kicking monster ass when the core drive behind doing so isn't set up from the jump as e.g. "you want power so you don't have to suffer the soul-shaking fear of not being in control of your life," whereas "you took power because you recognized that you could be like the thing that scared you at a soul-deep level" as a starting point for a more contemplative story arc about why something is scary and how that power can be used is a little less universally beloved as a narrative. People like their Heroes, and getting people to question a long-established narrative can be difficult.

                                Honestly I think it all comes down to some of the issues in how it was presented. Beast was my introduction to the Chronicles of Darkness, so when I read it I just took for granted that, for instance, beast was this kinda existentialist game that expected players to decide for themselves where they drew the line between acceptable feeding behavior and going off the deep end and the somewhat discordant authorial voice was meant to highlight that (the fact that my only point of reference at that point was D&D may have played a role). Basically even if the violence of Beast isn't inherently worse than than violence of Hunter, Hunter still better at explaining itself and therefore less likely to get this sort of criticism.

                                    Wow, didn't know this topic was still... a topic. Huh.

                                    What now proceeds are my hopefully somewhat coherent ramblings on topics I've seen come up while skimming this thread. Please be patient with me.

                                    First of all: I don't really get the problem, I have to admit.
                                    The concept of "person with monstrous urges tries to arrange themselves with the situation" is not exactly new. Vampires try not to suck people dry, uratha try not to snack on people, Beasts try not to be a source of harm for people close to them by not Feeding, but sometimes, accidents happen, or the situation calls for it, or your character had a really bad day and the dice weren't nice...so what are you gonna do? Survive. Deal with the outfall of whatever foolhardy or stupid decision was made. Learn from it. Avoid in the future. Rinse and repeat.

                                    I was under the impression that this is precisely the intent of the games: to create dramatical situations, whether they be things like "Vampire is stuck between a rock and a hard place and killing this human he likes would make all the problems go away","Mage should better not touch this button to satisfy his curiousity", "Promethean misunderstands a core concept of humanity, resulting in tragedy" or "Changeling paranoia leads to bad times and broken relationships" - things need to go sideways, because conflict feeds the story, and these are horror stories at their core. Something should make you horrified - shocked, repulsed.
                                    Playing a character that might have to e.g. abuse the trust other people have in him as their psychotherapist to manipulate them into doing very bad things for themselves (as Enablers are wont to do)? Absolutely horrifying idea to me. I also relished playing this character and his seeking to come to terms with what is the ultimate betrayal in his point of view (sadly game ended too soon, so I don't know where we'd have ended up with him). Do I get that not everyone would want to play this? Absolutely.
                                    However: I don't see why one "I need to do terrible thing x and try to find a way to live with it" should be different from the rest.
                                    I get it is more niche than the others, but still, why all the hate?
                                    Not going to go into the already made statements regarding complex characters making partly bad decisions (with possibly good intentions) that will come back to bite them in the ass being something some of us like to explore in games. Or personal horror. Or playing monsters rather than supowerpowered friendly neighbourhood vampires (not saying that is a wrong way to play, nor that you couldn't play Beast that way.)​

                                    Secondly, I also feel like people are purposely trying to make splats like Hunter look good in comparison to Beast through their choices of character.
                                    Batman could very easily be a Beast template. Nemesis Eshmaki, teaching the criminal underworld to "fear the Batman". Would you call him an abuser?
                                    What about a cop who leaves the confines of his profession to go after criminals that slip through the system, like Martin Riggs and Roger Murtaugh from Lethal Weapon? Cops working outside the law, outright breaking international law to quite literally punish 'evildoers'? Abusers? I'll admit they mostly just kill their enemies, though.
                                    (In fact, jobs like police and prison wardens could be perfect for Beasts to provice opportunities to Feed on low Satiety.)
                                    Dexter could easily be a Beast. Is Dexter an abuser? No, pretty sure he's a serial killer. But is being a serial killer really that much better?
                                    Let's take Lupin, the master thief. Someone stealing precious belongings to cause emotional anguish? Maybe even going full anime and sending a fancy card to announce the upcoming theft? Make them worry first, grieve later. Quite the Collector.
                                    Heck, Robin Hood could be a Tyrant trying to offset his bad conscience due to all the people he kills during the robberies by giving it to the poor, thereby feeding into his own self-image of grandiouse generosity or justice.

                                    Re: Beasts are inherently more 'evil' than other splats
                                    Thirdly, I don't think Beasts are in any way shape or form inherently or objectively worse than other splats.
                                    Some, sure. But bad Beasts are more akin to Bale Hounds than Forsaken. Almost every splat has a faction embracing a monstrous darkness and as a result is considered something of a cause for an enemy-mine truce-fire situation. VII, Scelesti, Bale Hounds, Loyalists etc. Beasts have monsters like these too, but since you don't have as neat of a social axis, they are harder to identify, and don't quite fit the same mold.
                                    In general, though, every splat comes into situations where they could not harm the general populace, but still do so, to fulfill a personal need: curiousity, lust for power, pure survival instinct.
                                    Are Beasts egoistic for Feeding? I'd say not more than anyone else: if not doing something could kill you, you'd find a way to do it, even if you dislike doing it. Especially if not doing it comes with its own host of problems. Are Beasts encouraged to act badly? Yes. But that tension of "where is the character drawing the line and why" makes for interesting stories. Not having a Masquerade that makes certain demands of the character nor an "you lose control and the Beast/Kuruth makes you do bad stuff" mechanic makes acting or not acting on the Hunger more meaningful, and places more responsibility on the character (Not shitting on the Kuruth/Frenzy mechanics here, they are wonderful in their own gameline and offer different story opportunities).
                                    And I like that.

                                    Furthermore, I have often wondered why so many people fixate on this "Beasts are abusers, and thus the worst!" concept, and actually asked myself whether it is due to a lack of dissociation on part of some readers; lethal violence and murderous intent are, after all, not something most of us typically experience, and as such, depictions of it are cathartic or, at worst, offputting.
                                    Non-lethal means of violence, however, are within the realm of experience to some degree, and thus has a much higher chance to hit close to home, especially when there is not a huge veil like "for the sake of the Masquerade" on it, leaving it quite clear that this action is always a choice of the character. Maybe that is why so many dislike it? I don't know.
                                    Are some Beasts abusers? Sure. Like every Kindred with Ghouls, or a Herd, or abusing their power to, well, abuse humans and lower-ranked Kindred. Doesn't mean they all are. Some are simply more lucky than others, and exploring that can be a worthwhile experience.

                                    Re: "Different proclivities"
                                    Finding a way deal productively with deeply seated destructive needs and grasping for agency in one's own life is a story I find interesting, moreso than vampire politics, for example.
                                    I understand this is not the case for all people - to each their own, live and let live. Where does this constant need to put down Beast come from? If one dislikes something, one only needs to simply stay away - no more contact with the contentious topics or problems or sheer insanity of the others means more happiness. Why willingly drive into this again and again and again?


                                    Not saying Beast is perfect (nobody's ever said this on this forum, afaik), quite the opposite. The sharpest criticism comes from those among us who adore it the most, I'd say. "A Beautiful Mess", was it? But if we see worth in it, want to elevate it to what we believe it could've always been, why not just wait and see? Why this constant needling? Not rhetorical, genuinely trying to understand here.​

                                                        Another point.

                                                        CofD games seem to have a Light part and a Heavy part. The Light part is the ‘fun’ part where we get to be monsters trucking with other monsters, playing out varying degrees of dark fantasies and gallows humour. The Heavy part is the part where we look at the ramifications of monstrosity interacting with humanity, and hopefully find catharsis.

                                                        The Heavy part is what makes the CofD games distinct from other games, but the Light part is what makes us try them in the first place. More importantly, we can appreciate the games solely from the Light part, without diving deeper in to the Heavy. Indeed, not all people bother to do so. (And this is not some sort of intellectual laziness, or some sign of immaturity, or some sort of social injustice. It’s just how people are.)

                                                        Hunter follows the above formula. Get in the game for grittier-than-usual urban fantasy-ish monster hunting in our own world. The No Fantasy Filter On Violence part? The violence here is actually on the Heavy part. (“Reflection on vigilante violence and conspiracy
                                                        theories”? That’s Heavy as you can get be!) Hence the lack of ‘filters’ matters much less, because the people seeing it are already at the point of having fine-tuned their gamer minds to look at it.

                                                        Now Beast. Beast’s Light part is being a nightmare monster. Who… hurts and shocks people. Okay… that’s quite a hurdle to just appreciating the game on its own merit. The ‘filter’-less part slams people straight from the Light part. This requires some explanation from the Heavy part to make sense and be appreciated. Except that the Heavy part is, as said above, not the place most people go to when the Light part already failed to hook them.



                                                        Now, the existence of Beast fans and devotees obviously means that the ‘formula’ isn’t an infallible axiom or something. Never intended to present one! But I believe it’s a model that explains a lot.

                                                            Originally posted by Psicose View Post
                                                            The impression I got is that the book gives you that you should try to be the apex or use your skills to rise within an organization while you deal with your own needs and other supernaturals, but I imagine they fit better being part of other groups.
                                                            Broadly speaking, yeah, the main things one aims for as a Beast before they've burned all their bridges involve being gregarious among the local monsters and ingratiating them to you by facilitating their feeding and continued activities. The useful thing about the combination of Family Dinner and the Hungers is that they allow the Begotten to be notable lieutenants and fixers as well as End Bosses — the corebook's material focused more on characters being prominently infamous and upsetting the apple cart, which makes it a little harder to get a grasp on the day-to-day operations of a hive's Beasts running stealth, much like Mummy does not actually work under the assumption that its titular characters will be throwing around apocalyptic powers at every arising because cover-ups take time for their cults to orchestrate.

                                                            Even without bringing in Primordial Cults from the Player's Guide, a version of Beast that had some iteration of the organization mechanics to work with would probably look notably different if only because it would have a counterbalance in the full breadth of starting Satiety ratings a character can have — whether you're pulling the strings or running interference, lots of supernatural societies (especially ad-hoc ones) need enforcers, acquisitors, investigators, and otherwise persuasive individuals who can tie a bunch of disparate weirdoes together.

                                                            As you get stronger and hungrier it becomes harder not to burn bridges without sectioning the other end off from the mainland, but a better-managed go at the material would theoretically strike the balance between "despite everything, you and these monsters understand each other" and "make no mistake, though, they are terrible people, because it's hard not to be in these circumstances and one takes their joys where one can." Making the right friends and stewarding one's family ties means making moves to control the streets around a haunted alley, preparing bribes and/or blackmail to keep witnesses from ratting out your sewer-gator kin to Animal Control, riding along on a vampire's club night, and managing your relationships well enough that maybe-just-maybe those three things can get along and find common ground with the animal magnetism of your greedy toothy lindwurm-Horror what's currently doing a brisk trade as a local mascot/memetic grafitto. Eventually — for some sooner than others — a Beast will fail to keep up in the Red Queen's Race of making sure the lights stay off (or simply get sick of doing so) and hurt the people closest to them enough that they can't run it back, at which point they're back on the uglier side of monstrous upkeep like any other monster who hasn't got a secret society to help support them.

                                                            The tricky thing about the game where you play the human face of a storied cryptid is that leaning too hard into what the storied cryptid does is as uncomfortable as leaning into the dark side of any other splat, and that's usually reserved for climactic points in the story for a reason.
