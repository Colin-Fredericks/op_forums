            Originally posted by Mangaholic13 View Post

            Wormwood There are some problems with that hacker idea: When a place has it's security compromised will likely fire whoever was originally in charge of security.

            Also, that Priest from the sample had a brood member who likes to take those vow breakers, dangle them off a bell tower and threaten to drop them if they don't renounce the church, so that guy really isn't to good

            ...Though, said fired security expert could then make a good potential antagonist...
            I'll admit to not thinking that through completely (much as a Beast might stop at thinking 'no harm done, right?' ), and as I said in another thread, showing someone you have information about their misdeeds might have consequences, ranging from good (e.g. a couple talking out their problems; rare) to bad (e.g. overreact and break up in a nasty/accusatory way; probably more common) to catastrophic (e.g. someone becoming paranoid, dropping in work productivity and thus losing their job; uncommon?).
            Regarding the hacking: well, in most companies I know you'd only lose your job if the security breach was due to incompetency. If it was something like a zero day exploit or unknown method that was not easily prevented (without impeding running business), there is little anyone could have done against it, and if it was known, it is still not uncommon that the client takes a risk (e.g. following scenario: "We found a security risk, shutting it would however impede x and y. We are working on a replacement for x and y that will not be affected by shutting the security risk down, however, that will take z days. Option a: we shut it down now, and take the fall in revenue resulting from not being able to do x and y properly. Option b: we keep it open while we work on the replacement, and hope for the best.". Taking b is more common than you might think.)

            That priest is not responsible for his brood member's actions, is he? (Trick question, of course he is since the two grouped up that way). What I really mean to say is: taking the concept priest without his broodmate makes him a far less intense form of feeding, which is basically what was asked for.



            Now, something completely different: You said "that guy is not good". Of course not. No Beast is good. Applying human morality to a non-human entity is messed up in the first place, but black-and-white does not really fit into ChroD as a whole. Beasts feed by imposing their will on others without their consent, and while that can range from stressing people out to making them dread revision day, it can easily include violence and bodily harm. This is not to say that Beasts are necessary evil, though. You might dread revision day, but because you're scared so shitless of that, you don't procrastinate but work your ass off, develop amazing skills, motivate your people, and your guys make enormous earnings that year, getting nifty things like promotions and salary increases (or at least nice bonuses). A guy dies, but since he was head of a human trafficking ring and rapist, he won't really be missed. Neither of those things is good, or even okay, but we are more likely to accept them due to a perceived pay-off.
            All protagonists (to a degree even Hunters) are monsters. They transgress, violate and overstep social bounderies. What is okay to do, what is not okay to do does not apply to them, because their frame of reference is different, or so they claim. Some try to retain human morality, others dive into their monstrousness and justify what they do by some sort of "That's natural".
            The truth is simply that they have an actual need they have to satisfy or face the consequences - even good intentions can pave the road to hell, whereas even an absolute asshole could do a lot of good by accident (Nice Job Fixing It Villian! is a Trope for a reason).
            In some games, and for some people, Feeding in an overt and obviously evil manner is fitting and called for. In others, you might want some more dubiosity. One way to make that possible is by introducing good intentions (Common with Nemesis going Punisher/DareDevil and the like), good consequences (You're a piece of work as a trainer and people are scared to death of you, but they keep coming because while you put them through hell in training, you also make them WIN) or less severe forms of feeding or a mixture of the above (e.g. the Ugallu that raises through the ranks of a Mega-Corp. Everyone competing is a scheming, manipulative asshole - and yet, it is you that will slowly rise to the top and take the power bit by bit. Because this is what you want: make them tremble before you, before, during and after you fought for the some position, just to let them know "Yeah, I know you wanted it. I felt like taking it, though, so it is mine now. I could fire you for the shit you pulled, you know....? Kidding, kidding. Don't worry. I won't fire you. Yet.").

            I may have misread this (apologies in advance if I did), and I do not mean to come off as antagonizing (I often do, German works differently from English and makes me often sound rude online).

In the former he hires himself out as a secret shopper to the local Mage Orders (primarily the Guardians of the Veil) in order to teach young Awakened the value of teamwork and cooperation. He leaves a warning for the cabal phantom thief style and gives them a timeframe of when he'll break into their Sanctum and steal a valuable item from each of them. If they can catch him or prevent him from stealing the items then they win and hopefully come away from that wiser. If he can steal their valuables then he wins and keeps them, hopefully showcasing what they did wrong in the process so when lives are really on the line they can properly work together. Although, if he wins he does get to keep their treasures. That is what's first agree'd upon when the cabal's mentor or Order hires him.

But there's a higher chance of collateral damage as while his point is to steal magical relics from mortals who don't know what they're getting into, there's always the off occasion that those mortals really do need those relics to either save themselves or their family from some supernatural threat they would be impossible to do otherwise. By stealing these relics the Beast tries to save them from the supernatural, but in doing so dooms them instead. Which I think is still good because the aftermath of such a mistake can lead to some fantastic story moments with the Beast trying to balance his Legend with his Life.

                                        The answer to this question is less about how the player feels towards the feeding method, and more about what kind of character they're trying to play. Some Beasts need to hurt people in order to sustain themselves, that's just how they are. But that doesn't mean that every Beast has to do it, too.


                                        The only Hungers that have a frequent need to engage in physical violence is Prey and Ruin. The others might use it as a last resort, or when they're too desperate to come up with an intricate performance and just need a quick meal. So there are ways to you can go about imparting lessons without having to actually hurt people on the physical level.


                                        Generally speaking, the process is supposed to be intense. Beasts feed on the terror of their victims, and the most rewarding meals come from when the victim learns something from the experience. It's not required, but teaching the victim a valuable lesson helps prevent the Beast from becoming an unrepentant psychopath. Jump scare tactics aren't going to be imparting any deep wisdom upon the victims, especially in recent years where the horror genre is saturated in that kind of stuff.

                                        You need a scare that shakes them on a personal level, so that they reflect upon their situation and learn how to improve themselves.
