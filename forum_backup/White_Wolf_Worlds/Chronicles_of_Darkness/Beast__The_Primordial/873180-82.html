        Thematically Lair traits are about the Beast's Lair being a hostile environment for their enemies. I feel they wouldn't help others heal, or the Beast heal for that matter - that is the purview of spending Satiety or an Atavism. A Lair trait about healing is more likely to be one that prevents it, or causes uncontrolled and damaging cell growth or mutations. When looking to make my own I took the average effects of the existing ones, the bonuses or penalties they levy, and the example extreme environments as the guide on what is balanced. Ultimately playing with them in combination with other traits and abilities in the game is going to be the real test but as long as the ST and all the Players agree it can be used, then have at it.

                Originally posted by Aeveth View Post
                I mean, what are the limits? how much is too much when trying to give bonus using lair traits specially when it comes to Major traits?
                The modifier is presumably the same, because a modifier to Skill rolls and a modifier to Attribute rolls or derived traits are substantially different in scope. Assume, as with Circumstance and Equipment, anything flatly adding or subtracting more than three dice is a Big Fucking Deal, like the Darkness Lair Trait representing it being pitch dark in the affected areas. It's likewise safe to assume (for reasons discussed anon) that any bonuses a Lair Trait offers are just things about the environment that someone could exploit against vulnerable targets, like how Wanton, Competitive, and Bestial make it easier for certain actions rolled against you to be exceptionally successful.

                Originally posted by Aeveth View Post
                And there aren't places that creatures can draw power from? that instead of a harmful trait it turns to be a possitive one for the creature? Like a Locus for Werewolves or a Demesne (is that the word?) for a Mage
                There are places that creatures can draw power from, but the qualities of those places don't represent themselves directly through mortal experience the way the various stage hazards common to Begotten soulscapes do. Humans don't know that there's a locus of pain in the werewolves' territory that's been ritually strengthened to make everyone on the beach uncomfortable near the rock where an unfortunate swimmer stumbled upon a float of box jellyfish, but they sure can tell that the water and sand there is Stinging to the touch.

                Of the published Lair Traits, five aren't direct hazards (all of them from the Player's Guide); two of them are upgrades of another two, three of them are just ways to draw the Horror's attention to a particular character, and the remaining two are ways to give your Lair native inhabitants that aren't the Horror. The trend is consistent: Lair Traits make the Beast more powerful by being dangerous things they can bring into a scene without fear of direct harm to themselves, because they are part of the hunting ground that their Horror uses to bring down its prey.

                Until you get into the realm of Incarnation, the Legend a Beast embodies is a creature of nightmare who brings some form of harm or ill upon the people it directs its hunger toward. Boons in the standard Begotten toolkit skew toward making other monsters' magical powers work better through Kinship and terrifying people into particular forms of effectiveness through Nightmares. More involved fare requires borrowing from unique phenomena or outside sources or throwing yourself into the part of the endgame that lets you rewrite the narrative in your favor.

                                I'm not saying it isn't possible to turn Lair Traits into benefits for some characters. The most obvious example is a Lair Trait (say Darkness) which only affects one side in a conflict because the other side happens to be immune (because they don't rely on sight) or are made immune to the effects thanks to the Beast. It's not directly affecting anyone in a beneficial sense, but can absolutely be made beneficial through circumstances.
                                So I'm really not opposed to Lair Traits that can be used to people's advantage, but they still need to be a hindrance or danger first and foremost. You brought up Hoard. The main effect is that interacting with the Hoard attracts the Horror. The fact that you could, if fast enough, carry some of the false treasure away and spend it is a side effect that requires effort to facilitate. Note that the treasure is still false. You're never actually wealthier by stealing it; you're just able to pass it off as real for a short while.
                                Your Healing example, however, is the exact opposite. It's a beneficial effect that can be used as a tool for horror if the Beast sets up the right circumstances for it. Unlike Hoard, the "treasure" is absolutely real and by itself it's almost always a purely positive effect. The Beast needs to put in a lot of effort to gain anything thematically relevant from it. (It's also not a particularly good lure since it's only noticeable for a small subset of the people it can affect, and only if they experience prolonged exposure to the Lair Trait. Basically, only people with amputated limbs who live within your Lair will notice the lure.)

                                I do like the Joyful example, though, but that's because the way I see it the main effect is that it's distracting for the victim. Much like Hoard it's turning something that's often seen as positive (joy/treasure) into a direct and tangible drawback (distraction/attracting the Horror).

                                                    Then you may want to consider that the solution might not be to try and lean on the part of the game with an attached rule called "Environmental Immunity" for beneficial character aspects instead of the part of the generalized framework where bonuses that aren't managed by other purchaseable Traits go.

                                                    Beast's corebook has some problems with it, being the first edition of a new gameline whose initial development was not as together as it could have been with systems that were still relatively new to the overall mechanical structure; it is not difficult to extrapolate that, given two big batches of Lair Traits at either end of a three-year release span, the corebook mentioning bonuses is a mistake. Taking the one-off mention of non-negative modifiers as guidance for coming up with new Lair Traits is obviously not going to help you in this context.

                                                    Lair Traits are things about the environment that make the Lair the favored hunting ground of the Beast and other monsters like them. The ones that represent natural phenomena work like those natural phenomena, down to replicating their increased or decreased effectiveness against beings for whom those phenomena would be more or less effective; the ones that don't represent natural phenomena are minions, methods of denying respite, and ways to target the Horror's reach and attention further.

                                                    The presence of Lair Traits makes a Beast's Atavisms — the supernatural qualities representing more-or-less bodily aspects of the Begotten's Horror — easier to leverage without burning fuel. They are not themselves the things to use for "the Beast is better in this one circumstance," because that is not an environmental quality in the same way a Hollow being a changeling's Home Turf or a city block being a vampire's Feeding Grounds is; a Beast derives advantage from their Lair by being able to live in it where most others would not — it is a place in the collective soul of humanity set aside for them as close to the mind-scouring breath of the world-soul as mortal minds can exist without constant exposure to the Mother's Land.

                                                    It is geography, and that means it doesn't provide personalized boons or direct mental effects, because those are what Atavisms and Nightmares and Kinship abilities are for in the Begotten toolbox. Trying to bend the Chamber-agnostic qualities of the Lair into bonuses is going to give you trouble in a game where the core of your magic comes from filtering down the power of a land that communicates in pain and hunger and fear into a cryptid/urban legend descended from Fear Herself.

                                                    Bonuses personalized to the Beast are Merits or Atavisms. That is what those things are for.

                                                        To try and address your root question about balancing Traits: there isn't a lot of guidance on how to design them in general. The best guide is to just look at the official traits. However, this is more difficult if you want to frame the Traits as beneficial, since there aren't really any official beneficial traits. That said, when it comes to direct bonuses, you're probably safe with just inverting the penalties that similar Traits give. So I think a +3 to Stealth rolls (like you mention above) is well within the bounds of what a Minor Trait could give, probably even on the low side. However, this is less helpful if you want to include more unique effects like the aforementioned healing. Part of the reason I suggested using Merits is that it's easier to price a particular effect in dots than it is to cram said effect into the loosely defined Minor and Major Trait categories.

                                                        To repeat, Lair Traits in general don't have brilliant design guidelines, so creating custom ones, beneficial or not, is going to be more art than science. Trying ideas out in play, and getting other's options, is the best way of achieving balance.
