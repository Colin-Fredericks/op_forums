    [Beast] Help: I Don't See How Beast Deconstructs the Monomyth

    06-23-2015, 05:51 PM
    So Beast's supposed to be a deconstruction of the monomyth, but I don't really see how it does that. I've seen some comments that it does this by killing the Hero, but that seems to me like it just confuses Heroes-the-Character-Type for Heroes-the-Narrative-Role. I should admit that I'm only familiar with the Campbellian form of the monomyth, but things still seem to line up quite well. What am I missing? Aren't Beasts just the subject of the Hero's Journey?

      Beasts are a large step in the monomyth, namely the Abyss and the Threshold Guardians. However, those archetypes, when you think about them are actually really constricting. If you're a Threshold keeper, you exist to be overcome in a build of dramatic action and then promptly forgotten about. If you're a Abyss, all the impact you have is helping define who the Real Main Character is, which while important, also condemns you to being forgotten about and cast aside. Simply by being more than their roles and often directly objecting, Beasts cast the whole thing in a darker light.

      On the Heroic side of things, though, it also shows just how...myopic it is. It's assumed by the monomyth that the main character is ultimately the star of the show; there may be other good guys, but they're supporting roles to bow in and out as the situation requires. The thing is, that kind of attitude, put into the real world, is narcissism. More than that, the very fact the Hero is the main character also somewhat babies him (and I am using male pronouns quite deliberately, because the half-baked kind of monomyth Heroes embody has a lot to do with the most poisonous aspects of masculinity): because he's never called upon to make self-reflection and personal development, he never really progresses. The very fact the story's about him prevents him from following it to its ultimate conclusion, just going around in circles at steps one or two. The Hero assumes any problem can be solved via strength rather than wisdom, knowledge rather than self-awareness. About the only problems that solves are the Beasts themselves, sometimes, and even that's a bit iffy, because again, the Beasts are more complex than simply being the Guardians or Abysses.

      In effect, they're the danger of thinking in terms of monomyth without nuance, in that if you just use the monomyth itself instead of simply seeing it as a pattern or deliberately toying with it, you miss the point; the Return with the ability of the Hero to teach others to be Heroic. And a Hero who doesn't share, doesn't evolve, not because he can't, but he doesn't want to...

      Well, Doctor Doom is a villain for a reason, despite being a savior to Latveria. He can't let himself see beyond his own Beast - that would require understanding that he screwed up somewhere.

      (Speaking of, really boring superhero stories tend to make that mistake too-a criticism of the monomyth as an aspect of American culture alone I found outright says that a lot of the monomyth-based stories is preserving the status quo. Not always a good thing).

      EDIT: Also, I just found that another big thing is that the monomyth tends to confuse solving problems with personal growth. That...can be really scary sometimes (see what would happen if Orpheus, instead of realizing he missed his shot and moving on, declared war on Hades to get his wife back).

              A major part of The Hero's Journey story is that, after slaying the Beast, he's supposed to gain some kind of wisdom, elixer, or something that helps improve the day-to-day lives of the people. In part, that's where the save-the-princess trope comes from- they're restoring the system of order and government to people's lives.

              In fact, that elixer, princess*, whatever it is? The McGuffin? That's the real point of the journey, the monomyth. Heroes never really achieve that Mcguffin in the real world. There's no universal fix-all. In fact, we can argue that the mcguffin is just a stand-in for wisdom and learned lessons. And Heroes are specifically the ones who DON'T learn the lessons the Beast is trying to teach with their nightmares.

              In a way, Heroes are actually failed heroes where those who learn the lessons of fear are the ones who've successfully "slain" the dragon and gained the lesson-mcguffin. That's why the Heroes are low Integrity and trapped in an endless cycle, unable to go on. They bypass the pearls of wisdom for swine, and keep moving on and on, looking for the "real treasure."


              EDIT -
              * Yes, I realize I'm reducing "princess" to an object here. That's the issue with the monomyth - we're dealing with abstract symbols, not real beings. A princess isn't a person, she's the governing body. I don't like reducing people to objects, but that unrealism is one of the things that's being deconstructed in the game. Realizing we're not dealing with symbols, but actual flesh and blood people

                      Not really? A Beast certainly doesn't have any specific Call To Adventure, though they do have a moment of awakening/rebirth (possibly aided by a mentor figure) that truly makes then a Begotten. Each one has her own life (and Life) with details and a myriad of possibilities that exist outside the boundaries of the monomyth.

                      It may still exist, but their role in that, the role the world tries to force them to play when their actions slip into what humans expect of it, is not that of the adventurer/hero. When the monomyth intersects with their existence, they are drawn into the place of the monster, provided to be at best an example and at worst an obstacle. Beasts contest this, both because they are more than what the shallow model of these stories say they are, and because they were originally of much greater importance in the /proper/ takes on the tales (or so Beasts believe).

                      They have a place in myths and stories, but it's not that of the hero. The fact is this: Beasts fight the pull of the monomyth because they are more than what it says, and they know this; because they have the awareness that going along with it is being nothing more than a slave who gives Heroes reason.

                              Just because Heroes are flawed and mistaken campbellian Heroes doesn't make the Beast a successful one. It's not really a "modern interpretation" either. Beast The Primordial is a subversion of the narrative because its protagonists are in many ways the exact same figures stories cast in the role of the monstruous antagonist or malicious obstacle without caring for what they are beyond that (and these stories include more than those which fit in the monomyth model), and because it is integral to their conflict as independent characters they possess the ability to defy expectations and have their stories outside of the usual narrative, despite what they truly are.

                              If the book is a deconstruction of the monomyth, it's in the sense that it criticizes it. The Begotten defy its restrictive and straightforward generalizations, and Heroes in its cosmology, especially in the context of their relation to the Primordial Dream, contest the assumptions of the structure of the monomyth, as well as critique the shallowness that the persona of the hero so often possesses, reduced to solving issues through violence and little else, even if that's not the only thing that may be in the tale.

                                  Maybe people are having problems with this question because they misunderstand what deconstruction means?

                                  Deconstruction means to nothing more than an in depth analysis of the story. Naturally, since this is a game, we analyze the myth through playing it. We play it by having a reinterpretation of the story as the setting. In this way, its a deconstruction, no matter if it was a subversion, reconstruction, or modern interpretation, simply because the analysis is a natural consequence of playing the game. That's all there is to it. We're looking at the different parts of the monomyth as they come up in play.

                                  Its not like we're talking about opposite terms here. Deconstruct, modern interpretation, subversion, and reconstruction are not opposites here, they're not mutually exclusive terms. Honestly, they're all part of the same thing.

                                  I really hate reducing things down to dictionary definitions, but that's what it sounds like to me. A misunderstanding over the terms used. We can't really have a discussion if we're all misunderstanding what each other say.

                                                      Well, the monomyth, in my opinion, can easily be described as "basic storytelling structure." You have an introduction, challenges, a climax, and then a conclusion; no surprise, since the monomyth is a literary analysis of stories, not a cultural interpretation. But I think we're starting to move into Changeling territory here.

                                                      Despite the occasional reference to stories, I don't think that Beast is really about stories, or rather, myths and legends. Yes, you're a "legendary monster" modeled after those from mythology. But I feel like that's the extent of it. Beast is about choosing your own path in life, just like its about choosing your own family, and choosing what being a monster means. I suppose one could say that its all about choosing your own story instead of a pre-determined path. Its all about Nurture over Nature and self-determination.

                                                      The entire monomyth thing is a distraction, because the point of the game is to deliberately defy any such classifications. Its not like Changeling, where you take up classical roles. Its where you do your level best to shred the roles into smithereens. Its where people try to tell you that you can or can't do something, and you do it anyways. Its about overcoming bullies, shrugging off cultural and peer pressures, and being your own person.

                                                      Its about giving the monomyth the bird and walking off into noon day sun, because eff the sunsets, you're better than them.

                                                          Originally posted by MCN View Post
                                                          I really hate reducing things down to dictionary definitions, but that's what it sounds like to me. A misunderstanding over the terms used. We can't really have a discussion if we're all misunderstanding what each other say.
                                                          That's fair. I wasn't trying to come off as claiming the definitions aren't all similar (they are, and they're pretty close to each other), but for the sake of clarification, this is what I meant:

                                                          "Modern Interpretation": Reusing the story elements and framework, but in a modern setting, replacing outdated forms with current ones. Romeo And Juliet is a pretty overdone example, just change the feuding noble families into rival gangs, warring mafia families, or rival family managed corporations. The monomyth in Beast to me doesn't fit in this, because its most important elements are founded on fantasy notions, rather than necessarily medieval or anachronistic ones, so work in nearly every time period. I do know this is probably not what Rick meant by it, but for the sake of clarity.

                                                          Subversion: The deliberate challenging or inversion of notions common to the genre and the exploration of what it means for the story. Beast does this with its most basic element of the premise. In a game with heroes and monsters and a cycle based around their conflict, you play the monster, not the "hero".

                                                          Deconstruction: Taking apart the literary devices that make up a genre or type of story, critiquing them, and reassembling them in a new way, exploring the narrative in a manner that is not usually done (far as I know this definition is also used in cooking). Beast is this in how it discusses the monomyth, the relationship between Beasts and Heroes, and the standard ideas relating to both.


                                                          But, as MCN said above and others (myself included) already pointed out before, one of the points of Beast is precisely in defying the regular ideas. and I would go as far as to say that one of the driving struggles of the Begotten is their fight against the classifications thrust into them as a matter of fact.
