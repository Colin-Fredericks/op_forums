    Sell me Beast

    01-12-2016, 09:50 AM
    Thread like this popped up in the Changeling forums and it was a joy to read, so I figured I'd post the same sort of idea here because I am on the fence regarding Beast. I read the blurb and it seems pretty awesome but I want to know people's actual experiences the game. I also want to know the places where the game lacks, the houserules that you've incorporated, the stories you've told, your favorite character ideas, etc. I want to know as much about the game as I can before I decide to buy it. So please, sell me on Beast.

      Beast is probably OPP's most ambitious Chronicles of Darkness game line as it tries very hard (and mostly succeeds) to position itself as the "cross-over game" line. And the following is a "warts and all" kind of assessment.

      Unfortunately many of its core themes are a bit on the underdeveloped side. Beasts have kinship with some (but not all) supernatural entities but for practical purposes this kinship works best with outcasts from the other splats. It plays with the "I choose my family" theme but doesn't deliver on how that impacts the family left behind (failing to engage with many of the most drama producing themes of commitment and obligation that actual "family" has to deal with). There's also a whole divisive aspect to how the game's fluff evolved over the summer (you can dig those discussion up here and elsewhere -- i.e., the rpg.net forums). There's also the 1 ounce of fat on the 8 ounce steak that takes the form of repeated ephemera rules (from the God-Machine Chronicle) that add nothing to the overall game. That's more of a missed opportunity to flesh out the Hive and Primordial Dream and the entities that populate them than some kind of huge negative.

      As far as I know there's also an extant problem with chapter 6 (being the wrong version?) of the (pre-)production copy being distributed to Kickstarter supporters and via DriveThruRPG. (This may have been resolved or it may be the case that that portion of the text reverted to the original draft. Not sure that anyone at OPP has remarked on that one way or the other -- I might have missed it over the holidays.)

      It is the worst of the 2nd ed game lines produced so far but, you need to take that statement with a grain of salt. While it isn't as good as Demon, Vampire, or Werewolf it is still head and shoulders above most of the 1st edition new world of darkness game lines. And I'm confident that a good storyteller's guide will patch most of the issues because it's primary problem is lack of available word count to fully address the many themes that its ambition demands of itself.

      Outweighing the veritable laundry list of "problems" I've listed, the game provides the very, very large benefit of providing a structured way to run cross-over games. Among other things to consider is the relationship that Beasts have among themselves and among other supernaturals. All in all, Beast presents a very valuable addition to the book shelf of any storyteller whose troupe is interested in cross-over play while also providing many opportunities for its own novel play. (Don't let its imperfections act as a barrier. Consider if it fulfills a role at your table.)

      For my own setting (already heavily crossed-over) Beasts are a highly dynamic population. As predators among themselves, they strive for power. However, since the mechanics tend to level the playing field between high and low powers this means the top dog of a hive is tossed over relatively frequently. Beasts group together in clan-like groupings (not unlike the structures that samurai families built in the 10th to 15th centuries). Those Beasts who interact with the big 4 (Vampires, Werewolves, Mages and Changelings) tend to associate with outcasts (and end up hunted along with them) or end up as "pets" for powerful members of those societies. Those Beasts who interact with the less hegemonic supernaturals (Monsters (aka Prometheans), Revenants (aka Sin-Eaters), Mummies, Demons, and Deviants) have relationships that are more egalitarian. I should mention that this divides the Beast community in my setting between "purists" who only associate with other Beasts and work the top predator of the Hive system and "slummers" who associate with other supernaturals to take advantage of the easy feeding opportunities that Kinship provides.

          Beast is ambitious in a different way in that the titular characters are predatory, but their predation is enacted without distancing factors. By which I mean Beasts feed (mostly) through human actions and interactions that hurt people just a bit - ways that people are hurt or harassed all the time. Which can be an uncomfortable aspect, leading to the widespread criticism that Beast is a game just about abusing people (which I don't think it is, at least no more so than Vampire). But if you want to explore the dynamics of power, harm, or abuse that lie behind seemingly mundane aspects of life and dark side of humanity stuff, Beast is one of the best vehicles there is.

          At the same time, Beast feeding is incredibly diverse. Each character has a Hunger. One of them is Power: this means the Beast must dominate people to sate themselves. Examples in the game include becoming a corporate shark, lording their way up the hierarchies, making sure every subordinate knows who's boss. Or becoming a judge: it doesn't mean you have to be a hanging judge; the power is the same whether you exercise it fairly or not, and whether the accused is acquitted or not. Or engaging in contest after contest, winning in as many ways as you can. Or being a debating champ (though this example also includes beatdowns of people who respond to losing debates with violence). Or becoming a zealous health inspector, checking every aspect of each restaurant you're assigned to, scrupulously reporting any attempt to bribe or mislead you, and not letting them fob you off even when it might be reasonable. The other four Hungers are just as diverse.

          Consequently, Beast is also a surprisingly good game, despite its outre protagonists, for playing supernaturals who go on living their human lives as much as possible, and interacting with the supernatural world much as mortals do (albeit with more oomph). Which flies in the face of what Jacob said about running very crossover-heavy and supernatural-heavy games, but it can do both (though probably not at the same time).

          As for what you are, you're mostly a human, but instead of your soul, you have a Horror: a monster from a realm of nightmares called the Primordial Dream. The Horror can look like anything you want from the Kraken to Jason - its form is reflected in your choice of Atavisms (one of the main power types, which provide physical powers). It's hungry, and wants to be fed, which is what all the previous paragraphs are about. The more elaborate, specific, or heinous the actions you use to feed, the better the meal for the Horror; you can leave it hungry, but if it's too hungry for too long it goes out into the dreamscape and feeds itself (this mostly means people getting vivid nightmares, but may come with consequences you can't monitor or control). You can fill up, but it's hard to do so while maintaining any semblance of ethics, and if you get too full your Horror gets satisfied and goes to sleep, robbing you of most of your power until you wake it up again. Finding a balance between the metaphysical and moral implications of feeding and hunger - a balance you can live with - is the main psychological question Beasts deal with (their Integrity analogue and fuel stat are the same).

          Another part of you is your Lair. This is a subrealm in the aforementioned Primordial Dream, where your Horror lives. Different parts of your Lair might look like primeval forests, sand-blasted deserts, castles, crushing depths of the ocean, stiflingly hot boiler rooms, libraries filled with books that whisper spidery temptations as you pass, suburban houses that seem relatively normal until you look out of the window, empty streets filled with straggly weeds and the rusted wrecks of cars, and so on. When there's an aspect of the local area that resembles a part of your Lair, you can merge them - which also merges your physical body with that of your Horror. So you can turn into a dragon (if that's what your Horror is), it just needs some prep work.

          As I said, your Lair is part of you, just as much as your Horror and your human self. You can let people in if you like - you can also let them back out - if you like.


          As for weaknesses... as I said, it can be an uncomfortable game. I've come across abuse victims who've read the preview who found it almost triggering - and others who found it cathartic. But it is something you're going to have to think about if you emphasise that side of it. I'd also agree that it doesn't have many differentiating themes beyond family, though to me that actually translates into it having different slants on the themes mortals explore, and those of the World of Darkness as a whole. I find it satisfying in that light, anyway.


          As for house rules. I've not done this in any depth, but the X and Y splats are Family (the flavour of fear your Horror represents), and Hunger. Which seems to me more like two X's - while Hunger can change, it's not common, and not really chosen. So I'd add a philosophical faction too. More or less: Beasts' dreams of their nightmare realm tend to provide them insight, which means leads them to believe their own predations can do the same for humanity (that's in the book); but I'd have a faction that gives that special primacy. One that takes family as the imperative, seeing itself as community builders among other supernaturals. One that gives its human side primacy, living as much of a human life as possible. One that takes its Hunger as the imperative, feeding without guilt or shame (not necessarily without conscience). And probably one that explores the Primordial Dream and its interactions with the Earth beyond their own Lairs and Horrors.

                          The teasing of Transmutations is getting me all excited- count me as another person who loves Promethean. I'm actually playing one in a crossover game right now with a pair of Beasts and a pair of Unchained. It's an interesting group dynamic, and I find as the Promethean that I'm conversely learning a lot and perpetually on edge. Both of the Beasts have Dragonfire as an Atavism. One of them discorporated a GM Angel in two blasts and only suffered a scratch (Hurray for the armour Atavism), so I'm keenly aware that as a Promethean I'm significantly less durable.

                          In terms of Beast, the two players of my game are both very, very different characters. One of them took a page from Secret of the Ruined Temple, which discussed the idea of Beasts long before they were Beasts, and has their PC as, essentially, a reincarnation of an utterly ancient Horror who ruled as a Beast Incarnate until they were taken down by a powerful Seer. They intend to purchase Mimr's Well to represent their ancient wealth of knowledge as soon as they can. Her Hunger is for Prey, but it hasn't really come up yet- Family Dinner means she doesn't have to hunt, really. (We also have a Vampire in the party) She plays as sort of an ancient sage, a hunter with a sense of real decorum and honour that would do a Storm Lord proud. The other is an extremely young dragon in comparison (both of the Horrors are dragons) who looks up to the other in utter worship, and has absolutely no problem whatsoever placing herself at the feet of her elder. The character has a Hunger for Ruin, so our first adventure in a piece of GM Infrastructure really got her going. Her player handles the character with a lot of really immense intensity, which kind of makes you forget she's supposed to be this tiny woman and instead constantly treat her the way you'd treat a dangerous animal.

                          One of the biggest things I've noticed, playing alongside two Beast characters, is the dissonance between what you see and what is really there. They look like two university age women, but you can just tell something is up. The younger can't help but be constantly predatory, the older is always distant in a way that suggests the weariness of old age until she decides to act, at which point you see that there's still a lot of fire left in her. Atavisms really heighten that dissonance- the neatly manicured hands loping off arms in a single blow is a really jarring image.

                                  For me Beast offers a really compelling opportunity to play someone trapped in the role of one of humanity's villains, and to determine how they handle that. Unlike most ChoD games Beast doesn't have a mechanical penalty for acting monstrous, no integrity spiral with associated conditions. If anything, the mechanics tend to encourage monstrous behavior. And there are people out there, Heroes, who no matter who you are or how you act, they're going to see a monster and their first instinct is to kill you. So with everyone telling you that you are the monster, you're the villain of the story, do you agree and embrace your role? Or do you fight it? Becoming truly monstrous is in many ways the easy choice, it's how Heroes are going to see you no matter what, and at least this way you get to have fun feeding. But you can also choose to play a Beast who picks the harder path and limits how they feed, searching for low impact or even net positive methods. Your hunger is always going to make it difficult, but you can try to make the world a better place for your presence in the face of those who claim you're everything wrong with it.

                                  I have a lot of fun devising and playing variations on the second kind of Beast, people who try their best to do good despite a host of incentives to abuse their powers. This is a lot easier if you make friends with other supernaturals. Family Dinner is invaluable for managing hunger, and Beasts have potent mythic abilities that allow them to form symbiotic relationships with most supernats.

                                  And every once in a while it's fun to play a character with power and no compunctions about how they use it, too.
