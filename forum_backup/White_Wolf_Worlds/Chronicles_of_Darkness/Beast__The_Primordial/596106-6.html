    Its possible to live comfortably withotu any real, tangible consequences in pretty much any game line. Set yourself up with a nice territory out of the way of other packs, and you can pull it off easily. Mages lack any drama outside of their obsession with seeking magic. Be a recluse from mage society, live mostly like a human, and viola, no drama. Changelings that kill their fetch and reuinite with their family can mostly avoid most of the Huntsman drama. Demons can move away from any Infrastructure and do the whole Mountain Man routine and not worry about angels in the backarea of nowhere. A vampire can set up a herd of terminally-ill ghouls and guilt-free feed off them down through the years, and set up in some area away from vampire society.

    There's certain buy-ins for every game. Prometheans want to become human, full stop. None of this "better than human without Flux" ideas that are floating around. Mages seek Mysteries. Changelings build new homes so they'll no longer be Lost. Demons engage the God Machine in their search for enlightenment and Hell. For Beast, its the search for Family. That family can be other supernaturals, it can be other Begotten in your Brood, it can be the heroes. Its as much of a drive for them as politics is for vampires and fighting the Fae is for the Lost.

    All of them have the potential to be living comfortably without problems. If the whole looking for Family thing isn't to your tastes, that's one thing. But saying we're lacking the kind of worrisome drama the other games is another.

        I suppose what I want in terms of worrisome drama relates to the Slumbering and Starving Conditions, especially in how Beats are received from the two. With Slumbering, a Beast may have a chance to be rejected due to the fact that they are, effectively, human for the time being. With Starving, a Beast may end up doing something that they didn't mean to as a side-effect of going nutsy-cuckoo and hurting someone they care about. These two ideas tie in very well with the theme of finding family because it deals with the idea that the Beast may do something that will alienate themselves from their new family. It's something minor, but it's an important idea and I feel that it should be expanded upon. A Beast may be rejected by someone they were close to because they were too monstrous, so the Beast feels that they must hide some harsh aspect of themselves when they find someone else. A Beast may end up killing a member of their family, causing them to be abandoned by the people they thought were family. I feel like these ideas weren't expanded upon aside from in the Conditions. If they were in the book, then I must have overlooked them and I would be grateful if I could be pointed in the right direction for them.

        I apologize if my arguments seem to be poorly-formed or cyclical, but it is a hang-up that I have with Beast that I feel has yet to be resolved.

                                I'd focus on a Theme of Victimization, and a Mood of Tragic Absurdity.

                                I would try to turn narrative into Narrative. Beasts believe that there is some outside force (perhaps even the Mother of Monsters) that drive the Horrors and Heroes to be what they are, that tries to dictate that the Horror will terrorize the populace only to be defeated by the Hero who will share his wisdom with the outside world. But the Narrative doesn't fit the real world anymore, especially not the modern one. Perhaps it never did. Teaching lessons through pain and fear without proper authority has be disapproved of for centuries, perhaps millennium. Rare is the Beast who has such authority, rarer still is the one who limits their feeding solely to the exercise of same. In stories wolves can blow in houses and eat their inhabitants as a demonstration on why work ethic is so important, but no one's going to care about work ethic if you do that in real life. The Horror doesn't care - it just wants to eat.

                                (Maybe this is why Demons aren't kin. The Narrative teaches wisdom through pain, while the God-Machine teaches insignificance through dehumanization, or so the Beasts who know of it say.)

                                And Heroes aren't Heroes anymore. They've gone from being Heroic to being Protagonists, from making mankind better to making mankind better because of them. It may seem like a small change, but that shift of focus from the people being helped to the person doing the helping was warped them, broken them somehow. The Primordial Dream stops attracting those who seek to spread wisdom and starts attracting those who need a purpose, who need to feel special in this darkcog world. Whether or not this too is a side-effect of the God-Machine, directly or indirectly, is impossible to tell for sure. Protagonists have stopped seeing people as beneficiaries and now see them as adulators, lackeys, and collateral damage.

                                A Hero's story has an ending. A Protagonist has endless and ever-escalating sequels. When the Protagonist kicks in the door seeking the Beast's head and the Horror lets out a feral scream that is equal parts rage, confidence, and hunger the Beast can't help but feel stuck in a predetermined scenario written by someone who has long since forgotten the original point and just wants to see excitement and blood, anything that will pierce the lull of humanity and hopefully get them to learn something. Of the Beast, the Horror, and the Protagonist only the Beast has a vague awareness of how the Narrative presses on them all, of how they are used for a purpose that often doesn't work as intended. They are the only ones with the self-awareness needed to evade or break the Narrative (and they know it), and to do it they'll have to not only fight off Protagonists but find a way to guide a Horror anywhere but the meat-filled path that leads to oblivion.

                                You want your purpose? It's a tricky position at best and an outmoded rationalization at worse. You want your power? It comes in spades, spades which will dig countless spiritual and literal graves. You want your place? It's not among the humanity you were expecting your place to be but among the darkest shadows of the world. You will have the chance to reach out to monsters and find that even they are deserving of a place to call haven and a group to call family, both of which you can help them build. You can protect them with your powers while still appearing as human to other humans, helping to guard the secrets of your brood. You can be their best friend - and the Horror inside you can be their worst enemy, gladly leading them off a moral precipice if you let it. For all the power the Horror gives you you feel its hunger even more.

                                The Beast will be screaming at you to justify as badly as you need to (or even better, to cut through the justification crap entirely) to reduce the world to victims you feed on and enemies you have to fend off so you can keep feeding. It's not going to be a noble scream, the bellowing of a dragon demanding to display its power. It's going to be an almost pathetic screeching that you take that iPhone right now because he clearly doesn't care enough about it but you will, even when you know you'd just add it to the pile. And in the darker moments your voice will join its. This is absurd, you tell yourself. I don't need to justify myself, and even when I do the world doesn't listen. Screw them. It'll be easy, even tempting - what human doesn't fantasize about bringing reprisals upon others while escaping repercussions themselves? But that's a trap, and if you do that it's over. You and your brood'll enjoy the lack of inhibitions, sure, but only until you bite off more than you can chew and the Narrative summons the actors who will bring your part to a violent end.

                                The Narrative victimizes the Beasts, giving them paths that all say "TO THE QUICKSAND." It victimizes Horrors, birthing them with the instinct to fatten themselves in ways that call in their own butchers. It victimizes Protagonists, giving them their purpose and praises while taking away the humanity that would make it worthwhile. And these three in turn prey upon humanity, leaving them victimized and traumatized far more often than they are left wiser, giving them power that is barely significant at the best of times. Everyone is given power but no one is given the means to use it meaningfully or (for Horrors and Protagonists) even the desire to do so.

                                No one except the Beasts who have a chance to turn away from this, to give themselves a higher purpose that will give them a chance to escape their predetermined fates. If they work together, with themselves and with their kin, perhaps they can say they teach wisdom through pain and not have it feel hollow. Perhaps they can evade or even beat the Narrative and forge a Legend all their own. This is something all Beasts realize sooner or later, something they all tell each other. And yet it is who they are that cries out in a voice so much louder. Can a Beast listen to their families and control their Horror? Do they even want to?

                                I know the 2nd revision makes a lot of inroads towards this, but I really hope they can work towards making all the parts into a cohesive whole.

                                And if players are willing to make themselves uncomfortable, I'd focus on the idea of victim-blaming. To teach a lesson through pain presupposes that the student is deserving of pain. Stories take this for granted, and Horrors will do the same. To a Horror everyone deserves what they get and it is on them to learn to avoid being victims again. Fault and Proportionality mean nothing to a Horror, and they'll gladly exploit ambiguities or even contradictions in human morality, which means a Beast will hear things like this all day:
                                "That guy said his race was better than yours. Teach him respect by breaking his arm in (and break the other one when he calls your kind violent thugs)"
                                "They're ignoring their fellow man. Make yourself noticed. Whatever they drop, you keep."
                                "He's fat because he has no self-control. He's fat because he'd rather feed himself than those starving around him. Show him what it means to be a meatbag."
                                "That one who commits microaggressions? That one who makes everyone else miserable by being hypersensitive about microaggressions? That one trying to silence people who are talking about microaggressions? That's breakfast, lunch, and dinner right there."
                                "That pastor who speaks so proudly of his congregation? Burn his house down and teach him how charitable they really are."

                                For every feeding the Horror will say "they deserved it," whether anything was taught or not. Whether the Beast agrees, when the Beast agrees, and how far the Beast will go to decide who "deserves it" should say a lot about the Beast and how the world around him reacts.

                                    I think I would have focused more on actually being a Beast. Players are actual immortal beasts out of legend who were slowly worn down by the passing of time and diminished into humans, often forgetting what they were for large periods of time. Prosaic reality generally binds you as a human, but Beasts have the ability to force some sort of a "mythic break" where the world around them temporarily changes to become simpler and more storylike -- more vibrant and mythic, but with some essential part of what makes it real in the modern sense suppressed or removed. While this is in effect in their area, they can recall their true selves and take on their beast form.

                                    Heroes are reincarnated figures of legend. Like Beasts, they feel this yearning for a simpler mythic world, but unlike Beasts, they don't have the power to force a mythic break on the world themselves -- they can only do it when attacking Beasts. And some of them (the ones that you're likely to end up fighting, generally) find the rush of that simple black-and-white mythic world exhilarating, so they hunt Beasts in order to go back there. Heroes often see the mythic reality as more "real", as if they're tearing away illusions by hunting Beasts, but the truth is more complicated -- a Beast actually is also a human.

                                    I would generally tone down on the moralizing aspects. Beasts are vast and terrible in their true forms, but I think that the idea of "compulsory horror as part of your nature" is already explored in far more depth elsewhere in the WoD -- and Beasts are meant to be good for crossovers, which in my mind means that they should generally avoid forcing unnecessarily heavy concepts. This doesn't mean that Beasts can't be horrific serial-killers or cannibals or whatever, but I wouldn't give them any sort of hard hunger-to-hurt-people mechanic. Mostly the main mood I'd go for is melancholy. Beasts are beings from another time that no longer really fit into the world. One of the main themes would be the split between accepting that and slowly fading away, or fighting against it in various ways. (Unlike, say, CtD, though, fading away wouldn't necessarily be portrayed as a horrible thing. You are still a beast. When people say that you have no place in the modern civilized world, they're not entirely wrong. But simply forgetting everything and having your Beast nature go into an eternal slumber wouldn't be seen as a good end-goal -- Beasts would still be trying to find a way to exist in the world on their terms.)

                                                Originally posted by mp122984 View Post
                                                I'd focus on a Theme of Victimization, and a Mood of Tragic Absurdity.

                                                I would try to turn narrative into Narrative. Beasts believe that there is some outside force (perhaps even the Mother of Monsters) that drive the Horrors and Heroes to be what they are, that tries to dictate that the Horror will terrorize the populace only to be defeated by the Hero who will share his wisdom with the outside world. In stories wolves can blow in houses and eat their inhabitants as a demonstration on why work ethic is so important, but no one's going to care about work ethic if you do that in real life. The Horror doesn't care - it just wants to eat. . .
                                                I like some of the ideas you're playing with . . . but not all. As I read the text, the role of a Beast is to make humanity react, almost like a supernatural vaccine. And that's the problem with a Hero. The depredations of a Beast force humanity to face their problems, to get stronger, to be their own heroes. But a Hero short circuits the process - if there's some guaranteed "easy" solution to the problem of a Beast, humans learn nothing. They don't get stronger, they did fix the underlying issues.

                                                Way back in the 8-bit video game era, there was one called Lemmings. A variant on the build-a-pipeline genre, you have to get a bunch of NPCs safely through a dangerous obstacle course. The only catch is that they have no sense of self-preservation - a lemming would walk right into a fire or off a cliff. They're literally not afraid of anything (you could even turn them into suicide bombers), so had no ability to keep themselves safe.

                                                And a lot of the supernaturals of the World of Darkness would probably like to turn humanity into something similar, into nothing more than "sheeple". Something you can farm for food or slaves, and which would pose no danger to their supernatural overlords. Already, a lot of them, from the Exarchs to the Seers of the Throne, are pouring a lot of resources into such a project. Humanity can't even see (or remember!) some of the threats.

                                                But a hunting Beast forces humanity to fight back, as if they knew of the danger. For example, its in the best interests of vampires to keep some slums around for easy snacking. Places where life is cheap, and the law doesn't go. But that's exactly where a Beast with a Hunger for Punishment can gorge themselves sick. And to fully sate their soul/horror, they have to go to some pretty extravagant methods. The vamps may want to keep the law out of the slums, but once said Beast starts sending the severed tongues of mob bosses to the President of the United States, or streaming the slow, torturous deaths of drug lords live to the world . . . watch out! There will be task forces, urban renewal committees, exposes . . . Humanity may not understand the threat, but - thanks to the Beast - they're responding exactly as they need to.

                                                Or for a contemporary example, look at ISIS. In the World of Darkness, there's almost certainly a Beast with a Hunger for Ruin is almost certainly involved with them - hence the reason they keep blowing up all those ancient ruins and relics. A lot of people are prepared to just ignore ISIS, since they're so far away, and fighting against equally bad people. But every time this nameless Beast blows up another ancient landmark, the rest of the world suddenly takes notice . ..

                                                So what I'd like to see (for Beast) are rules governing humanity's reaction to a Beast's feeding. At the low end, probably not much. But at the high end, when a Collector has a hoard of grimoires that even an archmage would envy, or a Nemesis hangs dozens of flayed corpses off the Christmas tree at Rockefeller Center . . . What are the stages of humanity's reaction, and how long does it take to force real social reform? And how much does a Hero suppress that?
