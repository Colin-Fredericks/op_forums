                        Originally posted by Rater202 View Post
                        A victim stops being a victim when they get to a point where they plot to murder anyone even remotely like the one who hurt them and take disproportionate revenge, such as plotting to murder the person who gave them a bad dream.

                        Even in the cases where a Hero is one hundred percent justified in killing their originating Beast, once they move onto killing beasts indiscriminately just because they're beasts, or if they cause significant collateral damage or loss of life in the process, they stop being a victim and start being just another monster.
                        Stepping away from the issue of the morality of intentionally inflicting traumatic experiences on people, since we clearly just have very different views on the weight of that, I don't buy that victims ever stop being victims barring changing history. That event, that position, still exists no matter how many horrible things they do. What changes is the weight of that condition in the consideration of others.

                        In fact, I'd argue that this understanding is very important to how Heroes are written in Beast, where so much focus is put on how they lack self-awareness, and how they interpret that initial encounter with the Beast. Part of what makes Heroes so insufferable is that they refuse to see themselves as compromised. Even Thaddeus Pearson, who thinks that everything in his life is someone else's fault, has this problem: It would never occur to him that any of the things "done" to him have changed him. It's why that step to Integrity 5 is characterized as a realization that he or she is "messy, complicated, and unfair".
