                    Originally posted by Aspel View Post
                    Most RPGs actually do still sell things the old way.
                    No. Most individual RPG book sales are done the old way. But that's because the majority of sales go to WotC, Paizo, and FFG (aka D&D, Pathfinder, and WFRP/the new Star Wars license), all of whom have strong markets in physical products that are not (yet anyway) capable of being brought into a digital product.

                    The majority of RPGs are available in various forms of digital focused publications, because the new market place has drastically reduced the barrier for entry for small publishers by removing the traditional distribution system.

                    Of course, plenty of the digital focused publications are available in traditional forms (Onyx Path does that), but if you want to read Beast before you buy it... you can go to its Kickstarter page and do so.

                    If you care about influencing publishers towards making their games available for free and rely on the market to pay them for more than the basic manuscript, you can do that by voting with your wallet and supporting companies that have embraced such strategies. The industry doesn't need people to "prove" non-traditional methods work. We have things like Fate to show that they do (Fate 3e and FAE were released PWYW and Evil Hat still had a hugely successful Kickstarter for a set of products you can legally get for free).

                    You have no way of knowing what happened, because it was settled out of court.
                    Well no, I know what happened. They settled out of court. That's what happened. What we don't know is the specifics of the deal.

                    Settling out of court with some kind of monetary settlement also doesn't mean that the case was valid.
                    "Validity" isn't really an important judgement in IP lawsuits, because infringement is highly subjective even by judicial standards. The strength of the case though, can be inferred from the settlement because we're talking about a tiny publishing company taking on a massive international corporation. Sony could have used legal tactics to bankrupt the old WWP a few dozen times over without it even being a blip on their finances.

                    Did they need to settle for optics? Not really. Sony had public opinion on their side because most coverage of the suit portrayed it as frivolous.

                    Was the possibility of fighting the case in court a significant financial risk to Sony? Again, no.

                    That means that Sony wanted it gone, and felt that crushing WW wasn't a good idea. Obviously it's one of Sony's cheaper options so that could be the reason, but Sony is in pretty much constant IP lawsuits as a company (even if rarely on their creative side of things instead of their hardware and software stuff). The biggest risk for Sony is that WW would have been rewarded part of the profits from Underworld, since they means a cut into the profits of the film and all the sequels; a settlement is a great way to ensure that doesn't happen.

                    The case was farcical because almost every aspect cited was something that White Wolf had been clearly violating from someone else, mostly the Vampire Chronicles.
                    Maybe go back any watch that video again? Because part of the point is that the individual "parts" aren't protected. The goal is to protect the originality of the total product. That's what people completely skip in the "farce" argument. The pick out individual points as, "White Wolf didn't come up with this, that's silly!" missing the reason those points are brought up.

                    The list of points of similarity is to make a case that all the individual points of similarity combine in an original fashion, and that the other product is copying that original arrangement of unoriginal parts. That's what the lawsuit was about. The arrangement of all the unoriginal silly parts, not the individual parts.

                    It's also just as likely that the much larger Sony threatened to grind White Wolf into a fine powder and the company backed off.
                    That's not a settlement. That would be WW dropping the suit. If Sony was willing to go this way, why settle instead of, "we'll grind you into a fine powder so by the time this case even gets scheduled to see a judge your great grand kids are doomed to inescapable debt, so drop it now" because they certainly could have done that if they felt the suit was superfluous enough that it wouldn't backfire on them.

                    I'm also not actually sure how much they actually could enforce such a C&D, since you can't copyright mechanics (which that video points out). But it doesn't really matter if they can or can't enforce them because tiny little people aren't able to stand up to them, as ROMzombie points out.
                    Subnet violated copyright directly by copying text directly. The fact that you can't copyright mechanics - applied to RPG systems - means things like you can't copyright, "gather up one ten sided die for each dot in all relevant traits and roll them." You can still copyright the exact wording of the text. Subnet provided the exact wording of the text.

                    ROMzombie and his Wiki doesn't violate copyright by copying the text itself. Which puts it in entirely different categories.

                    The "little" guys in this situation can stand up just fine; when they have a leg to stand on. Subnet didn't.

                    What I'm worried about the most is homebrew creators.
                    I wouldn't be. Regardless of Paradox's history, the people in charge of the new WWP have been very clear they want to encourage such things; not that they can actually stop it.

                    Also, Project Vaulderie was canned by CCP - the old owners of the VtM IP - and was not consulted about the remake. Project Vaulderie contacted Activision, who made the game, for permission, but not CCP who's IP the game was based on.

                                            Originally posted by Omegaphallic View Post
                                            I fucking hate copyright law and legalese. It gives a head ache reading this kind of stuff.
                                            Intellectual property law can sometimes be dense, confusing and even downright counterintuitive.

                                            However, remember that it's designed to protect the creators of works, not fans of the works. IP law allows everything from music, movies and books to role-playing and computer games to be economically viable. Without it, we almost certainly wouldn't have the White Wolf properties at all (and for various period, we effectively didn't).

                                            In order for individuals and companies to maintain and legally protect their IP, certain rules and procedures have to be followed.                                             Although I'm personally disappointed in the loss of Subnet, more run of the mill fan sites are not in danger, and Paradox can still implement policies and compromises to ensure a active and engaged fanbase, including a return of Subnet, albeit in a more reduced form or different format.
