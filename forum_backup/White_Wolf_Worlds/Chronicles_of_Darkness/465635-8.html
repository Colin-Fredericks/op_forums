                No offense MO, but I get the feeling you haven't thoroughly read the God-Machine Chronicle. The God-Machine is quite explicitly not omniscient nor omnipotent. Its influence is limited only to the physical world, and it relies on complex infrastructure, which takes a great deal of time and preparation, in order to complete occult matrices. It is a powerful, but strictly temporal and limited enemy, and stories about it are meant to focus on the protagonists gaining small victories against it, by exploiting linchpins (weak points in its infrastructure) to destroy occult matricies or prevent them from being completed. There's even a sample story in which the protagonists prevent the end of the world. As for Demon, the GM is definitely not the canonical nemesis of every PC. It's more of a menacing piece of background material that forms the impetus for the espionage aspects. Yeah, you don't want to be found by the GM or its angels, but as long as you take proper precautions, that doesn't even have to be the focal point of the game.

                Now, if you don't like the God-Machine, that's fine, nobody can fault you for that. But most of your criticisms of it are just factually inaccurate, so it might be worth giving a second look, since the reasons you give for disliking it are actually not the case.

                            Originally posted by Magnum Opus View Post
                            How am I supposed to feel I have a chance against this? WoD is not Call of Cthulhu. The fun does not lie in how long I can stay alive. NWoD went above and beyond in order to break from OWoD's habbit that every threat is cosmic-scaled, and then they give us the God-Machine, a giant robot that spans the whole universe and lives in all realities at once.
                            See, I'm very sympathetic to feelings like this — I've never had much of any interest in Call of Cthulhu for pretty much mostly this reason. But one of the big strengths I loved about The God-Machine Chronicle as a book (not the rules update, the full book) was how it's all presented in a format that is very practical about the ground's-eye view of things. The upshot is that the bigness of the God-Machine is its weakness as much as its strength. Its parts are poorly aligned and prone to miscommunication; the right hand doesn't know what the left is doing.

                            It's not monolithic, and so "the God-Machine" can't rewire your brain or do much of anything, really, not in the sense that any agent of the God-Machine has the means to do this. It has lots and lots of projects all around the place, each of which can do some scary thing, but they don't share resources well; one station might want to rewire your brain but have to kidnap you and ship you on an interstate convoy to reach the nearest brain-rewiring station, affording you or your friends plenty of time to bust you out.

                            And that's also where you've got your chance: since "the God-Machine" isn't really one thing so much as lots of loosely connected projects, and your chances against a given project at a time are vastly better than your chances taking on every project in the world at once. Destroying every project in the world is a feat on par with destroying the Exarchs or exterminating the Moon-Banished, but destroying the God-Machine's influence over upstate New York is entirely doable, even for a group of mortal humans (for whom the GMC is designed).

                            The book does a pretty good job of setting down limits for the thing as a whole, too. Its projects mostly span the material realm; it doesn't penetrate very far into the otherworlds by default. There are several story hooks in the book about the God-Machine miscalcualting or getting interfered with and the consequences running amok; it's hardly all-controlling. And every project is designed to have some kind of Achilles heel that presents a practical means of tackling the problem.

                                                Originally posted by Magnum Opus View Post
                                                As far as fighting projects go, I, as a player, do not feel rewarded for defeating a building or a train (as demon-possessed as can be) as much as an agent. Especially if the organization I defeat is only a drop in an ocean, because I understand that the real menace is still looming over and waiting for me to turn my back to reimplement its project.                                                 It sounds to me like it's as much your Storyteller's approach to the God-Machine as the written material that left a sour taste in your mouth. Which, well, the material isn't innocent in allowing those impressions to be made, but you're not generally meant to feel like every project you defeat is a meaningless drop in the ocean, or that the Machine is constantly looming over you.

                                                The Machine isn't meant to be presented as an agent. It's an organization of agents, essentially; the kind that's larger than having one leader to pinpoint, like how the CIA isn't just an extension of whoever is the Director of Central Intelligence. The Machine is not the antagonist for a story; the Screaming Angel is, or the chief economic officer of Eidolon Marketing, or the viral control program living in the city's electrical grid. It's important that the projects aren't just presented as all faceless extensions of the larger Machine; they're staffed by middle managers and angels that have their own personalities and goals and outlooks (yes, even angels disagree on logistics — the God Machine can often end up fighting itself).

                                                The God-Machine book, again, has some good material specifically addressing the issue that you don't want your players coming away feeling demoralized in a futile struggle that will only end when they slip up and die. Projects can't just be replaced; they're based on confluences that don't repeat for hundreds or thousands of years, or cost tons of manpower to reproduce. Each project defeated by an outside force is a major setback, and yes, for that outside force, a lasting victory.

                                                That's the spirit it's meant in. I think the GMC book does a good job conveying it — you don't really need to read two full books cover to cover, the Introduction does a pretty good job right there in my opinion. But I can acknowledge that the information is parceled out such that if you're paying peripheral attention since you're already focused on other stories, you can end up with just a little summary that conveys something different.

                                                Originally posted by Magnum Opus View Post

                                                Most important and most effective game fiction example representative of what an adventure against the God-Machine looks like? Stew Wilson's Go Back, from the God-Machine Chronicle Anthology short story collection. Most notably, while most of the stories in that book are horror stories more appropriate for setting the stage for some PCs to come in and address the situation, Go Back is about a character who ends up ahead in the conflict, and ends the story positioned to begin to push back.
