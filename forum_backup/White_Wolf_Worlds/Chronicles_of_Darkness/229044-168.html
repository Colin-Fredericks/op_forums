    Originally posted by Maina View Post

    Let's try default as "in the absence of extenuating/non-standard factors which may include, but are not limited to, supernatural powers not ubiquitous to the splat or magical wingmen to leech off of."

    That is, a vampire who is not frenzying, who is not on an extreme fringe of Humanity, who has no access to powers that provide alternate feeding methods, who does not have a benefactor that hand-feeds them, will almost certainly feed in one of the two above methods. Violent, victim-making feeding is the exception, not the rule.

    The opposite is true for Beasts. Barring non-standard circumstances (like having a power that grants alternate feeding methods, or having a buddy from another splat that lets him play remora), a Beast feeding is a harmful act, unless I missed a standard power Beasts have to let them feed without extra help from powers or other supernaturals.

    If Beasts do have such an easy, benevolent option, then they are more like Vampires than I gave credit. The part I take issue with, however, is the idea that vampire feeding is innately harmful. The comparison between Beasts and Vampires doesn't bother me so long as it's accurate, so I'm not particularly bothered if it turns out I missed something on the Beast side.

    It'd be rather hypocritical for me to continue to perpetuate bad information on Beasts when I'm trying to stop that very thing vis-a-vis vampires.
    Tbh, on the Beast side of things, there is no culturally mandated expectation of secrecy that necessitates killing, kidnapping, or other morally/ethically suspect behaviors on the part of the Beast. There's no Masquerade to breach, so there's no extra incentive to remove victims or witnesses. And not all feedings by Beasts do lasting harm, beyond causing emotional trauma. Some, particularly the example High Satiety feedings for Tyrants and Nemeses, and most of those for Collectors, are hardly traumatizing at all.

You can't really make the argument that one or two types of Beast feeding harmlessly makes them all, or even most, harmless, when you're using exceptions. I'll admit I've only skimmed; it's why I'm primarily arguing on the Vampire side, not the Beast.

                                                I am going to just throw in my 2 cents on the Beast/Hero debate:

                                                What I got from this book is the idea of a mythic arc drawn from the Astral given flesh. The Beast acts as the central point, providing the "antagonist" and the setting (their lair). They create a "whirlpool" around them that pulls those who lack identity (due to low integrity) into the work by giving them the identity of a hero.

                                                And Beasts are aware, or at least learn, that they are the villains in a story. That is where their claims of victimization comes in: they were cast in the role at birth of being the monster in a story, and they don't find that as fair. Especially if they try to avoid the role that was cast for them, and yet are still forced to play along. They don't view the heroes as victims so much as others caught in the same myth as they are, but the heroes don't fight off the effects. They don't always realize that these humans aren't quite in control, or that they could be rehabilitated, but jit's psychopaths who are playing along with the classic story. Even those who do realize they don't have control seem (based on the way the book is written) to view them as weak minded men and women who were forced into becoming .

                                                And the thing is, the heroes are assholes, at least from the viewpoint of the Beasts and anyone around them. It doesn't matter why or how they became an asshole, because most people aren't aware of the metaphysics driving them. When they blow up a bus to kill the Beast driver, or kill the mage who they thought was serving the dragon (they just grabbed drinks once), both the mortals and supernaturals are going to view the Heroes as jerks. This doesn't mean everyone views the beasts as being good, just that the Heroes aren't good guys. The beasts can be just as horrible as the heroes claim, it is just that the hunters don't realize how horrible they are as well.

                                                And it makes sense that the heroes are so one-dimensional and the story they are in is making them into the role of a myth. The fact that they can recover by reasserting their own identity by regaining integrity points to the behavior as due to the overlaying of the "heroic" trappings in a lot of cases, or exaggerating existing "heroic" traits at the cost of being a well developed human.They act like soliphistic jerks because the story being woven by the beast is making them the center of a story. It is just beasts don't like where that story leads, and everyone else just sees them as self-centered zealots.

                                                A Beasts "win condition" (become Incarnates) is defined as them hijacking the classic "hero fights monsters and win" myth so thoroughly that they reset themselves as the focus of the story (versus the hero). Doing do frees them from creating new Heroes. I think an incarnate is a beast who has taken on the role of both beast and hero, thus ending the cycle. This seems to point to the idea that there is some higher metaphysical element driving the hero/beast dichotomy than just the Beasts nightmares making someone becoming a Beast-hunter.

                                                Then again, I may be putting too much thought into it. I do think that more emphasis needs to be put on the "this is how it is" v. "this is what beasts think is the case", but I don't mind the hero/ beast dichotomy when it is cast in a tragic, myth spinning where only one party is aware of what fate is forcing them towards.

                                                **changing gears**

                                                As for the crossover, I play mage, so the wide open cosmology is an issue. I feel that (as I said above) the "beasts believe" v. "This is the case" could be better defined. I do feel that the book does create the feel that the Beasts don't really understand the mechanics of their world, or at least not in as a systematic way as the mages do, and a lot of their "explanations" are more akin to "myths and legends".

                                                I mean, I could come in as a mage, who after hearing their explanation of kinship, then (ic) ties the Beasts existence to a fallen supernal symbols of primordial fear and epic myths that is trapped in the Astral realm, which shapes souls in a manner akin to awakening and allows for kinship by drawing on the aspects of other splats that relate to those fallen supernal symbols in a manner akin to how mages use the supernal. And the beast (ic) would nod his head politely and later talk about how mages have to make everything more complicated than they have to be, since it obviously is just that we all descend or draw upon the dark mothers powers.

                                                And as for why the other splats put up with them? I don't think they all do on the social level. Or at least they don't view them as family. Mages would likely see a new Mystery to solve, or at best a powerful ally that may not earn him points with the Orders. Vampires likely see a new pawn in the politics, or a hunting buddy. Werewolves see this being that seems like one of the claimed, but isnt, and I could see them kill a beast as readily as have one join the hunt. Sine ates probably would be okay with them (they seem to be okay with most splats, if most aren't okay with them... see claimed and werewolves). And the other splats (except demon) are so rare that each wouod interact on an almost individual basis.

                                                I think the emphas is should be on that the Beasts view everyone as their best buds (except those soulless robot demons/angela, thank you very much), but this doesn't mean that view is reciprocated by the other communities as a whole.




                                                (As an aside, I love how Demon's don't get along with the Beasts and vice versa... on a meta level, it is fun to think of them as 2 of Matt kids not getting along)

                                                        EDIT:

                                                        Originally posted by YeOfLittleFaith View Post

                                                        That was a single example of the many strokes of nuance you might find in the text. Far from the only one or even the most engaging.

                                                        We may have different ideas of what constitutes nuanced writing with thoughtful and emotional engagement, but I still find the best of it in the relationship between Beasts and Heroes. I won't go into detail because, well, at this point it's really been discussed and elaborated enough and I doubt repeating will suffice to change a deeply different opinion.
                                                        It can still help to communicate an idea, though. I clearly could have used more examples of what you meant, because the one Nyrufa mentioned really didn't really address my concerns at all. It's certainly an element of nuance, and one of many situations in which multiple options are presented. However, it does not provide much that much insight into the different approaches and beliefs of Beasts.

                                                        I don't know if Beast actually needed a section on social organizations, but that's usually the part of the book which provides a lot of insight into life as a supernatural entity. That's where Refinements come up in Promethean, and with them a real glimpse at how the Created approach their inquiry into Humanity. It's where Courts are explained in Changeling, and alongside it a sense of the way Changelings react to and try to move on from their trauma.

                                                        Beast talks a lot about the monomyth, but only as a plot structure. It's also about the ways in which people develop as people (which, ironically enough, Heroes don't). Beast talks a lot about humans inheriting the stories of Hercules, but not about the 2014 movie imagining Hercules as wandering hero who may or may not be super strong and have killed supernatural beasts, but is certainly horrified that he killed his children. Beauty and the Beast fits in the game's narrative, but not Monsters Inc. That's what I mean when I say it doesn't feel like a thoughtful and emotional engagement with its concepts. This is also, I think, part of the reason why its treatment of Heroes is rubbing at least a very vocal minority the wrong way.
