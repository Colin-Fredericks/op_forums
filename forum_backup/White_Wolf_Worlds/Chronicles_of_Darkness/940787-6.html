                                                The reason this issue sticks out to me is because the rest of the game so elegantly integrates game mechanics into the game fiction, to the point that this issue in particular stands out by the fact that it's not acknowledged.

                                                For example, you have mages generating conditions with magic to gain Beats. Do Beats exist as a tangible concept within the fiction? No. They're an abstract game conceit. Nevertheless, even though game characters cannot study Beats themselves, they can study their effects on the game world, and conclude that to become stronger, they must overcome adversity. The consequences of a Beat-based experience point system are integrated into the setting, and it even becomes a viable mystery to study. The game mechanics define the setting.

                                                There's other examples of this too. Mystery Cult Influence gives you double returns on merit dots, but that's ok because it's there to encourage mages to be the illuminati, with all that that entails. Concilium/Order status is basically a license to print merit dots, but it's there to entrap your character into the game's politics. Legacy Oblations are like getting a hallow for free on top of your legacy, but that's ok because you have to adopt strange habits that alienate you from others and make you act like a mage. Demesnes allow you to go wild with Reach, but give your character a huge weak point that can be exploited by your enemies.

                                                Every time I find something that seems "overpowered" or an "exploit", I find a good reason for it to be that way. Either it plays into a major theme that the game is trying to emphasize, or it reveals an aspect of the game world, or it sets up dramatic plot seeds. You have all of these other "overpowered" game mechanics that are set up precisely in the hopes that you will exploit them, but not this one. You have those other mechanics that define parts of the setting, but with exceptional successes, you're meant to look away and ignore the setting implications. So to me, the game is purposely setting up players to take on a min-maxing mindset, and then slapping them in the face when they do.

                                                "Take all the 'overpowered' merits. They'll open up tons of interesting plot hooks."
                                                "Take all the negative conditions you want, but be careful they don't come back to bite you."
                                                "Make a soul stone. It'll let you cast that crazy powerful spell. Don't worry about the Seers spy that's been stalking you."

                                                But then it turns around and says

                                                "What, you want to cast your praxis a bunch of times to get mana? What are you, a min-maxer? Get out of here. That doesn't make any sense"

                                                That to me seems wrong. You can't build a good game around encouraging the player to take all of these choices in the pursuit of power, and then say that this road to power is off-limits.

                                                    I'd feel weird both as a player and a storyteller if the storyteller didn't use this as a juicy opportunity for drama anyhow. Imperial Mysteries talk about Siddha becoming concerned when repeated spirit magic thins the gauntlet, implying that the effects of magic aren't always neat or predictable. If a player kept doing this, I'd probably introduce all sorts of fun shenanigans.

                                                    Perhaps there nimbus starts flaring uncontrollably for the praxis, spreading further along their sympathetic connections then there wisdom would normally allow, as the supernal rush of clinching the imago so well every time does *whatever handwaved solution that you prefer - I'd go with bleeds their supernal gnosis over their sympathetic connections like an overfull dam*

                                                    Supernal or ephemeral entities taking an interest, spontaneous opportunities for character development, such as the praxis moving back and forth between a rote and praxis based on a random roll, wierd and unusual conditions such as the spell becoming unsupressable or dispellable for a brief time, mage sight blinding effects (Like an ascended ochema, all mages see when they look at you in mage sight is the same blazing imago) and paradox anomaly esque effects all seem like an invitation to a fun time.

                                                    If you want a mechanical hack, the house rule of once per scene or dramatic tension only or after the first exceptional success in the scene the storyteller chooses the benefit or the storyteller can generate new options seems appropriate. I'd probably go with crossing out each option after using it once in a scene, so players can still get every benefit, but can't experience a beautiful supernal rush thirty times a minute.

                                                    If you want narrative consequences, lots of the suggestions above can give your players things to toddle off and look at under a microscope or learn to cope with the consequences of their actions.

                                                    I'd like to stress that it doesn't have to be a punishment if you and your players are okay with this stuff happening, but my biggest problem with the hack is that it seems really dull, so add some spice.

                                                    Hopefully these suggestions can patch that part of the game for you.
