                Originally posted by branford View Post
                As a commercial litigator who's also engaged in a great deal of public interest work, the defense counsel analogy is inapt (and my pet peeve when lawyers are scapegoats or examples of greed and corruption).

                Representing a murderer or "evil" company is not a lapse in ethics and is not justified by lame excuses about "how the world works" or anything for a paycheck. The USA and most Western countries have an explicitly designed adversarial system of criminal and civil justice. Everyone is entitled to criminal defense and representation by counsel. Each lawyer is supposed to advocate only their client's interests, and any failure to do so is actually considered a serious breach of professional responsibility that could cost an attorney his or her license to practice law.
                I can understand your frustrations - I think every field has its stereotypes that frustrate those within it; mechanics being treated as swindlers, bankers as cold and selfish, and so on.

                As far as a lapse of ethics goes, though, I think you're brushing against two very different philosophies here and two very different forms of moral culpability, which may actually be incompatible. It strikes me suddenly how curious it is that they're expected to co-exist.

                Justice commonly encompasses two ideals, one being that a person should do their utmost to ensure that the guilty are found to be so and made to answer for their crimes, the other that everyone deserves their day in court and the utmost defenses due to the law. The problem is, you can't actually be a lawyer doing the latter without potentially violating the former. A lawyer who defends a client they know is guilty cannot do both; they cannot simultaneously ensure that their client has their rights protected while also doing their utmost to ensure that the guilty are sentenced to be so. Indeed, they abnegate that duty to properly serve their client. At the same time, they can't just tell the police "My client confessed to me" without violating the rights they are due before the law.

                This is without even touching on the other issue; that someone who takes a retainer from a company they know to engage in unethical practices without any guarantee that said company will cease said unethical practices openly and knowingly agrees to provide shelter and advice - albeit within the law - for how their client can avoid reforming their ways.

                There's no resolution to this, I don't think, that doesn't introduce entirely new conflicts of interest to the system, but the Silver Ladder seems to try and solve it with the institution of Lictor; judge, jury, and investigator, rolled into one. Even so, the adversarial system is maintained in many regions.

                I feel the need to add that I have the utmost respect for litigators - as I could probably say for just about any profession - and intend nothing in this as disrespectful; just an observation on the disparate ideals hidden within the common notion of justice; one that really doesn't allow a "win" scenario for lawyers.

                                                            What's the comparison between that and the Silver Ladder? I can't quite piece the connection together myself, so I'm curious what parallel you're drawing between the two.
                                                            I'd say there's a pretty direct comparison between the Quiescence and addiction. Both are conditions that addle the mind and deprive the sufferer of the ability to properly perceive the world. Both are conditions which force the sufferer to reject aid, no matter how dearly they might want it. Both limit the "natural" functioning of a human being.

                                                            Thearchs live in a world of people like that; people who cannot see the Truth and who, indeed, actively recoil from it. Thearchs cannot possibly get informed consent from Sleepers because Sleepers cannot possibly know what they are consenting to; the transformative act of Awakening utterly separates the enlightened from the masses.

                                                            Similar comparisons can be made to developmental disabilities, where someone with a mental handicap might refuse treatments that could potentially assist them in living a normal life or at least a healthier, easier, and more independent one. Can such a person be considered to have the capacity for informed consent to a treatment whose outcome they can scarcely fathom?

                                                            Consent is a concept that really grinds and sparks when there is a differential in power and capacity for understanding between the provider and subject. With the Awakened and the Sleeping masses, that differential is extreme. The Ladder seems to assume that it's extreme enough that informed consent is either impossible or that bringing the world closer to Hieraconis is sufficiently important that it is not necessary. Maybe both.
