    Basic action questions from first timer

    03-26-2022, 07:12 AM
    Hi,

    I am reading through the rules in preparation for our first attempt at CofD. In consequence, I've encountered a few questions (and will probably come up with more down the line) that I have a hard time figuring out.
    • Contested vs resisted actions and the examples that follow
    Page 70 informs that if number of successes in an action is important, it should be a resisted action; if only the failed/passed state of an action is important, then it should be a contested action. Okay, makes sense.
    However, examples of common actions presented on pages 70-72 confuse me, as they seem to contradict what was written and mostly use resisted rolls and do not differentiate how the number of successes is important for them. Even more, reading the interpretations of the results, they seem to be simple failed/succeeded actions and as per the guidelines above should be mechanically used as contested actions.
    Finally, to add to my confusion, the only count of number of successes being a factor is given in the Jumping action, which is a simple Strenght+Athlethics roll.
    Is there a common agreement on how to tackle the resisted/contested test differentiation? I know that every ST is free to do as he or she likes, but in your experience, is it common to go against the basic rule and implement count of successes being a factor (as in the jumping example), or do you mainly stick to the 1 success is the same as 4 successes rule?
    • Teamwork dice pool
    Pg 72/73 instructs that if characters assist in an action, the main actor assesbles a dice pool, the helpers roll the same pool and successes add dice to the main pool. Is the helper's 'same pool' understood as their own dice pools from the same set of Attribute+Skill (thus it can be lower or even greater than the main one), or exactly the same pool (ie it does not care how capable is the helper in the task at hand)? If the latter is the case, it escapes me why it is set up in such a way. Do people often house rule it?

      The guideline for contested/resisted is good. The examples that break that guideline are not. Just go with the guideline.


      It's almost always simpler to just check if the action is successful or not. You could say that more successes means you do it better (1 success means you stumble through it while 4 successes means you do it almost effortlessly) but that's just flavour. Mechanically speaking, it's a pain trying to come up with a scale of how successful you can be based on number of successes whenever you have to improvise a dice pool. Especially since the first step is that you're actually successfully doing what you attempted.

      Speaking of which, the jumping action is a pretty bad example. It works if you want to measure how far you leap (i.e. where leaping at all is counted as succeeding at what you attempted, and distance is an extra bonus), but in play you'd only ever be interested in seeing if you leap far enough. For that, you'd impose a penalty based on how difficult it is to jump that far, then roll the dice pool. A single success means you leapt far enough.


      For teamwork actions it's the same set of Attribute+Skill (or Attribute+Attribute), not the same number of dice. In general, the person with the biggest dice pool tends to be the primary actor, but it is possible for a helper to be more competent.

              The main example of a resisted action is attacking someone with Brawl or Weaponry. Their Defense penalizes your dice pool, and since the damage you deal is based on the number of successes you roll characters with higher defense are harder to land effective hits on.

              Resistance also comes up with some supernatural powers.

                  Don't read these books expecting there to be no errors. They're written by multiple authors who aren't always on the same page, and the developer doesn't catch everything before the book is published. The examples of common actions that use resistance for rolls where number of successes doesn't matter are largely just poor design.

                  (There is one arguable other angle: resistance can be a good option for a roll that doesn't represent a big narrative turning point but still warrants a dice roll. Resistance reduces the number of times you have to roll dice pools and weights the result in favor of the initiator more than a contested roll typically does. This is better for things that inflict minor Conditions or alter dice modifiers, rather than actions that directly shift the action and compel others. A lot of this type of action, mind, is often better without a dice roll at all.)

                          Just for clarification, most of those bad examples draw from how the rules were in 1st edition or in the old World of Darkness. Among other things, number if successes used to be a staple of the system instead of an outlier, you were expected to count those every single time.

                          In practice, ease of play and drama are far more influential on when you'll use Resisted vs Contested actions. Most actions that are Resisted by default are combat or supernatural in nature, though.

                          One way to ease your understanding on this is that Contested actions are exactly that: contests. Even if not conscious on the character part, narratively they pit two actors with conflicting goals against each other, even if one of those goals is to counter the other. So for dramatic purposes, you have two characters trying to actively achieve opposing results and it is interesting to literally see both as active parties in the action, hence both rolling dice.

                          And in practice, most actions that would require such a roll are just that, or wouldn't be rolls at all. Be it a debate, a lie being told, or one character trying to open a door and another holding it, seeing how far each goes in contesting the other to get things their way is more interesting than reducing the roll to a single character, unless this contest is too meaningless for the game. Combat and Powers are exceptions, among other things, because they're already complex systems that will have characters rolling dice frequently, so it is too easy to bog down the game with excess rolls if they have Contested rolls all the time.

                          Now, there's a caveat that must be addressed: not every roll that has another character as a target is any of those. We have a certain tendency to conclude that if you're trying to affect another character, your action should be opposed if not clearly accepted. A great example of this would be trying to persuade someone. But it actually doesn't have any reason to be opposed.

                          Do not oppose actions in any of the two ways if it's whole unnecessary. If the action is something the nature of the target matters to, but the target doesn't actually resist (like hearing an argument), just go with a simple roll (penalized or not). Of the target is too unimportant for the story and unlikely to pose a significant barrier to that action, just do the same. Unless the target seems significant enough to affect the scene, a roll against them probably doesn't need to be either Resisted or Contested.

                          For Investigations and Social Maneuvering, those are not staples, they're optional rules to be used when they're interesting. By default the system is already built to achieve such actions with just a couple rolls, and most of the time this will be enough. When to use them? When the proposed activity is interesting enough in context to monopolize a long scene or more. This usually happens when they check a few requisites:

                          1 - The action shouldn't be possible, or at least be unlikely, in a single roll;

                          2 - Effort can be reasonably made through time to make the action possible;

                          3 - Characters, or at least their players, have freedom deciding how they'll conduct this effort, there's not a defined correct action or series of actions;

                          4 - Going through all of this is fun and the very process is relevant to the story, instead of only the final outcome being relevant.

                              Originally posted by monteparnas View Post
                              When the proposed activity is interesting enough in context to monopolize a long scene or more
                              I'd contend that this isn't quite the takeaway from the subsequent requisites — Mummy uses the Investigation system in its capacity for managing ongoing investigations for both tracking relics and learning Utterances, and Social Maneuvering pretty much by definition isn't going to be managed in a single scene (or even a single day) without boosting your way above a good impression, and I wouldn't characterize the running examples of either system as "monopolizing" their respective scenes.

                              The Social Maneuvering and Investigation systems exist to throttle the pacing for a major achievement down without bringing in quite the extent of setbacks as the Extended Action mechanics would entail — you can put a social maneuver or an investigation on the back burner, and failing a roll isn't going to force you to choose between starting over and taking a Condition, nor do the rolls have a set time investment or dice pool. The Social Maneuvering section encourages the ST to pick a variety of rolls to use to open Doors, and the Investigation rules outright penalize attempts to have a single character brute-force their way through the Scope of an investigation with just their best pool.

                              They're definitely not staples, but I'd say Social Maneuvering and Investigation are for running projects of those sorts moreso than for focusing entire scenes on those activities the way action scenes like chases and combat typically do.

                                                  Originally posted by Fenixpp View Post
                                                  What happens in a contested action if it's a draw? The person acting and the resisting part have an equal amount of successes?
                                                  Per the iteration of the mechanics presented in Changeling 2e, Hunter 2e, Mummy 2e, and Deviant — i.e. the most current iteration of the text used for the general mechanics, with minor variations — ties have both parties reroll their same dice pools until one comes out with more successes than the other, with the specific exceptions of Dodge rolls (which simply negates any damage the attack would have done) and hunters using teamwork (in which case the primary actor always wins ties on contested rolls).
