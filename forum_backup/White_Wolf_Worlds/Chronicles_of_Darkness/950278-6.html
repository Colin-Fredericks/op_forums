                Originally posted by The Kings Raven View Post
                Really?

                When I read the question "What has your character forgotten" from the Breaking Points section of Chronicles of Darkness I read the word forgotten as implying that mortals outright suppressed memories of the supernatural rather than simply hoped they'd go away. And if that in turn implied that they didn't believe in monsters, why else would they be suppressing memories if not to protect disbelief?
                Every game has built in "you will forget!" measures. Quintessence, for instance. The mind-blurring effect of the Kiss. Lunacy. Angels of the God-Machine. And so on. Its not that people WANT to forget, its that they're forcefully made to forget. The occult is forcefully hidden from reality. And that's on top of supernaturals that go out of the way to debunk existing measures to make people doubt. (*cough*MIB*cough*)

                Then, there's also the Cthulhu-effect of Integrity. Exposure to the supernatural tends to be tramatic, unsurprising for a horror game. At best, the occult aspects of the game are neutral towards humanity, while a good chunk are actively harmful. Seeing a vampire chew on your best friends neck and covered in his blood? A werewolf taking on an inhuman form that strikes primordial fear in your heart? Fae that rip out the very strength of will from your soul? Humanity tends to be prey for most supernaturals. Suppressed memories tend to be a human reaction to spotting scary things.

                People are constantly exposed to the supernatural, and implicitly acknowledge it, but there's this nagging fear that happens. If you don't look directly at the scary thing, it won't look back, and you'll be safe. Open acknowledgement is not safe. Humans are not blind and dumb.

                                                I also thought hm3458a was using "ableist" to mean "stigmatizing mental illness". And like I said I never got the impression that Genius was stigmatizing mental illness.

                                                Quite the opposite really. Rather than stigmatizing mental illness it humanises it. It deconstructs mad scientists like Doctor Frankenstein and shows that they're not an Other that can or should be defeated, but a metaphor for fears inherent to human existence, fears that in the end we have to make peace with because making peace or living in perpetual fear are literally the only options.

                                                Moving past the metaphorical. The PC organisations promote a healthy attitude, encouraging Geniuses to find self-acceptance, manage their condition and support each other. I've got plenty of friends who're also nero-atypical and a couple of them have told me that the fact I can casually mention my Aspergers like it's not a big deal (I even have it on a t-shirt) was revelatory and helpful to them. Messages of self-acceptance are important.

                                                In addition the book presents a wide range of mad scientist characters, both heroic and monstrous, which shows that Geniuses are not defined by their madness.


                                                I mean, Moochava isn't the next great philosopher writing profound wisdom about the human Condition. I don't think Genius would qualify as reading material in a therapy session. But the messages it has are solid.


                                                (I do however think the term "ableist" is too accusatory due to the cultural context surrounding any word suffixed with -ist. Mental illness is a ridiculously broad and complex topic that's not fully understood by experienced professionals. Expecting the man on the street to understand fully is unreasonable and there's no need to make people defensive over it).
