    Firstly let me mention something about Mummy (of course). I think a lot of people are slightly disappointed that the game wasn't second edition especially since the game afterwards we knew would be, but that there's one thing that's the root of a few issues (like non-Iremite Mummies): people mistaking backstory for metaplot.

    Originally posted by Dataweaver View Post
    Is it? I've never noticed that. Usually, I see it followed by requests for South American or Asian mummies. Not that there's anything wrong with being Caucasian, mind you.
    I think in the game's early days the issue was more about its approach to racial phenotype rather than cultural variations of a supernatural condition.

    With that out of the way there's a couple things I wanted to chime in on:

    Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
    My personal beef is primarily with Mage, which I've always argued has something of a thematic disconnect with the rest of the CofD; in a world about horror and mystery, having godlike power and focusing on finding answers seems to somewhat miss the point. They're the line with the least drawbacks, and while one can warn about the dangers of hubris or point to all the scary Abyssal beasties they, it stands to reason that Awakening is gonna be a straight upgrade from mortal life for most people. Look at the "Who are you in the CofD?" thread and see all the people excited to have magic powers.
    Man, that is why I never even looked inside that thread. I think second edition, at least, put Mage back in a personal horror spot but it is (for better or worse) still very much, in my eyes, a game of wish-fulfillment. Someone else in the thread talked about how Mage exists in its own secluded world that no other splat can interact with (which is interesting because that's the second biggest jumped-to-conclusion people make regarding Mummy), but isn't that the point? That being a Mage gives you secret occult knowledge that drives you mad and isolates you from friends and family because they don't get it? Simultaneously and ironically, Mage is probably the best game for crossover despite Beast being designed for it, which, incidentally, explains that Beasts are native to a realm described in Mage.

    Originally posted by milo v3 View Post

    Which is why I've accepted that my preferred 'ghost game' is Wraith and my preferred 'faerie game' is Changeling the Dreaming. I know when to fold 'em.

        I always saw the cWoD s a horror setting where all sorts of scary things are coming to get you, specifically (the Sabbat, the Technocracy, the Wyrm, etc) while the nWoD was more a Urban Fantasy where the scary things where there, but not specifically after you as long as you didn't get in their way, or else they were reasonable challenges, like hunters, bale hounds or banishers.

        The cWoD also made things worse (or better!) by making the protagonist very distant from the normal human experience, fostering horror by the callousness that resulted, while in the nWoD protagonists felt more like freaks and pariahs angsting over their damaged links to humanity (I often felt a bit of Anne Rice in those titles).

        The CoD feels like a mix of both, with protagonists being monstrous rather than being supernatural misfits, but also having deeper and more complex (and thus complicated) links than ever to mundane people. Once again, the scary things are after you, from Angels to Strix and Huntsmen, but they are not the quasi omnipotent and omnipresent threats the cWoD had to deal with. Often things will start like Urban Fantasy with the protagonists juggling with the mundane and supernatural aspects of their lives, and then turn to horror as one aspect intrudes on the other.

        When will you rage?

                                                        Originally posted by reseru View Post
                                                        Firstly let me mention something about Mummy (of course).                                                         I think a big part of this has to do with people having different notions of what metaplot is. For many, it's some aspect of the setting that tends to intrude on the story you're trying to tell — that is, the game tends to push you to tell (some variant of) the story that the game's designers had in mind rather than the story you have in mind. In this regard, it's related to the question of how “toolboxy” the game is.
