        I have to say, I am skeptical of playing up the prevalence of technology in WoD. To me it feels like it detracts from the personal experience necessary to a horror game. I build my games around the idea that players will have to be more directly involved in their interactions. Computer warfare is more seems a better fit in Shadowrun because the game is built around technology. Really with the powers and fear effects in this game I am starting to feel it might be more appropriate to just have early nineties as the implied setting to rid players of the notion that they can circumvent their problems with a hacking attempt or something on their phone. It's one thing to come up with a creative solution to a situation, but at some point the prevalence of tech (especially if the game was not built with it in mind) starts to feel too easy.

            As a security focussed Software Engineer and former System Administrator, I have a fundamental dislike for all things "hacking" it isn't really an actively contested thing like they show in the movies. Some things can be, like any remote Denial of Service. But you're trying to breach network security? it's highly unlikely anyone is on the other end watching you. Contested hacking is for the movies. Most of the time it's either A hacker can't get in security too good, or B hacker gets in "organization" finds out later or they don't. It's basically more of a question of did you get in? how well, did you cover your tracks? I'd say it's an extended roll, and requires 1 exceptional success that can be applied to covering your tracks.

                                                            One concern I have regards the sheer disproportion I am seeing in power between these merits and the ones in the GMC. As opposed to simply providing more options, several of these seem to me to be too good to pass up. While Small Unit Tactics and Inspiring provided a nice edge in combat but were no means definitive, a party with a social tank utilizing Cohesive Unit can expect to drastically outperform a party which is even on average better at what they do. I worry this will create a "everyone uses it or no one does" mentality among groups when it comes to this book, limiting options rather than granting them.

                                                            To continue with the above example, another concern is that with merits like it and Know the Territory mental and social characters now are able to "double up", so to speak: i.e. using their non-physical skills and abilities to become combat powerhouses. This feels more apt for a game like DnD or Exalted (switching out your attack skill, using certain special martial arts) rather than NWoD to me. While there have always been things like Fighting Finesse (though that was with another physical attribute) and supernatural activation rolls, I think these merits are much more extreme. Whereas before if your fast-talking con man wanted to be useful in combat he had to pick up a gun like everyone else (with the appropriate expenditure of dots and experience if he wanted to be anything like good at it), now he can take a three to five dot merit and with just the attributes he undoubtedly already has be perhaps the most, and definitely one of the most, invaluable members of the party in that sphere.

                                                            That sense of groundedness, of creativity working within certain limits, was always one of the things that greatly defined NWoD for me. While you could make characters with a great degree of flexibility, skills, and interests as opposed to cardboard cutouts, you needed to pick your choices. You could be a superb academic and a good marksmen, but even then that meant you were not going to be a superb academic and a good talker (to use an obviously oversimplified, stripped-down version of character creation), and you probably wouldn't be as good as someone who prioritized marksmanship or physicality. This was important, to me at least, as it reinforced the idea that you were not playing a superhero. Even supernaturals were at base humans, who had many of the limitations and vulnerabilities of your average mortal, as was befitting to a horror game. So the idea that suddenly you can get two for the price of one is something I have issue with both due to the gameplay concerns raised above and from the standpoint of tone.

                                                            This is an issue that I think needs addressing, as while I do understand that there are varying degrees of artistic license and breaks from reality (and appreciate the clarification), it is also a somewhat subjective issue. Several times a very cinematic fictional example has been put forth as emblematic of the idea behind Hurt Locker, only for another one to be called not in keeping with the spirit/too unrealistic. I am not sure what parameters we are using at this point, and am not sure how helpful such examples are given that we all come from different backgrounds both fictional and in real life. Someone might very well say that cinematic hacking has no basis, while there is precedent for dual-wielding, or vice-versa.
