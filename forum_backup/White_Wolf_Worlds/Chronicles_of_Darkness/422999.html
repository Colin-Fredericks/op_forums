                                      Well, call it my own biases, but I honestly prefer not to play too much to the stereotypes.

                                      I don't find playing a fundamentalist nutbag "if she weighs less than a duck, she's a witch!" witchunter appealing. I don't find people who actively follow the folktales to deliberately be a fairy tale creature as much as possible in Changeling appealing. And just overtly re-enacting some famous movie is not appealing to me.

                                      Rather, Frankenstein, as it has come down through the years, has had its "Divine Fire" rewritten with every generation. Lightning was the original, because at that time, electricity was the newest buzzword. Scientists were doing experiments where they would shock a dead frog, and see its legs twich, and wonder why. Hence, to the uninformed, "Lightning + !!SCIENCE!! = Resurrection".

                                      When it came to the classic movie, it wasn't lightning, it was radiation, because we all knew what electricity did, and you couldn't BS people into believing you could just slap corpse parts together and then hit it with a defibrillator to make a new person. And hey, back then, radiation was MAGIC! It could make ants grow into colossi! If it were redone now, you'd pull out the latest pseudo-science-as-magic buzzwords, instead - nanomachines, genetically engineered stem cells, quantum something-something!

                                      Beyond that, the core tenant of the story was the evils of science as "playing God". At that time, even the concept of medicine as a science was controversial. At that time, the simple idea that one could actually heal a disease was seen as a transgression upon God's domain, whereas we actively look upon ideas of refusing blood transfusions because of worries that it might taint the souls of the people involved as insane ramblings today. In the recent past, the new front was in vitro fertilization, but now that's accepted. Now we're talking genetic modification, especially as it relates to curing genetic diseases as Playing God.

                                      This was, ultimately, why I never liked the story of Frankenstein - it's always been anti-rationalist propaganda.

                                      That isn't to say that Promethians, themselves, can't be compelling characters. But it's always been as a de facto rebuttal to this story. Being able to escape the same constant plotline by moving beyond it.

                                      Hence, I actively dislike the "Mary Shelly Promethian", or "Cleopatra Mummy", or "Spanish Inquisition Hunter", and have misgivings about "Grimm Fairy Tales Changeling." It's throwing the gamelines into a box, and saying they're not allowed to be actual explorations of the themes of their subject matter viewed through different lenses, no, they're stuck just retelling the same story over and over again. (If there were a Mummy set in Asia or Mesoamerica for that matter, it could include rules for a non-Phaoronic mummy, and I might actually be more interested in the game...)

                                                          Originally posted by Seidmadr View Post

                                                          I completely disagree with this take. The core tenets of Frankenstein are about acceptance of the strange, and taking responsibility for what you do. In many ways it can be seen as a criticism of the European view on God, in that he created man, and then set him free to do whatever he wanted.

                                                          And when Frankenstein was written, people had started to use electricity to stimulate dead muscles, and it wasn't at all seen as impossible that with electricity people could be revived. Just because we know better now, it doesn't make the bleeding edge of that age's technology anti-rationalist.
                                                          Basically, we're talking about the most intense period in the clash of Romanticism versus Enlightenment, here.

                                                          Following the Protestant Reformation, you had the giant witch scares and social divisions that were played out in deeply religious terms. The couple of centuries of religious bloodshed and witch hunting spawned a backlash in the 17th century in the form of the Enlightenment movement, which tried to push people away from religious understanding of the world, towards a more scientific one. The themes they generally embraced was one that all the universe could be observed and manipulated through science and rationality. THIS caused its own backlash in the form of Romanticism in the 18th and 19th centuries, specifically pushing back at the evils of the Industrial Revolution in general, but also the ideals of the Enlightenment and its knowability of the world. Lovecraft wouldn't come around for a century, but Romanticism as a movement spawned the earliest forms of most of our modern horror tropes (including Vampires and Edgar Allen Poe) during the 19th century, where exploration of the unknown was generally seen as dangerous, evil, and the work of madmen, rather than heroic promotions of the frontiers of humanity.

                                                          In fact, if you want to get down to it, the whole "Law Versus Chaos" thing you see recycled in D&D spawns from this movement, through Der Ring des Nibelungen, as Law being the force of Rationalism and Chaos being Romanticism. (With Wagner staunchly on the side of Romanticism, and openly fetishizing Gotterdammerung as a nearly Communist-style overthrow of Industrialism.)

                                                          ... Which is all part of why I really wish they'd assemble this book chronologically, and have just a few paragraphs to tie in the general sweep of history, such that people could get more out of the historical context of all this...

                                                          In any event, Mary Shelly was very much a part of the Romanticist, anti-rationalist wave. The subtitle of the original Frankenstein was "The Modern Prometheus" (gee, wonder where the gameline got its title...) as a specific allegory to the nature of how the monster was created as an attempt to steal the powers of God, as a mad trespass. Frankenstein has been used as practically the defining example of the debates over ethics in science specifically because of its overt themes that have basically been the crux of every argument against research that have been laid down before or since.

                                                          Now, you might look at it with your own eyes, Death of the Author and all, and put other themes into it, and if you want to do that, then more power to you, you can rewrite the morals of the story to fit your own worldview, and play Promethian to a different theme than the author intended. In fact, I generally find that to be the best way to really enjoy the game, which is sort of why I prefer a Dustbowl version of Promethian to a "Let's dig up Mary Shelly's corpse for one more dance" theme. Still, the original themes of the work were very much part of that general Romanticist zeitgeist, which is precisely why her works were so popular and famous in their day. I could write a whole freaking OTHER giant post or two on how popular villains are generally reflections of the underlying social malaise of the era that produced them, but that's neither here nor there.

                                                          In any event, I much prefer these eras have been used to juxtapose a creature made of the sensibilities of one era against a different era's values. That would give players and storytellers the chance to really expand what the different monsters actually mean as a reflection of humanity, and its past. Vampires as the antithesis of Victorian values is already a part of their modern myth, and part of what makes them interesting is how much our own social rejection of Victorian values has turned them into bizarre anti-heroes (or outright heroes if they sparkle in the daytime and are just moody guys looking to settle down with YOU). Vampires during the Middle Ages, when their myth was entirely different, and about the sins of gluttony rather than lust, were entirely different figures.

                                                          Promethian during, say, the 16th/17th century Englightenment wave that was at the science-rather-than-religion Enlightenment wave that gave us the modern trope of Science Versus Religion could have been more interesting. Promethian Galileo, as some sort of fallout of the schism between science, philosophy, and religion, would have been a much more interesting era. For that matter, skipping ahead to Jules Verne or H.G. Welles, and the re-establishment of science as the weapon of the hero would have made promethians either the ultimate paragons of, or refutations to the zeitgeists of their eras.
