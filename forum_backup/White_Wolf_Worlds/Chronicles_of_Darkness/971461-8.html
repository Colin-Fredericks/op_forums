                    The definition of 'morally compelling" seems to shift with the wind and strikes me as falling into a larger internet fallacy derived from dreams, ghosts, and half-remembered community college English 101 courses; the fallacy bastardizes "it's generally better in stories when antagonists are, for one reason or another, interesting" to "an antagonist or villain who is clearly doing harm is ONE-DIMENSIONAL."

                    This is a nonsense, and nobody is obligated to entertain it.

                    People on the internet love to froth about "mustache twirling," and yet some of our favorite villains in fiction do exactly that. "But mustache-twirling isn't realistic!" (More properly, it doesn't adhere to that person's expressed notions about strict realism - but sadists do exist!) It's true, though, that most harm in the world isn't caused by gleeful, thorough-going sadists; but then, neither is it caused by misguided idealists or super-secret good guys. It's inflicted by workaday people punching a clock at EvilCo because they're scared of the personal consequences for them if they were to ever stop. Some of them like money and power, but even those people have people they love and who love them in return.

                    If someone's players are treating those people like kobolds, that sure seems to me like a player problem and not like an anyone else problem.

                        If you're calling Wisdom a "morality" system, then you're missing the point entirely. Wisdom is not morality, it's self-control. Killing someone is a wisdom violation not because it's immoral, but because it's reckless. It's an irrevocable action with far-reaching consequences. That person could have an important destiny, or could have friends and family who will be hurt by his death, or who will go out to avenge him. That's why premeditated murder is more wise than manslaughter. Contemplating at least some of the consequences of a murder shows more self-control than just wildly acting out on emotion.

                                                Yes, the Seers are a little underwhelming, and Dave B pretty much said in this thread that they hard coded them a certain way so people wouldn't try running around saying they were the real heroes of the setting. I guess the authors and fans had a dissonance in their values/beliefs, so they intentionally misread a book to flip the script as kind of a jab at the authors. Those games were before my time though so I am not sure if that is exactly how it went down. I think its a little heavy handed, but I actually want to work with the Seers as they are written.

                                                I would say in the Seers defense though, is that almost everyone can be sympathetic, even Stalin has an interesting story, and even has some admirable qualities. I wonder, if I grew up in Russia during that time, how good of a government could I have put together? Was anything I wanted from a government even possible during that time? Or would the slow decent into paranoia and decades of brutal warfare turn me into a soul-less man of steal, if I was even strong enough to survive?

                                                If you want a sympathetic villain, just think about what people's constraints are and what incentives they face. You just have to ask yourself, what would you do in their shoes? Yeah, you will come out either estimating that you are stronger than them, and feel disgusted by their weakness, or you will come out realizing that you are weaker than them and try to use that fear and guilt to improve yourself, or maybe you will find that your moral judgement was incorrect and there was no better solution. Any of these ways its a powerful experience.

                                                Honestly, I would probably be a Seer for a time. Then probably leave after something freaked me out and turn into a banisher.
