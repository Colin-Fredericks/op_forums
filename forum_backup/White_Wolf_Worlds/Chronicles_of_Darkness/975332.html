    How dangerous are Obsessions?

    09-23-2016, 10:10 PM
    I know that mechanically speaking Obsessions are more carrots than the sticks to the characters. Players are assumed to go after them on their own willing. Still, there are some mechanics that change forcefully one of Obsessions on the character sheet. How much 'obsessive' is this Obsession then for character?

    I ask, as I prepare to run Mage/Promethean story line and I think about hacking a bit Disquiet rules for Created on Awakened. To me, more logical than revulsion on artificial life being would be that mages are get Obsessed on that pseudo living thing more and more - leading finally for the proverbial 'ending on Mysterium table' for Created. Then question is this can be assumed to spring from Obsession mechanics itself, or does it need to be spiced up with combination of Disquieted Condition on mage character?

    There is also question of where to start automatic change in mages Obsessions and when he starts to have murder intentions to dissect Created?

    I was thinking on the start of mage Obsession on stage 1 or 2 of Disquieted Condition. If going with first option, it would mean that every interaction with Created would end in Obsession on him - every Mage Sight centered on him or social interaction would lead to this. Maybe it's too much? Maybe mages need to be around him few times to finally make 'click' and go all over the need to know secrets of Prometheans?

      Reading the rules characters who risk Wisdom because of an Obsession lose a die on the check. If you can provide a path to fulfilling an Obsession you automatically open a Door in Social Maneuvering. Making progress on an Obsession can grant a Beat.

      Basically, Obsessions aren't that dangerous in their native form. Mages who impose one of their own or a new Obsession on another character can more easily coerce a target in social situations.

      What you could do is have Disquiet run the risk of placing the Obsession Condition on Mages which would give them 9-again in their fascination with Prometheans and remove their 10-again to everything else. That would be more dangerous and hit a broader level of application which is what I think you're shooting for.

              I will say this: Telling your players they'll get a few A-Beats for an unethical action is a great way to watch their ethics remodel. Confronting the character with it later is a great way to bring it home to the player how obsessive the Awakened are, and how hard it is to justify the action later.

              Carrots are used to lead the mule forward, after all.

              That out the way: Different characters, different players, different Obsessions (and obsessions), different answers.

              From my own games, all these things are important considerations. Some Obsessions are more important and driving than others; different players will want different tones for the game and thus be more or less likely to accept the bait; different characters will be vulnerable to different angles of persuasion.

              One of my players has a character who is rigid in his determination that the ends justify the means, but that the suffering inflicted through said means must fall on him. As such, he's knees-deep as a Scelestus and is missing a few bodyparts because of it, but he's never tempted to sacrifice a baby to the Dark Powers. For him, seeing his Obsession go dark places requires offering him the solution to a problem in a way that costs him personally. In the end, he'll self-destruct gloriously.

              Another player character from the same group, though, is the polar opposite: The ends do not justify the means. He's far more cosmically focused than the other PC; seeing the Corpus Mysteriorum and how compromising on important values, even to seemingly noble ends, weakens and wounds magic itself. By degrading sympathy to noble concepts, you weaken their influence on the world. He will not compromise his values readily. As such, rigidity will destroy him if allowed to do so.

              Most important of all, though, the player of the Obrimos wants the character drama; he wants to watch his character spiral down into oblivion, flipping the gods off all the while. The Moros's player doesn't. He wants a noble-bright sort of story; one where the darkness exists to exemplify the heroism. As such, in his stories, I tend to go a bit easier. The times where I do emphasize it, it's when his decision to cling to his values is pivotal and monumental.

              So yeah. That's my advice.
