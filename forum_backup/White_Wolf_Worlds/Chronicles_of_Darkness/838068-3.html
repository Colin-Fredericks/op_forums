                there is also no mechanical reasons to decide Disciplines must be powered up with Vitae, or that regular people won't be able to activate Contracts using its Catch. The reasons are that Disciplines are designed to represent what a vampire or a changeling can do, and other beings don't. The same is for Endowments- notice I don't say "an Endowment", but plural. There is nothing wrong in a certain person having a Relic, for example- but having the same access to Relics an AKD hunter has makes no sense. Mechanics are meant to represent the themes of the game, after all- and in the game, they are not meant to be "cool and shiny powers", but a representation of the conspiracy's resources, knowledge and connections. The fact that something is a merit doesn't mean it does not have prerequisites, and membership in a certain organization is more than an appropriate prerequisite.

                This is a very old argument in the forums, but the point is this- a game is about telling a certain story. Mechanics are meant to bring that story to life. Opening Endowments as whole to anyone harms that story's themes which were established by the developer. No one force you to play it that way- but the game itself has stories it is meant to tell, and it usually draws fans who enjoys those kinds of stories. Such a change feels, to me at least, unnecessary because it both harms those themes and because hacking it into your setting in case you don't like them is not something hard to do- you just need to decide "in my table, everyone can buy Castigations". That's it.

                        If you want a mechanical difference, Risking Willpower. Honestly, that's all I need-that expression of the sheer depths of desperation a Hunter can draw from and how far it can take them.
                        I haven't yet read the whole topic, so this may have already been addressed; but to me, the chief mechanical difference between a blue-book mortal and a hunter is Practical Experience — which can be interpreted as mundanely as “if you're constantly running into the supernatural, you learn quickly or you die”, or more along the lines of “you know how most people forget the supernatural (and, by implication, forego some of the lessons that might otherwise have been learned)? Hunters don't. Those extra lessons learned because you don't forget? That's Practical Experience.” Either way, hunters are always confronting the supernatural, and they're learning to adapt very quickly — faster than a blue book mortal would if he was constantly confronted by the supernatural.

                        Also: when this came up in a discussion with Monica, someone suggested that a hunter's five questions for Breaking Points should replace “what have you forgotten?” with “what can't you forget?” implying that regular mortals try to forget about supernatural encounters while hunters tend to be driven by the supernatural encounters they've had.

                                                        Hunter is a different gameline because its genre, theme and mood are different from the theme of the Core book- a mortal game is a game about mysteries and fear, madness and things which lurk in the dark. It is a game about losing control, getting pulled into the darkness and getting out of there scarred and broken- if you get out at all. A mortal deals with things he can't understand, without external support, which draws him more and more into the shadows.

                                                        An Hunter game is about sending light into the shadows- it is about people who use what ever they have, any desperate resource in their hand, jump into the darkness and win. It is a game about mortal men and women who can actually make a difference. You spit in the eyes of the devil and get out alive. You have support in the form of a local cell, of a compact, of a great conspiracy who is able to withstand years and decades and maybe even a millennium or two and surviving the countless assaults made by the monsters in the dark. They are the reason vampires uphold the Masquerade, this is why the Herd Must Not Know. It is about humanity taking a stand- and doing what ever it takes to fight the endless battle against the monsters, even if you need to sacrifice some of your humanity by doing so.

                                                        As for why it should not be a Blue Book- Blue Book supplements are about certain phenomena, something which doesn't really have its own setting, philosophy and influence on the rest of the world in which the Chronicles of Darkness takes place. Inferno? everyone has their own Hell. Innocents? Everyone has children. Slasher and Changing Breeds are a bit different, but the first was originally developed as an Hunter book and it feels that way for many reasons, while that is the CB's least of its worries, especially since many people prefer the WAtP's version. Hunter could be a Blue Book, but that would require you to throw away most of the things above 1st tier and some of the lore of the game, making it into a vary different game. At best you would get something at the style of Hunter's Hunted and Year of the Hunter in WoD, which was kinda all over the place IMO.

                                                        Hunter has its themes, which usually create certain types of stories- it is just as "sandboxy" as Mage or Vampire, in that that Mage eventually leads you into investigating Mysteries while Vampire focus around political struggles and personal horror. Hunter is a game of "sending light into the shadows"- which allows you to tell a wide range of stories, but eventually there is something common in those stories: you are meant to be small and meaningless in front of the darkness, yet you still punch Cthulhu in the face. Why? Because that is what hunters do.
