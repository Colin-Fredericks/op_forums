                I'm just going to say, all it took for Sarah to escape the Labyrinth was to say, "you have no power over me" and mean it. All Dorothy had to do to leave Oz was click her heels together and say "there's no place like home." All Alice had to do to wake up from her dream of Wonderland was to tell the court they were all just a silly pack of cards.

                They all had to go through an ordeal in their respective Arcadias to find the strength to make those declarations, but it was within them all along, they just didn't know it, and it took a journey to show them their own power.

                That's what I think a Changeling's escape should be. Yes, they rebel or betray or lead or fight or let go or better themselves as part of their journey of self-actualization. But the actual moment of escape is the moment when they declare that they are stronger than their captors and they will not be held back any longer. Everything before that moment is just part of the learning process they had to go through to reach that point.

                    Originally posted by The Kings Raven View Post
                    The Wyrd definately helps, but it also hinders because the True Fae have their narrative too and their narrative says you're an unimportant bit charachter.
                    I would actually argue the opposite point. The stories of the True Fae thrive on conflict and opposition, at least in 1e; one of the Gentry who exists in opposition is one whose story will soon enough wither and die. I recall some line to the effect of two Strangers who are bitter enemies being about as close to besties as they can get, because they both provide an endless avenue of conflict for each others' tales. From the perspective of a True Fae, it's to the benefit of their narratives that an unimportant bit character become so elevated. It's a plot twist that gives them a new and personal avenue for conflict. Perhaps it hurts a bit in the short run, but can ultimately be much more advantageous to them.

                    I could honestly see that the True Fae might themselves not want this, but the Wyrd certainly does. It wants the stories of the world to be interesting. And what's interesting about the Changeling being an unimportant bit character? There's nothing there. No conflict, no growth, a complete narrative dead-end. But when the Changeling gives up something of significance in pursuit of a Seeming? There's a fine narrative seed to be nurtured, there. Far more valuable than what was previously a stale bit of crust on a Stranger's story. I have this image of a time-lapse film with a sprouting plant swallowing or tearing apart an unmoving rock. It's not about overpowering the True Fae's narrative, not completely -- which could instead be expressed by actually wiping the Stranger out. It's about overpowering it in a place and manner in which it is vulnerable.

                    As to the point of the Changeling's being a weaker story, that's not really a determination one can make just by describing what happened. Two different stories can have the exact same events, but the respective framing and telling of those stories can make the difference. One may be dull as dishwater while the other is absolutely edge-of-your-seat level compelling. "Someone truly giving up a significant part of herself in pursuit of freedom," is kind of tricky to not make an interesting and significant personal development, and it can be the seed of a fascinating story. Never mind that it fits the Wyrd's notion of bargaining, agreements, and give-and-take to a T.

                        I would also like to note, in support of my thoughts that the Changeling seizes freedom by making their own narrative and escaping from the likely diminute role their Keeper placed them in; and in following the argument that there are some fundamental differences between the Lost and the Gentry that affect the responsiveness of the Wyrd to their respective metaphysical declaration of existence and individuality (as Arc noted in his last comment); Changelings can create. The True Fae can't, not truly.

                        To validate their self, the True Fae must draw on existing narratives, bonds and stories, fabricating them in imitation of a dynamic whole, deliberately working to cause conflict in which they have a part, and sublimate other things to build up their own importance as vital and superior to them. Changelings, and indeed humans, do not require this. They are grounded in reality and possess a spark of originality and imagination, of true and new potential that allows them to create a unique narrative just by living.

                        The Others need to justify their mercurial being existing as separate from the Wyrd. Changelings do not, and are all the more real for it.
