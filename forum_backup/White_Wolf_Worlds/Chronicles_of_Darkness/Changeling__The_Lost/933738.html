    Mage: The Transgression dev thread

    07-19-2016, 01:40 AM
    Moving this to the general chronicles of darkness board, since it doesn't really fit in the Awakening one- people there are people who presumably really like the Awakening setting and don't want their forum clogged with remake stuff.

    Here's the fluff for the remade Abyss- I can move the other notes from the original thread over here, or a mod can move that thread or merge them. Short version: MtT is my attempt at remaking Awakening using elements from Kyle "Moochava" Marquis' Genius: The Transgression and Mage: The Ascension, with the goal of creating a setting that retains what people loved about Ascension while staying true to the themes and essence of the ChroD setting. (Disclaimery note: nothing from Genius, Ascension, Awakening, WoD, or ChroD belongs to me or was in any way designed by me, and if Moochava doesn't like me messing with Genius this way I'll take it down and cease work on the project.)

    So, the Abyss. It's one of the things I had to keep from Awakening, due to personally liking it and because it's widely regarded as one of the coolest parts of the setting. I'm aiming to streamline it and keep it from stepping on the toes of ChroD's other assorted sanity-shredding horrors. First of all, I'm combining it with the Lower Depths. Having both was confusing and didn't really serve any purpose. The Pit from Inferno is now left ambiguous, speculated by some to be part of the Shadow. So, the Abyss is anti-reality. But such complete anti reality that it isn't even a place, and would annihilate any substance it formed before it could harm reality. That is, until Mages started doing what they did. See, magic makes the impossible possible. It breaks the rules and gives you something out of nothing. It makes 2+2 equal green. And that's why Enlightened magic causes the Abyss to coalesce. No one knows how this started- the countless legends of a paradox-less golden age before a Fall mention that there was no risk of calling the Abyss in that era. Some say the Fall created it, some say it helped cause the Fall. But it's here now. Paradox is what happens when a Mage's personal reality has a dramatic clash with everyone else's, and this makes tears. Sometimes, depending on the type of magic used, the tears weaken the Gauntlet or bring the Hedge closer or something else, if that's the kind of energy the Mage is working with. But most commonly, they call to the Abyss- and the impossible magic in the atmosphere reacts with the nothing of the Abyss by giving it form enough to persist beyond an instant in our world.

    Abyssal entities and phenomena exist to destroy. When they first form, they're nothing but mindless anti-reality, crushing anything near them. But as they absorb more and more mana and become more and more substantial and "real", they begin to focus this inherent destructiveness towards a target. They need to- if they retained their mindless automatic destruction, they'd soon destroy themselves.

    Abyssal entities have four basic types. The lowest manifest when the raw nothing of the Abyss absorbs just enough mana to interact with reality. Level One manifestations take the form of Phenomena- not entities, but explosions, magical effects, rips in the Gauntlet, inexplicable events, shifts in fate, and so on. Phenomena lack the reality and definition to become entities or persist, so they destroy themselves almost as soon as they form, sending out damaging magical backlash.

    Level Two are Abyssal Reavers. Reavers break stuff, sometimes themselves. But they're growing towards something. They form from more powerful Paradox backlash or when a Mane is consumed by the Abyss. A Reaver always looks incomplete. Even one that takes a complete alien form will always appear unfinished and broken to an observer. Some scholars divide Stage Twos into Lesser and Greater Reavers based on their ability to manifest. Lesser Reavers stay in Twilight when not consuming mana due to insufficient reality, wrecking anything near them until they fizzle or absorb enough mana to become Greater Reavers, which can physically manifest for short periods even without mana. Reavers want to destroy everything, but depending on the circumstances that spawned them and the mana they've consumed, they start to gravitate towards specific favored targets. All Reavers have the power of Unmaking- they can make anything around them start to break down and fail. But Unmaking is subject to the same tendency towards fixation. Once a Reaver uses Unmaking, it can only use it against the same type of target for the rest of the scene. So a Reaver that cancels a spell with Unmaking can only use Unmaking on Enlightened magic for the rest of the scene.

    Level Three are the Void Princes. Void Princes have settled on what they most want to wreck- magic, the Shadow, the ecosystem, New York's subway system, etc- and have come to define themselves by it, gaining powers specifically for their target. Their Unmaking only works on their chosen target, but is much more powerful. They can exploit existing tears and other Paradox phenomena, and often deliberately seek them to gain more mana. Abyssal beings cannot create or reproduce, but Void Princes can subsume existing Reavers to their purpose, turning them towards the Prince's target.

    Level Fours, known as Disassociations, cease to exist as single beings, and instead become self-replicating viral patterns devoted to the destruction of their target. These can take almost any form- Clockstoppers, The Nemesis Continuum, and the Prince of 10,000 Leaves are examples of Disassociations. Disassociations are much more likely than Void Princes to use humans or other beings instead of Reavers. Some Disassociations have formed cults, while others alter humans into better suited forms. Banishers are sometimes theorized to be the result of a Disassociation. A few Mages well acquainted with the Kindred wonder if the Strix might be a disassociation.

    There are rumors of a fifth level, known as the "Void God" stage. No actual examples of this stage have been proven to exist, and some scholars believe that it is impossible for an Abyssal entity to reach this level and remain Abyssal- a true Void God has become so defined and ingrained into reality that it ceased being Abyssal and finally becomes truly real.

    Some Mages do deliberately invoke the Abyss, though many of those would object to the claim that they serve it. These aren't common, though. A few try to deal with it as they would with any other magic, reasoning that all magic and supernatural phenomena are dangerous but knowable, and the Abyss is no different. The Cauterizers believe the Abyss serves an important purpose: eliminating those parts of reality that need to be eliminated as thoroughly as possible. They compare the Abyss to fire or acid- you just let a controlled amount out and you manage it carefully, and you burn out the infection. A few Abyss-cults take it even further, making their lists of "things that should be destroyed by the Abyss" longer and longer, to the point where they decide that the world is so rotten and corrupt there's nothing to do but raze it and start over. A handful of these apocalypse seekers want complete annihilation and seek unity with the cosmos for oblivion, but others want to replace the world with something better once it's been burned down. This camp is split between those ready to sacrifice themselves when the time comes as no longer needed relics of the old world, and those who believe they have the right to rule the new one as gods. Both are fine with using the essence of pure nothingness to wipe out human society.

          And here's the basic outline for anything magical: spells, rituals, wonders, artifacts, and so on:

          Effect: You start with "what do I want it to do" and/or "what do I want it to be". Examples: Throw a fireball (Rote), summon and bind a specific spirit (ritual), a robot with a gun for a head (wonder).
          Practice: The Axiom and Sphere it takes to create a particular effect. So a fireball is Katastrofi+Forces, a Spirit-binding ritual is Epikrato+Spirit, and a robot is Automata+Matter.
          Reagents: Reagents are the cost. One of the central themes of MtT is that everything, especially the supernatural, has a cost. In a Rote, Reagants are resources spent to cast it. In a Ritual, Reagants must be present to perform it. In a Wonder, Reagants are what it takes to build it. So, the fireball might cost one point of Mana/Mania. The Ritual costs two points of Mana/Mania and requires a bonfire. The robot requires an amount of metal and circuitry equal to Size 2, a Resources 1 expenditure.
          Ban: Bans are the weaknesses and frailties of a particular effect. Taking Bans lowers the cost of making something, as does increasing the cost of Reagants. The fireball might have a Ban that it requires that the Mage have his hand free to cast it. The ritual might have a Ban that it cannot make a spirit do anything against its nature. The robot might have a Ban that water causes it to short out.
          Pool: The dice pool used to activate the effect, as determined by the player and the storyteller. They might determine that throwing a fireball uses Wits+Occult, or Dexterity+Athletics- depending on the Mage's Paradigm. A ritual likely uses Occult. The sample Spirit-summing ritual might use Intelligence+Occult or Presence+Occult. An independent wonder like a robot has simplified PFR stats based on the skill level of its creator. Other Wonders require rolling to use based on what they are and their creator's Paradigm. So, a laser gun would probably be Dexterity+Firearms, but a Matter-based disintegrator might be Dexterity+Science.

          The reason I'm coming back to this is because of Mage 2e- I read it, and there's no other option but for me to go Up To The Roof with this (basically, that means I need to homebrew something to make it usable. To quote the Blue Man Group song of the same name: "Tried to use the tools you sold me, no matter what the cost. Tried to go the way you told me, but each time I got lost.")

              Here's some more, including simplified rules for Workings.


              Workings: Any magic effect, as divided into Rotes, Rituals, and Wonders. I'm going to include a list of sample Workings, but Workings are intended to be highly customizable and personal to individual Mages. Each Working has Rank (an overall measurement of how powerful the Working is, determining how many points of Mania must be committed to it and how high its Paradox risk is), Reagents (the cost of activating or creating the Working), Practice (the combination of Axioms and Spheres required to produce the effect), Pool (the dice pool rolled by the caster to use the Working), and Frailties (the weaknesses of the Working. Adding Reagents and Frailties lowers the Rank of Workings, making them cheaper and safer).
              Rotes: A "spell" in the commonly understood sense. A Rote is a magic effect that the Mage pays some cost to activate, which works similarly to other supernatural powers. So to cast a "fireball" Rote, the Mage spends whatever cost the player agreed on with the ST when they created the Working (Mana, Willpower, Health, Gold Coins, Blood) and the effect happens. Fireball would be a Katastrofi+Forces Rote. Rotes are primarily used by Mages with the Sorcerous or Gifted Paradigms, but can be used by those with Scientific Paradigms as well. For example, a mad scientist might have the Epikrato+Matter Rote "Reprogram", which allows her to easily reprogram any programmable system regardless of tools or situation, meaning that she could make a standard Intelligence+Computers roll to take control of a rampaging robot.
              Rituals: Rituals are effects that require a specially prepared space and more involved casting than Rotes, in exchange for less risk of Paradox and greater power. Scientifically perceived Rituals are known as Procedures.
              Wonders: A Wonder is any supernatural item created by a Mage. Wonders can also be Foci. Enchanted items are Wonders, as are super-science inventions. Mages with the Relicmaker merit can turn their Wonders into Relics, which can be used by anyone without incurring Paradox (though there is always a drawback that "absorbs" the backlash that would otherwise manifest as Paradox).
              Focus: A Focus is a tool related to a Mage's Paradigm that a Mage requires to fully focus their power. Foci are needed to create Workings and they make Freecasting much, much safer. Mages with the Phylactery Condition are entirely reliant on their Foci and cannot perform Workings or Freecasting without them.
              Paradox: What happens when a Mage argues with reality and loses.
              The Abyss: A sort of non-place. Believed by some to be a Bardo embodying the platonic ideal of nothingness and by others to be an unintentional side effect of the Fall, the Abyss can flow through cracks in reality made by Paradox and take an infinite array of forms by absorbing magical energy.
              Freecasting: Freecasting is the difficult and dangerous practice of directly calling on Axioms and Spheres without first building a Working. It has an increased cost and risk of Paradox but can be very useful.
              Mana/Mania: Known by both names, Mania is the "fuel stat" of Mages, a sort of energy creates through sheer reality-rending force of will. The amount of Mania a Mage has access to is determined by Gnosis, the "power stat". Mages must commit Mania to Wonders and Rotes in order to keep them "active", meaning that the amount of active Workings a Mage can have at one time is determined by Gnosis.
              The Open: Open are mortals who, on some deep primal level, "accept" magic. This means that they do not count as Paradox witnesses, can use Wonders without causing Havoc, and they can count towards providing Reagents to Workings. Lemurians refer to the Open as "Beholden", and believe that their condition is the result of lacking an internal will of their own, while the Peerage has several diverse explanations for the phenomenon. The ST may rule that all minor and major templates count as Open.
              Proximus: A Proximus is someone who has become Open to a particular Paradigm through a long period of conditioning. This condition is not actually genetic, though many believe it is. Instead, Proximi are those whose lives, education, and circumstance have firmly convinced them of a particular Paradigm.
              Unmada: A Mage who believes that their Paradigm is either true in some way or should be true.
              Illuminated: A Mage fully consumed by their Paradigm, becoming a dangerously solipsistic magical being. Rumors say that some Spirits, Kerberoi, Deathlords, Qashmallim, True Fae, and others were once Mages who became completely absorbed by their Paradigms.
              Seers of the Throne: A secret society with members in the Peerage and the Lemurians. Seers are the polar opposite of Unmada: they believe that magic is purely a tool, and that it should be wielded to control the world from the shadows. Some Seers truly believe that they serve a greater good, while others simply want power for themselves.
              Zotheca: An open gathering of Mages, resembling something between a chaotic marketplace and a busking convention.

                  Update: Magick is back! I spent a while agonizing over what to call the magic that the Enlightened wield, since the Supernal is no longer a thing (well, it's part of a popular Paradigm known as Atlantean Awakening Theory, so you can play a Mage on one of the five paths if you want). I needed a new name, and Enlightened Magic didn't really work, since "Enlightened" is primarily used as an epithet for Mages in a mocking or bitterly sarcastic way. I ultimately settled on Chaos Magick. It's a callback to Ascension, and it also actually spells out in a meaningful way what differentiates the magic Mages wield from other forms of supernatural power and mortal sorcery. In modern occultism, Chaos Magick, to put things very very overly simply, rejects the hierarchy and structure of other forms of occultism and instead stresses that magic is dependent on personal meaning and structure, and, as I've heard it described, "You can pray to Superman instead of Anubis if he resonates with you more". And that's what Mages do in Transgression. They Catalyze with a deeply personal Paradigm that they can force into reality.

                  Now, as an aside: I am not someone who practices the occult, as I do not believe that anything supernatural exists. I do not think that chaos magic as practiced in our world functions as anything other than a placebo or a cultural practice. I simply chose the term for the reasons above and to provide a link to real-world occult practices, not to suggest that the game depicts magic which is in any way real or doable.

                  Continuing on, drawing this divide sums up a lot of the themes and changes I'm going for with this project. For one thing, it turns mortal Sorcerers into something other than "Mages Lite". I actually got the idea to use the term from a review of Second Sight that mentioned that the rules for magic there clashed with the modern idea of Chaos Magick, so I figured that would be a nice way to contrast Mage magic with Sorcerer magic. Importantly, it also doesn't imply that Mage magic is "truer" or better than Sorcerer magic the way the Supernal does. It turns Sorcerers into their own thing, and gives them a reason to come into conflict with Mages. A Sorcerer could easily say that because his magic relies on existing occult forces and patterns and does not cause Paradox, his is "natural" in a way that Chaos Magick isn't. This is explicitly pointed out in the document as a reason for Mages and Sorcerers to come into conflict. Of course, there are Sorcerers who see Mages as having reached a higher stage of enlightenment and as something to aspire to, but this view is most prominent among Proximus dynasties and organizations which have been indoctrinated to believe this.

                  This solves the "why would anyone want to be a sorcerer" problem, a subset of the "why would anyone not want to be a Mage" problem I'm working on with MtT. Even in Pendragon's awesome Mage hack, which I would probably use if I ever decided to play Awakening and which would make a fantastic standalone game and is superior to the official 2e, "half-Awakened" and mortal sorcerers just ended up feeling like store-brand Mages, without the edge other minor templates have. Ghouls, Wolfblooded, and the minor templates of the other games all have definite and enticing advantages over the game's primary splat, while Sleepwalkers and Proximi didn't have the same balance with Mages.

                      Update: The Seers make their triumphant return! In Awakening, they lent themselves very well to paranoid conspiracy-drama, and that's their primary role in Transgression. In contrast to the personal horror of being a Mage, the warped reflection of Lemuria, the surreal horror of Manes and Orphans, and the cosmic horror of the Abyss, the Seers represent a human but incredibly calculating and elusive horror.

                      If the two main factions are the Peerage and Lemuria, where are the Seers? Everywhere. Transgression makes them into a secret society with operatives in both factions, their existence unknown to quite a few mages. The Seers provide an ideological contrast to the Peerage and the Lemurians, with enough overlap that they can work within either organization. While the Peerage believes that Mages should strive to safely regulate their power and must remember that they are forever separated from humanity, and the Lemurians long for the day when their genius is recognized and they can rule as revered Sorcerer-Kings and Emperor-Scientists, the Seers take what they see as a much more practical and effective approach. They wish to rule over humanity, just as the Lemurians do, but they have no fantasies of returning to a mythical golden age without Paradox or somehow proving that their Paradigms are correct. The Seers of the Throne might be more accurately called "The Seers Behind the Throne", as that is what they aspire to. The Seers see their magic as merely a tool, but believe that those with it have the right and/or responsibility to use it to rule over the human herd. Essentially, they're wannabe Illuminati who can make a pretty compelling case for themselves.

                      Mechanically, the Seers function as a Legacy with Attainments that allow them to temporarily remove information from their memories to thwart interrogation, eliminate magical residue left by their spells, and create a completely separate identity using a magical mask (something they take from the Guardians).

                              Being doing some thinking about Wisdom and how it works, and I have some ideas for it, partially based on the thread about immortality in Awakening.

                              - Doing things to yourself is considered less of a Paradox/Wisdom loss risk than doing them to others. As far as reality is concerned, you're already a walking wound in the laws of physics, so picking at that scab isn't as bad as putting a fresh wound on something else. This also feeds into an interesting narrative and gameplay element: higher Wisdom Mages are likelier to do things themselves and use magic through their own bodies or to strengthen themselves, or support others through indirect means, while lower-Wisdom Mages are likelier to have legions of cyborg minions or zombies.
                              - Using any magic at all on a human is considered a Wisdom transgression. Only at Wisdom 10 or 9, mind, but still a transgression.
                              - Deliberately causing Paradox for no other reason than to cause Paradox is one of the biggest Wisdom transgressions there is, as means that the Mage literally set out and decided to damage the fabric of reality itself simply for the hell of it.
                              - The way Wisdom works leads to some interesting experiments with other systems of morality. For example, killing someone with magic is generally considered worse than transforming them, from a Paradox and Wisdom standpoint. Killing someone takes a brief flash of lightning or fire or energy, and while it might put a large hole in someone's head, it puts a fairly small hole in reality, which heals fairly quickly. Transforming someone with magic, however, is like making a wound in reality and deliberately keeping it from healing.

                                  Okay, things are coming along. I've been mostly taking a break from this to focus on The Lightning Chronicle, but I have been working on Bardos and the Astral when I can.

                                  MtT treats the Astral as sort of a "universal" realm, that no one supernatural force has a claim on (though Changelings, Beasts, and Mages are probably most closely connected). The Astral maintains its "collective unconscious" nature from canon, and you can probably use a lot of material about it without much modification (though if the Bardos/Astral book emerges, I'll probably try to simplify and streamline how it works).

                                  Bardos and Manes have a connection to the Astral, and are the result of Mania being drawn to the Astral by a particular strong surge of emotion: for example, when a popular "dream" such as cities on Mars or neon cyberspace is disproven, the area of the Astral corresponding to that dream tends to flare up in a conflagration of disbelief and disappointment that draws Mania to it. When enough Mania is sucked into an Astral Realm, it becomes a Bardo. Likewise, Astral beings can become Manes using Mania: the difference between the two is that Manes are semi-real, and exist in the Shadow or rarely in the material world.

                                  Bardos need a regular flow of Mania, or they risk being sucked back into the Astral- a traumatic experience, as it essentially consists of the realm having its reality sucked away from it. Bardos are notable in that it is impossible to teleport directly into one, because their reality is dependent on a thick "sphere" of Mania that surrounds and insulates them. Instead, a Bardo can be entered from either of its two Correspondence Zones. A CZ that resonates with the dreams that created the Bardo in the first place. Each Bardo has two: one in the material world and one in the Astral. So the Martian Empire's Material CZ would be anywhere above or on Mars itself, while its Astral CZ would be regions of the Astral corresponding to Mars, space travel, aliens, or science fiction. Normally, entering a Bardo requires an expenditure of Mania, meaning that only Geniuses, The Open (equivalent to Sleepwalkers or Beholden), and Manes can do it. The enterer can also pay a "toll" of their own Mania to allow another being entry: this, sadly, is most often used for kidnapping purposes. However, many Bardos have "gatehouses", Sanctums built by Mages on their borders. As Sanctums are powered by Mania, Gatehouses are threaded into the Mania Bubble around Bardos in such a way as to provide a stable passageway that does not require Mania expenditure. Gatehouses can be accessed for either the Astral or the physical. Beasts, Demons, and Changelings can travel to the Astral with the most ease (though all other beings can theoretically do it by various methods, though Prometheans and Mummies in particular tend to have rather deleterious effects on it), they often make use of Gatehouses to travel in and out of Bardos. However, many Bardos ban Demons or regulate their entry: Bardos are not wholly out of the God-Machine's reach, though it does not usually expend the effort it takes to penetrate or infiltrate them...unless a valuable fugitive might be hiding inside. Another oddity of Bardos is that their Material Correspondence Zones are accessible from the Shadow as well as the physical realm, meaning that sometimes rather confused Werewolves find themselves at Gatehouses.

                                  Atlantis has a much-disputed relationship to Bardos, one which forms one of the great mysteries of the setting. The common story is that Atlantis was once a large and prosperous Bardo, perhaps even the first, but an event known as The Great Transgression or The Fall permanently destroyed it and caused massive magical damage to the fabric of reality. The Peerage's story is that The Great Transgression was an attempt by the precursors of the Lemurians to make Atlantis truly real, and use the story as a cautionary tale about attempting to tamper too much with the fabric of reality. Lemurians tell the tale that Atlantis succeeded in becoming real, and served as the seat of enlightened sorcerer-kings who ruled the world in times before, until it was crushed by either a rebellion, infighting, or some outside force (some Seers point to the God-Machine for this role), thus creating Paradox and the Abyss. To them, Atlantis is a lost golden age that must be reclaimed. In fact, the Bardo Lemuria was originally created as an attempt to he a "second Atlantis", before the massive midcentury Future War between the Peerage and Lemurians ended with it being designated neutral ground. The Seers of the Throne have investigated it as impartially as they can, and too they great frustration have come up with little concrete evidence to show for it.

                                  Illuminated can create Bardos by exposing areas of the Astral to the raw fires of uncontrolled Mania burning inside them.

                                  As an aside, I'm considering a different name for Mana/Mania, something that captures the idea that it's the energy of the impossible.

                                          I'm renaming Reach to Transgression. I'd wanted to rename it for a while: Reach is already game mechanic in Mummy (and plenty of players use it in other games, since the Mummy cult mechanics work great), and I'm responding to feedback (thank you for the people who gave it, as much as I flung shit at you like an asshole for petty reasons) to keep it on theme as much as possible. I'm also adding that Mania (which I'm planning on renaming) can only be generated by doing something somehow Transgressive.

                                          So, Transgressions. Mage is a game about Transgression. It's about breaking rules. So Transgression Actions can break the rules. Don't have the right Reagents on you? Cast the spell without them. Your death ray doesn't effect wood and there's a tree man chasing you? Do some quick mods and give it a shot. Low on Mania? Just generate some more. And so on. Of course, there's a reason for those rules in the first place. Mage is also about paradoxes, and one of those paradoxes is that a game about transgression is also about the power of rules. Well, not so much power as "making it possible to use power without blowing up". But you can break those rules. It'll ding Wisdom, and it's how you cause Paradox (or make it worse, if you're already being Vulgar), but on the other hand, it generates Mania like nothing else, and it just feels good, so there's some Willpower you get out of it. And when I say it feels good, it's not really the druggy sort of good, like vitae. You know that thrill of doing something you're not supposed to? It's that kind of high, magnified by a few thousand. What it really is, is your Catalyzation in miniature. That moment when you threw yourself against the walls of reality and crashed through? You feel like that again. Every Catalyst feels it differently. Transgression is a raw rushing whirl of blazing emotions for Zorns. It's the high of staring down despair and pushing on for Klagens. And so on.

                                          Transgression isn't like Diablerie, or Lacuna, or Blowout, or even Going Loud. Everyone does it. Every Mage needs to do it sometimes, to save themselves or save others. Sometimes it just feels really good. It's a dirty little secret that every single Mage has in their closet. Younger members of the Peerage are trying to encourage more open discussion, but the old Lemurian standard remains. The Lemurian etiquette was that Transgressions were an often necessary but rather impolite part of life, and an embarrassing sign of losing control at worst. Remember, the Lemurians are fighting a battle with reality, and they expect members to fight wisely. Going overboard with Transgressions is like charging a trench naked.

                                          And I've been thinking about how to keep the players together. Basically, Isolation is a semi-theme. Isolation is a form of transgression: we're group animals, after all. Mages feel Isolation in their own awful way: each Mage is stuck on the island of their Paradigm, burdened by a view of an ideal universe they can never quite articulate to others. And this is why they form groups. Paradoxically (and paradoxes are another major theme), it's this isolation and individualism that makes Mages stick together. After all, no one else truly understands your Paradigm, but other Mages come close. Some of them even share it with only minor differences. Social connection also helps with Wisdom loss and avoiding becoming Unmada: that's why Mages have Touchstones. Now, socializing with regular humans is best for this, but socializing with other Mages helps a lot. The Peerage and Lemuria realize. The Peerage has it as part of their ideology, while Lemuria grudgingly realized that Mages who go off alone in volcano lairs and never come to meetings tend to be much likelier to bring their careers to an early, and often explosive and/or radioactive end, which does not help The Serpent at all. This is why the cabal structure was created: the idea of small groups of mages forming, taking on a particular territory or area of study or task, and building a shared mythology and a clublike structure around their Paradigms is an excellent way to stabilize and alleviate some of the burdens of Magehood. The Peerage pushes the cabal ideal most strongly, while Lemurian cabals tend to be looser-knit. Some gently guide new Mages to form cabals, while others bluntly say "if you don't want to blow up, find some friends".

                                          Overall, this means that more-stable and more-playable Mages tend to cluster in small groups, while less-stable Mages tend to be hermits or leaders of large groups of minions.

                                                          Okay, news again. I'm still focusing on The Lightning Chronicle right now, but the question of whether or not to continue this is a bit less clear. It was some threads about the "but why isn't there a heroic splat" that made me think of it. No, I'm not intending Mages to become that: they're already too much like superheroes for me. It's that I realized there's still a niche for a game primarily about what happens when you try to change the world. None of the other games have that as a primary: they mostly concern beings who did not choose their supernatural status but had it thrust upon them, and their conflicts are mostly driven by survival and reaction. Also, I want an entirely selfmade splat- no one can force you to Catalyze. Those two work well together: trying to change the world and being responsible for what you are both make for excellent personal horror in ways that none of the lines really explore. Minor templates explore it amazingly well, though, so I'm also making sure to have a lot that differentiates Mages from minor templates- not just "mages are way better and stronger and know more." Reasons why someone would choose to be a minor template and not a Mage.

                                                          I've been too hard on Awakening, and since it wasn't what I wanted I ended up resenting it more and more until I was just lashing out at it, ignoring its merits. So I'm not sure if I want a totally new game or an Awakening hack. I admit that Awakening fits that niche, it just doesn't fit it in the way I'd prefer.

                                                          To summarize:
                                                          • No mad science options automatically makes me predisposed against something. Plus, mad scientists deserve a major template.
                                                          • The paths are waaaaaaay to restrictive for a game about changing the world with the power of your will.
                                                          • Not enough focus on mages as monsters.
                                                          • Mages are way too powerful and Mage supremacy is still too much of a threat.
                                                          • Simultaneously too many and not enough possibilities. Magic doesn't feel dark and dangerous and self-destructive.
                                                          • The Astral is cool, but Bardos and Manes were cooler.

                                                          I'm thinking of adding a new aspect to Mages to focus on what makes their condition a curse: The Light. The light is the inner fire that powers Mages- not just magical energy, but their connection to their Paradigm and Catalyst. The Light makes demands of Mages: I'm thinking they risk willpower loss, power loss, derangements, or other things if they go a certain amount of time without expressing their a Paradigm: using magic, working on a project, completing an Oblation. I really want being a Mage to feel like a curse, and Awakening just doesn't do that for me. Most Mages wouldn't want to go back to being human, and that means they aren't monstrous enough.
