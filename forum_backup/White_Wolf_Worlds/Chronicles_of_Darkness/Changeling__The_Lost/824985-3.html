                I don't actually have any problem with character optimization. In fact, I'm a big optimizer myself, especially in D&D, which I love. That said, if you are really bothered by some choices being more exp efficient than others, you are going to have a rough time with CofD because there are many such choices in the game. That's not a value judgment, it's an observation. Just look at the afformentioned Professional Training, or Mystery Cult Initiation. The physical disciplines in Vampire are strictly better than buying up Attributes and they cost the same amount of experience out-of-clan, less in-clan. Heck, Striking Looks is just a Skill specialty with Interdisciplinary Specialty (and Area of Expertise if you take the 2 dot version) for 1 exp cheaper. These kinds of optimally efficient and inefficient choices are all over CofD, in no small part because the majority of the developers subscribe to the "balance is an illusion" philosophy. YMMV on if that's a problem, how severe of a problem it is, or whether it spoils your enjoyment of the rest of the game. I'm not saying "if you don't like it, get out", I'm saying if you're arguing from a place of designing based around curtailing char op, you're not going to make much headway given CofD's design philosophy.

                        "Balance is an illusion" isn't a bad idea, but it shouldn't be used as a way to wave away all balance concerns. The basic idea, that perfect balance isn't even something you should try to achieve, is sound. The problem is that you do actually have to take the time to maintain the illusion. Options that are obviously better (and Professional Training is one of those, but the theory that it's only intended for mortal characters has always made a lot of sense to me) aren't a great idea on principle. They cause the illusion to break down, and any player with the slightest mechanistic bent will gravitate towards them and eschew other options. Not the worst thing in the world, but it may lead to a situation where you invalidate text written elsewhere.

                        Where it gets really bad is when some members of the group are interested in creating strong characters with powerful internal synergies, while others don't care about reading the mechanics so closely. If your system naturally leads to a situation where mixed groups of players include some who are clearly and evidently outclassed, there's a very good chance of a negative play experience and a negative impact on the game. The illusion is important. Strict balance isn't necessary, but the perception of balance is vital.

                        It's yet to be seen whether Dual-Kith is one of those "obviously better" abilities, but it was in first edition so I sympathise with Heavy Arms' concern.

To me the argument that it still might be a non-choice due to character optimization concerns doesn't hold water because unlike games that lend themselves to char op like D&D, there's no easy metric for what makes a character optimized. Sure, if I want to build a character that's specialized in, let's say, Social Maneuvering, it would be a strong choice to take a Kith that helps with that and Dual-Kith into another Kith that helps with that. But I could also spend those dots on Sympathetic and Pusher. Or some dots in Fast Talk. Or maybe I want to diversify a bit, cause my first Kith gives me all the Social Maneuvering skill I think I'll need. Maybe I don't want a cripplingly overspecialized character and I'd rather branch out into some Crafting abilities. Maybe I'll Dual Kith into Smith for that, or maybe I'll pick up Automotive Genius and Area of Expertise for my specialized Craft. From where I'm standing, the ability to mix and match Kith and Seeming without taking Dual Kith is exactly the the reason it's an actual choice now instead of a tax like it used to be.
