      It's a curious division and it seems to make arcadian contracts more uniform in terms of purchasing and acquiring them, but I'm not sure the in-universe distinction between Common and Royal contracts (or their origins, rather) serves that significant a purpose.

      There only being two levels of potency also looks more restrictive than helpful bearing in mind the myriad of contracts within a wide band of power that have previously existed. I'm reflexively concerned it's going to lead to some flattening of the potential of contracts, or more pertinently, significant skews in usefulness between contracts that are categorized as roughly the same level.           In designing new Contracts, you use the power level to determine the origin. If it works with existing resources, the Others would have willingly let their servants have it. If it's more overtly creative (or so transformative as to essentially be creative), it's the kind of thing they would have kept to themselves.

          (There are exceptions, of course, where Keepers might have added changelings as beneficiaries of Royal Contracts.)

          EDIT: The behind-the-scenes reasoning is that we did initial drafts and playtests with a full five dot range of Contracts. That ended up feeling like too much granularity: the powers we found were most fun for changelings didn't break down naturally into five power brackets, with distinctions between, for example, 4 and 5 dots feeling really fuzzy. So then we thought "why would Contracts vary in power in the first place?" and that led to breaking away from the old WW structure and more towards DtD's categorization of "moderate power" and "major power."

          I've found it works pretty well, and it took a lot of guesswork and arbitrary decisions out of building additional Contracts.

                                  Originally posted by Robert D View Post
                                  I for one would be very interested to hear the reasoning. Game mechanics and background lore are as fascinating as game play itself for me
                                  Okay.

                                  So, when I first got asked to pitch a new edition of Vampire, one of the things I knew I wanted to emphasize was becoming a vampire. In first edition, we had written some setting stuff as if vampirism was a slow slide from light into darkness, and other stuff as if the Embrace was like flipping a light switch. Based on a lot of experience running the game and having talked to lots of other players over the years, I felt that the former was more narratively compelling.

                                  The obvious mechanical scales of transition in becoming a vampire are Humanity and Blood Potency, but I thought there was the potential to show powers themselves as forms of evolution away from human life. And they're forms of evolution that the player has a lot of input into, so the "you did this" theme of Vampire gets heavily emphasized.

                                  I also felt that every vampire power should be scary and predatory -- the tag line for that was "horror from dot one," and it was the first guiding principle. The second guiding principle was that if a player buys even one dot of a power, they should be able to do the thing that power's known for. So, for example, one dot of Majesty lets you impress people even when your hands are covered in blood, one dot of Obfuscate lets you go unnoticed, and one dot of Auspex lets you dig out secrets.

                                  So the dev team came up with what I called a telescoping structure for the dot ladder. Dot one is a broadly useful power that summarizes the concept of the Discipline. Dot two builds on dot one, and sometimes introduces an element of risk. Dot three and dot four are bigger boosts that expand the breadth of the power. Each of these steps so far is a form of growing toward monstrosity -- the more you have these powers, the more you'll use them to solve your problems, the more you'll need to drink the blood of the living. And then dot five... well, dot five is a bit of a big step up. It's the one that tells you you're really not bound my the definition of "human" anymore. It makes you impossibly beloved or gives you a form that's not even a body. Using it is bound to change your perspective.

                                  The particular set of Disciplines was retained from 1e because they covered things that everybody who's read vampire books or watched vampire movies know that vampires can do. Vampires are, in many way, our most traditionally archetypal monsters.

                                  This is a solid way of designing a power structure, but it's very particular to the themes of Vampire and the way we felt individual Requiems might develop.

                                  But not every monster in the World of Darkness is like that. And for all that Lost have in common with Kindred, that's almost the opposite of Changeling's arc. The stories of the Lost are about building and reclaiming lives that were taken away. They start pretty supernaturally potent as a result of the ways they were changed in Arcadia and the ways they fought for their freedom. For a changeling, powers represent parlaying the difficult hand you're dealt into the tools you need to get the life you want.

                                  So rather than a self-reinforcing ladder of horror, gaining power as a changeling is about snatching advantages where you can, turning the rules and laws of your Keeper and their kind against them, using every trick you've got to make it in the world. Goblin Contracts are a thing for a reason -- sometimes you've just got to cut a deal with a downside.

                                  Changeling powers don't represent evolution, they represent opportunities. And so instead of dribbling out power a little at a time in narrow channels that make you into something else, we present bigger powers that ask for trickier, slipperier, more fairy tale use.

                                  The next question becomes "where do those powers come from?" And the answer for Arcadian Contracts is that they come from deals made by the rightfully-despised Gentry.* A changeling taking a power isn't becoming less human and more fae -- they're snatching an advantage and using it to become more themselves, or at least to protect that life and identity.

                                  Combine that with the design/testing experience we had, what we've learned from the half-dozen other lines we've played with power structures for, and the desire to make it easy for players to make new powers, and you get what we have here: power categories with bright-line divisions that represent risks taken, opportunities snatched, power claimed from the very things that sought to box you in.


                                  (* Court Contracts come from a somewhat different place, which will be described in the book. Guidelines for making new Contracts of various kinds are in Chapter 7.)
