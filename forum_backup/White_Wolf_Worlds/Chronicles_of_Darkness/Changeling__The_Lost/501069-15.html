                I definitely did not come away from reading the draft with that impression. If anything, it made me feel like they were the least like the Keepers.

                They're all about others. The Gentry are all about themselves.
                They sacrifice. The Gentry demand sacrifices.
                People naturally look up to them. The Fair Folk traditionally use trickery, fear, temptation, or magic to get what they want.
                They appear genuine and fit in wherever they are. The Keepers are otherworldly and alien, often even showing through their disguises.
                The Fairest takes it hard when their decisions hurt others. The True Fae are callous and cruel and treat people as pawns.
                The example concept mentions real love. The Fair Folk aren't well acquainted with real love.

                To me they read as the most human, not the most Fae: like the mortal protagonist in the Fairy Tail instead of the supernatural creature. Even the way they deal with clarity is more human; anyone's sense of self would be reinforced by taking charge and pulling through in a pinch, and normal people would certainly be shaken if their actions lead to someone being inadvertently hurt. They're like the anti-Fae.

                            That's just it though. The Fairest are the closest to the True Fae, but they are at their core human (in part, at least). Their role comes with a figment of the authority of the Gentry, but ironically they (most) defined that part for themselves by making a decision that is opposite to everything the True Fae are.

                            The boon the Fairest receive from acting on the authority of the crown placed upon their heads is greater than the inherent risk that comes with the burden. The act of ruling, of using that enthralling presence to lead others is always a thing of self-assurance; when things may go wrong, and the weight of leadership lashes back at the changeling, nothing guarantees it will make that crown stumble from their brow and their grip on themselves slip. And even if it does, does it matter? You're just back where you started. You can account for myriad possibilities all you want, seek to gain that clarity with no losses, but you don't need to worry if you find yourself shaken by it. If in the end your mind is on the same footing, you still lead. You still acted upon who your Seeming makes you, and possibly succeed in every other front. Your guidance will still be looked up to.

                            This interplay between the Blessing and the Curse encourages the Fairest to act like the True Fae, despite their capacity for human morality and selflessness. You don't need to really care for collateral damage, for unexpected consequences, for those you did not plan on getting hurt. You command, and you will not lose yourself from it. And this mentality pushes the Fairest, especially those who genuinely do care for others and believe they can do better and have to lead them, to detach themselves from the risks that come with taking all that responsibility on your shoulders. For some, it might even be putting the cart before the horse - you isolate your heart from accounting for those you are trying to protect, from those you are doing this for, so you may better lead them; and reaffirm yourself.

                            It actually works quite well when you think like that, in my opinion.

                                                It is. Even when they're hiding among the humans and fae, there's something magnetic to them. It's why you can still lose Clarity around them even if you don't know what they are.

                                                I just skimmed the core book and Autumn nightmares and couldn't find the text to support this. Trying to find the relevant passages, could you point me in the right direction? They're referred to as terrifying and/or awe-inspiring pretty regularly, but I couldn't find anything about them being magnetic or people wanting to follow them.

                                                To the contrary, under The Mein in Autumn Nightmares (p. 68), it reads: "When encountering a Fae whose Mask is dropped in favor of it's Mein, a mortal sees it and reacts accordingly. Most run. Some attack. Others collapse into gibbering piles."

                                                And under An Imperfect Mask in Autumn Nightmares (p. 71), it reads: "In drawing attention to themselves, the Fae earn the unease of those humans around them. Social rolls made in a True Fae's favor while wearing the mortal Mask are made at at -1 die penalty, and they do not gain the 10 again benefit from such a roll. Other story-based consequences may result, as well. Some humans may flee, become belligerent or even attack. Of Course, the Fae will find other ways to get what he wants from foolish humans..."

                                                These passages (and some other similar ones) seem to indicate that rather than being magnetic or enthralling, they are unsettling, terrifying, and repellent. The response isn't "this guy seems trustworthy and competent, I'll follow his lead". Instead the response is more "holy #$%&, kill it with fire"! It seems like they'd have to use Contracts, Blessings, or good old fashioned tricks to get what they want from mortals.

                                                I'm not saying the books don't say what you said, just that they also say this. Wouldn't be the first time a World of Darkness game line contradicted itself (sometimes even in the same book).
