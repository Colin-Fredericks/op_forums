    Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post
    Not entirely. Huntsmen can be killed, but the section that explains how was left out of this preview. It was brought up in the thread that a Huntsman's own weapon may be one ways to do it. It's also possible cold iron might work? Both are speculation, but we do know while it is difficult to kill them, it's not impossible. Also, while it takes them a fortnight to regenerate, it's unlikely that they'll keep throwing themselves at a wall every 15 days. They are cunning hunters, so they're likely to take their time, develop a new strategy, and possibly wait it out just to keep their prey on its toes.

    All that said, yes, when a Huntsman comes for you, you're only safe for brief windows of time until either it catches you, you give it a Token of Reprieve, or you kill it.
    Well, if they can be killed, that's better. I imagine that will end up being the default issue in most game - a lot of players I've seen balk at doing blatantly evil stuff against other Changelings in CtL games (an amusing change from Vampire) and there's only so long you can stomach perpetual attack by cunning opponents who may be prone to casualties and torturing your loved ones to draw you out.

    I have... many issues with Huntsmen. They're game-warping in a way, say, the Strix aren't. A Strix is universally bad news, but it can be bad news in many different ways, and it can be bad news for different people, for your city as a whole, for you in particular, for some third party. You can be peripherally involved, you can mitigate the bad news, the bad news may happen and leave you much worse off but still alive (well, undead). The Huntsmen are bad at this: they come after you, and the only possible endings to your confrontation are 1) game over, 2) you kill it, 3) you betray your fellow Changelings. This is much more narrow and restrictive.

    One thing I am particularly annoyed at is that, at several points, the write-up says that a wise Changeling who managed to temporarily drive out a Huntsmen picks up and moves to a new town. This is irritating because it's a non-starter for most game. You don't leave town: you can't. CtL, like most nWoD games, thrives on the building of a single community, one city or town, and the exploration of its characters, intricacies and plots, its factions and conflicts, its secrets and opportunities. If you leave town, hours upon hours of work by the ST and emotional investment by the players are dropped down the drain; plots are left hanging to never be resolved. Beloved characters go off-screen forever. I know of more than one group in which this would effectively end the game as players lose interest.

    On a different topic, the Huntsmen also morally compromise the Changelings in a way that didn't exist before. A Changeling who chooses to live is a Changeling who accepts that, every so often, a human being will die to power up their nemesis. Maybe one in a lifetime. Maybe a dozen in a year. Now I don't want to appear like I am blind to what CtL has always been - there's always been danger and ambiguity to the existence of Changelings and the formation of their society, much interesting material to exploit in how their relationship to others and the world could be symbiotic, parasistic, abusive. But this is a far cry from "by choosing to live outside of Arcadia, you choose to have unknown strangers die every so often." It adds a measure of tremendous selfishness to the existence of Changelings which makes them... closer to vampires thematically.

    The above wouldn't be a problem if it weren't connected to something which other people like and I don't: the inevitability of Huntsmen. They seem to be not merely a new antagonist, but *the* new antagonist, a strength which terrifies Changelings and brings them together to defend themselves. The Huntsmen will always come for you, sooner or later. This narrows down Changeling games; it gives their society a focus which I think is too strong, too tight. It closes off story options rather than opening them; it takes over narrative space, reducing Changeling to a story rather than a toolbox for stories. The setting of Changeling is now the setting of the Huntsmen's preys, and it defines them.

    Another way in which it brings Changelings closer to Vampire (which I don't think is a good thing, much as I love VtR to bits) is how it fosters distrust. One of the main antagonists of Changelings were always Loyalists and Privateers: but those were outsiders, even when infilitrated in the Courts. They were the Changelings who had made the wrong choices, the enemy with a friendly face, the ones who escaped and yet are still in chains. The Courts were, fundamentally, about how victims of the Fae draw together, share suffering and find a communality in them. Now, everyone you meet - friendly or not - is a potential traitor, someone who can use what they know about you to hurt you. It used to be that helping a Keeper catch a Changeling was the greatest crime, and those who did it pariah. Now it's something anyone can do.

    This fosters the wrong kind of paranoia: an inward paranoia. Rather than being frightened of the world outside your tight-knit group, rather than having to confront the complex interplay of the safe space of a Lost community and the dangerous opportunity of the 'real' world, you now have incentive to hide from everyone and everything. Courts exist as a way to defend yourself against Huntsmen, but they're also the main reason Huntsmen threaten you: you need the Court, but you also need to hide from the Court. From your friends. No one can know enough to hurt you.

    This is, again, thematically closer to Requiem. This sense of inward, rather than outward, paranoia, is something I have never associated with Changeling and do not want to associate.

    I also don't like the Huntsmen being "spirits" and being universal in the way they are - it jars strongly with my vision of Arcadia, gives it a consistency and predictability I do not like; but I imagine this comes with changes to what Arcadia is that depart from the old vision already.

    I also, frankly, find the Huntsmen boring, and find them to swallow space previously occupied by more interesting opponents. They're anti-Changelings, humen with a tell that can hide as things, and exist only to hunt you down. Despite all claims to the contrary, they cannot truly be unique. A brutal, wrathful monster that wants to tear down everything in its way to catch you, and a patient, sadistic monster that wants to exploit your attaches to slowly draw you out in the open and snatch you, are still pure-octane hunter-abducters who cannot be bargained with (except in this one specific way they all share) and want to abduct you. They have none of the variance and potential of goblins, Loyalists, Privateers, True Fae acting in person, all of whom can have many varied objectives and desires and can be interacted with in many ways - including socially or mentally.

    Now sure, I can always still use these antagonists, the Huntsmen don't take them away from my toolbox. But they do take primacy in the narrative, and this alone has considerable impact on the general framing of the setting and its characters.

    ...I think that's it. That gets most of my issues down.

        The essence of Changeling, to me, is about carving out a space for yourself in the world where you are safe. Not safe from physical harm, only, but also from stereotype and control and deceit. Can that actually be done? Open question. But the structure of Changeling society, from Pledge to Motley to Court to Freehold, is to me about wrapping yourself with protective layers of understanding. The Pledge is an understanding in the mafia sense, where you're protected by reprisal. The Motley is an understanding in the friendship sense, where you're protected by companionship. The Court is an understanding through perspective, where you're protected from tiresome argument. The Freehold is an understanding through identity, where you're protected by common feeling. They're all the same, but all different.

        The True Fae are, of course, the essence of what these things are protecting you from. The Strangers have to be strangers. People who don't understand. People who can't understand. While a mortal can be uplifted into allyship, the Fae will always, at best, be uncomprehending. This makes the protective layers effectively impenetrable: in order for the Fae to really attack you, they have to have some kind of understanding and that subverts and undermines the essence of the Stranger. What gives them their opening is that people who do understand, but are still willing to enable attacks upon you. That's the Loyalist.

        Here, though, we have a slight problem. Not insurmountable, at all, but a problem. There's a vast gulf between Stranger and Traitor: between complete lack of understanding and complete understanding. That's the hole filled by the Huntsman. The Huntsman's job, in my view, is to have gradual understanding. They start uncomprehending, but with time and patience, they learn. They understand the onion skins and are able to infiltrate them by subverting those layers. They're theoretically redeemable, sure, but it takes a lot of time and a lot of collateral damage will happen on the way. Whereas the Loyalist can be turned back, with comparatively little damage, the Huntsman can't. Not without massive damage. Choosing not to kill a Huntsman who is at your mercy is a lot like Batman choosing not to kill Joker: do you choose to break your own mind, or do you let them hurt so many more people?

        Which is where we get into story. (Speaking of OOC story, not Arcadian stuff.) The story-purpose of the Huntsman is the essence of the antagonist. By exactingly confronting the very things that make a Changeling who he is, the Huntsman highlights why those things are the way they are, how they work, what bends them, what breaks them. Can the Strangers and Loyalists do this? Sure. Absolutely. But the Strangers are extremely external, whereas the Loyalists are extremely internal, and this makes it hard to really give weight to both of those forces. The Huntsman straddles the boundary and spells out both sides by negotiating it. They're built to negotiate that boundary, and it makes them ideal for exploring both. By hitting the center, it frees up narrative room for the Strangers to have their strangeness emphasized, and Loyalists to have their loyalty emphasized.

        Let's change the story. Let's posit the Changelings as ghetto-dwellers. The Stranger is the Man, the impersonal, bureaucratic machine that lays down a highway through your world without ever understanding the damage that was done. The Loyalist is the drug-dealer, the pimp, who's willing to knowingly get you hurt for their own gain. But the Huntsman is the cop, who has profiles and statistics about crime and training and authority to break down your door and kick you while you're down. Who thinks he understands you, because he's been briefed a hundred times and has met a thousand criminals, but who approaches you with hostile prejudice from the beginning because you're not really a person to him: you're a target, a threat, an objective.

        Anyways. I'm not 100% sold on their details myself; I'm still waiting to see how everything else shakes out. But they work for me.

        I call the Integrity-analogue the "subjective stat".

                                I strenuously disagree with the idea that the Huntsmen killing innocents makes Changelings somehow "selfish" or that they are morally required to commit suicide or go back to Arcadia. Changelings are literally just trying to live their lives, and then these supernatural serial killer assholes come after them, and kill at least one bystander in the process. Blaming the Changeling for that is like blaming Jodie Foster for the attempted assassination of Ronald Reagan, or blaming FDR for the death of the mayor of Chicago, who was killed during an assassination attempt on Roosevelt. I mean, I don't think very many people watch Terminator and think that Sarah Conner is a bad person because the number of people the terminator kills trying to get to her.

                                At the same time, however, I could see the murder of innocents being a source of drama, since the Changeling may very well feel responsible for the acts of a Huntsman pursuing them. Heck, even if the Huntsmen didn't have to kill anyone, committing a series of murders and leaving some kind of calling card for their target could be a pretty effective way of drawing out a changeling they're pursuing. This brings an element of guilt and self-recrimination to the lives of many Changelings, which I think is very much in keeping with the theme of abuse and trauma.

                                                    Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post
                                                    I think it's a realistic (if tragic) outlook for characters who are metaphors for survivors of severe trauma. To me, this is a plus side to the Huntsman, but I can definitely see how it would be too dark for some folks' taste.
                                                    The problem is that, from an unemotional perspective, it also removes the reason to play the game, and for changeling societies to even exist. If the most logical and practical thing a changeling can do is commit suicide, and their emotional state supports that conclusion (which is believable after the trauma experienced in Arcadia, coming back to see that they've been replaced and they have no place in the world, all of which are likely to lead to depression), then one wonders what's the point in playing the game at all, or how changeling society exists at all when it's extremely hard to present a changeling reasons for them to not kill themselves. Because no, it actually doesn't get better.

                                                    See, contrast with Promethean, where the characters are allegedly in an even worse situation than changelings. The core difference between the moods of that game is that the very point of Promethean is reaching the New Dawn. It's hammered over and over across the book, all the mechanics revolve around it, everything circles around the idea that you can get rid of all the bad things in your life, and not only that, but it's expected of you to do it, to the point where the majority of Prometheans and ex-Prometheans are either on the Pilgrimage or have already reached their New Dawn, with only a minority becoming Centimanni or dying permanently before they can reach their New Dawn.

                                                    One of Promethean's core themes is hope, and the really awful circumstances a Promethean has to deal with enhance that hope because at no time do you feel like suicide is legitimately a more practical option.

                                                    Even in Vampire, you can argue that the justifications a vampire tells herself are quite believable and hard to contradict. Maybe she can only feed on animals, or feed without killing. Maybe she can get used to a night schedule. Maybe she can keep her head down and stay out of politics. Maybe it isn't so bad to be a vampire. And while those justifications can be proven false, they don't necessarily have to, and are completely up to the Storyteller and player.

                                                    With the Huntsmen, it's assumed that they will come for you, that they will kill innocent people, and that they will cost you a lot to get rid of them, perhaps causing irreversible damage to you and your relationships in the process. And hell, because every changeling gets one, killing your own Huntsman doesn't mean you're free from them. You can still be targeted by one to get at someone else, or just become collateral damage. And even if you finally become powerful enough to deal with them, there's the True Fae, which we've been told are even scarier than before. Like, it just doesn't end. There is no New Dawn. There is no rest, no reprieve. It's almost like the game says "You can't actually survive trauma, you can't move on. It will always haunt you. You can just try to run for as long as you can before it finally wears you down and catches up to you."

                                                    That's just not the Changeling game I want to play.
