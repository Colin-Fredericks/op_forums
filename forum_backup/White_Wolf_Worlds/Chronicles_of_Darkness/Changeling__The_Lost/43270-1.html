    I decided tonight to ban guns and other such explosives from my game. Several people in our group are 'run in and kill it as fast as we can' types, so I thought it would be better to ban guns outright in order to make them focus more on their powers. I'm am going to have each court leader have a contract in place counteracting the explosives, but only within the city. Their reasoning behind this is so that if humans ever tried to come in and wipe out the changeling population, they would have a harder time without guns.
    Now I just need to come up with a few repercussions the town will face as the game is set in modern times, and guns and such work just fine outside the barrier specified by the contract.

                        That's... a tricky problem to work around. In that it might be more a problem of "different parts of the group just want different or even mutually exclusive things." Some players are really into taking that approach in a game, don't see the appeal in any others, and aren't going to see that appeal even if you make them do it. There's nothing wrong with anyone's wants, everyone just has a different idea of what's fun and what's not, and those of multiple people aren't always going to overlap. It's just something that may produce conflict and it may not even be obvious as an issue to all involved. Doesn't necessarily mean that's the situation you've got, but I do recommend having a discussion on the topic. While you've likely tried it already, talking things out like that, trying to find a compromise or some other way to deal with it on the player-level will likely work better than any character-/game-level solution.

                        That said, further talk on the atmosphere may not hurt. Changeling has a lot of deals and bargains spun around, so most characters have a lot of connections and ties to other people, organizations, and the like. As a result, killing any given person -- or even making the attempt or harming them -- is very likely to get a number of those affected parties on the heroes' cases. Maybe try to convey to your trigger-happy players that the threat any given NPC represents may be WAY bigger than their personal combat prowess, and that this threat may not merely extend beyond their death/defeat but may be fully realized through it. Doesn't mean violence or lethal force are never useful tools, they just need some careful consideration.

                        Again, I think it's likely a social issue that needs talking out if it's to be solved. If they find social dealings way too boring to go on, just pointing out that non-immediate danger may not make it any less boring for them. It may just frustrate them into feeling their hands are tied and the campaign's railroaded.

                                I have run into similar scenarios in my games (the aforementioned master swordsman regards most obstacles as something to be removed with his swords). The other members of the group take a much more balanced approach to the game, which is good. As a result of him starting fights in inappropriate situations, friendly NPCs regard the swordsman as reckless at best, and an unbalanced idiot bringing far too much mortal attention to the Freehold at worst. I'm reasonably bullheaded when it comes to how events play out as well. If the characters kill the only person who could have told them how to stop the evil man's plan, then the evil man's plan will probably go ahead and the players will have to cope with the fallout. Actions should have consequences. It is treading a fine line between that and railroading, I suppose, but I tend to believe that if the players willfully sabotage the chances of their own success just to feel like a badass then they should have to copy with the ramifications.

                                I'll admit that I have asked the players not to aim for ludicrously high levels in combat skills in the past, but I quickly found that it just doesn't work in Changeling. Three quick pledges can take an average fighter to the edge of mastery. Playing up the social ramifications of violence, as suggested, has been considerably more successful.

                                    I come from a theatre background, and I have found that the rules of improv are invaluable to anyone wanting to run an engaging game.

                                    The following are the rules:
                                    1) Say Yes-and!
                                    For a story to be built, whether it is short form or long form, the players have to agree to the basic situation and set-up. The who, what, and where have to be developed for a scene to work.

                                    2) After the `and` add new information.
                                    An improvised scene can't move forward or advance unless we add new information. That is why new information is added after the Yes of Yes-and!

                                    3) Don't Block.
                                    The opposite of saying yes-and is blocking or denial. A good GM will not shut down players and take away their agency.

                                    4) Avoid Questions.
                                    A form of blocking (in its more subtle form) is asking questions. Questions force our partners to fill in the information or do the work. It is a way of avoiding committing to a choice or a detail. It is playing it safe. However, on more advanced levels, questions can be used to add information or tell your players the direction to go in.

                                    5) Focus on the Here and Now.
                                    Another useful rule is to keep the focus on the here and now. A scene is about the people in the scene. The change, the struggle, the win or loss will happen to the characters in the scene. Focus on what is going on right this at this moment.

                                    6) Establish the Location!
                                    Good scenes take place somewhere and at sometime. They do not take place on an empty stage. A location can easily be established in one or two lines without breaking the scene.

                                    7) Be Specific- Provide Details!
                                    Details are the lifeblood of moving a scene forward. Each detail provides clues to what is important. Details help provide beat objectives and flesh out characters.

                                    8) Change, Change, Change!
                                    Improv is about character change. The characters in a scene must experience some type of change for the scene to be interesting. Characters need to go on journeys, be altered by revelations, experience the ramifications of their choices and be moved by emotional moments.

                                    9) For serious and emotional scenes, focus on characters and relationships.
                                    A long form improv set should contain a variety of scenes. Some scenes will be emotional, some will be tense, and some should be funny. The easiest way to make a scene serious is by focusing on the relationship of those on the stage (their characters).

                                    10) For humor, commit and take choices to the nth degree or focus on actions/objects.
                                    A good long form set is balanced. Shakespeare knew that too much pathos was wearing on the audience; hence, he had minor characters in humorous scenes such as the drunken porter in MacBeth.
