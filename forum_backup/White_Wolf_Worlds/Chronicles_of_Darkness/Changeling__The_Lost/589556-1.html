    1. As far as the "getting them to go there" part goes, I recommend leading by example. Have some middle ranking characters in the freehold (e.g. not the heads of the Courts, but with more status than the players) ask for some assistance/body guards while they proceed on hedge business. Maybe they gather goblin fruits for the feast at the next big gathering at the Commons, or maybe the king wants to make sure the trods haven't shifted locations since last time they were checked, or maybe the freehold frequently patrols the Hedge near the mortal world for signs of Gentry and to keep dangerous Goblins out of the area. Along the way you can have them point out locales not to enter and which roads are "most often" safe to travel down. Let the veterans spin a few stories about how they narrowly escaped from their rivals by popping into the Hedge, or how they used the trods to sneak into a bank after closing time. Illustrate how it can be both dangerous and useful.

    2. I'm lucky in that I have a highly wooded park near my house. Lots of trails, and I've sometimes gotten lost exploring them. If you want a feel for the Hedge, try to find places like that in your area.

    3. I like to assume that Hedge gates close to the mortal world reflect something of the near side. For instance, a gate leading into a library in the mundane world is filled with bookcases (full of books containing gibberish or tokens of forbidden knowledge) forming a maze on stony ground with long thorny vines growing over the stacks and the sky open overhead. Other places might look like a junk yard. I like to assume that the Hedge has a taste for lost things, so many of the signs of humanity are indeed objects sucked into the Hedge (insert joke about socks missing in the laundry here). Occasionally sounds intrude from the mortal world...given the timey whimey nature of them a changeling can't ever be certain if the sounds or whispers are from the past, present or future. Occasionally if you listen hard enough you might hear some snatch of conversation pertinent to your interests...or the screams of someone you care about.

        A few bits of advice for dealing with Goblins:

        1. Goblins are strange, and they only get stranger. The ones lurking near the human world tend to at least have somewhat comprehensible goals. They are either things and people from that world who slipped inside the Hedge and became something else, or things born in alien realms who have reason to linger close to humanity. Do not mistake them for benign goals. Some of them simply have a love of humans, or human dreams, or human things. Some like to hoard mundane treasures, perhaps sneaking into the human world with this in mind. Others have an appetite for human flesh (or other creatures from the mundane world...but human flesh is often a safe bet as these things go). Others work for the Market, or trade in the Market, or beg at the Market...or are sold by the Market. None too few make a decent trade by kidnapping humans and changelings to sell to Arcadia. The point is...whatever they are, they are often the most reasonable. Go further in and their goals and cares are less centered on the real (ergo, understandable) world. Here a goblin troop might try to catch a rainbow in its nets to sup on juicy colors, or stand unmoving in a living chess game two Gentry began a century ago.

        2. Mind your manners. If a Gob says good morning, respond in kind. Please and thank you are key. This doesn't mean you should reveal yourself to a Briar Wolf or a Harvest Man. But if one does catch you, do pay it a compliment on a successful hunt. It might save your life. Goblins by and large are vein and egotistical. The Gentry tend to treat them with disdain. There a bit of bitterness inside every Goblin, and what they will not stand for is to be treated that way by a human or a changeling (with the clear exception of Entitlements and Courtly Monarchs). A goblin might still be dangerously no matter how you act, but even one inclined to be civil can turn horribly deadly and maliciously cunning if it takes offense. Etiquette is king, and you insult a Goblin at your own risk. They all have long memories. One final warning, don't expect the Goblin to return the favor. Gobs can and will hurl all manner of abuse at changelings. Don't let it get to you- they relish the chance to treat others as the Gentry treat them.

        3. Goblins cannot be trusted. Never tell yourself otherwise. Tell them as little about yourself as possible while remaining civil. Do not turn your back on them. Be prepared to defend yourself at any point. Even ones that do you a good turn are best thought of as illicit business partners, willing to work with you for mutual gain but likely to abandon you the moment things get difficult. This rule goes even as you abide by the second rule. In the mind of the Hobs life is cheap. Attempted murder is not a breach of etiquette (although when the fighting is done they might expect an apology from you...be it for wounding them or getting blood on their lovely floor). Goblins lie frequently, and their 'gifts' are as often traps as presents. Refuse (politely) their invitations and offerings unless you have special knowledge about that particular Goblin.

        4. Listen closely. Ask a Goblin a question and you might hear a lie, a riddle, gibberish, or a real answer. Speak when spoken to and you might hear something useful. Goblins have a habit of talking amongst themselves or speaking their thoughts aloud. Their attentions can drift far when their guests (or prisoners) become innocuous, and none too few like to hold conversations with themselves (or speak their thoughts in poems, rhymes, or songs for that matter).

        5. Give simple aid. Goblins frequently have strange but simple goals, and any bit of information or middling trinket that might hasten the completion of that goal is welcome. Remember rule 3, of course. The simpler your assistance can be the better. If possible, simply offer that one nail it needs to finish its house or a sprig of wine to dull the sting it suffered from a Hedgebeast. Labor should be offered only to the Gobs you understand the best or the most desperate situations ("My, the fire in your cooking pot looks dim. Perhaps I can collect some wood before I go into the stew?). Goblins are rarely loyal or truly friends of changelings, mind you, but they tend to treat those who do them service better than those who do not (on the simple logic that one who has done them a favor once is more likely to do so again if the Gob is on its best behavior). And even the most pragmatic of Hedge things understand that the Wyrd tends to favor or ignore those who return kindness with kindness, and damns those who do not (not always, mind you, but why tempt literal Fate?).

            On The trouble of getting players in the Hedge:

            It's tough, getting this niche of the archetype just right. I hear you and this is kind of my explanation why.

            See, the thing of the matter is that if there's an overarching motif to this sort of thing, it's that of "Only the foolish go into the woods". It is the nigh-omnipresent idea put up in fairy tales. Here within our horizon, we know our ways and our laws, but in those woods of the horizon, everything changes. It's not a secret, the Hedge is the woods, and the woods are the wide world, exotic America and foreign Ukraine. It's Araby and the Misty Mountains around Everest and so on and so forth.

            And almost without fail, the first person to go into the woods is always a fool. The first sister from Goblin Market, the grandmother from Red Riding Hood (Why is she out in the middle of nowhere with no way to defend herself from the wild?) as well as Little Red herself, the father in Beauty and the Beast-lots of people go out into the world unprepared for it. And the fae? They take advantage of it.

            The second ones to go might be better equipped, but they the ones who would have kept the rule in the first place, who would have stayed in the realm between the horizons. The second sister knew better than to listen to the goblin men, Belle only left because her father's life depended on it. They know better than to leave and succumb to the goblin mischief that rules those lands. Only the Kindly Ones take to those mad places beyond the borders.

            Which is where Changeling the Lost hits a snarl of perspective, because they are the Kindly Ones.

            This strained balance for changelings narratively and psychologically is weird for them, existing as fae creatures....who are still very humanistically-inclined mentally. By all rights, they belong in the Hedge, they are the strange folk of the world, and have every right to know the ways of the woods and to make it twist to their whim and will. They Belong. Except, they Don't. They're more Frodo, or perhaps more on the nose, Bilbo. They are now the weird things of the world, but their memories tie them to mortality. The narrative of Lost actually places them only being steps ahead of the first fool, playing the role of the second(people who know the rules and can make them work, but are still moved to stay out of the woods), and it's still their lot to be sort of fools for entering the Hedge. The dichotomy of difference between the two is tied into the fear of losing themselves to the tale-the more powerful in the Wyrd you are, the more like Them you are, the more the Hedge does twist to your command. And that's not a dichotomy to ignore, because that sets up the narrative you're players are out to break, the third option between the two.

            But that said, it might pay to break some of the narrative of the Hedge.

            I've been following David's mentions on DnD, and I've been poking around Ryuutama, and it occurs to me that both are games that are all about going into the weird and wild world with glee, and I feel like the first ideas for finding the happy medium is between the old cautionary theme with some of the actuality of both those games. In DnD, you don't have to work hard to draw players into their Hedge environment-they go because that is where the enemies are, because that's where the loot is, where the sights can be seen. Ryuutama dispenses the site based idea and indulges in the idea of travelling the Hedge for the sake of it. Taking a cue of memory from Mage, Warehouse 13's pitch to agents was to indulge in a world of endless wonder that they made safer. They went out into that world and dealt with terrifying and life/sanity threatening stuff on a regular basis, but for the most part, the thrill of experiencing that endless wonder drew them in and kept them going(er, for the most part).

            I feel like the Hedge has the elements of this, particularly when you account for how the psychoactive nature of the Hedge means that person looking for adventure is going to find adventure. The rewards are there, baked in. But in regards to its' presentation, there probably should be some dial fiddling going on with the presentation and tone. The Hedge should be scary. Going into the Hedge should always be an evaluated risk. But it needs to be a risk players are willing to bet on.

            Part of my thoughts on this is the idea of 'idle' challenges here and there. I have a philosophy that players shouldn't enter the Hedge without facing some kind of challenge in their time in there, or at least have some kind of meaningful interaction. But there's no reason these challenges need be life threatening(either fatal or capture). The Hedge is supposed to be a place filled with moral and ethical challenges, because part of it's nature is to shift perspective. Present challenges on the road where there are two outcomes and finding another way around, but those outcomes will affect a character.

            Anyways, this is just stuff I've been thinking on that might help if they came into play with the text on the Hedge. I'm still mulling it over.

                Getting player characters into the Hedge has worked best in my experience as having them just taken there for the first time by someone else, almost a reenactment of their original abduction by one of the Others. It's not EXACTLY like that, of course, but part of the challenge of Changeling: the Lost is that players don't automatically know what a character can or ought to do... We don't have the same cultural touchstones for fae folk that we do with, say, vampires or wizards or werewolves, right? The very notion of the Hedge is just WEIRD when presented in a vacuum.

                Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                See, the thing of the matter is that if there's an overarching motif to this sort of thing, it's that of "Only the foolish go into the woods". It is the nigh-omnipresent idea put up in fairy tales.
                This is the best we have going into a Changeling game, and the Hedge embodies everything scary, wondrous, deadly and just plain weird about the woods of fairy tales. Where *I* run into a conceptual barrier is justifying player characters' going into that place when they already have all kinds of scary, wondrous, deadly and weird options in the 'real' world by dint of being changelings and dealing with what comes along with that state. Why go seeking more of that in a place that isn't our known world?

                In that first chronicle we played, I had the players' characters guided into the society of the Lost by an old changeling whose allegiance lay with his Entitlement called the Jackdaw Regency. I totally made it up as a group who had vowed to shepherd newcomers into the larger society of the Lost, and this bloke introduced the characters—and allowed me to introduce the players—to the marvels and dangers that were now part of their shared world. The Hedge was just a piece of this, but the way it came out in the story allowed both characters and players a point of reference for this place-that-is-not-a-place.

                That point about risk that ArcaneArts mentions is vital to any use of the Hedge, of course, and it's something that I bring up as often as I can. Every time the characters need to visit a goblin market or they are tasked with finding some wonder that only grows in the Thorns, the players always wind up discussing it at some length before heading off. Honestly, I'm not sure HOW I instilled this in them, but I'm glad for it. There are all kinds of wonders and horrors in there that my players haven't even imagined, for all that they know about the Hedge already.

                        My previous post wound up truncated because I ran out of time at work... Posting on forum boards between library calls isn't always the best idea. Anyway, I think that I was working towards an idea similar to this:

                        Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                        A large part of what can happen is that players should feel like the Hedge is their domain, regardless of how true that is.
                        Yes, but when it comes to Changeling: the Lost, even what you think is yours ought to remain scary as F#@K on some level. Terms like 'truth' are relative in our world, more so in the World of Darkness, tremendously so when applied to anything to do with the Lost.

                        We all find our ways through, and that's the biggest element of Changeling that I love.

                        Still on topic, I'd say that I also struggle with how to describe and use goblin markets in the Hedge. The book Goblin Markets is a wonderful resource, as are the chapters in Rites of Spring and Dancers in the Dusk, but I find myself wanting to describe every minutia available in the market, give every shopper and hob a full personality and back story, make every stall a little world in its own right. That's just impossible, and even trying to do so is exhausting.

                        I chose to alleviate that stress by offering two shoppes where the players' characters can find most of what they'd want in a goblin market. Each of these places is run by one of a pair of hobs, brothers who are each 'straight where the other is crooked.' Mr Ochre is stooped, chatty, always willing to haggle on prices but more than willing to swindle a customer who isn't very canny. Mr Blinderbaz is tall, reserved, his wares priced clearly and not open to discussion, though he'll happily accept any offer that is ridiculously in his own favour. Between their shoppes is the every accommodating 'Foode Courte' that rounds out as much of the Upper-Mid-Lower-Round Side Manhattan Market as I've currently sketched out.

                        I do kinda dread my players wanting to explore the rest of the market, but my philosophy of solid, troupe-style play gave me the opportunity to tell the players this. One of them is currently working on a larger description of their local goblin market—sans the above mentioned shoppes and 'Foode Courte'—which I hope will give the players more buy in AND make my job a little easier.

                                Oh yeah, don't get me wrong, the Hedge should be scary as fuck. It should be an engine for dredging up intimate fears and worries for the Lost.

                                But that established, the catch to this is that if you make it too scary, then it becomes harder to motivate characters(or their players). Now, truth be told, I never had too much trouble with this at my table-every other changeling in the freehold used the Hedge, so it was established pretty early on that it was a place you could work in, and even if you don't like it it, this is where the fae live and work. You want to get things done, you go into the Hedge.

                                But going with a more at home parallel, you very rarely see Werewolf fans express doubt about going into the Shadow(their relationship with the Shadow, what says about the characters, and how the players stereoptype what the game is about is a whole 'nother thing). Now granted, they are predators going into something they are bred for, and werewolves as characters are designed to fee like big bad scary mofos. But they live in a literal dog-eat-dog world, where everything can be summed up by predator-prey relationships, and truth be told the Uratha aren't that much better equipped to face down the Shadow than changelings are with the Hedge.

                                The Hedge is scary in different ways-it's a different game, so of course that's the case. It's less in in your face. Changelings also have more reason to be afraid of it than werewolves do the Shadow, because of the very nature of the game. And I wouldn't want to sacrifice that. But this game is a game of Beautiful Madness, and part of the confusion the keeps up the fear is that the fae is something players should want to experience as well. The majority don't want to go back, but how many of them struggle with the mixture of fear and desire as they hide in their bed,screaming at themselves, because they remember another bed that was so divine to lie down in, and part of them wants that even if they don't want anything else?

                                My point is that, in acknowledgement of the people who have trouble persuading their players to go into the Hedge, it needs to be established that the Hedge is both terrible and beautiful, a playground to make and a cage waiting to happen. It's not a case of one covering the other, it's both. There's no stinger saying "Much to their inevitable regret". Players should have mixed feelings about the Hedge, not wholly negative.

                                That said, I do want to reiterate that I've never actually had this problem at my table. My players recognized the danger, but they've also been able to go in tons of times and feel more or less at home.

                                        Anyone remember the story of the Glutton? The rest of the Hedge I described as somewhere between those two, full of risk but also potential gain.

                                                        To nothri: while your guidelines for using hobgoblins are amazing, I do have qualms about making them out to always be backstabbing jerks in the end. Anything that limits story options without a damn good reason is a bad idea. That's how I roll, but you don't have to do it like me.

                                                        As per requested by our resident Fabulous White Mask, I will share my experiences with the Hedge.

                                                        In my main game, I and my friend are sort of co-STs. My PC is from NWOD, her PC is from her homebrewed setting. We mix it up and the results have been more than satisfying. My PC introduced the Hedge into the story as a sort of uncharted omni-road that leads to lots of useful stuff, but he made it extremely clear that going deeper and off the trod is A Very Dumb-Ass Idea. Lo and behold, their first time out of the path, her PC breaks her ankle and they ride the back of a Laughing One in horse form in their desperation. Moments later, they are holding on for dear life as the damn thing goes super sonic through thorns and branches and dumps them in the middle of nowhere. To get back, they promise to help the Stripling Prince (Autumn Nightmares) in his quest to figure out how Hedgespinning works. They do come home, but the game has moved quite far from that encounter, and I have yet to apply consequences for helping that arrogant fox man. Oh, and her PC was attacked by an Enticer (Autumn Nightmares) when she thought it was a real bleeding person and tried to help it, but she kicked its ass. A legged talking oyster hob offered to make her PC the prettiest necklace ever out of one of my PC's eyes (he has lots of them). Her PC vehemently refused, but the hob kept comically missing the point of why she was refusing.

                                                        On the other hand, my PC and her PC have this adorable little gentlemanly Hedge Beast in the form of a crow as an adopted son.
