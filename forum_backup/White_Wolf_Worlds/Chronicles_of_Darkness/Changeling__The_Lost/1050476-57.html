        Originally posted by Horodrigo View Post
        I know that this contract is not rolled, but what defines "Object" in game system terms?
        The section in the book with the header "Objects," Horodrigo. Page 191. It has an entry in the Table of Contents.

        My question is: As Written, what prohibits me of freezing someones gun or shirt in place?
        The fact that people's clothes are not usually treated as separate elements in storytelling. This is not a simulation game — you are telling a story with characters using these powers that they got from magic bargains between reality and fairy-tale-resonant supernatural beings.

        In serious discussion, "nothing says" is a terrible justification for applying effects in ways that clearly aren't the things they're meant to evoke.

        Also, if the only precedent needed for this is a written history saying that a fairy did it
        This is intellectually dishonest and unproductive and you know it.

        The body of work that Changeling: the Lost draws upon has established trends that exist in aggregate. Much like 1e's Talecrafting sidebar providing a URL for TVTropes doesn't make everything written on that publicly-edittable wiki fair game for pattern-enforcement, "someone could write fanfiction of a sci-fi story with fairies in it" does not invalidate the fact that there is a cultural basis for What Fairies Do.

                I would like you to read the text of the Contract again — more than the dice results and the seeming bonuses, because that descriptive text is not an elided-over optional part of this supernatural deal with the passage of time — as well as the definition of a turn on page 17 and the systems for Falling on page 190 (which ostensibly do not provide a per-turn drop-rate because most stories do not handle falls from heights greater than the napkin-math 144 feet the average character will drop in that length of time).

                You're not going to draw a clock in the air between yourself and the clothes you are wearing in the time it takes you to fall farther than the height of most non-skyscrapers, and even if you did the result of freezing your clothes in midair as a means of arresting your descent is not going to be a terribly effective tool for avoiding the effects of terminal velocity on the living body you have that isn't a valid subject for the Contract.

                I understand that this would be blatantly overpower in some fights, like, if you could freeze an armored person in place for an entire chapter this would be a win button, and I'm not looking for that, but by reading things, what's stopping me from doing that?
                The books are not written in technical language and the fact that wordcount limits exist make forestalling every possible misapplication based on "there isn't an explicit statement against it" a fool's errand. If you are looking for me to produce a magic-bullet citation for why the Peasant Railgun and the Locate City Nuke are obviously not intended then you are out of luck, because my interpretive lenses are broad "does this make sense for what the book says it's supposed to be?" and "does this seem obviously goofy for what the book says it's supposed to be?"

This is a deal with the passage of time through Arcadian pledgecraft to make things younger or older or unchanging for a bit, not a short-fall physics exploit in a video game.
