    PC losing a social contested roll, dilemma

    02-16-2015, 10:54 AM
    Hello,
    I'm about to start a campaign after a long break. This period of GMing abstinence has given me a fresh perspective and I started asking myself some questions I was used to ignore.
    Here’s one of them:

    During physical struggle it’s quite easy to determine who wins – the pc or the npc. We just roll and count the lethals. Fair enough.
    What should we do with manipulation/presence based struggles, from my own experience if the NPC tries to influence the player with a social roll and wins the result is not always ideal.
    If I tell the player to comply, only because they lost a roll and require them to roleplay compliance, they’re usually very unhappy. They do it considering themselves good at what they do, but they later on remember this social confrontation as physical injury. It often happens that they try to get back (even unconsciously) on the npc for this social attack whenever they have an opportunity (especially if it was a supernatural manipulation they were really not aware of).

    All being said basing social interaction on rolls is super unsexy, however we can’t just make the players immune to mental influence due to skills or powers of others. There should be a consequence for having very low composure. How do you fellow GMs deal with this?

      The God Machine has more sophisticated rules on this matter than nWoD core does. I actually made a thread about this very matter a good while ago. The link is below.

      All that being said, what I do now if a player loses a contested social roll is, they can choose to comply with it, or suffer a "Breaking Point." That is, your character has to roll to maintain their Morality-or-equivalent rating.

      Why? Because even though the character has "free will" of sorts, the social roll has convinced them that doing so is against their better judgment, full stop. The character feels they are done goofing, and might go a bit nuts over that feeling.

          Sadly, part of the life about roleplaying games where outcomes are determined by dice is that this type of event can and will happen. Just because a character is good at combat or good at social, does not mean they will have a 100% success rate in those endeavors. From what I have read, it sounds like your players want to be infallible and win every encounter they come across.

          If your players think logically, even the best of people can have a social screw up or be persuaded by an unnaturally good argument from another person. Simply having more dice means that the possibility of failing is less which is an accurate reflection of them being good at what they do.

          Now I for one disagree with social interactions being super unsexy as those types of encounters often lead to some of the best parts of game sessions. It is easy to run a monster of the week campaign where you just go kill things without consequences, but when you are trying to convince someone you belong in that part of the building and they simply refuse to believe you, that is something different altogether.

          As much as I hate to say it, this sounds like something you need to work out between yourself and your players since social interactions are part of the game and that includes both winning and losing them. This goes doubly so if a supernatural power was used. People don't like being told that the character they are playing absolutely believes this man is trustworthy, but this is the World of Darkness and that happens.

          EDIT: I just noticed this was posted under the Changeling section. Everything I said is even more relevant in this case as deception and lies are absolutely rampant in this game line.

              Typically, what I do as a GM, is not force the players to do something with a roll. I don't believe in allowing a PC to be mind controlled by an NPC, because of a die roll. So the only time I have NPCs roll during social combat is when they are trying to mind control other NPCs or when they are lying to the PCs. If they are mind controlling the NPCs I set it up so that the players get a chance to also mind control the NPCs. When they are lying to the players, the players are entitled to their roll to see through the lie. If they don't make it, I tell them the lie again. If they do, they find out they were lying.

              As for the supernatural mind control things, if a player does not make the resistance to not be actually mind controlled then I, the evil heartless GM that I am, take control of their character. I've found that by doing that, I remove the part about forcing them to play compliant. When I do that though, I don't take the sheet so while I know the basics of what the character can do, I have no more knowledge of specifics than does the NPC doing the mind controlling.

              I hope that helps you.

                      What happens when one side or the other loses a contested social roll is something your group should work out in advance, and something that should be worked out to everyone's comfort. The GMC has the Doors system, and a lot of groups have social "health levels" equal to Willpower...after all, one successful contested hit does not end a fight.

                      But it is something that needs to be worked out early, so players can build their characters knowing how the social system works. One where they can be easily manipulated if they fail may lead to more investment in resistance attributes, while one where social rolls mean little can and should result in less investment in social skills.

                              If I may, ignoring a social roll as a Breaking Point is something that really rubs me the wrong way. I don't feel like it makes good sense nor good gameplay. It both makes social tests largely useless and reduces Clarity to a resource for ignoring a part of the game the player doesn't like without addressing why players tend to dislike it.

                              Often I find the reason people dislike social tests is because it takes away their choice about how their character behaves. You can get around this by just letting PC's chose to ignore social tests but that's not very satisfying for players (such as yourself) who want social pressure to be a thing.

                              Instead I would recommend a system that gives social tests teeth that can't be ignored but still gives the characters choices in which direction to go. As a quick and dirty solution:

                              • A social test cannot make a character violate their moral code. If the actions asked of them would be a Breaking Point the character can instead perform a compromise that would only be a violation at one Clarity higher then they currently are. For example, a Clarity 4 changeling asked to kidnap a fellow Lost might instead kill the target.

                              • A character can spend Willpower on a one-for-two basis to boost their Clarity for the purpose of this compromise. The above Clarity 4 character might burn 2 Willpower to boost their effective Clarity to 8 and only injure the target.

                              This would give some good incentive to keep Clarity high and gives characters tools to choose how to respond to social sway without being able to outright ignore it.

                                  In PC -> NPC interaction or the other way around I can't really see a simple situation, where a single roll would have an effect of forcing a Breaking Point. It would be possible if you are in a unhealthy close relationship where the other side is submissive, or you're manipulating a extremely mentally unstable person. In other words it would be a very complicated and extended on the storytelling side action. Even if you're mid-powered Ventrue vampire it wouldn't be that simple.
                                  In my view a social roll triggers a strong emotion a person feels compelled to follow. However even if we consider making a roll, there would be common sense involved first. If the guy intimidating you is 2 feet tall, has a speech defect he'd better have some good tools or a scenery set up to scare you. Cold iron bolted crossbow aimed at your fellow changeling, or a very good blackmail leverage, or situation where you're threatened by your bane. There have to be some sound requirements to be met before we consider a roll and bonus/penalty dice. If the common sense tells us that it wouldn't be possible to persuade/lie/intimidate in a certain way we just say no to a roll. If the PC wants to jump over a 30 story building you don't give them a -5 on athletics, you just say no.
                                  As overly criticizing I may sound I'm just defending the idea I really like and try to explain it better. A successful roll makes the character strongly feel in a certain way. A way of overcoming this compulsion to comply introduces a cost of losing a social challenge but allows the player to take initiative while still feeling the blow.

                                              Another way of handeling these situations - which has the downside of making you look alittle petty and unfair while also forcing you to potentially change's on the fly - is to simply have whatever outcome the players end of doing, serve your antagonist in some fashion. Say that they're a bunch of Hunters who're dealing with a Mage. He pleads with the Hunters not to go after the Vampire's down by the slums, because they're his allies, in spite of everything they've done. The Hunters dismiss the Mage, and go after the Hunters anyway, in spite of their failed social rolls against the Mage.
                                              Loe and behold, when they stand triumphantly over the Vampires (if that is even the outcome), it becomes aparent that the Mage actually wanted them to take out the Vampires because their Haven were located on top of a magical nexus, which him and his Cabal now has free access to. Thanks Hunters.

                                              Of course if it was paramount - or just a very ingrained fact - in your story that the Mages were allied to this particular Coterie, then that makes this approach much harder to pull off.

                                              I usually handle this with a few discreet nods and prods. Say I have an NPC who is selling a new drug, and the PC in question owns a drug palor. Instead of laying emphasis on how the gentleman in question seems to wrap his words in honey and silk, specifically emphasising things that the PC in question would be pleased to hear and that he gives off an eerie wibe and smell of sex and sulfur, I'll instead emphasise how the new drug sounds really amazing, and will help the PC's economy - which has been rather failing recently, after all. I'll emphasise all the reasons why the PC should be willing and happy to make this deal, instead of all the reasons he might not, or even the reasons why it might be a neutral idea. If the PC lost the roll, that means he doesn't pick up on the reasons it might be anything but unquestioningly good. Thats the intend of the NPC after all. To emphasise why its a good idea, and to obsfucate all the reasons why it might not be a good choise. If he still wants to decline the deal, knowing only that the deal is unquestioningly in his favor, thats his own choise.
                                              Of course I'll probably remind him a few times in the comming days/weeks, how if he had made that deal, he could've bought that gold watch he coveted, or how that woman who turned him down, might've been willing to go out with him if he had been wearing that expensive suit that he could've bought, had he only made the deal.
                                              Thats how life works, after all. If you turn down something that you cant really see the detrimental effects of - probably because you're afraid of the abrupt change it could bring to your life, and you're afraid of change - you're going to be thinking about it for a while, everytime you encounter something that might've been different, if you've made that decision.

                                              I think taking it as far as assigning a Breaking Point to this kind of thing as a general rule, is alittle tough, but I can totally see it happening in specific cases.
                                              I certainly remember being torn up over not having made a few choises in my own life and I consider myself a relatively grounded individual.

                                              You could also veto that the failure of the players in that social encounter, means that they accidentally promise the NPC that they'll comply with his wishes, while they're in the room with him, and afterwards once they're out of there, they start thinking of what it actually was they agreed to do, and decide against it (perhaps), thereby betraying their promise to the NPC, which can be kinda dangerous in some cases.
                                              I think its generally a good idea to think less of mechanical consequences, and more of narrative ones.

                                                  I certainly tend to veer away from contested social rolls, in any system. Not in a conscious way, really: I usually just trust my players to act according to their characters during role-played dialogue.

                                                  Here's how I see the problem: it's the difference between active and passive statements. The standard mechanical approach to, say, an NPC trying to persuade a PC is to make some defended or contested rolls and, if the roll succeeds, the PC is persuaded. The subject of the roll, effectively, is the PC: the player now needs to incorporate that into his/her portrayal of the character, probably with the full OOC knowledge that they're being manipulated.

                                                  Here's how I tend to do it, instead. The NPC wishes to persuade the PC. I don't make the player roll anything: I roll for the NPC, subtracting suitable modifiers. Based on the successes of that roll, I tailor my portrayal of the NPC and their rhetoric to match. A failed roll means stuttering and badly conveyed arguments. I high roll means utter conviction and clear, valid arguments. The subject of this roll is the NPC, not the PC.

                                                  The players can then *choose* whether to be persuaded or not, based on their own judgement. The roll just acts as a guide for how to play the character for that particular dialogue. Instead of the result being "The PC is charmed," it becomes "The NPC is charming."

                                                  As a result, I'm also a fan of letting players roll their social actions before they say what they want to say in character. They can then tailor their mannerisms and dialogue accordingly. Little deflates a player more than improvising an awesome, moving speech only to have the dice throw up a botch.

                                                  Maybe this is all obvious stuff. It made me reflect on how I run social encounters, though.
