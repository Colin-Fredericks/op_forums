    To put some context on this:

    In Deviant: the Renegades, a lot of the Clade Variations for Invasives are specifically built as Swiss-Army Powers — the core list is five items that include the "gain your pick from this subset of Merits" power, the "gain dots in your choice of Skill" power, the "gain specialized forms of perception/perception filters on the fly" power, and the "you can produce useful equipment at will" power.

    This works for a couple reasons, chief among them being 1) Deviant powers manifest through the basic concept of their highly-individualized supernatural state and almost universally come with attached drawbacks for all but the weakest Variations, and 2) even without an attached drawback, the driving impetus of a deviant's Touchstones gives a very clear direction to bend that versatility towards.

    The Invasives are also often one of the more consistently gross/blunt/mundane Clades in their manifestation. Their powers come from bodily merging with nonliving material in a way that lets them shrug off a substantial amount of harm by "damaging" their Variations instead, which, while a striking concept, is somewhat unglamorous compared to their counterparts' ability to ratchet up the strength of their powers, drop the intensity of their Scars down to the "take a Beat for roleplaying it" level," or temporarily trade out their Variations for a weaker pick from the Universal list.

    Notably, that last one is the signature trick of the Mutants, who 1) as the "biology is fucking weird" Clade, are the other archetypically "ugly" category of deviant, 2) basically flip the Invasives' shtick by having a Swiss-Army Power in their innate kit and a set of Clade Variations built chiefly mostly around survivability (the "regenerate" and "eat anything" powers are on their list, as are "bleed acid" and "don't need to breathe/eat/sleep").

    There's kind of an inverted "form follows function" thing going on there, with versatility coming at the price of visual refinement. (Not to say the other three Clades can't be right messes, but psychics, half-human hybrids, and people infused with mundane or otherworldly energies are a lot easier to romanticize next to the more hard sci-fi-adjacent categories of cyborgs and mutants.)

    In Changeling, the closest thing on the books to a kith that's constantly shifting for myriad small bonuses is the mechanics for Actors being able to switch kiths at will and even gin up unique pseudo-kiths representing a particular shape or attitude of that Actor, such that they get a persistent bonus of a die or two to represent those minor talents.

    Actors are True Fae as they're most recognizably encountered, and Equinox Road, where these mechanics are presented, is the endgame book for Changeling 1e, covering high-Wyrd changelings, journeys into Arcadia, and the nature and mechanics of Faerie beyond the feverish blotted-out recollections available through the haze of Clarity and beginning Wyrd. I would not use them as a basis for a kith, specifically because their mechanics represent something more and less than a kith.

    Also, in Changeling, the Mask specifically covers up or glosses over overt supernatural qualities, which is the main reason why my suggestion involved modifying dropping the Mask; Changeling's take on body horror lines up with its take on alienation through the fact that, maddeningly enough, most people cannot see the evidence (as clear as day to your Wyrded eyes) that you are no longer entirely human unless you shred their comforting illusions in a burst of Glamour or let them see you in their dreams or in the hungry thicket that wants to enslave you and them and everyone.

    Gamelines like Deviant (and to a lesser extent Demon) play with body horror that is persistently visible under certain circumstances, and hiding your inhuman state is a recurrent aspect of those games. Oftentimes, if a changeling's supernatural state has left them with odd needs or hungers, the ease with which they can turn to the Hedge to help them get patched up or keep them in rare goblin methodone means their physiological problems aren't what directly drives them into conflict with people.

    A deviant who can't survive more than three hours out of the water has to steal, borrow, or improvise an environment suit or an aquatic route to make a major trip inland, and even if he wasn't incentivized to stay in the material world where there are more people to hate and/or protect, he doesn't have a near-native's access to a dreamlike dimension where anything and everything is up for sale for the right price. Demons inhabit a world where they can exploit a great many shortcuts but their pursuers are that much closer, and breaking into secure facilities is necessary for a few major forms of damage control from people knowing more about you than they should.

    Changeling is a game of Beautiful Madness, and while it was established early on that "beauty" means a lot of things, there should still be some sense of elegance in how a piece of fae magic manifests — "ugly" things are off-putting on purpose or in a way where someone could theoretically admire the "craft" put into making something repellent, and the lens's takes on the Uncanny Valley tend to be the result of "looking natural" not being the artistic goal of those specimens' maker's view of perfection (when they aren't just deliberately made to be creepy in that particular way).

    Applications of fae talent can be appreciated through the senses. Generalizing this to whole-body affairs like kiths, the thing that makes it compelling should probably factor into the thing that it's good at — form follows function, and the form is appealing at least in the sense that the design is coherent and does what it's set out to do (which, as changelings' miens tend to factor in some of their inner lives in their development over time, makes that Dolly Parton quote about finding out who you are and doing it on purpose extra useful advice).

    This kind of got away from me in the composition, but hopefully you can see what I'm getting at.

    I think the best option is one that leverages their body as a tool, closely followed by trying to adapt to or tough out a hostile environment.                                         I'm going to draw another comparison that should hopefully illustrate why this doesn't quite sit right with me:

                                        In the initial drafts of Changeling 2e, Chimeras were mechanically fairly similar to their 1e incarnation — their kith blessing gave them bonus dice on Social rolls to interact with stuff from the Hedge, and they could spend Glamour to gain the rote quality on a roll to resist or avoid targeted supernatural effects. That's fine and good.

                                        The draft also said that some Chimeras were made by the Fae's attempts to develop new kiths by deliberately combining random bits of other kiths, but that most of them were the result of changelings being accidentally infused with too much of the stuff of Faerie and altering their bodies in an uncontrolled fashion. This… doesn't really work for a couple reasons, chief among them being that the initial drafts were still leaning very much into the Wyrd as a narrative force and the result is that there's a bunch of characters with a mechanically identical Special Quality whose sorting category that hobgoblins at the market chat about is basically "none of the above" (as distinct from "none").

                                        Arc's big post elucidating the differences between Changeling and Demon comes to mind here, because the anthropomorphic tendencies of faerie stories feels at least adjacent to the fact that if something is distinct enough to have a name, it has definite qualities that can be attributed to it. (There are gamelines in the Chronicles of Darkness for which this is not a metaphysical truism, but Changeling is not one of those games at the player toolkit level.)


                                        On the Other end of things, if one of the Gentry is being characterized as "too lazy" to remake their servant for a bunch of different tasks, there are a number of less intensive processes they can go through instead of crafting a wildly variable kith that needs to be able to do any given one of the tasks they're set — they could enlist the aid of another Stranger, retain the services of a Darkling with Riddle-Kith, or just get more slaves. (Or swallow your pride and employ hobgoblins. The Hedge provides!)

                                        Remember, in their own domains the Gentry are functionally gods as far as their servants are concerned, and their sense of attachment can be weird, but the changes they wreak on the Lost are generally related to what they were taken for. If a Keeper decides, quite regularly, that they want to remodel their entire realm, they'll probably just get new people that suit the new décor rather than try to alter all of the existing help; if the old staff are lucky and the Keeper's sensibilities shift in cycles, they may still be alive when their job comes back around. If you don't have the resources to retain multiple specialists, the solution is typically to have a good generalist rather than try to create an omni-specialist.

                                        I guess, going back to the design process again, two operative questions come to mind for moving forward:
                                        1. How available is this kith supposed to be in the game it is being developed for? (Prolific, Unique, or somewhere in-between?)
                                        2. What is the seeming of the character this kith is being written for? (My first guesses are Beast or Wizened.)
