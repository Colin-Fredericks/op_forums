                    Originally posted by Tymeaus Jalynsfein View Post
                    So, does anyone have any great inspiration for a Court System in the Iconic Town of Arkham, Massachusetts (of Lovecraft fame)? I am currently using the Seasonal Courts Structure (though further grouped into Seelie (Spring/Summer) and Unseelie (Autumn/Winter) classifications), and while pretty decent, they are not really grabbing me by the Mivonks and squeezing till I cry, like I would like them to.

                    Well...

                    First of all, it's important to realize that in HP Lovecraft's Arkham, assuming you want to base your setting on the original source material, all of the terrible, supernatural goings-on generally go unnoticed by its citizenry and the world at large. Whereas Innsmouth is literally run by a sinister cult and is plainly a weird and sinister place to outsiders who stay there for any length of time, the people of Arkham are mostly perfectly normal New Englanders. Although there are various dark rumors surrounding the place, it's primarily a college town, and Miskatonic University is presented as a highly respected academic institution... which just happens to have a rather interesting collection of rare books.

                    One big theme, then, could be the duality between the apparent normalcy of the place, and the hideous secrets that it conceals. Perhaps you could have an autumn-like court that deliberately delves into terrifying occult secrets, but another cult that's dedicated to reclaiming a normal human existence.

                    Another potential theme is decay. Lovecraft seems to have had something of a phobia regarding aging and old things in general, the most obvious expression of which is his referring to horrible godlike aliens as "old ones" or "elder gods." This is a common feature of the way he describes places as well: many of his characters are antiquarians, and bad things usually happen in old houses or decaying towns past their prime. Parts of Arkham could exemplify this; according to the Encyclopedia Cthulhiana, Arkham used to be a thriving seaport, and formerly was home to a fair amount of industry, but in time the sea trade failed and the mills mostly shut down following a major flood and a cholera outbreak in the late 19th century. In spite of the economic stimulus provided by the University, there could still be a fair number of economically depressed neighborhoods full of moldering, abandoned buildings dating back two hundred years or more.


                    Overall, I'd say that the seasonal courts are probably a decent fit, if you spin them right. Both the Spring and Autumn Courts could be based around Miskatonic University, with the former reveling in the vibrant campus culture and the latter delving into secrets man was not meant to know with the aid of the famous Miskatonic library (and perhaps their own "special collection"). The Summer Court might take on an unusually academic bent as well, patronizing college sporting events and taking on roles like Dr. Henry Armitage from "The Dunwitch Horror" or the protagonist from "The Case of Charles Dexter Ward," men of learning who use unconventional methods to combat supernatural threats.

                    The Winter Court, on the other hand, might hide out among the poorer sections of the city, feeding on the general despair and doing their best to avoid the strange goings-on in the town, and at the University in particular.


                    In keeping with Lovecraft's fiction, the Gentry familiar to Arkham's Changelings would probably be particularly alien and unknowable. Some might have an obvious interest in humanity, like Nyarlathotep in many of his guises, but others might seem entirely unaware of the lives they destroy, coming and going without any clear rhyme or reason and sometimes dragging people back to their incomprehensible nightmare realms seemingly by accident or as an afterthought. The Huntsmen who serve them wouldn't necessarily understand them any better than Changelings or humans do, and might be more like deranged cultists, attempting to serve their masters' unknowable desires out of fear. These strange Gentry may or may not actually care about getting their captives back, but the Huntsmen have a largely irrational belief that they can win favor with their "gods" by offering up Changelings as "sacrifices."

                    One notable characteristic of Nyarlathotep in particular is that he's often described as the "soul and messenger of the Outer Gods." It may be that he's simply another title and guise of a more alien and aloof Keeper, who enters the world to ensnare Changelings and bring them before his bizarre alter-ego for reasons that make no sense to anyone but him.

                    Another figure that might be particularly appropriate for a Changeling game is Cthulhu, whose influence on the world is based on his ability to manipulate the dreams of certain individuals around the world. He could be one of the Gentry who takes on the form of a sleeping alien giant in Arcadia, but also invades the dreams of sleeping mortals and Changelings, driving them to madness and perhaps also into the hedge, where his capering Huntsmen-cultists can capture them and drag them down to dread R'lyeh under the black seas of Faerie.


                    You might also take a look at Lovecraft's Dreamlands stories. They're more fantasy than horror, and there's an argument to be made that they're mostly inferior imitations of Lord Dunsany's style, but they have a somewhat fairly talish feel to them and involve all sorts of strange places and people that could be parts of Faerie.

                    Speaking of which, Lord Dunsany is awesome (if very much a product of his time), and great inspiration for Changeling. Plus, all his stuff is in the public domain. Look him up.

                                    La Maison du Regret-The House of Regret and the Birth of Paris's Bulwark

                                    In 1862, during the construction of the grand opera house the Palais Garnier, workers digging out foundations ran into the problem of flooding. The site for the building had unusually high groundwater. Wells and pumps were installed, and yet the water kept coming in. Finally, the workers decided to use the water to their advantage, designing a cistern that would fill up until the water pressure made it equal, keeping it steady. The opera house would house their props, costumes, and set pieces down there in time. And to this day, the water cannot be removed.

                                    Maybe it was waiting to fulfill it's purpose.

                                    Paris,traditionally, has been a proud sponsor of the Seasonal Courts for ages, and took pride in how they brought together the courts into the freehold system around the time of the Sun King. World War II changed all of that when Germany invaded France, with Nazi Lost taking advantage of their knowledge of the freehold and breaking their Bulwark. French Resistance spy and former Spring Courtier Nicolette Chaput rallied the Lost of the time and quickly developed the Community of the Teeth-and-Forest Threshold, a freehold of hard lessons and sacrifice built off of the story of The Grandmother's Tale, making sacrifices every so often and taking advantage of their hard knock lessons. During the war, it worked-the Parisian Lost sacrificed their enemies and traitors alike with each defeat, and hit as hard as they could without being found out. When the war ended, though, the courts were on edge as their means of turning the True Fae away began to demand more from their members. The Community threatened to devour itself in its' adolescence.

                                    A shift occurred in 1946 when Chaput met the Belleroses, a changeling couple that fled to America before the war. Marcel Bellerose was a historian, and in fact studied much about the Court of the Sun King and the advent of freeholds during his reign, and proposed a dueling system that would allow the war-torn Lost an outlet for the traumas they picked up from the resistance as well as bringing in the old grandeur of the Sun King to reinvigorate the spirit of the Parisian Lost. Diane Bellerose was a film critic and an early fan of Jean Cocteau's Beauty and the Beast, and she grafted that story to Little Red Riding Hood to create the Cycle of the Maiden, Wild One, and Woodsman, softening the demands and broadening the symbol base the Wyrd had to work with. Together, the three of them constructed the Bulwark and laid the groundwork for the courts, and prepared for the sealing of the ritual.

                                    At the start of spring, the three of them snuck down to the cistern of the Palais Garneir. No one really knows if it was just out of convenience for secrecy, or if the three had divined a secret from the water of the opera house. Diane Bellerose filled the role of the Maiden, Nicolette Chaput was the Wild One, and Marcel Bellerose served as the Woodsman. The intent was not only to begin the cycle that would defend Paris, but allow Chaput to retire her role as a leader of Paris and quietly slip off into retirement. Almost as though by the hand of ravenous fate, the ritual was instead consecrated with the brutal stabbing of Marcel, his body tossed into the cistern and greedily taken by the deep darkness. The closeness of Diane and Nicolette from then on spoke volumes of the story and true intent, though the details were never revealed.

                                    The Bulwark of the Cycle has never needed to be as bloody since, but changeling occultists since have wondered whether or not the act defiled the House of Regret ever since.

                                    The House of Regret is comprised of three places: The Palais Garnier in Paris, an abandoned house called the Wrecked Hut(Cabane Naufrage) in the Hedge, and a 'dream bastion'* called The Water Between(L'eau Entre) that connects the two by the cisterns in both of their basements. It's called a dream bastion because that's what it most closely resembles, but as it stands, no one is really quite sure what the Water Between actually is-anyone can float through and breathe and wander the waters as easily as a dream. The three can be traversed by anyone as much as any Hedge Gate, but rarely is it used this way.

                                    This is because the House reacts strangely to creatures of the Wyrd. It brings haunting visions and realities that dredge up loss and trauma, damaging moments for the ruins of a fae's mind. On days other than the ritual days, a changeling in either the opera house or cabin suffers a Clarity 1 Breaking Point every hour, though this can be avoided for a day by being invited in by the Wayward-after the day is up, the invited cannot receive the benefit again for a number of days equal to the Wayward's Mantle. Mortals and other supernatural creatures witnessing the visions that plague Wyrd creatures suffer related integrity loss as is pertinent to their respective scores.

                                    In the Water Between, the free-flowing and ill-defined space of sorrow that it is, a changeling suffers the same breaking point every ten minutes, and mortals suffer an Integrity 0 Breaking point at the same points in duration(Vampires, Mages, Mummies, and Demons suffer the same for their respective stats. Werewolves suffer the next breaking point towards Spirit every hour. Prometheans risk Pilgrimage 1 breaking points every hour, but can be averted for a day by earning a point of Pilgrimage from their time in the water. Sin-Eaters, curiously enough, do not suffer Synergy losses of any kind, though they and their Geists are noticeably shaken by the experience, and suffer the Shaken Condition during their time and afterwards as a result. Beasts are treated as Satiety 10 within the Water and seem to think they have every right to be scared shitless by that place, similarly suffering the Shaken Condition).

                                    Hobgoblins and True Fae will not enter any part of the House without invitation from the Wayward, and Huntsmen prefer to avoid it, suffering a penalty to their actions equal to the Wayward's Mantle within the House unless invited by the Wayward or earning a Yearning off of the Wayward.

                                    On ritual days, changelings who are friends of the courts(Status Freehold, Court Goodwill or Mantle 1) can enter the Palais Garnier or the Wrecked Hut without fear of the House's retribution, but that doesn't mean it stops warping reality and perspective. Instead, the House's illusions and dreams set the stage and prepare in anticipation of the ritual performance, namely in providing the Final Gate that each new Wayward is pushed through upon failing the ritual. These illusions do not cause Clarity or Integrity loss unless a particular elements related to the character is evoked in the spirit of the evening(ie, the story would include iconography and staging that would hit on individual breaking points for a character) The Water Between remains as grim as ever, though. No one knows what will happen to the House if the Bulwark fails. Waywards who have kept the House don't know any better what would happen, but always seem inclined towards fatalistic outcomes.

                                    It is one of the most potent mystical landmarks within France, but only in the presence of the Wyrd. It is the only place the Parisian Lost would enact their Bulwark, seemingly a central point for the strange powers of the fae that brings the rituals to life. Bridge-burners claim it's an egg for an Arcadian Realm. Necromantically inclined fae suggested it is touched by the Underworld. Lord Sages of the Unknown Reaches sometimes wonder if it connects to the Shadow or the Astral. It is the Rose Court's greatest achievement and most terrifying secret, and it seemingly only loosely answers to one individual.

                                    The Wayward.

                                    More on her later, I think.

                                    *I call in a dream bastion to denote a sort of physical place that operates like dreams, rather than the Bastions founds in Swords at Dawn. It's a thing I use in my games.

                                                For random background purposes, Nicolette was an Ogre, Marcel was a Fairest, and Diane was probably a Wizened. We'll have to see.

                                                The Wayward

                                                The Wayward was never an essential role. Sure, in Beauty and the Beast, the story is very much about a daughter shifting her affection for her father onto another man, and his stealing the Beast's rose is what prompted the story. Sure, the grandmother needed bread and milk. But the story doesn't hinge on them, and quite often they either get written out or killed-sometimes subsequently eaten. They serve as impetus for the Maiden to enter the Forest, and they often serve as a tool of transition for her, but they are not an actor in the dynamics of the story. As such, the Bulwark isn't ruined if there is no Wayward. The role is supplementary, a convenience. At it's core, the only reason the Wayward is preferred is because the alternative is a body of corpses.

                                                Of course, the Wyrd recognizes the Wayward as so much more than a victim or a vain plot point. The Wayward is the one who dwells at and brings forth the Threshold, a creature half in light and shadow, an incarnate of both the familiar and the foreign. So, much like anything else in faerie culture, the Wayward is a mixed bag, a curse mixed in with some blessings.

                                                The Wayward is chosen with the completion of the Cycle's Bulwark. When the Maiden, Wild One and Woodsman duel to determine which one of them is to 'die', it is sanctified by the wounding of the one acted against, whether it comes a result of a fight or the conclusion of a long and rational discussion. This wound must be at one lethal damage. The first curse of the Wayward are her wounds-they don't heal for the next nine months, when the ritual is performed again. They don't get progressively worse, but a character's health is effectively capped by the damage. Few forces under the moon and the sun can heal this damage, and the consequences of defying the Wyrd on this precedent are unknown.

                                                The Bulwark is not properly completed until the Wayward proceeds through the Water Between to the opposite side of the House of Regret(Palais Garnier to Cabane Naufrage or visa versa, based on which location she was wounded in). The Wayward stumbles throughout the opera house or cabin as the House focuses it's dreamscapes solely on her, creating a descent into a personal hell of loss until she makes it out of the Water Between. The second and third curses come through the passage of the Water. First, the Wayward suffers a Clarity drop, reducing her current Clarity by 1. Secondly, she ages to a proportionate equivalent of ten years for a mortal(So if a changeling can live up to 200 years, she ages 20, so on and so forth). The House takes it's toll.

                                                From then on till the next commencement of the Bulwark, the Wayward is banned from the City of Paris and it's equivalent Hedge territory. The Cabane Naufrage is a rare Hedge Locale in that is not a fixed point like other Hollows and settlements within the Hedge, always being at least a six-hour journey away from the furthermost Hedge settlement that belongs to the Parisian Lost. Hedge cartographers often use the Cabane as the measuring stick in relation to the fixed hollows to define territorial spaces, much to the consternation of the Hedge inhabitants. But for the Wayward, these boundaries are important-The Hedge actively closes up at her approach into the Parisian Territory, routing her away from it. Should she try to come in the mortal world, the flow of the Wyrd manipulates events and conspires to keep her out. One way or another, if she enters Paris, she will either be stopped or pay for it.

                                                The exception to this rule is the Palais Garnier, and here is where the blessings begin. The Wayward is welcome in the House of Regret, and treated as though she belongs in all ways. No mortals question her presence in the opera house, and people who meet her for the first time in the House start with a Good Impression of her for social maneuvering uness she goes out of her way to make a bad impression. She may join in the theater troupes, mingle with the elite who come for shows, and in general never faces trouble that may cause her to be rejected or removed from the premises. The presence of a Wayward often ensures that the Lost can have the place open to them on ritual nights. The Wayward's connection to the House means she can dip into it's well of regret at any time, harvesting the emotion at will, and so effectively also has infinite Glamour while in the House-something no other fae may do. Finally, the House does not shift in response to the Wayward's Wyrd, and cannot be the cause of any further Clarity Breaking Points for the Wayward while she bears the role.[I'm debating about whether the House absolves her from Clarity Breaking points that her role might otherwise force her into]

                                                The Water Between still plagues her with visions of regret, but in the closest thing to an act of kindness the House ever does, the Water emphasizes things the Wayward regrets that the Wayward can do something about when her time is up. Retiring Waywards often know exactly what they are going to do when their time is up thanks to this.

                                                The Wayward may invite people in for up to a day and spare them from the effects of the House on their Clarity. She may put as many stipulations on her invitation as she likes, and if accepted, the invited must maintain them. Changelings, mortals, and other supernatural creatures suffer the normal effects of the House the instant they break her rules. Hobgoblins and True Fae who break the rules of the invitation are filled with dread at the oncoming sorrow, and can take no action that isn't directly related to leaving the House until they are out.

                                                Huntsmen can accept the invitation, and should they break it, they must focus on leaving. They can take other actions on the way out, but they take a penalty to their actions equal to the Wayward's Mantle, with each action that is not related to leaving after the first increasing the penalty by 1(So if the Wayward has a Mantle of 3, the Huntsmen would take a -3 penalty to their first action not related to leaving, -4 to their second, etc.). Huntsmen can also avoid the House's curses by gaining a Yearning off of the Wayward, which gives him access to the House without penalty for a number of hours equal to the Wayward's Mantle.

                                                Once the day is over, the particular invited members cannot be allowed back in for a number of days equal to the Wayward's Mantle. If a invited member leaves before the day is up, it counts as a visit and as such must wait the allotted period.

                                                The Wayward also gets +3 dice to traversing the Hedge and finding things within it. Asides from making them adept Hedge explorers, this makes them invaluable in defense, since they can use this blessing to find approaching enemies and use their curse in relation to the Hedge to block off the ways in. Of course, this puts them at risk of being right in the line of fire.

                                                Despite the potency of the Role, changelings who are cast for the Final Duel do not envy the losses and vulnerabilites the Wayward faces, or the isolation of the Freehold. The Bulwark must be maintained, and such a life if preferable to killing, but many would rather win, even if the blessings of the Union were not there, than be cast into the deathly archetype.

                                                *For the record, I realize there's a nasty stereotype beginning to develop, this idea that marriage is must-have goal for people and that people who don't or for some reason can't should kill themselves. This is one of the reasons the Wayward role exists rather than simply dying-a character cast as the Wayward can struggle to prove there is more to life beyond that. But also I wanted a system that is flawed and archaic in some ways, so as to keep a door open for characters with revolutionary aspirations. The Wayward, in this way, is also the rallying point for every person who feels outcast by the system and it's tendency to typecast people in terms of the roles, and can very well lead the charge to break the system.

                                                        Sidebar: Into the Depths

                                                        Why on earth would anyone even bother going into the House of Regret asides from ritual days? Sure, it can be used for defensive purposes for a limited amount of time, but asides from that, the consequences for anyone going into the House are so sharp, you have to wonder who would risk it. A night at the opera is not worth the Clarity.

                                                        The answer lies in its' strangely real properties as a physically shifting place of loss. The House of Regret does sometimes merely cause hallucinations, but it also sometimes is manifestly real, and it draws it's tools and images all the way from a person's own unique mythology to the broad archetypes of their regrets that have been around from the dawn of time. While that last aspect might allow for personal recovery tools, the more tempting aspect is simply this: anything that is lost can theoretically be found there. From the stuffed animal lost as a child to some of the most potent Tokens ever to slip the mind of the True Fae, the simple truth is that a changeling willing to brave the House, particularly the Water Between, can profit greatly from it.

                                                        This isn't a highly advised course of action. THe House is not just waiting to spill out spoils-its' nature is to drown a person in their own emotion. But for those willing to take the hit by spending the time, they may find what they need. Some of Paris's greatest treasures have come from the House.

                                                        Curiously, this is one thing a Wayward cannot do in their time minding the House. They will come to know the House and it's oddities more intimately than anyone else, but a Wayward will generally take nothing with them but experience.

                                                            The Bulwark of Paris, the Final Duel, and the Married

                                                            At it's simplest form, the Bulwark for the Cycle of the Maiden, Wild One, and Woodsman is superficially not complicated. Every nine months, three people representing the three roles gather together in the House of Regret and either literally kill one of three, or metaphorically kill them by wounding them so they bleed and having them pass through the Water Between. It's really that simple...in theory.

                                                            SIDEBAR TIME!
                                                            Can the Wayward Screw Up the Bulwark?
                                                            The loser of the Final Duel is not properly the Wayward until they have crossed through the Water Between after being wounded. Killing the Wayward on the way to the Water just completes the ritual, but if the Wayward is delayed until the day has passed, the Bulwark breaks, the Wayward loses the first curse, and Paris is vulnerable for nine months.


                                                            In execution, the Bulwark is actually very demanding. The ritual demands sincerity-sincerity in how much a person embodies the role, sincerity in the motives for the sacred matrimony and murder. The people doing this are not just acting out a play, they are making the ancient tale alive again, bringing age old conflicts and lessons up again and again. That means the people involved need to have stakes in the performance of the ritual. The ritual has been failed before*, and they mark some of the darkest times for Paris since World War II.

                                                            In game terms, each role must be filled by someone who meets the following list. First, they must be a friend of the Paris Freehold at the least, expressed by Mantle, Court Goodwill, or Status(Freehold) 1. Second, they must have each of the traits described for the role in each Court-these traits have been divined from experimentation with the House of Regret and what it seems count as passing. The usual way of confirming this is by having a participant successfully win a duel against a member of each court. Third, the participant must also exhibit a final trait that the house deems defining for the roles.

                                                            This final requirement is there to allow freedom to the Storyteller to set the tone for their stories. The House shifts it's opinions on this, leaving an edge of uncertainty to the entire state of affairs to fuel conflict. The Storyteller should have something in mind rather than leave it as an arbitrary decision.

                                                            SIDEBAR TIME!
                                                            What is Actually Needed?

                                                            You may have noticed some caginess to the phrasing above. Do Changelings have to be the participants? Are the Duels required?

                                                            Strictly speaking, the answer to both of those is "No, but it helps."

                                                            In regards to the Participants, not one of them has to be a changeling, so long as they have Court Goodwill or Freehold Status and traits regardless. It could be a mortal, a Beast, a Vampire, even a True Fae or Huntsman who has somehow obtained these requirements, though unlikely. That said, a changeling must be present at the execution of the Bulwark to trigger the House as the judge of the sanctity of the event, and few creatures are quite as in tune with the demands of the Wyrd as faeries. Still, if you're running a crossover game and you want your pretty little werewolf to be the Maiden, go for it.

                                                            In regards to the Duels, the Duels are semi-mystical rituals themselves that do tie into the Bulwark, but as a refinement process that was added on after the fact rather than a core feature. A character can just be approved of by a member of each Court for the role and be good, or even just inherently possess the traits to make them ideal for the role. It's rare, but it can happen that a character is a perfect fit without aid.

                                                            Does that affect the execution of the Duels? Sort of, but not by a whole lot. The Sovereigns keep their eyes on the Sphere Score(more on that later), and if they find people who happen upon these aspects, they tend to prefer keeping them in reserve and using the Duels to make primary candidates anyways. If the worst should happen and a participant be kept from their performance in anyway, the Sovereigns like to have cards no one had eyes on to quickly fill the roles to keep the Bulwark going in times of crisis.


                                                            Similarly, the Final Duel, whether it manifests as a fight or a deep-seated discussion with a vote at the end, needs to be performed with sincerity. The two who 'kill' the third have to believe, for whatever reasons, that this person needs to go based on the merits of their own defense. The politics that get involved make this tricky, as participants toeing a party line can ruin the ritual. There's no mechanical way to measure this, though a stop gap might be that each person must spend a point of Willpower in regards to their determining conflict against the person they think should be 'killed'.

                                                            The Final Duel is treated as though the participants were in a dream, with each one using their highest Power, Finesse, and Resistance Attribute for their rolls, although their Wyrd does not count as a equipment bonus.***

                                                            Beyond the basics, anything added is pagentry-and the fae do love their shows. While the simple version can be done at any time the ritual is required, the Parisian Freehold tends to make a night of it, arranging the reservation of the Palais Garnier for the evening and allowing mingling and eating and partying before and after the ritual. The Bulwark itself is made more theatrical in it's execution-not only because it brings the Lost together, but because the final play ensures a last shot at building sincerity before the Final Duel.

                                                            The common script begins with the Maiden and the Woodsman on stage, taking time to interact with one another. The scene is cut short by the Wayward(or someone filling in if the last one died) calling for the Maiden, usually asking for help. The Maiden 'arrives' to find the Wild One, and these two take some time on stage to also engage one another, before the Wayward again calls the Maiden to come to her. The Maiden leaves, and the Woodsman arrives, confronting the Wild One for some time, before the Maiden returns and the Final Duel begins. The proper metaphorical killing includes the remaining two shoving the newly wounded Wayward through a massive pair of gates into the back of the stage, where she will wander her way down to the cistern and through the Water Between. The play on stage concludes with the Sovereigns rising from their seats and declaring the winners Married through their bloody Union.

                                                            The time the participants spend on stage are spent engaging one another. They may argue and fight over personal affairs between them, or they may confront one another on their beliefs and the failings of them. It may be wholly intellectual and slightly staged, or it can be personal affair. No script is actually handed to them-sincerity must determine the dialogue and action.

                                                            The fulfillment of the Bulwark imparts the usual benefit of every courtier receiving their Mantle as a defense bonus against the True Fae, but an additional benefit is bestowed on all members of the freehold based on the Union that is formed that lasts for the next nine months. A Union of Maiden and Wild One offers a +2 to Investigation and Persuasion. A Union of Maiden and Woodsman offers a +2 to Occult and Survival. A Union of Wild One and Woodsman offers a +2 to Brawl and Subterfuge. On top of this, the Courts represented by the Union(through Mantle or Goodwill) are declared the rulers of Paris for the next nine months, and have full access to their Crowns.

                                                            As for the Married, the victors of the Final Duel are granted several privileges by Wyrd and community alike, though some aspects may be mixed blessings. The first and most immeadiate effect is that the two now benefit from an oath between the two of them. In game, the particular blessing is determined by the Wyrd, but players and Storytellers should devise an appropriate blessing for their relationship. Second, the Married are invited to the Council of the Sovereigns in an advisory capacity. There's not a lot of real power, as the role is similar to religious leaders, philosophers, and poets kept by political leaders today, but they still learn many secrets of the city(though not the Sphere Score) and can influence decisions in their capacity as a Voice for the Wyrd. Third, they gain 3 dots of Fame(Freehold), Barfly, and 4[/5?]**** Dots they can spread between Staff, Retainer, Contacts, Allies, and Resources for the next nine months. the 4 dots represent a shared benefit between the two of them, so they must handle that carefully, and their Fame and Barfly are not assured for after the nine months, though those can be maintained.

                                                            [I want to have a special blessing for each based on their roles, but I'm having trouble gauging how much is too much. The ideas are that a Maiden can basically stop a fight, period, so many times, a Wild One can convince someone of anything so many times, and the Woodsman can find anyone so many times]

                                                            The Married are not permitted to fill the roles of the next Ritual-the Wyrd rejects them outright. Many find that going one or two rounds before applying themselves again to the roles feels best, though some people certainly re-apply themselves again and again as much as possible. The Sovereigns usually dissuade people from doing this, following their own ineffable plans to ensure the safety and sanctity of Paris.
