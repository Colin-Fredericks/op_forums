        Originally posted by Satchel View Post
        A thing which tangentially relates to Loki's GM's cannibalism gaffe and more directly relates to one of those common reading attitudes about the game:

        2e's done a good job at making it more up-front, and Arc's old spiel about Never Going Back as the corollary to Finding Your Way Home is pretty direct about it, but it bears emphasis that the Durance and its related traumas do not render the Lost immune to casual malice.

        Even beyond the explicit "bad guy" narrative archetypes that the Wyrd can and does orchestrate, most changelings who've been around for a while are not going to be especially good people without deliberate effort. This is a group of people with multiple innate means of getting out of trouble and a natural bias toward a smaller-scale version of the same behavior as the beings that made them into magical-narrative actors that can talk to the whole world. The vast majority have learned better than to do anything for free, and no small number of them have deeply-ingrained agendas amplified by the loss of their emotional regulators.

                                To add on to this, and a bit of what Loki said earlier, take note of what kiths/contracts/merits the players take. Those are the powers they want to use, and will be the first thing they look at for solutions to any problems you introduce. Changeling has a wide variety of powers, some of which bypass certain types of problems entirely. If one of your players has Phantom Glory, for example, they're just going to ghost through most physical obstacles. That's not necessarily a bad thing, but you'll want to provide problems that can be easily solved by the players' skillsets so that they can feel competent and useful, and problems that they don't have an easy out for to make them worry and think, and that's a loooot easier to do if you have a handle on how their powers work.

                                This goes double for combat: it's a major subsystem and it works just differently enough from the rest of play to cause some serious dissonance if you aren't aware of it. The specifics of why could easily be their own essay but I'll leave that for later in favor of a specific example: My first Changeling character was a Summer Court MetalFlesh/FireHeart. I was looking to play an angry tanky type, and without really coordinating with the rest of the party I just picked things that seemed effective and thematically appropriate. I did too good a job, and it wasn't long before we realized that anything that could deal more than a single point of damage to me would kill anyone else in the Motley in two hits at most. This left the ST really struggling to provide fights that were interesting for me and not insanely lethal for everyone else.

                                                    "Unfun" strikes me as a useless word for these discussions, especially since we're talking about a horror game. Roleplaying games in general aren't necessarily about having a lighthearted good time. Extra Credits uses the term "engagement" for what many of us are really after. We want characters, settings, and events that are worth caring about. We want to fear for our characters' lives and sanity when they encounter danger. We want to share in their struggles, triumphs, and despair. We want to see how their world changes as we make decisions.

                                                    If Huntsmen don't look like they'd be engaging antagonists, explain what the problem is. While there are some poorly designed game elements out there in RPG land, many are just easily misused or misunderstood.
