                The way I'll probably rule this is that iron harms the fae because it's humanity's labour. With the True Fae as a thinly-veiled analogy for the upper class that does what it wills to the peasants (since they are, after all, the Gentry), the difference between iron and steel is not of composition, but of use. The ugly, black iron is the metal of the servants, the workers, the filthy underclass. Shiny steel, on the other hand, is a metal fit for a noble's sword.

                And cold iron is such a bane because it represents toil. Fae don't have to truly work for anything; what their magic can't provide, an enslaved servant will. Hammering on a lump of iron until it's sharp, making a crude shank to strike at your master, carries the weight of a million stories with it.

                                                    I think the big issue of all the cold iron debates is that they are all far too focused on "what". As noted up thread, just about all forms of useful iron are technically "steel" , and trying to figure out what makes it cold iron has been contentious to say the least.

                                                    I have latched on the Shadowknights idea that the "how" - the process by which the item was crafted, carries more weight. You can have two blades, one a well made but mass-produced item with optimum iron to carbon ratio and forged with the most modern tools available, the other a blade recently made, but using old techniques - working by hand and simple tool from ore to finished product. The mass-produced item wouldn't even tickle one of the Gentry, while the hand-made weapon could cut him down in a blow.

                                                    Ultimately, the iron is a symbol, and the forging is a story. And only a Story can kill a Story.
