            Even if it's a pledge to abide by freehold laws, the problem is that you're still relying on the computer/literal genie of the Wyrd as a primary defense. This encourages the enemy and unscrupulous citizens to look for loopholes or means of causing false positives to sow distrust of their rivals. Sane people, knowing that no law can be perfect and that no compact is without loopholes due to the way human language works, even if they want to abide by spirit of the law, are going to be looking for loopholes to defend against and be wary of potential accidental violations. This requires more mental resources be spent on interaction with people, as if things weren't already nerve-wracking. The more you tighten the laws, the greater the burden it puts on all individuals operating under those laws, and the more you increase the likelihood that someone could be ostracized for being an oathbreaker because they got on the wrong side of a technicality or ambiguous language. The more hoops people have to jump through because of complex laws, the harder it becomes to tell the difference between criminal intent from psychological fatigue and hyper-vigilance from being manipulated by another's "reasonable" demands for transparency. When I see the legalese arms race that's going to come out of such an approach to freehold law, I'd be worried that it was an Arcadian invasion trying and failing to masquerade as a freehold.

            This is why laws and enforcement alone cannot improve lives. We need intelligent, compassionate people who know when to exercise mercy, recognize extenuating circumstances, and view actions from a human perspective. That's something the Wyrd is not interested in doing. That's what makes beings who default to solving their problems with the Wyrd so terrifyingly alien to me.

            EDIT: Just after I posted, I figured out a way to summarize my perception of how using the Wyrd to create ideal laws would go.

            Step 1: Solve the Halting Problem.
            Step 2: Use the solution to determine if ideal laws can be reached in a finite number of iterations.
            Step 3: If so, begin iterating.
            Step N: Profit?

            Step 1 is tricky enough in the real world.

                        Again, the power of Pledges isn't in their ability to force people to obey, it's that those involved know when it has been broken. You don't put the punishment in the pledge, or rather you make the magical, Wyrd enacted sanction minor. Slip ups and false positives happen, those responsible get a warning to be more careful in the form of a minor stroke of bad luck or something. Far, far more importantly, everybody else, especially those with the power to do something about it, are alerted that there has been a violation. Then they can go and investigate and use their discretion on how to proceed.

                        Basically, you don't rely on the Wyrd to mete out significant punishment, you DO use it to flag you when something needs investigation.

                        My first Motley had exactly this concern, and we ended up having our Pledge punish betrayal with Vulnerability. Betray the Motley, and they would instantly know and you got no defense against attacks or contracts. But, this sanction was also completely meaningless if the other members looked at the situation and realized it was a setup, because nothing compelled them to attack if that was, y'know, stupid.
