            Alright, here's the first of the courts I've been developing for the Gold Mountain Freehold, the Court of the Mint (sorry for the wall of text >_>):


            The Court of the Mint, the Soeng, the Court of Greed
            “I know what it’s like to have nothing, and if there’s one thing I’ve learned it’s this: there’s no nobility in poverty.”


            In 1848, a newspaper editor named Samuel Brannon strode through the streets of San Francisco, holding aloft a vial of gold dust and shouting a proclamation that would change the history of a nation. “Gold!” he cried, “Gold from the American River!”

            Of course, by the time he made his proclamation, Brannon had already bought up all the picks, shovels, and pans he could find, and established what would be, for a time, the only store between San Francisco and the gold fields.

            Brannon’s proclamation resounded across the globe, and by 1949 men were flocking to California from around the globe, risking everything for the promise of wealth and a better life. Many found what they were looking for, but in the end it was men like Brannon, the shrewd and the ruthless, who profited most. It was men like Brannon who built San Francisco, as they have built all of the great cities and mighty empires of the modern age.

            The Court of the Mint claims to be the oldest of the Gold Mountain Courts, and it may very well be true. They take their name from the Old San Francisco Mint, which once was home to more than a third of the U.S. gold reserve and is one of the few notable buildings that survived the great earthquake of 1906. When the fires that followed reached the building, the windows melted and the very stones burst from the heat.

            The day was saved through the heroic efforts of the workers trapped inside, who drew water from a well in the Mint’s open-air courtyard and used it to cool the walls of the vault. In doing so they saved not only themselves, but the precious gold that was needed to back the nation’s currency. The Court of the Mint points to this as but one example of how selfishness and the love of wealth can inspire humanity to greatness.

            The Courtiers of the Mint are capitalists, first and foremost. They see themselves as merchants and financiers, and can be linked in one way or another with nearly every legitimate money-making venture in the Bay Area (not to mention many less than legitimate ones). Their pursuit of wealth doesn’t end with mere dollars and cents, however; it defines their relationship with the world of the fae as well. To them, Pledges simply represent another kind of business deal, and they can bargain like no others when the Goblin Market comes to town.

            Arcadia really isn't so different from the world of finance, after all. Both are governed by arcane rules and elaborate contracts. And if there’s one thing the Courtiers of the Mint know how to do, it’s negotiate a contract.


            Nickname: 49’ers


            Approaches

            Bad Investment: Some accuse the Courtiers of the Mint of being misers, but they see themselves more as builders, creating new jobs and institutions by investing their money wisely. Huntsmen gain a point of Yearning when they successfully sabotage a business venture initiated or financed by a Courtier of the Mint.


            Fool’s Gold: A wise man once said that the problem with money is that it makes you do things you don’t want to do. Huntsmen gain a point of Yearning when they entrap a Courtier of the Mint using the promise of money or other valuables as bait.


            Let’s Make a Deal…: The Courtiers of the Mint have a hard time passing up the opportunity to wheel and deal, and are most vulnerable to those who can best them at their own game. Huntsmen gain a point of Yearning when they trick a Courtier of the Mint into accepting an exchange of goods or services that leaves them poorer or more vulnerable.


            Trappings of Nobility

            Courtiers of the Mint bedeck themselves in symbols of prosperity, but always seek to present the appearance of professionalism and respectability. Collared shirts, tailored suits in dark colors, and Italian shoes make up the typical uniform of the Court. Courtiers are rarely seen without at least one piece of gold jewelry, but it’s considered crass to overdo it. Rings are common, as are gold watches and simple earrings and necklaces (for women). The color most associated with the court is green; members of the court commonly wear ties in shades of green, while green shirts and dresses are often seen on courtiers outside of a professional setting. Other symbols of the court include coins (particularly the California gold coinage minted in the 19th century), gold dust, gold pans, picks, shovels, weighing scales, the Chinese characters 商 (soeng “commerce; merchant”) and 財 (coi, “wealth”), and, of course, representations of the Old San Francisco Mint itself.


            Rituals

            Feast of the Annunciation (March 2): The name of this annual festival is actually a somewhat sacrilegious pun. Unrelated to the Catholic solemnity of the same name, it instead commemorates Samuel Brannon’s famous announcement of the discovery of gold at Sutter’s Mill. On the day of the ritual, the President of the Court hosts a formal banquet, to which all members of the Court (and only members of the court) are invited. The President is expected to be a generous host - a disappointing Feast is thought to portend dark times for the Court.

            The most important part of the ritual comes at the conclusion of the banquet: after offering a toast to the continued prosperity of the court, the President makes a pledge to contribute in some way to the continued growth and prosperity of San Francisco or the greater Bay Area in the time before the next Feast. After the President makes his pledge, each guest in attendance is expected to do the same.

            These pledges need not be altruistic in nature (and, in fact, rarely are); pledges to renovate a place of business or invest in a local start-up company are fairly standard. The pledge doesn’t need to involve a huge monetary commitment, either, though making ostentatious pledges is one way to impress the Court’s leadership. Failing to honor a pledge, however, is considered a dire sin indeed, with possible punishments up to and including expulsion from the Court.


            Mint Day (April 18): Held at the Old Mint building itself, this ritual commemorates the earthquake of 1906 and the heroic effort of the mint worker on that day. It’s an occasion for remembering fallen comrades and celebrating past victories. The event tends to begin on a somber note, but as the wine flows and the evening wears on it typically develops into an occasion for drunken boasts and humorous stories.


            Market Day (late spring/early summer): The Court of the Mint has a standing agreement with a mobile Goblin Market that sweeps into San Francisco each year with the summer fog. On the first day that the Market opens, only members of the court are to be permitted inside. However, on that day every Courtier must make at least one purchase. Members of the Court have a tradition of hosting parties on the evening following the market, where they boast about the deals they’ve made and mercilessly mock fellow Courtiers who got taken for a ride.


            Titles

            President (Mantle 5): The highest title within the Court, the President is selected by the Elders from among those Courtiers with a sufficiently advanced Mantle based on his or her skills in leadership and bargaining. The President is usually a lifetime appointee, but the Elders of the Court can depose him by a vote of no confidence


            Elder (Mantle 4+): Sometimes also called “Senior Partners,” or simply “Partners,” Elders are respected members of the Court who have abundantly proven their ability and commitment to the ideals of the Court. The title of Elder is only conferred by unanimous agreement on the part of the existing Elders of the Court. Other members of the Court look to the Elders for guidance, and Presidents and Vice Presidents of the Court are often (but not always) also Elders.


            Vice President (Mantle 3+): Originally, the Court of the Mint had only one Vice President, who was the highest-ranking advisor to the president and his appointed successor should he die or otherwise be rendered unable to govern the Court. In keeping with modern trends in business administration, however, there are now a variable number of Vice Presidents appointed by the President to oversee specific facets of the court. These include the Vice President of Human Resources (in charge of approving new recruits and monitoring the overall morale of the Court), the Vice President of Public Relations (charged with maintaining positive relations with the other courts, as well as keeping tabs on them), and the Vice President of Security (the chief military leader of the court).


            Officer (Mantle 2+): Sometimes also called “Secretaries” or even “Executive Assistants,” Officers of the Court are Courtiers who have been entrusted with special duties by the President or a Vice President. Most Courtiers compete eagerly for these appointments, as being entrusted with official duties is usually the first step on the path to becoming an Elder of the Court.


            Stakeholder (Mantle 1+): “Stakeholder” is an informal term sometimes used to refer to any accepted member of the Court who has no formal title. It is meant as a term of respect, recognizing that fact that even the lowest-ranking members of the Court contribute to its strength and survival.


            Mantle of the Mint

            • The mantle may manifest as a faint sound of jangling coins as the Courtier moves or the scent of freshly-printed dollar bills.
            The character develops an almost instinctual grasp of the art of the deal, gaining a dot of Persuasion.

            •• Courtiers gain a dot of the Goblin Vow merit related to the court.

            ••• A Courtier’s mantle may cause her irises to shine like gold or emeralds, her hands to appear stained with green ink, or even manifest as the familiar ring of an opening cash register.
            The character has no patience for mind tricks; if someone wants him to do something for them, they had better be prepared to pay for it. He gains the Indomitable merit.

            •••• Courtiers gain a Personal Approach related to how they fulfill the ideals and story of the court. Huntsmen cannot gain Yearning from a Court Approach unless they fulfill the Personal Approach first.

            ••••• The mantle is even stronger. The distinctive reek of cash clings to the character at all times, and she seems somehow surrounded by opulence no matter where she goes. Objects she holds or examines seem suddenly more valuable and desirable than they formerly appeared.
            New opportunities seem to positively throw themselves at the character, and he may distribute three dots between Contacts, Allies, Resources, or Retainers, which manifest through seemingly ordinary means shortly after this level of mantle is reached. Furthermore, as long as the character operates or remains invested in a money-making enterprise, he may experience a sudden and well-timed windfall once per story, sufficient for a single purchase of up to Resources •••••.

                    The flower courts seem a litle abstract and disconnected from the Freehold's story. Have you considered basing the courts off of the key rolls in the Bullwark instead? Here's my thoughts on your Freehold; perhaps something like this:

                    The city was once home to two different, warring, Freeholds, each based off one of the two stories (Beauty and the Beast and Little Red Riding Hood). Members of both courts joined together and founded the Court of the Daughter, modeled after the young girls from each story, then convinced the Changelings of each freehold to strike a truce and unite against some greater threat. When the Freeholds joined and the stories were merged, members of the Little Red Riding Hood based Freehold started the Hatchet Court, after the woodcutter from the tale. The courtiers who had followed the story of The Beauty and the Beast formed the Beastly Court.

                    The Hatchet Court
                    The Hunters, Wolfslayers, Woodsmen, The Iron Axe
                    Emotion: Hatred (Sometimes justified, other times... not so much.)
                    The Hatchet prides itself on it's fighting prowess, it's self-reliance, and it's knowledge of the local area (especially the Hedge). They endeavor to protect the Freehold from all manner of monsters, from hedge creatures to vampires. Unfriendly Changelings see them as arrogant, violent bigots who shoot first and ask questions later.
                    Approach: Use their arrogance; make them underestimate their opponent.

                    The Beastly Court
                    Lonely Ones, The Rose Court, The Court of Claws, Wild Ones
                    Emotion: Anticipation (The Beast and the Wolf are both patient, in their own ways.)
                    The Beastly Court (made up predominantly of Beasts, Ogres, and Darklings) likes to keep to itself. Their organization is loose and their meetings infrequent, to allow their members the privacy they crave. They dislike politics and niceties and are sometimes uncouth, yet they fancy themselves dignified and pride themselves on hospitality. When they do make friends, they are fiercely loyal - frighteningly so. When angered they are powerful enemies who should be feared for their cunning as much as their, often considerable, strength. Enemies see them as deceitful monsters, dressing up in sheeps clothes to lure innocents to their doom.
                    Approach: Cause them to drive their own friends away.

                    The Court of Daughters
                    The Golden Key, the Ring and Mirror, The Red Hoods
                    Emotion: Courage (To go into the woods alone is bravery, as is standing beside someone that others hate.)
                    The Daughters unified the courts and deserve full credit for founding the freehold, and they are the central element of the Bulwark, though they rarely rule. They prize the virutes of courage and compassion, as well as skepticism and caution. They often inspire a protective instinct in those around them. Those who dislike them see them as naive children lost in the woods.
                    Approach: Earn their trust and feign weakness, taking advantage of their naivete and compassion.

                    The Bulwark
                    The story changes slightly every year, depending on how the Bulwark plays out, but the roles are always the same: a maiden (the gender is fluid, but the term is used as a reference to both source stories), a monster who desires her, and her rescuer. The Bulwark is a complex sort of courtly game (with lots of dueling).

                    The Courts hold a series of contests (usually duels), to determine the courtiers who will fill the rolls. The winner of each court takes a role as appropriate.

                    At the outset, the monster (Wild One) and the rescuer (Woodsman) each choose a token (rose for the hero of the story, a thorn dripping blood for the villain). This determines their role in the Bulwark this year. It is not random; they choose deliberately. They cannot reveal their choices to each other. They may choose the same token. (So, you might have two heroes or two villains).

                    In the first stage, the maiden goes to meet the monster in the forest (sometimes this is a literal forest, other times the hedge or somewhere else dangerous and remote), where the monster will attempt to convince her of its good intentions.

                    Meanwhile, the rescuer must track the monster/find the maiden.

                    When the rescuer arrives, the maiden must choose a side.

                    If the party she chooses is a villain, she is "captured" by them. The other party must then defeat her captor, to secure her release.

                    If the party she chooses is a hero, then the third party's token is revealed. If they are also a hero, the conflict is over, and the maiden will rule for the next year.

                    If the winner of the duel between monster and rescuer is a villain, then the winner of their duel must face the maiden in a final duel, unless she relents. If she wins, she rules the Court for the following year. If she loses, she becomes The Captive Maiden and must serve the winners ruling Court for a year, and the Daughters find themselves politically disadvantaged for a year.

                    If the maiden wins the contest in any way, the Court of any "heroes" is honored and shown favor by The Court of Daughters while they rule.

                    If a "hero" wins the contest, the "villain" is barred from the freehold for a year and a day, and their court is unlikely to be treated favorably. While the maiden becomes The Beloved, a valuable advisor to the Freehold monarch with special privileges, and the Daughters are generally treated favorably throughout the year.

                    If a "villain" wins the contest, the defeated monster or rescuer is barred from the freehold for a year. (The maiden becomes The Captive Maiden as above.) Traditionally, a "villain" will rule with an iron fist and rarely grant favors to other Courts, furthering his or her own Court's goals as much as possible during the year.

                    This is a little complex and could probably use a flow chart. I like it because it provides a dilemma. If you choose hero, you are most likely to benefit politically, but you run the risk of just handing the crown to the maiden and you could still get royally screwed if the other side has played a villain and won. If you play a villain, you don't run the risk of the maiden getting to rule without a fight, and you're beholden to noone when you rule, but you risk making political enemies.

                                            First of all, I just want to say: Damn, I like your take Schulz!

                                            Second, that said, I wanted to avoid a deliberate one-to-one on the roles and the courts. I wanted it so that the Maiden, Wild One, and Woodsman could come from any court rather than having each one be a churning out of sanctified actors for each. I also wanted to make it more about the Courts to be commentaries on and interpretations of the story, creating conflict when those ideas of what a Maiden or a Woodsman is supposed to be while at the same time using them to sharpen one another, and finally have each one be a declaration of what each Lost feels in a focus in their life to keep from returning to Arcadia(The Rose Gate/The Treasure Hold of Diana/Grandmother's House[well, none of them exactly]). Stuff like that.

                                                    The Freehold of the Free and Brave

                                                    1 - What is the Tale?

                                                    "We stand for Uncle Sam, my friend - our story is the story of the Constitution, of the land of the free and the home of the brave. Not the way it is, a big mass of hypocrisy; no, we're the story good citizens feed to their kids - and it's a powerful one. If you work hard, and stay honest, you can be someone. The world doesn't care where you come from, only where you're going. It's the story that drew millions to our shores, and it's the story we echo in our movies, TV, and history books.

                                                    We've got the American Dream at our back - let the Gentry come."

                                                    2 - What's the Bulwark?

                                                    The Freehold has wrapped itself in the cycle of Election; every two years, the Congress is re-elected, and every four years, they elect a new President. As long as this cycle is unbroken, the Bulwark will hold. However, there is a quieter, more subtle side to the Bulwark - the Freehold must believe that they are working towards "a more perfect Union". If there are problems, they have to work to solve them - they can't give up, and any betrayal by those in power shows how weak the Bulwark really is...

                                                    3 - And the Courts?

                                                    The Court of Wheat
                                                    Oh beautiful, for spacious skies, for amber waves of grain, for purple mountain majesties above the fruited plains!
                                                    Emotion: Generosity
