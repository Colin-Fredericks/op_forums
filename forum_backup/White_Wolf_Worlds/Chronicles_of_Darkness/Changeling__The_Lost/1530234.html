    Book of Seemings preview/Q&A (coming next week!)

    02-07-2024, 09:33 PM

    Hello, folks. Time to return to Arcadia.

    Some of you who follow the doings of None More Dark Publishing might know that we have a book for Changeling: The Lost Second Edition in the works: the Book of Seemings. Well, I’m here to tell you it’s coming next week as a little Valentine’s Day present to all you CofD fans (i.e., Wednesday, February 14th). At 12 PST, that’ll be dropping on DTRPG and the Storytellers Vault.

    If you don’t know who/what NMD is, we’re a collective of RPG authors and artists who’ve spent the last two years creating supplements for Vampire: The Requiem Second Edition. Because the current IP owner has stopped approving new official releases for the Chronicles of Darkness (to the best of my knowledge; I have no other information in that regard beyond what everyone else has seen), we’ve been trying to carry the torch, at least with Vampire, and now Changeling (though I believe there’s one more official Lost book in the works regarding the Hedge?)

    So, what’s in this book? Let me quote the back cover. Book of Seemings includes:
    • A complete player and Storyteller’s guide to the six seemings.
    • Twelve kiths and twelve entitlements embodying each seeming’s beautiful madness.
    • Six seeming-specific systems to add to your chronicles, including sumptuous Feasts, vast Liebraries, and deadly Wild Runs.
    • The Forgotten, almost-changelings who lost their souls to the Thorns and fill the void with living flesh.
    • New Archetypes, frailties, and Merits, as well as 240 seeming benefits not included in the Changeling rulebook.
    I’ll get into the specifics more in the coming week, but for now, I’ll leave you with a short preview of two of the kiths we’re including.

    ALSO, NMD is going to be setting up a Patreon to go along with this release (assuming we get our asses in gear in time). So, if you'd like to support us in other ways than just buying our books, we'll have more about that soon.

    New Kiths on the Block

    Most of the following kiths are drawn from Changeling: The Lost First Edition, converted here to fit with the current system/setting. As such, they’ve retained their associated seemings from that version of the game: While the Gentry can shape a Beast into a Drudge, making her a farm animal continually sold for magic beans, the majority of Drudges are Wizened. Players and Storytellers shouldn’t feel limited to always using these kiths with the seemings listed here, but consider what factors might lead to any outliers.

    Dancer (Fairest)

    Only through movement are we truly alive — truly in control.

    The magnetism of Dancers is due not to how they look, but how they move. The Gentry steal away the lithest, the quickest, or the most flexible, or just those with the best rhythm. Then, the Dancers’ bodies are stretched, carved, or perfected with clockwork timing — whatever’s necessary to push their coordination beyond the scope of human limitations. Once their precision and swiftness are unparalleled, they’re set to their singular purpose. They make it look effortless — regardless of the agonies of fulfilling their task.

    Some seduce as lithesome assassins, drawing their prey in with hypnotic movements like cobras preparing to strike. Others dance mad jigs for weeks without rest until the Gentry finally let the music box wind down to silence. A few leap over the very moon and pirouette among the firmament so they can gather up the stardust for their masters to lay into the eyes of sleeping babes. Their physicality is spellbinding — literally.

    The trouble with perfection, though, is there’s no further pinnacle to reach. Dancers become so specialized they can’t bend to ever-changing Faerie whims. Once they realize their full potential, they rebel. They use their Fae-given grace to break free. Then, not even the wind can catch them.

    Out of the Hedge, many Dancers are faced with an equally fascinated audience among the Lost. Most fall back on their grace because it’s become all they can do, but now they get to choose for whom they dance and when they stop. That sort of bodily autonomy is the most freeing of all.

    Kith Blessing

    When a Dancer uses Expression to physically perform for an audience (through dance, martial arts, mime, gymnastics, etc.), achieving three successes counts as an exceptional success.

    Drudge (Wizened)

    Always the quiet ones you should watch out for…

    Humans often say the best servant is the one you don’t even know is there. The Gentry agree, and many of them train their changeling slaves to stay out of sight and mind. Drudges are discreet, keep their heads down, and avoid attracting attention as they flit from room to room, keeping everything in a state of perfection.

    Drudges escape Arcadia by falling beneath their Keeper’s notice and slipping out the backdoor so quietly no one realizes they’re gone until weeks — or even years — later. Some rig elaborate contraptions to fake their household chores and buy themselves just enough time to escape, while others pawn their duties off onto goblins without telling anyone, so all their overseer sees is that the job gets done as usual.

    The Winter Court makes a concerted effort to recruit Drudges; their naturally unassuming demeanor makes them excellent spies for the Silent Arrows. In freeholds where this is common, the kith may be treated with suspicion by other courtiers, but Drudges shrug it off and get back to work. Every court needs someone who knows how to run a home, and many relish the opportunity to maintain a base of operations or work with newly escaped Lost settling back into the mortal world.

    Kith Blessing

    When a Drudge uses Stealth in a domestic setting, achieving three successes counts as an exceptional success.

          So, I promised the Scarecrow Minister, one of two Darkling entitlements, but first, a bit of context.

          Entitlements aren't normally tied to seemings. This isn't true of First or Second Edition. However, we wanted to provide character options tuned to each seeming. So, we suggest that these entitlements are mostly borne by members of particular seemings (boy I wish there were an alternate word for seeming, because seeming has lost all meaning in the last 6 months). We don't lock other...fae legacies?...out of these entitlements, but we suggest that such a case would be the exception to the rule. This is explained more in the introduction of the entitlement chapter.

          As for the Minister, this is probably the entitlement you think of when you think of changeling entitlements. Like, that's the one we all know and love. The Minister is certainly my problematic fave.

          The Scarecrow Ministry here isn't too different from the original version, albeit he's just one Darkling now. One thing I loved about the way Oak, Ash, and Thorn handled entitlements was making each one a unique title, and that fit the Minister really well (who actually gets a few shout outs in OAT).

          I don't have much to add here in terms of development, because the developer wrote this one, and I didn't really change anything! So, without further ado, here's the Scarecrow Minister. Tomorrow, let's check out another new system: Ogre Feasts.


          The Scarecrow Minister

          It’s okay: Go ahead and scream.

          Bloody Mary in the mirror. The hook-handed man outside your car. The slender figure who flickers across the screen when you’re not quite looking. Urban legends terrify and fascinate in equal measure, passing through whispers and social media posts like a virus of fear with no known point of origin.

          The Scarecrow Minister knows them all by heart; he’s even started a few — the ones that make you shiver even as you laugh them off. The Minister wants you nice and scared, you see, because there’s safety in fear. Things far more dangerous than bloody ghosts and chilling serial killers lurk in the world’s dark corners, behind mirrors, and just off the path: The Others love to snatch away the curious and unwary. To thwart them, the Minister spreads tales of imaginary terrors so people don’t stumble into the clutches of the real thing.

          Privileges and Duties

          The Scarecrow Minister is unsurpassed in spreading fear, able to invoke horror in anyone they meet and ensure any disquieting rumors they spread find fertile minds to worm their way into. But the Minister doesn’t sow fear for fear’s sake; their acts of terror must serve a purpose, like keeping humans from following their curiosity into the awful places where hungry things lurk. To what extremes the Scarecrow Minister will go in the name of their mission varies. Some are content to use psychological warfare and rumors to scare people away from danger. Others take a bloodier approach, reasoning that a well-publicized and grisly murder is the best way to keep foolish humans away from dangerous places, even if the Minister has to wield the knife themself.

          This title is largely a secret one, not only because of the nature of the Scarecrow’s work but also to protect the titleholder. The Gentry and their agents despise the Minister, and plenty of other supernatural predators don’t appreciate potential victims being scared away. Thus, with the notable exception of the Autumn Court, the Scarecrow Minister takes pains to conceal themself from Lost society. With the Leaden Mirror, however, the title brings near-celebrity status, and devotees within the court follow rumors of the Minister’s activities with great interest.

          However, there’s another, darker, reason for this scrutiny. This title isn’t kind to its holder’s Clarity, and sometimes a Minister goes off the rails, losing themself in their role and turning into the kind of horror they’re supposed to be protecting people from. When this happens, it’s the Ashen Court that tracks down the rogue Minister and ensures the title goes to someone more stable.

          Mask and Mien

          Ministers who aren’t actively acting like an urban legend dress plainly to avoid notice. The terror-filled tales they spread are never about them, and they often need to disappear quickly after making a scene. However, no matter how inconspicuous they try to make themselves, thick, crude stitching appears on their clothing, and bits of straw poke out of their collar and cuffs.

          The resemblance to a living scarecrow goes even further beneath the Mask, with the Minister developing flat, button-like eyes and thick skin with the texture of old burlap. A Scarecrow Minister who holds the title long enough may even find their head deforming to resemble a jack-o’-lantern, hearing the distant cawing of crows wherever they go.

          Heraldry Token: The Bugbear Mask (• to •••••)

          The Bugbear’s Mask resembles a rubber monster mask, with a faded label marked with the title’s scarecrow-like sigil. When activated while worn, however, the Mask becomes unnervingly real: Crooked fangs glint in the moonlight, sores weep, and patchy fur ripples in the breeze.

          Effects: Every time it’s activated, the Minister can change the Bugbear Mask into any generic monstrous visage they can think of. Alternatively, they can instead spend a Glamour to have the Mask take on the appearance of a specific person or creature they know an onlooker is afraid of.

          [REDACTED]

          Catch: The wearer has scared someone they consider an ally or friend in the same scene.

          Drawback: The Mask adheres to the user’s skin. For the rest of the day, it can only be removed with a Strength action, dealing three dice of bashing damage as it tears off.​

              I promised Feasts, so let's sit down to dinner.

              One thing we wanted to do with these "other" systems was add a bit of changeling culture outside of the courts. The seemings aren't nearly as cohesive as things like, say, clans for vampires or even auspices for werewolves, but we felt there must be at least a few commonalities between them. Feasts are the Ogres' special system, and it's probably the most straight-up fun of the six. Ogres + food is a recipe for drama. We also liked the idea of bringing a bit of the medieval political banquet/mafia street party vibe to the Gargoyles, who aren't stereotypically as conniving as the other seemings.

              Not much changed in development for this one. The author (a new hire for us) knocked it out of the park from the start. I believe we expanded the systems a bit in second drafts, but this was a really great write-up from the start.

              Tomorrow, I'll be back with another entitlement. Since we're on the subject of Ogres and food, let's check in on what the Knights of the Knowledge of the Tongue are cooking up.

              Feasts: Food and Fightin’

              Feasts, in human culture, transcend mere sustenance. Around tables laden with fragrant dishes, we reinforce our bonds, celebrate triumph in battles, and observe sacred rituals. For Ogres whose appetites are altered in sometimes-disturbing ways, truly indulging with compatriots feeds the soul as much as the stomach.

              Behind the fractious nature of the Gargoyles is a desire for acceptance, and Feasts provide them with permission to let down their walls and enjoy each other’s company — not that there won’t be a little brawling.

              Background

              Some say the first Ogre Feasts came out of the Viking tradition, but this is a matter of debate. Many modern-day Bruisers believe this is just an attempt to associate themselves with an oft-romanticized warrior culture, arguing that assuming everything started with Europe is a misinformed way of looking at the world. They offer tales from the Kingdom of Mali in the 1200s, where Timbuktu held a feast that lasted a day and a night before the Ogres undertook a perilous journey into the Sahara. There were also the teahouses in Tang Dynasty China, where Ogres held civilized yet abundant dinners. Perhaps Feasting is as universal a feature of being an Ogre, just as much as their curse and blessing.

              What’s certain is that in 1600s Romania, Summer Queen Milena organized a week-long festival for Gargoyles across Eastern Europe. This became the blueprint for nearly all writing about Feasts. Supposedly, the destruction was so extensive that some freeholds banned Feasting for nearly a century, though Ogres continued to hold them in secret. Historically, the more repressed the rest of the culture was, the more outrageous the Feasts Ogres would throw, with Bruisers trying to outdo each other in feats of consumption and excess to the point that sickness was inevitable and a death or two not unusual.

              Beginning in the 20th century, Feasts became less violent, and guests from other seemings began to be welcomed, something unheard of before. Not everyone was happy with this change, and some believe this waters down what it means to be an Ogre. They call these gatherings derogatory names like “family-friendly feasts” or “corporate cuisine.” In response, these conservatives began holding counter Feasts — Ogre exclusive, of course, and often gratuitously violent and transgressive.

              Feasting Traditions

              The formality of Feasts ranges from potlucks to elaborate banquets. The location, the invitees, the décor — all of these are important, but they all pale in comparison to the food. The menu and its architects are the key to the success or failure of any Feast, and Ogre chefs are highly sought after. Rumor has it that the celebrated Swiss chef Friedrich the Knife was offered a king’s ransom to leave his freehold for Paris. When he said no, the French sent a team to coerce him by any means necessary. The part about him baking them into a casserole is probably false.

              The food varies depending on the gastronomic needs of the attendees, but it must be made by Ogre hands, either hunted, gathered, or cooked. It’s said that one gentlemanly Gargoyle refused to “lower himself” to menial labor and secretly had his wife cook the roast. As soon as he placed it on the ceremonial table, it began to rot. Within minutes, it was covered in maggots and worms, exposing his lies.

              Meat is obligatory — some cooked, some not. Any Ogre who takes down the game herself is encouraged to proudly display her kill and dramatically retell the story. There’s no such thing as too much bragging at a Feast. But while meat is the centerpiece, it’s far from the only delicacy. Many of the Terrible also appreciate root vegetables, mushrooms, and soft bread to dip in hearty soups. There’s always a table overflowing with desserts. Ogres are encouraged to bring anything unusual they have an appetite for.

              Finding out another Bruiser’s favorite food and bringing it to the Feast is a sign of intense respect, or even courting. One Ogre won the heart of his love by discovering she craved shiny rocks and metals. He presented her with a platter of the finest pyrite and quartz crystals carved to look like fish, with a crust of ground emeralds and a snifter of mercury on the side. The next Feast held in the freehold was in honor of their wedding.

              And what would a Feast be without copious quantities of mead, ale, beer, or wine? Whether it’s a fancy banquet or people eating from troughs, Ogres always bring enough liquor to float an army. More modern Feasts may even have a dedicated bartender, though some older Ogres grumble that this is pretentious; a mug of ale was more than good enough in their day.

              Even the most contentious Ogre puts aside his grudges for a Feast, though that doesn’t mean that they’re pacifistic affairs. On the contrary, brawling is a common enough occurrence that it’s said to be bad luck if a Feast goes by without at least one dust-up. In modern days, these fights never result in more than bruised egos and black eyes, and combatants traditionally have a drink together after they’ve picked themselves up. Ogres often feel rejected for what they are, and being able to appreciate and indulge in their nature is more important to most of them than they’d admit.

              Bruiser Boons

              Each Feast receives one to three boons. One is automatic, and another can be added by accumulating 10 successes in an extended action to prepare the Feast (e.g., Dexterity + Crafts for cooking, Presence + Socialize to get the word out, etc.). A third can be added if the Feast has at least fifty attendees.

              The following benefits are available, but Storytellers should feel free to come up with their own:

              Bread and Salt: Used almost exclusively for peace talks, this benefit forces attendees to swear an Oath not to harm each other, physically or otherwise, for the next 24 hours. Within that period, anyone who attempts to negotiate peacefully enjoys a +2 on all Empathy, Persuasion, and Socialize rolls and +1 on all (non-Perception) Composure-based rolls.

              Camaraderie Cuisine: Attendees gain a free Ally dot to represent a connection they made over the food.

              Chef’s Choice: The ultimate cooking contest. Winners of any “best dish” prizes gain a free dot of Status or Court Goodwill to represent the boost to their reputation.

                      Feasts with Chef’s Choice… the real Cutthroat Kitchen XD
                      Indeed! Let's sample what the Knights of the Knowledge of the Tongue have on offer.

                      The Knights are just an all-around fun concept. They're a much lower-stakes entitlement than the others in the book, which is something we felt like you need in Changeling. We've associated them largely with the Ogres here, and I think that adds an interesting dynamic to their blazon: they're the biggest, meanest, toughest seeming, and they're here to make foie gras (this part gets messy).

                      This is one of three legion entitlements in the book (technically four, but one is...tricky). Making them a legion title was a natural choice, and we thought that made their dynamics a little more interesting, especially given that the Knights are often at odds with each other, at least based on all the shouting coming from their kitchen.

                      Anyway, here are the Knights of Gastronomy (I got tired of typing out the full name). Tomorrow, let's take a day off, but on Monday I'll be back with something else. Do you all want to see another new system, or would you like to see a couple more kiths?

                      Knights of the Knowledge of the Tongue

                      Seared manticore heart? Pedestrian!

                      Some might snicker about chefs calling themselves knights — until they end up on the wrong end of a ladle wielded by an angry Ogre. These changelings, also known as the Knights of Gastronomy, or Epicureans, are more than just foodies: They’re fully committed to finding the most exotic ingredients on both sides of the Hedge and creating taste sensations for the most adventurous eaters in the world. However, unusual for a legion entitlement, the Knights of the Knowledge of the Tongue are almost always at odds with each other. No two Epicureans can ever seem to agree on anything, squabbling over whether the eclairs should feature ectoplasmic or phantasmal cream or if eating centaur meat counts as cannibalism if you only eat the horse half.

                      Some members spent time in Arcadia as chefs and are now determined to only cook exactly what they want. Others have always loved food, and now that they’re Lost, they realize an entirely new world of flavors and ingredients is open to them. And for those Ogres with specific dietary requirements, why trust someone else to do a job they can do themselves? They know they can infuse the briarwolf tongues with just the right amount of cayenne to not overpower the dish.

                      Privileges and Duties

                      The Knights of the Knowledge of the Tongue have a simple duty: feed the Lost. Anyone who needs a meal gets one, no questions asked. Anyone who wants one is a different matter. They’re also tasked with experimentation, searching out recipes for the most spectacular meals possible.

                      For this, they’re rarely disdained or disliked. At worst, they’re thought of as snobby, and perhaps a touch ridiculous. Most changelings like them, as an entitlement that isn’t invested in adhering to bizarre social mores or mystic traditions is a welcome change. Lost with these gastronomes in their freeholds often approach them for recipe ideas and tips, or even offer great sums to have them cook at their next dinner parties.

                      While the Knights are sometimes sought out for Ogre Feasts (p. 115), they often find these gatherings far too focused on quantity over quality. They’d rather nobody taste their creations than for someone to wolf them down without appreciating the flavors. Any Feast that boasts one or more Epicureans despite this is rare indeed, and their presence is widely advertised. Hobs who know of the entitlement bring the Epicureans exotic ingredients, like goblin fruit found only underwater or the whiskers of a bugbear. Of course, they also expect to be able to try the final product.

                      When someone joins this order, they vow to never intentionally serve someone food that will taste bad or make them ill. They do sometimes make creations where they’re unsure of the effects, but these are shared among the Epicureans themselves. They have evenings dedicated to trying out each other’s experimental ingredients and dishes, and on these occasions, they always have “antidotes” on hand (like activated charcoal) and never schedule anything important for the next day. On one occasion, a Summer Epicurean used her Regalia to see what would happen if she cooked with pure sunlight. The kitchen was utterly destroyed, but apparently, it did add a delightful zing to her piranha filet.

                      Mask and Mien

                      Those who join this entitlement look as though they enjoy food — at least a little bit chubby, bright-eyed, and healthy. In their mien, the most obvious change is the tongue. Each Knight’s tongue turns a deep, mystical blue, granting them supernaturally discriminating palates.

                      They also, unsurprisingly, smell of food, even behind their Masks. It’s always something pleasant, but the specifics vary from Epicurean to Epicurean: Baked bread is common, as is the scent of a holiday meal. Perfume and cologne can’t cover it up, and indeed, such chemical smells always seem a tad off if a Knight tries to wear one.

                      Knights of Gastronomy favor aprons or chef’s jackets even when not cooking, as all are proud of who they are.

                      Heraldry Token: The Magical Measuring Spoons (• to •••••)

                      To most, this is a normal set of six measuring spoons one would use for baking, each marked with the Knights’ symbol. Even when activated, they don’t appear all that different — more polished, perhaps, and nestled more together snugly. Each spoon is a different size, and when the chefs work together in a kitchen, they all claim one, though the arguments over who gets which are fierce.

                      Effects: When dipped in spices or other substances, the exact amount needed is always captured. There are no spills with these magical spoons; the cook always dispenses the perfect amount. When wielding a spoon, the user gains the rote quality on all cooking actions.

                      [REDACTED]

                      Catch: The user must eat three bites of a food they hate.

                      Drawback: The user develops hypersensitivity, and her player must make a Resolve + Stamina roll when she’s faced with particularly intense or unpleasant smells, sounds, lights, etc. On a failure, they suffer the Distracted Condition for the scene.

                              Alright, since we didn't get a vote on what to do next, let's look at one more entitlement: The Magistrates of the Wax Mask.

                              This is not one of our legion entitlements. Not to draw out the mystery too much, but these guys are more/less than they seem, and I think their twist is very Changeling in just the right balance of creepy and whimsical. The basic idea of this entitlement isn't too different from its original incarnation, but the original is also pretty dry, and I like the element of danger/intrigue this one provides. The artist who did the illustration also knocked this one out of the park.

                              In development, I believe some minor changes were made to this entitlement's curse (it was originally a bit too much like their Beat condition), but that's about it.

                              Here's the Magistrates. Tomorrow, as a last preview before the release, let's do a couple of kiths.

                              Magistrates of the Wax Mask

                              We'll take care of everything.

                              Wax is malleable. Apply a little heat, and it can be molded into anything, endlessly melted down and reformed. The Magistrates of the Wax Mask strive to be like the substance they’re named for, filling any role and becoming whatever a freehold needs.

                              A rather large entitlement at seven members, the Magistrates would call themselves problem-solvers. Each freehold has a variety of rituals, tasks, and celebrations that need to be completed, often annually, to ensure the safety and comfort of its members, but sometimes it lacks the necessary expertise or hands to make these ceremonies work. For a reasonable fee, the Magistrates can provide manpower and whatever skillset is required, from construction projects, to party-planning, to less savory — but no less necessary — tasks, all carried out in the shadows.

                              Does this dirty work provide this supposedly neutral group of Lost with a great deal of influence over key events in whichever freeholds hire them? Sure, but they’d never dream of taking advantage of the situation. Of course, that’s only because most of the “Magistrates” aren’t capable of dreaming, or thinking at all…

                              Privileges and Duties

                              What the people who hire these seven agents don’t realize is that there’s only really one Magistrate. The other six are puppets, wax masks animating store mannequins, anatomical dummies, corpses, or anything else approximately human-shaped.

                              The real Magistrate gives these homunculi roles when he affixes their masks. Does the Autumn Court want to host a gala but won’t stoop to asking Spring for help? The Magistrate creates decorators, wait staff, and hosts: They’ll have the guestlist set, dinner catered, and decorations up (in fall colors, of course) before the clock strikes midnight. Does a formal gathering require extra security but the Iron Spear refuses to serve since the guest of honor cheated on the Summer King’s brother? Six bodyguards made to order, each with a gleaming weapon held at the ready. So what if these hirelings are on the quiet side and decidedly unsociable? Are those not the hallmarks of well-trained professionals?

                              Magistrates keep the true nature of their coworkers hidden mostly for reasons of avarice. While the title compels service, it also demands compensation, and seven payments going into one set of pockets is too good a deal for most Magistrates to pass up. There’s also the greed of others to consider. To control the Wax Masks is to have half a dozen mutable, unthinkingly loyal servants at one’s beck and call — a far more attractive prospect to most Wizened than being one of seven titleholders bound to serve the interests of others.

                              As long as the Magistrate ensures the jobs he’s hired for are professionally completed, nobody asks questions. And the Wax Masks are nothing if not professional.

                              Mask and Mien

                              The Magistrate’s mien changes in a predictable way: His skin takes on a waxy sheen and becomes slightly malleable, deforming and shifting under pressure or heat. His Mask gets a more subtle treatment, with small flecks and smudges of dried wax manifesting on whatever he wears, no matter how clean he attempts to keep it.

                              Heraldry Token: The Wax Masks (• to •••••)

                              When not activated, these appear to be a set of seven nearly featureless masks made of hard wax, simple ovals with two holes for eyes and the title’s sigil carved into the back. The only difference the Masks’ miens reveal is that each has a Roman numeral stamped where the mouth should be. The ones bearing the numerals I through VI are Servant Masks, whereas VII is the Master Mask. When activated, the Masks become soft and warm, with the occasional rivulet of half-melted wax running down them.

                              Effects: To activate a Servant Mask, it needs to be pressed against the face of a human-shaped effigy while the Magistrate names a one-word profession, like chef, firefighter, or nurse. The token will animate the effigy and grant it an appropriate mask, using the same rules for a changeling strengthening her own Mask. Effigies remain animated for 24 hours and will obey any commands given to them by the wearer of the Master Mask, but only in the context of the profession they’ve been given. Left on their own, Servant Mask effigies will attempt to act out their granted professions as best they can.

                              • Servant Masks: The user must seal the token to the effigy with his blood, dealing 1 point of bashing damage.

                              • Master Mask: The user must pay each effigy he wishes to command an hour’s worth of the local minimum wage, which he cannot regain: It disappears into the wax.

                                                      I don't have much commentary here apart from saying that the chapter this excerpt is from was part of our efforts to tease out the character of each seeming a bit more. We don't usually do wholly narrative chapters in our books, but the seemings don't have quite as much meat on the bone or history as, say, the clans do in Requiem. So, we wanted to provide some guidance on what makes each seeming tick. This is only one part of a longer section.

                                                      Tomorrow, I'll be back with another excerpt after the book drops: Looking Glasses.

                                                      Fairest

                                                      Please stay for another drink! I don’t like what I see when I’m alone.

                                                      Who We Used to Be

                                                      We were singers, trophy wives, heartthrobs, and politicians. We were used to being treated like we were special for things we couldn’t control. We’re not always beautiful, but there’s always something about us. They call us “Muses,” but what we use to inspire could be anything from an enchanting voice to a snappy fashion sense. We had what everyone wanted. Money, beauty, talent, friends. It’s trendy to act like people with privilege have nothing to complain about, to sneer about “poor little rich girls.” Some of us do deserve that. Many Fairest have an arrogant, entitled streak a mile long.

                                                      Then there’s the beautiful ballerina who lost her childhood to endless hours of practice, considered a failure by 22 because she was never quite good enough to be the lead. There’s the rich boy who charms everyone in his wake, but if his father’s political enemies find out their rival’s son is a queer, he can’t be sure his father won’t choose his career over his heir. Everyone’s got something.

                                                      We were taken for our talents, but it’s our weaknesses the Gentry exploit. Humans are social animals, no matter how much misanthropy might be in vogue, and most Fairest need people. Perhaps it’s more accurate to say we need to be needed. Are we worth anything at all if we don’t sparkle? How would we even know?

                                                      Alistair: Born into poverty in early-20th-century New York, Alistair began rumrunning when he was 15. By the time he was 20, Prohibition was in full swing, and he was working for one of the most dangerous smuggling gangs in the Northeast. He took too many risks but got away with it through his combination of looks, charm, and practiced sincerity. It was impossible not to smile back at him, no matter what ridiculous plan he might be spinning.

                                                      Esperanza: She didn’t mean to be snobby. It wasn’t her fault if the people around her were terribly irritating. She’d sneer at anyone she considered not worth her time, and with just a look, she could wither every ounce of your self-esteem. Sometimes she used this to get creeps to leave her friends alone, but her judgmental eyes would too often fall upon an overworked barista or tired mother whose children weren’t being quiet enough.

                                                      Ian: He wanted to be a world-renowned violinist, and as a child prodigy, it seemed like that goal was well within reach. But the older he got, the less his talent was special. Oh, he was certainly good, but an adult playing the violin doesn’t get nearly as much praise as a child. And there just wasn’t that much money in it. In truth, he wanted to quit — but he was afraid that it was the only thing he’d ever be any good at.

                                                      The Beautiful Nightmare

                                                      For us, one of the worst things about Arcadia was the moments of wonder and pleasure. Many of us don’t wish to speak of our experiences with the Wizened who was forced to sew until her fingers bled, or the Beast who had his legs broken and reformed when he couldn’t run fast enough. We’re often met with scorn, with others saying we’re whining when our torments consist of performing at soirées.

                                                      Our horrors were almost always subtle. We were treated as a favored friend, pet, or lover — until the Gentry got bored of us and tossed us away, or just forgot about us. This often cycled, and we’d crave our Keeper’s attention as much as we feared it. We remember, to our shame, that we weren’t always chained up or kept in a cage. Instead, we were plied with fine foods and given infusions of ecstasy that, even half-remembered, have us waking in the night fevered and guilty. Fairest are most likely to have had a sexual aspect to our durances. Sometimes our Keepers took us as lovers, or even spouses; stereotypical human roles amuse them. One plays the role of “father” in a twisted Leave It to Beaver spectacle. Others have much darker tales.

                                                      Our bodies weren’t twisted; instead, we were perfected. Flaws removed — features exaggerated or minimized based on our Keeper’s desires. Some of us now lack the ability to make an unpleasant expression or even raise our voices. Some Gentry sculpt us to be mirrors of themselves. Perhaps for a performance, or so the Fae can use one of us as a spare face to try on clothes or new hairstyles. It may not compare to working in the mines or bleeding on a battlefield, but there’s a special kind of suffering that comes from seeing your tormentor’s face every time you look in the mirror.

                                                      Alistair: He was taken to the Palace of Unearthly Delights by the Princess of Flames and Sighs to entertain her guests. The more he pleased them, the more rewards he received from his Keeper. It became as easy as breathing for him to see who wanted only a light flirtation with her wine and who wanted him as a bedwarmer for the night. When the Princess went into one of her dark moods, there were no more parties, of course, and she would often forget to feed her vassals. But when she smiled, the world transformed into dances and light.

                                                      Esperanza: The Lord of the Killing Frost was handsome, wealthy, and had perfect manners, and he took Esperanza to his Citadel of Crystal Winter to be the perfect consort. She was treated like glass, but every day he allowed a little less freedom. When she resisted even a little or showed any sign of unhappiness, it was her servants who were punished instead of her.

                                                      Ian: The Court of Disgrace and Vilification was an awful parody of a chivalric society. The Last Knight-Errant snatched Ian after a concert as a new “performer,” and he was made to play the violin while being constantly heckled. All day, courtiers would throw things at the performers. The insults they shouted took physical form and helped mold the captives into more perfect entertainers. Bitterness infused Ian day after day, and he learned to survive by becoming as vicious as the worst of them.

                                                      Treasured (Fairest)

                                                      Didn’t anyone tell you it’s rude to stare?

                                                      Faeries are fascinated by beauty, but what they deem beautiful is anyone’s guess. These changelings were already exceptional in some way: exceedingly tall, strikingly blond, symmetrically built, or whatever else a specific Other favored. The Treasured might be lured with flattery and adoration, or maybe other humans never appreciated their uniqueness.

                                                      Once captive, the True Fae exaggerates this quality further, sometimes to grotesque (by human standards) degrees. When satisfied with the results, this living objet d’art is placed on display for fellow Gentry to admire. Gentry stroll through galleries of Treasured captives, enjoying them as humans would paintings or sculptures. Others probe or examine specimens under glass or behind zoo bars. The Treasured’s sole purpose is to be observed.

                                                      Fashions change, though. The Keeper might choose a new theme for the gallery, or keeping alabaster changelings may become passé. Thus, the Treasured gets packed away in the vault and forgotten, or discarded with all the other broken toys. But once their Keeper’s watchful eyes are turned elsewhere, a Treasured can slink away, finally out of everyone’s sight.

                                                      Unfortunately, many Treasured still get stares after they return. Humans glimpse something exceptional about them, even if they can’t see through their Masks, and other changelings can’t help but be riveted. They see the true mien, and that kind of “perfection” is hard to ignore. It leaves Treasured second-guessing the sincerity of others’ interest in them. But don’t let looks fool you — they’re keen to prove they’re much more than pretty faces.
