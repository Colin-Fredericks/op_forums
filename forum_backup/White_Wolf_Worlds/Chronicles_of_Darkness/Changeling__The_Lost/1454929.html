    Questions arising from my first ST experience

    07-14-2021, 10:38 PM
    Hello everyone

    I have just finished my first CTL game using the Hearts on Trial adventure from the Jumpstart book.
    My players and I are all new to WOD games, so we had to stop during the game from time to time so as to look up relevant rules.
    Fortunenatelly, it still turned out to be an inspiring game.

    So here are some doubts that came up after my first run:

    1. How can I add flavour to the combat scenes and make them more interesting?
    In my game, both parties were making their normal attack in turns. My players could hardly pierce through the hunter's high defense and 3 points of armor to hurt him, and it could take the hunter 10 combat rounds to take the player characters down.
    We tried from both ends using Contracts and Dread powers, but still the combat was long and boring. I literally had to stop the fight halfway and start a hedge chase back to the freehold.

    2. There is too much to come up with on the fly
    Like what I said at the start, I'm a new storyteller and my preparation certainly couldn't cover all the spot rules of the game. And when the story heads toward a path, interesting but never thought about before, I feel it hard to just pick up relevant rules and use them.
    And more story twists prolong the game. I don't know how to control game time because there are so many interesting things players can do in the game.

    3. The chase
    Does each player character roll for the chase separately? The success needed is counted on a party basis, so I assume the whole party should have a representative to roll for the chase?
    If not, then the party with the most people will have an advantage over others, which doesn't make sense to me.

    4. Monster frialties
    I noticed that monsters have their frailties, but how could the player characters find these out? Is it a dice pool test or some contract power?
    Are player characters supposed to know possible frialties of trolls and other hedgegoblins?

    Thanks in advance for your comments and suggestions!

      1. Did you remember to lower the Huntsman defense for each attack against him in the same turn? Normally, on CofD, a group has a huge advantage against 1 oponent, but with a high armor on top of a high defense I can see the issues starting to pile up. Normally, with combat manuevers(like grapple and localized attacks for Tilts) the oponents tend to end up in a pretty bad place. Sacrificing your defenses in an all-out-attack and spending willpower to get +5 die on the attack can help as well. About the boring part... well, my table usually have fun in the combat describing the crazy shenanigans they came up with, like mounting on the enemie back and them punching him in the head and stuff like that (this scene is a simple grapple and damage manuever).

      3. I didn't read this jumpstart, but chase rules on CofD core book don't specify rules for groups very well. It says that each party defines a lead, this probably means that only one player will lead the party during the chase. If you want to allow them to use Teamwork on this, they'll probably escape very fast and I don't know if it works that way.

      4. Normally, this requires an Investigation. There are rules for that sort of thing in the CofD core rulebook. You could also allow them to roll occult to know or a perception roll to notice certain things that the creature can or cannot do, like enter in a house warded with milk in the door and stuff like that.

          Originally posted by Flat Cat View Post
          Hello everyone

          I have just finished my first CTL game using the Hearts on Trial adventure from the Jumpstart book.
          My players and I are all new to WOD games, so we had to stop during the game from time to time so as to look up relevant rules.
          Fortunenatelly, it still turned out to be an inspiring game.

          So here are some doubts that came up after my first run:

          1. How can I add flavour to the combat scenes and make them more interesting?
          In my game, both parties were making their normal attack in turns. My players could hardly pierce through the hunter's high defense and 3 points of armor to hurt him, and it could take the hunter 10 combat rounds to take the player characters down.
          We tried from both ends using Contracts and Dread powers, but still the combat was long and boring. I literally had to stop the fight halfway and start a hedge chase back to the freehold.
          It can get that way if sides are evenly matched or defensive pools are too high. A common complaint of new STs/players which often gets handwaved with "use all-out-attack for +2 dice, and spend willpower for +3 dice on every attack", but those aren't always enough.

          That said, if your players aren't using willpower on attacks, remind them that they can. It does help a bit. All-out-attacks for a +2 can help, but then the Huntsman is likely to one-shot whoever used the all-out-attack on their next turn, so it's risky.
          Like Horodrigo said, do indeed remember that defence drops by 1 for every attack in the round.

          Also, using checks other than combat checks is perfectly viable. When fighting in a warehouse, a character could climb up to an overhanging level above and then have another player goad the Huntsman and guide them to a spot where a heavy crate can be dropped on them, for instance. Or maybe a character can kick the valve off of a steam pipe to have hot air blow into the huntsman's face and temporarily blind him.

          There's also called shots, but I personally don't rate them. The extra penalty they take + still needing to do X damage to even make it do anything often makes them statistically and mathematically non-viable.

          Also, a question: did you remember to add weapon modifiers to hits? It seems unlikely it would take 10 turns to down someone assuming you can hit them.
          And one more: even if armour reduces damage, it can never be reduced below 1B. So after 7-8 or so hits, most opponents will have wound penalties and start to roll for falling unconscious, armour or not.
          Granted, 7-8 hits can make many more rolls, which is imo a slog.

          Finally, as a sort of last resort, you could look into my WIP alternative combat ruleset. It looks to bring combat rolls more in-line with other rolls in the system by taking away defence as a negative dice pool modifier (since it is the main cause of attack pool modifiers often starting off larger than modifiers for any other rule can even possibly ever get) and instead treats it as ablative armour that is worn down by successive attacks as characters lose their footing, get worn out, and so on. This also makes stacking defence and armour on top of one another less likely to turn into a slog, as defence and armour apply at different times in the fight.
          I don't recommend it, since you should first get a feel for the normal rules before you consider if you want to tweak them, and because this system will likely need another 2-3 revision passes and playtesting before it gets where I want it to be, but it's here if nothing else seems to work for you and you want to give it a try: https://docs.google.com/document/d/1...mvWcY6q0/edit#

          2. There is too much to come up with on the fly
          Like what I said at the start, I'm a new storyteller and my preparation certainly couldn't cover all the spot rules of the game. And when the story heads toward a path, interesting but never thought about before, I feel it hard to just pick up relevant rules and use them.
          And more story twists prolong the game. I don't know how to control game time because there are so many interesting things players can do in the game.
          Honestly, I think this only gets better with experience.

          At the timestamp in this video, the people of World Anvil talk about how to prepare some encounters ahead of time to drop into your campaign as needed to "buy time": https://youtu.be/ZKzub4qxOxY?t=2440
          Essentially, you keep some story threads in your backpocket that you can unleash on your players when they do unexpected things to stall them and keep them busy for the rest of the session. Then you can end the session already knowing what they'll do next, since their characters are heading in that direction. Then you can prepare it for next session, and to them it will look like you were ready for them to veer to this totally random spot all along and that you're super-duper-prepared.
          I encourage you to watch the entire video, it's really good. The preceding one is also great and might be the best place to start if you want to watch it all. (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EJwM0nMugwc)
          Some of it is caught up in their world-building software, but most of it is tips that can be applied to any and all game. Just that you might use OneNote or google drive while they use World Anvil. ^^

          3. The chase
          Does each player character roll for the chase separately? The success needed is counted on a party basis, so I assume the whole party should have a representative to roll for the chase?
          If not, then the party with the most people will have an advantage over others, which doesn't make sense to me.
          Everyone rolls on their own. And individual characters might need a different number of successes to win than other characters, especially in the Hedge.
          I honestly don't like the Chase rules and they easily turn into a bomb of rolls, but it is what it is.

          If I made a hack I'd likely make it use a long / medium / short range band and have an "escape" meter that starts out partially filled based on the circumstances that start the chase. Then I'd make it so that all "runners" and all "chasers" within Short range declare an action, and both sides roll one teamwork test, with successes either filling or emptying the escape meter based on who wins.
          Chasers at long/medium range can't contribute to the catching roll and have to try to catch up, with dice pool and difficulty depending on environment and how they try to make up the difference. Meaning that if no chasers are within "Short" range, the runners roll unopposed and will escape faster.

          Characters who fail their roll completely might have to roll on a chase event table or something, could be fun, and might take damage or be knocked back 1 range band or whatever. Or you can just come up with a fun circumstance without rolling on a table, which is probably what I would do.

          It's just an idea off the cuff, the numbers would need to be balanced, but it sounds more fun to me than the current Chase rules, even in an incomplete state. ^^

          4. Monster frialties
          I noticed that monsters have their frailties, but how could the player characters find these out? Is it a dice pool test or some contract power?
          Are player characters supposed to know possible frialties of trolls and other hedgegoblins?

          Thanks in advance for your comments and suggestions!
          Occult rolls, or buying the information from hobgoblins, or perhaps there's an ancient riddle that states their weakness that you have to decipher... Think fairy tale stuff. ^^
