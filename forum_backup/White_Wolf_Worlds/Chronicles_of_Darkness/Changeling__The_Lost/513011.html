          So congratulations. I should be doing homework of a time-sensitive nature, and beyond that I should be doing homework of a time-sensitive nature, and another one of not-as-sensitive-but-still nature, and beyond that I should be continuing my verbal ping-pong match of order of awesomeness with David on Gundam or contacting people for a game idea I'm trying to build, but NOPE. You've won the precedence by touching on a subject I'm been working over in my head, so I'm gonna respond to you in spite of that being a bad idea right now. This won't be a full essay-I'm saving that for when I finally decide to just brainbarf on the Hedge to get my thoughts straight-but you get a hefty bonus.

          *digs around for music to start on, settle for Feels Like Falling by Digital Daggers*

          Arcadia is the thing that tempts and repulses, that pulls and pushes on changelings, a core feature of the central struggles of Lost.

          Wait, wait, wait. No, I can't start there. I need to back up a second.

          Satchel once described Changeling the Lost, very aptly,as "a game whose protagonists didn't want to completely abandon the routine and mundane." It's absolutely true-Arcadia is the heights and depths of experience within the mortal coil(and maybe even beyond in some ways). Changelings don't just run from trauma when they escape Arcadia, they escape endless wonder the likes of which they'll never really have, because they have chosen to prioritize the simpler, the quieter, the more mundane. Now, a large part of that comes from how the Lost tie the two together, and a big part of the recovery process when they get back as well as one of the big deciders of how a changeling decides to live their life comes from decoupling the trauma from the fae and allowing themselves to enjoy the goblin markets and the moonlight dances without slipping into the trained traits they picked up in surviving their time in Arcadia. There's an allure as well as a fear, and both can be as dangerous as they can be healthy(if there is such a thing as a healthy state of being for a changeling).

          I know, I know, Can't you substitute that with the Hedge? Can't you do that by having Changelings be haunted by their own dreams?And the answer is!

          No. Not really. And a large part of that has to do with what the Hedge stands in for and what Arcadia stands in for.

          Let me start off with a sort of visual contrast. The Hedge can be a pretty trippy place. It can be a thorned collage of office spaces(and Office Space), the 1893 Chicago World Fair, an underground kingdom, and Tony Stark's Happy Fun Fun Toyland of Technology. It can be these things because it represents the world slightly beyond the edge of what we see. When you got into the woods, you're leaving the security of what you know and into the wider world....but there's a buffer where the limits of what you know exceed the infinite variability of the strange and exotic. When you enter into the woods, you don't just drop off into the desert or into the Snow Queen's hive-castle or the labyrinths of either witch or goblin. This transition, this orphic journey into the subconscious, has challenges that make it distinct that must be passed before being passed, but I'll come back to this later. THe key point is that as strange as the Hedge can be, there's a reason it's limited to a certain expression of 'exotic worldliness', at least as of First Edition. And as such, there are things it can't be.

          See, the Hedge can't be cubist, or post-impressionist, or straight up surrealism. Arcadia, on the other hand, can. Arcadia is the end of the road and the extremes of human expression. You might have a maze in the Hedge, but in Arcadia you have Escher drawing the landscape and rules. Arcadia is everything great and terrible about human imagination turned up to eleven and caught in acts of intimacy that blend the two sides together like sex partners transcending themselves.It is terrifying and traumatizing, it is sensual and boundless, and each is appealing and rejecting in it's own right. It's almost nonsensical. It is the Gauntlet, the Tower of Revolution, the dark wellspring beneath the human family we enter to self-actualize and maybe even self-transcend in. The Hedge(by itself, not with outside help like the Strangers), prepares people to enter into this conflation of the widest world and most inner-self, to either fully accept it or reject it by challenging everything, taking every preconceived notion and asking "Oh Really?". A person, be they human or changeling, if they follow the roads the right way and play the story out the right way, will arrive at Arcadia ready to become, whatever it is they will change into. All that's left is the last door to open, the last threshold to walk across.

          Stop, I hear you. I'm coming around to your question.

          So, first off, what is Arcadia was a dream, one you could never return to?

          The first problem with that thought is that it presupposes something contrary to Lost's themes, ideas, and struggles. The game begins with that pre-supposition that characters value mortality more so than the Arcadian wastes, that they take the muted over the vibrant. The conflict from the game stems from the then-growing balance of traumatic response and nostalgic recollection and pushes the tension/drama button of having your choice taken away from you-that you'll be forced into the wonderful and the traumatic without your choice, your control. Having Arcadia be a real place that you stand a real chance of returning to is bad enough for this sort of game, that potential subtle slide to glorious want and indulgence, and it becomes a twistier, scarier, and more interesting affair when Arcadia has the power to come after you. The conflation of these issues are what drive the game, create the story, set the tension.

          You could do that with the Hedge.....but the only way you could do it fully is by making the Hedge more like Arcadia.

          THe descent, the journey, the progress-it all lends to a natural segregation, and if you fought against that, it would lead to a dilution of the ideas. You ran from something terrible, and because it was no more yielding than a dream, when you struggle with what is worthwhile and what is now, all that's left is to wander the Hedge meaninglessly for the rest of your life. There's just nothing to twist the issue,the knife, nothing to make the character writhe with, nor is there any conclusion to that story. In the end, the Character must accept mortality, because there's nothing for them back in the world of their nostalgia. That can be an awesome story, but it becomes so much more awesome when it becomes a choice among a sea of viable outcomes rather than an inevitabilty. And the instant you start creating the choice, you by necessity create Arcadian Realms, because it is the Crucible where these choices are made manifest and whole. It becomes more than a dream, it becomes laws.

          Changeling the Dreaming is the game about dealing with an eternal loss of the great and wonderous that may also be a collection of torments, and it does it extremely well, mechanically(Well, okay, a lot of the machiancis need work, but there's some really good ones in there none the less) and idealistically set to explore the death of nostalgia and the birth of realization, and all things beautiful and terrible about the experience. Dreaming is the game about the careful balance of moving forward while hanging onto the past. But Lost isn't set to play that game. It explores the other side of the game, and the game would be crippled if you tried to balance many of the features and ideas inherent to the game while trying to take out Arcadia, because Arcadia is the central and final manifestation of the thousand complex progressions at work in Changeling the Lost.

          All paths lead to Arcadia, else they lead nowhere at all.

          I'm going to come back to this later and ask myself what I smoked from Dodgson's stores, so ask questions-you just might expose me as being full of shite.

              First, I think that our dear ArcaneArts started AND ended perfectly well:

              Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
              Arcadia is the thing that tempts and repulses, that pulls and pushes on changelings, a core feature of the central struggles of Lost.
              ...All paths lead to Arcadia, else they lead nowhere at all.
              I'm of an opinion that no changeling really leaves Faerie/Arcadia/Hell/Heaven—whatever one chooses to call it—completely by choice. That is, every one of the Lost stumbles out of the Hedge with a piece of Arcadia stuck inside her forever. That splinter gives her the Changeling Template (in 'mechanics' speak), but it also acts as a temptation, one that she dare not voice to any other of the Lost. Thing is, each and every one of them feel the same way.

              Without this fundamental notion of Arcadia, the Hedge is still scary. Without using Arcadia, some version of the Others—maybe hobs, other Lost, or whoever else populates the Hedge—may still act as terrible threats. Without Arcadia, the game can still be played, but it's not the same game without Arcadia. The above treatise (NOT as essay, as Arcane Arts says!) wonderfully explains the need. I want to build on one piece of it, though, concerning theme:

              Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
              The conflict from the game stems from the then-growing balance of traumatic response and nostalgic recollection and pushes the tension/drama button of having your choice taken away from you-that you'll be forced into the wonderful and the traumatic without your choice, your control.
              This thematic piece of Changeling: the Lost resonates more that any other with my troupe. The Lost don't ever truly share any experiences in Arcadia, and even those who did serve Durances under the same Keeper will return with different scars, different nightmares, different memories. What they all DO share is that experience of having all control taken away, of having all choice robbed in a place that doesn't exist on any map, taken by a being that has no body to touch. In Arcadia, in their homes, the True Fae are as gods, at least when compared to a mortal's conception of such things. Within those realms, anything can, does, and did happen, but the game doesn't take place there. Why?

              This takes a little unpacking, but I'm gonna answer it.

              The theme of Changeling: the Lost that most appeals to me—and is listed first in the book—involves the notion of running towards something that might be more than running away from anything known. No matter how happy Lochen of Redleaf was in her Keeper's realm, no matter how powerful and hardy she felt, no matter the adoration heaped upon her, she had no power to choose her destiny. Life out here in the 'real' world requires a job, rent payments, stinky commute on the tube and the rain every bloody day... But it is HER life. That monster in his palace of glass and pure joy has no say over what Lochen—now 'Lauren' on the cheques she writes—does with her own time or energy. Sure, the pampering and the perfect weather was nice, but that wasn't her life.

              Our changeling characters maybe DO run from the horrors of the Hedge and Arcadia, but the thematic meat of the game is that each and every one of them seeks acceptance, home, a place to be who he wants to be. If Arcadia were closer (i.e. just the Hedge), that struggle would maybe gain something in terms of immediacy or 'lookoutbehindyou' sort of scares, but it would lose the life-shattering, pants-shitting, sanity-breaking POWER of the True Fae in their own realms of Arcadia.

              ...So yeah, I reckon that I partially just regurgitated a bit of what ArcaneArts wanted to 'brainbarf' on us (brilliant term!). That's not the point. My ACTUAL point is that I really don't see the above theme very well supported in what I've seen of Second Edition so far. I mean, the ONLY 'mechanical' hindrance laid on a starting character is her kith, which comes with a decently kick-ass Blessing and yeah, a weird shape that the Mask covers, hence not much of a hindrance. Seeming is chosen after her escape and comes with a 'curse' I don't quite understand yet—still awaiting information on how Clarity will work—and another Blessing. Then she gets to choose a Court, possibly an Entitlement, her Contracts (possibly more 'a-la-carte' than linear?), and that is a LOT of character (NOT player, mind you) agency involved. Throw in the nigh omnipresent threat of these 'Huntsmen' and we wind up with a version of Changeling based a lot more on running away and using powers to keep the 'bad guy' (the True Fae, hey?) from getting you. Between those two, it's a lot of serious change in theme I think.

              Please know that *I* know that I'm way off topic here, but I needed this space to get my notions in order. Sorry all...

              A little more on topic, my better half told me after our last game: 'I think it's the very notion of HOPE that makes this game so scary.' We'd spent hours making characters with some pick-and-choose elements from First Edition and kiths modeled after Second Edition, all jumbled with some 'homebrew' Anchors and a slight change in Clarity that I'd crafted for years. The process of defining character didn't use a LOT of Arcadia stuff (I'm super-willing to let those details come out later in play), but just knowing that a horror beyond mortal understanding and beyond the mortal realms forged these characters into something that won't ever QUITE be human again, knowing that the world in which these characters live must be chosen and built from the ashes of that experience... Oh, that gives these characters something precious that they dare not let slip away. That hope and the certainty that it could all be taken away, THAT is the horror I so adore in Changeling!

              To answer my question from above: It cannot take place in Arcadia, but the experiences in THIS world wouldn't carry the same contrast between hope and dread, freedom and captivity, chaos and order without Arcadia as a feature. Disagree in whole or in part, but that's honestly how I feel about it.

                                                  Originally posted by amechra View Post
                                                  Just an idle thought - how different would Changeling be if Arcadia... wasn't? Oh, the Hedge is there, and the Gentry may or may not be real... but Arcadia is nothing more than a fever dream, one that you can never return to?

                                                  What difference would an absent Arcadia make to a game of Beautiful Madness?
                                                  To me, not much. I personally always ran things as if "Arcadia" was just certain areas in the Hedge shaped by powerful Fae to their whims.

                                                  Arcadia, itself, is not important. The trip away from a powerful Keeper through the Thorns is... and all that can be done with just the Hedge itself, shaped by dreams and a powerful will. Some people call this re-engineering it... I don't think it is. There's already hints of it, from what we know of it, in the Hedge of 1e. Arcadia is just more of the same thing, which can be accounted for by powerful wills imposing over the landscape.

                                                  Changeling starts off in a different manner - you start off as not a victim, but a survivor. You escaped, you beat them once. Then, you come home to rebuild your life. What are the important parts of the story here? Taken away by a powerful Keeper, changed/abused, and then the victorious escape back. Then game starts with learning to cope. The Keeper, the abuse and what they made you do (2e kith), and the escape (2e Seeming) are the important parts here, not the actual location.

                                                  When you get right down to it, you could engineer the game so that there's no Hedge OR Arcadia, but little pockets of hidden reality akin to what the God-Machine does. Getting rid of the Hedge would require a bit more tweaking, however, given how involved it is in Changeling by default, but Arcadia itself? Not important. Remember, most changelings fully remember their experience in the hedge in 2e - its not a fever dream, except for those poor unfortunates that spent their time as unthinking animals, plants or objects (which is because not being humanoid is an issue).
