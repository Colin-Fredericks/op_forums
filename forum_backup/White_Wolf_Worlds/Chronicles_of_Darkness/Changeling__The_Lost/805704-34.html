                            The fact that a Huntsman could come for you at any time doesn't make life any more pointless or hopeless than the fact that there are a thousand things that could kill you on any given day,or the fact that, someday, every one of us will die. Nobody knows how much time they have left on this earth, and the Huntsmen don't really change that very much.

                            Plus, the fear of threats from Arcadia has always been a defining feature of Changeling society in Lost. One doesn't have to look any further than the Seasonal Courts: each court is literally defined by how it reacts to that threat: by preparing to fight (Summer), by turning the tools of the Fae against them (Autumn), by going into hiding (Winter), or by living it up and not worrying about tomorrow (Spring). The whole point of even having a rotating leadership is to try and keep the True Fae off balance, because nobody in a freehold really believes that the Others will simply forget about them or leave them alone.

                            If anything, I see the Huntsmen and the system of Approaches as giving Changelings more hope. The True Fae are alien and powerful beyond mortal comprehension (and have pretty much always been depicted as such), but now we have both a good reason for them not to come after Changeling's in person and a type of Arcadian foe that can realistically defeated, if only for a while. They never really go away, sure, but that doesn't mean the courts cant keep sending them packing back to Arcadia in agony. As scary as the Huntsmen may be, Changelings have the home field advantage, not to mention the advantage of numbers and a whole court full of magical folk with more tricks up their collective sleeves than any one Huntsman could match.

                            Incidentally, I feel like, from a practical standpoint, Loyalists and Privateers are really no less inevitable and relentless than than the Huntsmen. They may not be magical robots with a single all-consuming purpose, but no matter how effective the Changelings in your Freehold are at defeating their schemes, you're never actually going to get rid of them for good. Some Changelings will always go bad, and the Fae will always be able to break humans to do their bidding. Sooner or later, these threats will reappear, and just as with the huntsmen, all a Freehold can do is stick together and take care of the threat as it arises.

                            Honestly, I think it all adds up to a pretty decent metaphor for dealing with trauma. No, it never goes away, and it will always be a part of who you are. There will always be bad days when you feel those old aches and life might not even seem worth living. But you have good days too, and great days, and mediocre days, because you are so much more than the trauma you have suffered. And even when things are looking bleak, you don't have to face your demons alone.

In 1e, the True Fae were used very sparingly as direct antagonists. Their threat was always present, but distant. The way the Huntsmen work needs that they are extremely hard to defeat, inevitable and very competent at what they do, or else there would be no point accepting a Token from them. The narrative value of the Token is directly linked to how effective they are as antagonists.

                                        What are the numbers? How effective are the Huntsmen? If they are highly effective at their job, it's further proof on the "better consider suicide" side. If they're not, what's the value of a Token? Why are the Gentry sending out something that doesn't want to do its job and isn't even that good at it? And if the Huntsmen are only moderately good at their job, how do the numbers change when you add the loyalists and privateers? How many changelings die or are taken back to Arcadia every year?                                                         Okay, so ostensibly, this is a response primarily to Omicron, which is something I never really thought I'd have to do in my life, but here we are. And ostensibly, this is also about the Huntsmen.

                                                        But, really, no, if I'm honest, I think it's time we had a "back to basics" sort of chat, because I've been seeing a lot of funny ideas about Changeling that's been bothering me some-stuff like Changelings being the most moral of the splats and a strange twist of perspective on what, exactly, people think Changeling seems to be doing. It's really, really weird, and I think it's worth tackling the subject. But before all of that, I have an apology to make. It works out, because it let's me set a stage for this discussion.

                                                        My Right for My Crown, My Left for My Dagger-Societal Oaths and Why No One's Going to Ban Tokens of Reprieve.

                                                        Soooo a lot earlier in the thread, it was asked why the rulers of a Freehold don't just demand everyone make a Pledge to not take Tokens of Reprieve and let the Wyrd sort it all out. I countered at the time that doing such would require a lot of individual Oaths to be laid down on the rulers, and that would inevitably break them. And, were I not forgetting some of the other functions of Oaths, I'd have been right on that. But I wasn't. So, let's quickly chat about that.

                                                        As the Demon Storyteller's Guide points out, there are three kinds of Pledges in Second Edition, being Sealings, Oaths, and Bargain. Oaths break down into three other categories-they work more or less the same regardless, but the distinctions are important. My focus when I made my declaration was on Personal Oaths, namely because the majority of mechanical diversity lies in that area, but Personal Oaths aren't what you'd use to swear to the Freehold, the Court, and the rulers. No, that belongs to Societal Oaths, which binds you to the concept and the definitions laid out in the groundwork of these constructs. You can have as many people swear their allegiance to Hold and Court that you want, and so long as the Bulwark stands, you're golden.

                                                        So, yes, actually, you could just include a clause in the Societal Oath that basically says "I will uphold and keep the laws of the Freehold and Courts" and then make sure one of the laws is to not take a Token of Reprieve, and BAM, anyone who takes it is an oathbreaker, and you can expect your happy ass to become grass if you don't make headway out of town. Hooray, problem solved, right?

                                                        Well. No. Pay attention, and feel free to challenge this as you read on, because this tenet is important for what we're going to chat about. It's can be seen as a sort of crux for Changeling.

                                                        Here's a simple fact-Tokens of Reprieve are powerful currency in a game like Changeling the Lost. No one wants to be the ones who earns it, of course, and no one wants to be the one caught with it-but it is something everyone wants to have in their bug-out kit. When you need to bargain for a mighty favor and you think you can do a deal under the table, a Token of Reprieve is how you you pay the champion of the Freehold to break into a Valkyrie base for your mate. When anyone asks where you got it, you of course say you nicked it at a Goblin Market, or picked it up off the corpse of a bloody Loyalist, or you made a deal with someone else for it. Of course you do. It doesn't matter if it's true or not. Changelings are mortified to find out you have one, but if they can get their hands on it, they'll at least be willing to hear the deal. And hey, so long as you don't have to betray anyone, you'll gladly ignore the clause of not mentioning the seller at all. Or you turn the person in, but hey, that Token should go to someone, and you gave up a traitor. You earned it legitimately. Of course, you have to persuade the ruler that it should remain with you than 'get locked up in the treasury'. You know, for the good of the Freehold. The simple fact is is that having Tokens of Reprieve in a community is going to be good for you, whether you be a Sovereign of Paris or a poor exile newly returned home-it's just a matter of getting your hands on it. You can't do that if the freehold keeps them from even being grabbed.

                                                        No one wants to earn it, but everyone wants one. And that's just the way the Lost are, because, deep down, there's a conflict of interests for changelings.

                                                        Torn Apart-The Tug of War Of Changeling Between Hearth and Nightmare

                                                        Originally posted by Omicron
                                                        I have... many issues with Huntsmen. They're game-warping in a way, say, the Strix aren't. A Strix is universally bad news, but it can be bad news in many different ways, and it can be bad news for different people, for your city as a whole, for you in particular, for some third party. You can be peripherally involved, you can mitigate the bad news, the bad news may happen and leave you much worse off but still alive (well, undead). The Huntsmen are bad at this: they come after you, and the only possible endings to your confrontation are 1) game over, 2) you kill it, 3) you betray your fellow Changelings. This is much more narrow and restrictive.
                                                        I'm not going to address this point directly at this point. I probably will later, but at this point, I wanted to sort of frame it and look at it on the wall as a contemplation piece, something I stare at while I mutter to myself, and really to the room. Because, I feel like while I understand and can even appreciate this sales point, it highlights the problem of the image of direction with Changeling.

                                                        From day one, Changeling the Lost has been about The Long Way Home. That's what was sold in the theme section of the introduction. This game is about finding your way back 'home', back into a sense of security and comfort. It's about losing the flinch, the quickness of breath, the dilated pupils, when you see the carousel, the china doll, the scarecrow. It's about knowing everything is in its' place because you put it there, it's about knowing which laws of the Wyrd you obey and why you do them, it's about knowing when the goblins in the shadows are allowed to take a step over the threshold because it's okay for them to do so. It's about becoming safe and becoming comfortable. Lost's grand ol' image for a characters final act is one where they can walk around cool and confident, not worrying, because they know the world and they know themselves and can keep themselves and their own safe without becoming recluse or a siege engine or any number of other extremes. And you can do it!

                                                        ...it's all so....pretty, isn't it? So nice, so clean, so hopeful. It is. It can be done. It's a big reason this game is as popular as it is. You don't have to be scared, of others or yourself. You can feel safe, once upon a time and forever after. So pretty, so precious.

                                                        It's kind of naive. Maybe we lost sight of where Changeling goes because we like that dream too much. Maybe when I say "Bleakest game, my ass", I'm ignoring something.

                                                        Second Edition isn't letting people forget. It's taking a lot of moves to drag our eyes away from the dream and back to reality, looking us square in the eye and saying, clearly, "It's about the long way home, not just the way home." Because, at the end of the day, The Long Way Home is just a way of re-expressing an idea in a high-minded way for people higher up the hierarchy. But down at the base of a changeling's needs, The Long Way Home reads a different way, and it's between those two readings you find Lost as a whole.

                                                        See, strip it of glamour and hope, The Long Way Home reads "Don't Go Back to Arcadia". And really, in the mud and shade of the forest, that's all that matters. Even if a Lost has no house, no friends, no family, nothing but their name and their flight-there, dressed in the blood of maggots with the dreaming toads and the sleeping snakes coiled around the singing mycelium, there, Lost is found in the simple promise that shatters worlds and breaks fates, there in the words "I am never going back."

                                                        Changeling 101, right? But it's strange where people think this takes Lost as a game. See, you can have adventures centered around The Long Way Home. Grand comedies and tragedies full of romance and horror and sorrow and humor and exquisitely strange and beautiful things you could never hope to see in life put next to quiet and simple and serene things that you yourself have in abundance. Lost always has and always will have a place for that.

                                                        But deep down, in the cracks of beauty and hope, down where the seeds can no longer take root and the water freezes and yet the sludge gathers and mingles and stews, there, in the advertisement promising a psychological horror, on the shelf in deepest green and under your pillow in colorless truth, I am never going back sinks its' teeth in and binds the story and the players and gives it the power that makes people not only dream of better, but fight for better. By way of necessity, though, it must also suggest the darker, more terrible answers. It is here where changelings fall, where changelings find themselves in the darkling fright of grim choices. And truth be told, it is always stronger. The Long Way Home is never as strong as I am never going back by itself.

                                                        In a way, Changeling as a game is always going to come back to a singular direction, because of the intrinsic strength of I am never going back. It will always turn it's eyes down Arcadian roads. It has an inexorable gravity to it. The lower need of I will never go back crumbles and devours the higher need of The Long Way Home, and the only way to truly overcome that, to give The Long Way Home it's true due, is to dive through I will never go back and come out the other side.

                                                        So, why is this important? Why do I feel the need to emphasize this weird dichotomy of two phrases that sort of mean the same thing? Well, that's easy.

                                                        Because you aren't the only one dealing with it.

                                                        Images They Sell-Trust and Distrust in Changeling the Lost

                                                        Originally posted by Omicron
                                                        Another way in which it brings Changelings closer to Vampire (which I don't think is a good thing, much as I love VtR to bits) is how it fosters distrust. One of the main antagonists of Changelings were always Loyalists and Privateers: but those were outsiders, even when infilitrated in the Courts. They were the Changelings who had made the wrong choices, the enemy with a friendly face, the ones who escaped and yet are still in chains. The Courts were, fundamentally, about how victims of the Fae draw together, share suffering and find a communality in them. Now, everyone you meet - friendly or not - is a potential traitor, someone who can use what they know about you to hurt you. It used to be that helping a Keeper catch a Changeling was the greatest crime, and those who did it pariah. Now it's something anyone can do.

                                                        This fosters the wrong kind of paranoia: an inward paranoia. Rather than being frightened of the world outside your tight-knit group, rather than having to confront the complex interplay of the safe space of a Lost community and the dangerous opportunity of the 'real' world, you now have incentive to hide from everyone and everything. Courts exist as a way to defend yourself against Huntsmen, but they're also the main reason Huntsmen threaten you: you need the Court, but you also need to hide from the Court. From your friends. No one can know enough to hurt you.
                                                        Okay, minor deviation here-I fucking love Omicron. Easily one of my favorite people here on the forums, has been for a long time, and is a incredibly sharp dude. One of my frequent regrets is that he isn't more active here in the nWoD Threads. ALL THAT SAID, This paragraph here is the reason I'm responding, because I read his stuff, and I was feeling a little crestfallen until I read this, at which point I freely admit I laughed and asked my screen "Dude, have you been playing the same game as me?" None of that negates what I said about him, and it's going to stop being about him explicitly after this-but seriously, wut?

                                                        So, back on track, it's an intrinsic part of the game, this divide between the high-minded Long Way Home and the base need of I am never going back, and it fights on every level of Changeling the Lost. More importantly, it fights in every changeling. No one wants to be that guy, no one wants to be that asshole, but every changeling has in them that I am never going back and the questions of how far that goes. Hell, this is why loyalists are even a thing.

                                                        Actually, let's take a deviation and talk, bluntly, about how loyalists and private-No, let's start at an even more base level. Let's talk fairies.

                                                        Fairies, quite simply, are outsiders. Their ways are not your ways. As expressions of nature, of foreigners, of the mentally and socially disabled, of dreams and of strange inner workings, of all this and more, fairies as a wide, cultural base of icons that live beyond the edge of the village are the 'dwellers of the outside'. Offer them a kindness and they curse you, never thank them and you get blessed. They act in ways that don't make sense, according to their own internal logic that you might be able to discern with empathy and observation and care, but would never be able to make sense off just by way of your own framework. You have to slant your point of view, see under the table and behind the curtain, to really get them. On the whole, fae are liars and deceivers, less by choice and more by fate or chance. You can't always trust how the fae will react, because they are subject to laws and worlds within that aren't your own. The images they sell are illusion and dream, always a measure of dishonesty, and you have to play the game well to avoid becoming addicted to goblin fruit or asleep in a fairy circles.

                                                        In Changeling the Lost, the majority of people you interact with are fairies. Welcome to the norm. Hell, in First Edition, every changeling has the Contracts of Smoke and Mirror as an aptitude-you were expected to never be what you appeared. Many things are up for grab and trade, though the most important resource was always too expensive for you to ever just barter for.

                                                        Put even more simply, at it's core, basest level, there was never a reason to trust anyone around you. This is why the cornerstone of any changeling society was Pledges, which forced people to behave in accordance to laws.

                                                        From this emerges the Loyalist and Privateer narrative, the teeth of I am never going back sinking into the fairy tale schema and emerging on the other side as antagonists. Now, while it can be fun to just have roving bandits and such all around the place, really the first reason why changelings built complex pledge systems for societies stems from the fact that there are Loyalists and Privateers among them. It doesn't stop them, either. Loyalists and Privateers have always been assumed to infiltrate freeholds and find clever ways to work around and subvert the Wyrd frameworks in order to casually make people disappear. See, the Lost are all inside, and the Loyalists and the Privateers have the advantage of being both inside and outside. This was the default from day one.

                                                        As a natural extension, then, the game by and large becomes a big game of Coup or Werewolf(the card game, not Forsaken or Apocalypse)-everyone is keeping track of who is doing what and in accordance to what's been said, and finding incongruities in the executions, trying to find out who's going to stab them in the back before anyone else does. At it's basest level, that's what goes on in a freehold. It's forcing levels of trust and seeing what emerges from the remaining distrust of world and others. It's stabbing people with something immutable and seeing if that person is a traitor or not. This game has always been about paranoia and distrust, and what trust and camaraderie can grow from it.

                                                        It doesn't help that the goal posts have always been shifting, because Pledges don't just save you from the obvious traitors-they keep you safe from everyone else.

                                                        Gnarled Roots-Changelings Are Not Good Guys, Folks

                                                        It hurts to write that. Actually, it hurts to write a lot of this, because it's a lot darker than I usually deal with Changeling at. Hang in there, I don't intend to end on gloom, but I sure need to walk through it.

                                                        See, Changeling, as it works out, continues to be as intrinsically deceitful as its' subject matter because it's so easy to find fifty shades of heroism in the Lost. Sure, the Winter Court are selfish assholes and Summer Court has bullies, and sure, everyone's a little broken, but in the end, in comparison to True Fae, Huntsmen, Loyalists and Privateers, we have us a clear divide of good guy and bad guy to root and hiss for, right?

                                                        Yeah, mark that as a no.

                                                        A point of interest, to spark this section off, is the Morality systems of First Edition. What to glean here? Well, consider Humanity(Both Vampire and Promethean) and Wisdom. Really, these were just the same old morality system as mortals had, even if Wisdom had some additions. And, well, yeah, sure. These are all things that mimic the human worldview and spiritual foundation. Vampires strove not to lose it, Mages balanced it, and Prometheans sought it.

                                                        But Changeling is the second gameline of the First Edition lot to feature something that deviates much more strongly from the Morality standard after Werewolf's Harmony, and for good reason: Changelings aren't humans.

                                                        This, honestly, was one of my favorite things to come up in Hunter the Vigil's Mortal Remains. The simple fact of the matter is that every changeling is a broken person with a mercurial, transforming basis-their context is always questionable. This can lead to a lot of unclean and unpredictable responses. They can grow, they can progress, sure, no doubt, but until they get 'home', every changeling is a dangerous wreck of a person who can't even claim a human mindset anymore-they must adapt and come to understand themselves and their worlds through the eyes of a fairy.

                                                        Freeholds have to protect their citizens from each other because of this risk of 'sudden eruption', particularly in a world that encourages. Hell, stop and consider, for a second, the Hedge. One of it's biggest qualities is that it forces people who enter to reconsider their values. Think stealing is a sin? After weeks of starvation in the Hedge, will you still think that when you see a goblin leaves his stall unattended? That's more or less a straight up example from the book of what the Hedge does to people. And that's just one of several forces that tug and pull on any given changeling.

                                                        The simple fact is that Changeling, as a game, can't ever really have a game about good guys and bad guys in a clean cut manner, because it's entire basis embraces a system of shifting perspective, and it has to live with the fact that a character who would never, ever do a thing one day might find the wrong set of colliding glassworks of interests and circumstances the next day and do that exact one thing. This is the truth, this is the fight. Changelings have to crawl through mud and shade to find the high ground, to see things as they are, and the entire time, illusion and dream and dishonesty will pull them this way and that way and keep them ever and ever lost.

                                                        So, yeah, always and forever, anyone in Changeling could turn out to be a traitor, the one who most threatens your own, personal I will never go back. It has always been that way. This isn't new. It's easy to lose, under the game's hopeful lights, but it has always been there. This has always been the game.

                                                        That's just how it is. You always risk going back. Not all roads require your legs.

                                                        Crossing Your Heart, Hoping to Never Let You Get Away-The Huntsmen in the Scheme of Things

                                                        Originally posted by Omicron
                                                        I have... many issues with Huntsmen. They're game-warping in a way, say, the Strix aren't. A Strix is universally bad news, but it can be bad news in many different ways, and it can be bad news for different people, for your city as a whole, for you in particular, for some third party. You can be peripherally involved, you can mitigate the bad news, the bad news may happen and leave you much worse off but still alive (well, undead). The Huntsmen are bad at this: they come after you, and the only possible endings to your confrontation are 1) game over, 2) you kill it, 3) you betray your fellow Changelings. This is much more narrow and restrictive.

                                                        I also, frankly, find the Huntsmen boring, and find them to swallow space previously occupied by more interesting opponents. They're anti-Changelings, humen with a tell that can hide as things, and exist only to hunt you down. Despite all claims to the contrary, they cannot truly be unique. A brutal, wrathful monster that wants to tear down everything in its way to catch you, and a patient, sadistic monster that wants to exploit your attaches to slowly draw you out in the open and snatch you, are still pure-octane hunter-abducters who cannot be bargained with (except in this one specific way they all share) and want to abduct you. They have none of the variance and potential of goblins, Loyalists, Privateers, True Fae acting in person, all of whom can have many varied objectives and desires and can be interacted with in many ways - including socially or mentally.
                                                        So, here we are, in this den of uncertainty about who and what and why and when and where and how, with the world being this ever uncertain thing that you just need to find your own personal certainty in, and who do we have represent that? Really, the whole lot of Arcadian forces. Hobgoblins can fill in that niche, but their function is actually markedly different as this goes, and they hew more towards the Long Way Home side of the argument rather than the I will never go back. So, by Arcadian forces, previously we meant True Fae, Fetches, and Loyalists, occasionally with Privateers. But really, when you splinter the argument down, each of these makes very different cases, nevermind the power gap we're about to chat about in a bit. The Fetch is simply "You can't go back". A compelling antagonists, sure, love them to death, but it's a pawn. It holds the fort while other forces come on. Loyalists and Privateers? Compelling, but not inexorable enough, too human, too fragile, too broken. They are too 'you' to properly hit the buttons of I will never go back. And the True Fae themselves? Eh, they could be, but the terror of them is more compelling when they are the place to come to.They are the back you will never go to, it's strange, off-rhythm for them to seek you out. There's a hole in the world, there's a hole in your heart, there's a hole where your strength and fear used to be.

                                                        That aside, the gap really was an issue. Loyalists just didn't get the attention they needed to be something between the Fetch and the True Fae, and really, they couldn't be-as changelings, they were best on a sliding scale. Fetches don't have a long enough span given their place to really go far in the Mid-Wyrd Area, and the True Fae became cheapened when they became weaker for showing up. Again, there was a hole in the world. We needed an icon who could stand just outside the door, the one who whispered gently, with the muck and the shade and the blood of maggots, into your delicate ear "Let me in." Someone on the threshold, with a hand firmly on the chain of I will never go back, one who could throw a city in uproar.

                                                        The Huntsmen. The Huntsmen can be intimately outside. The Huntsmen can break every dream and delusion you have about the Long Way Home, and they can make it all the stronger by smashing them down. The Huntsmen can be powerful but not godly, the Huntsmen can wear the thousand faces that is missing, faces to fill the hole in the world, in the narrative, in the changelings themselves.

                                                        And, really, I find arguments of narrowness astoundingly wrong. The Huntsmen have a target, sure, and they have a fairly simple goal-bring that person back-but you don't move queen pieces strictly like a rook or a bishop. I think a lot of this comes down to some story misconceptions.

                                                        I mean, why can't a Huntsman go after a city? What's keeping him from grabbing the Bulwark and flipping on it's head? Why does a Huntsman have to go after you? Who says a Huntsman even has to exist for you immediately? Who says that indiscriminate chaos can't happen? Who says you can't bargain with them?

                                                        I mean, when you get right down to a very basic level, the True Fae, the Loyalists, the Fetch(knowingly or not), all of them want to abduct you and ship you back to Arcadia. This is a one directional game, all roads lead to Arcadia. I will never go back requires conflict with the many faces of back. It's not like this limitation hasn't always existed, cause it always has-if it's in service to Arcadia, it's there, like Hitler lingering over a forum thread, waiting to come in.

                                                        If I am honest, Changeling the Lost Second Edition has been plagued more by shallow, narrow, rigid readings since day one than any other second edition came out. I sometimes worry that in a fit of irony, the fandom is now so stuck in its' own nostalgia that it can't see what's really go on. The Huntsmen thread has had some of the most superficial readings I've seen to date.

                                                        Because a Huntsman has a goal, but each one can have their various ambitions, and they have the tools to do so much beyond just capturing one fae. The Huntsmen can ramp out the paranoia by hiding among the courtiers, ousting them to the world and each other with a swing of their weapons, tempt the Lost into their own death spiral, lie and deceive their way into the heart of a Bulwark and break it over their knees.

                                                        In fact, Huntsmen are something of a Bulwark breaker, stirring the pot and causing chaos and disharmony until they can dance their way into the ritual and shatter them. They come with a hall of mirrors and pointed fingers and lies and viciousness. The Huntsmen may be here to grab one person, and that may be the goal, but they have other goals, other tastes, over attitudes, that weigh in just as much

                                                        On the other extreme, we have people who think they plague the game, who read inevitability and constancy where, honestly, there isn't. The changelings of first edition were constantly afraid that the True Fae were going to come for them, and somehow everyone was okay with that. If I were a twee writer, I would suggest that the frantic worrying and complaining over similar sentiment over the Huntsmen is just proof that they thought the Huntsmen actually would. But that's farking useless.

                                                        Still, it's worth noting that this pervasive sense of the True Fae just swooping in and taking the changelings until they were dead or given a better offer is as old as Lost itself is. People used to bitch time and again during the early day of the games.

                                                        And then reality settled in. Like reality settles in with the Huntsmen. You'll notice there's not a whole lot of bitching about how the True Fae just sweeping in anymore.

                                                        In game, again, it's not always immediately worth it to make a Huntsman. The Huntsman doesn't always spring immediately back into the game upon coming back, despite the time limit. The Huntsman does not just dogmatically pursue a single target, because while that target is central, it can do so much more that make it's time worth while. It has it's own balance of The Long Way Home and I will never go back to handle. All these reasons and more is why they are both worthy of being the cart upsetter for a chronicle and why they are not as pervasive as people make them out to be.

                                                        Out of game, it just makes sense to use them where narratively it's appropriate-they are creatures of narrative, as are the True Fae. You can do a lot with them if you dream with their abilities, and you get the most when you don't play them like the Terminator, but rather as what they are-wickedly clever and persistent stake-raising, tone-changing knives.

                                                        Flex a little, dream a little, breathe a little. Dive into the dark and pull out on the other side to find what's there. Because the Huntsmen are just one half of the equation, much the same way I will never go back is only one half of the game.

                                                        There's a Star For Me In That Night-Between the Bleak and Hopeful

                                                        So, I've been doing an indulgence of bleakness. Congratulations TvTropes, I give you some due.

                                                        Now to tell that bleakness to go choke on a bag of baby dicks like I always do.

                                                        I've dug deep into the darkness of this game, but I want to make it clear that I don't fucking much care for a bleak game. I never have. I never will. I don't truck with fatalistic tragedy, as many people in this thread seem to think Changeling is becoming. And I'll tell you, here and now, this game is not a stage for fatalistic tragedy.

                                                        I've been harping on and on about the due of the darkness of I will never go back because, yes, I feel like so many people seem to have lost sight of it, but this game was not pitched as having a theme of I will never go back. It's theme is the Long Way Home. I will never go back is what informs the Long part of that phrase, it's what makes the changelings Lost, it's what makes it psychological and terrifying and deeply, beautifully important to so many people.

                                                        Because despite every bit of mud and shade and blood of maggots that is drawn from I will never go back, at it's heart, Changeling tells you, the character, you, the player, you,the world-

                                                        Changeling tells you that you can win.

                                                        Changeling tells you that you can have a happy dream, that you can have the happy story.

                                                        It tells you that by challenging you to take I will never go back head on, to dive through it and tear through it, that with cleverness and trust and hope, always hope, that one day-

                                                        One day, you'll be okay. That you and yours will be okay. Because you fought your way through it. Because you didn't run, or hide, or just tell yourself a story about how it might be better, it'll be okay.

                                                        It won't be safe. It might not be comfortable.

                                                        But it'll be okay. Because you can match it. Time and time again. With each new and old shadow on the doorstep, with each new growth of towering trees, for every Huntsman you face.

                                                        You'll be okay. Whoever you are in this case.

                                                        That's Changeling the Lost. It needs the bleak. It needs the traitors. It needs the confusion and the loss and the weakness. It needs the Huntsmen and the storms and the easy lies to believe. It needs them because you can't be okay without acknowledging them, without fighting them, without winning and losing to them, until you transcend the story into living, 'til you transcend moments of time into a life.

                                                        That's Changeling the Lost. That's the game I fell in love with. And that's the game David Hill and his crew are bringing you all with Second Edition.

                                                        Maybe, if you read it again, with clarity, with a broadness and flexibility of reading, you can see how that's happening. It's my hope that you will.
