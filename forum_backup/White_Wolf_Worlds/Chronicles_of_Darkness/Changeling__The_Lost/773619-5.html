                                                    My interpretation:
                                                    What qualifies as iron for the purposes of bypassing Faerie defenses is a matter of its conceptual purity, not it's chemical purity. Whatever the origins of the weakness, it comes from a time well before the periodic table. So the weakness isn't to the chemical element iron but to what earlier (possibly prehistoric) cultures thought of as iron. It refers to the raw material as it's pulled from the ground. Iron ore is iron in it's purest form by this way of thinking. The more man alters it or manipulates it, the less conceptually pure it is, and the less effective it is against the Fae. Pig iron, wrought iron, and cast iron qualify because although the iron ore has been melted and worked, it hasn't been changed sufficiently to affect its identity. Steel on the other hand has been transformed through lengthier processes that either require a herculean effort (using ancient methods) or a large-scale industrial process in modern times. The transformation into steel is considerable enough that, conceptually, what is created isn't iron in the sense that was understood at the time of the contract or curse or whatever it may have been. It's not thought of as iron, so much as something that's made from iron. A completely pure sample of the chemical element Fe wouldn't work as a bane, since it doesn't occur that way in nature and would have to be produced by complex scientific procedures. Plus, it's nothing like the conception of iron that existed at the time the bane came into existence.

                                                    Determining what works is less a matter of science and more a matter of legality. When a Judge has to make a ruling on how to interpret the language of a contract, they take into account what the terms meant when the contract was written up to determine the intent. In this case the Judge is the Wyrd, and it has determined that the iron in question is the ancient material and not the chemical element that people named after it much, much later. You could try to get the Wyrd to reconsider its ruling, but the Gentry aren't interested in expanding their bane and they have better lawyers than you, metaphorically.

                                                    The books do muddy the waters by saying contradictory things about what constitutes "pure" iron, and by either not understanding how wrought and cast iron are made or just being written in a confusing way.

                                                    Side note:
                                                    I also felt that the requirements for making hand-forged iron presented in RoS were both vague and excessively difficult. Personally, I allow any iron made with ancient methods (not using any modern processes or machinery), to count in my games. This makes it possible for regular people to acquire hand-forged iron, but means that characters either have to go on a search for a smith, find an antique that suits their needs, or learn to craft the items themselves. This is just a personal preference/house rule, obviously.
