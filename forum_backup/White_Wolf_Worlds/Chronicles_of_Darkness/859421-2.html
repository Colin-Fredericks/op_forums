    With regards to the proliferation of positions I think it depends a great deal on how many Covenants are active in a city and how large they are. I would argue that the more Covenants there are in the city the fewer internal positions they're going to have by dint of being a smaller percentage of the overall Kindred population. Not impossible by any means for all five to have entrenched infrastructure and a robust interior hierarchy, but a domain large enough the hold all that would be populous indeed. So say for instance you have Detroit, and Detroit is top to bottom Ordo Dracul. Detroit is going to have a full complement of internal positions within the Ordo in addition to the various city court positions (if they even bother with the latter). Over in DC though where the Ordo is a junior partner in a coalition with the Invictus (hypothetically), they're going to have a smaller share of the population, power, and prestige and will be less likely to have a full suite of titles. Meanwhile in New Orleans where every Covenant is carving off a piece they may barely have anything.

    Alternatively one could also make the argument that the more engaged a Covenant is with the politics of the city the fewer internal positions they will have. It could be a matter of focus and priorities. The difference between being oriented toward the exterior life of the court and the interior life of the Covenant. The Lancea Sanctum may be all but shut out of city affairs and mainly keep to themselves rather than mingle at Elysium, which if they have any numbers also means they have Kindred with nothing better to do than ecumenical politics.

        Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
        Let me preface this whole topic by saying that this is not meant as a criticism, but rather something of a thought experiment. I adore Requiem and quite like all of the myriad factions and mythology that's been built up for it so far, and have no beef with it continuing.

        What's the "point" of the large focus on elaborate factions, games of reputation and rank, and all the other politics so central to the society of the undead that Requiem revolves around?

        Vampires are, at their core, hungry monsters who were once human, and hold to that former identity with varying degrees of dedication. Most of the vampires we see in folklore, legend, and fiction are largely solitary creatures, with the most famous (Dracula) being sire to four others, none of which he seems to care terribly much for. The immediate and obvious metaphors represented by vampires - either as abusers or as addicts - don't have much related to elaborate social plays either, which has me asking the question at hand.

        The average chronicle of Vampire: the Requiem is one that deals with five globe-spanning conspiracies, each with their own beliefs and detailed hierarchies, all intermingling in a Danse Macabre that resembles nothing so much as feudal court. In order for these factions to have any meaning whatsoever (and faces to attach to their varied positions), every domain has to be stuffed with a hilarious number of undead, many of which seem to revolve around their new unholy jobs.

        I have to ask: why? Is this merely inherited from Masquerade, which (as a consequence of its ever-expanding setting via continuous supplements) had to constantly add new details to the point of absurdity? Is there some vital connection to either vampires in general or the themes of the game that this relates to that I'm simply not seeing? For other games, it makes some sense - wolves already run in packs for Werewolf, there are countless stories of wizard schools and secret societies for Mage, and even at the stranger end for Demon you still have spies being tied to wider organizations - yet I see no such connection for vampires.

        I'd love to hear everyone's answers, since this has been stuck in my brain for the last few days.
        I know very little about Blood&Smoke, but Requiem was pretty vocal about vampires never leaving their hometown. As such, none of the covenants is a "globe-spanning conspiracy". Being head of Invictus in Vienna means fuck all, because neither you nor your subordinates are ever leaving Vienna.

                    The gothic isolation being described (and the elder technological incompetence) are both pretty soundly phased out in 2e. Refer to the 2nd Chapter of the core for details, but specifically the "Grace of Monsters" artifact and the section on Cloisters for some idea. Neighboring domains definitely talk to one another as is discussed, and the concept of nomads are certainly present. It's not easy for vampires to physically travel, but they have, and evidently have been doing so for millenia, if the existence of the Gallow's Post (discussed in the core and detailed in Requiem for Regina) or the described reason the Post became defunct are anything to go by. This says nothing of the Camarilla, which ruled over the largest swathe of territory ever controlled by one Kindred body, or the Empire described as having existed in Hong Kong, which apparently was the second largest connected network of Kindred domains next to the ancient Roman Camarilla.

                    All this comes with the important caveat that no, there is no single governing body of the Lancea et Sanctum or the Ordo Dracul, but there are certainly domains controlled by these covenants that neighbor one another and travel between one another. Secrets of the Covenant will obviously expand on this more, but the playtest merits also refer to travelling and fugitives from different domains and such, so it again is very clearly something that goes on. I wouldn't be surprised by an alliance of say, Invictus domains between a couple of different ones in North America, but they might just share mortal resources between one another, with other vampire domains geographically breaking them up and preventing the formation of a large power bloc that could conceivably threaten others.

                        Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
                        Potential new question: is the average Requiem setting overpopulated?

                        There's that old 1:10,000 number I always see tossed about unsourced, meaning that a fairly large city like San Diego has ~140 Kindred in it, while Portland can only have ~61. Sure, London can have nearly a thousand vampires under this metric, but that's at least thousand mortals getting fed upon per week; surely this goes noticed?

                        I can't help but wonder if a greater scarcity of Kindred might be more realistic and more entertaining in play.
                        Having fewer vampires sucks (pun intended), in my opinion, because in low population settnigs it's that much harder to encounter a new vampire the PCs don't already know. In my games, I never, ever, define a specific number of vampires. I don't even estimate it. There is always just the idea that there are more than the PCs know. If there are, say, 40 vampires only, in a city, the PCs might know every one of them. It seriously restricts the setting and it could give more violently inclined vampires the idea that they could theoretically take over the city by force (and hell, with 40 vampires, they probably could).

                        In my current game (which has run for roughly 14 or 15 sessions), the PCs know the names of the top ten or so vampires in power, their fuedal lord, another group of five vampires in an adjoining territory, and three scattered other vampires. That's it. Now, they've been in the presence of at least three dozen other vampires, but they don't know their names, nor have they had meaningful interaction with them.

                                My take on things:

                                First of all, vampires are creatures of appetite. For blood, first and foremost -- but also for power, respect, revenge, occult knowledge... The elaborate social games and hierarchies of the Covenants are a way to feed those more abstract hungers in a relatively controlled manner. All the elaborate protocol and ritual means that vampires have to act with subtlety when pursuing their ambitions. Vampiric political life acts as a filter to weed out the most reckless before they have a chance to seriously endanger the status quo.

                                Secondly, the All-Night Society in general and the Covenants in particular help vampires pretend they're still people, as opposed to being soulless parasites. Socializing with other vampires can be risky, certainly, but it offers different risks than opening up to a human. Other Kindred can understand what you're going through in a way no-one else can. As for the Covenants, they offer comforting ideological justifications for your otherwise horrifying existence. It's so much easier to stay sane when you can convince yourself that you're a part of the natural order, or God's chosen avenger, or that you might one day transcend your damnation and become truly superhuman.

                                Finally, there's safety in numbers. Mortal hunters are a real threat to a lone vampire, and the Strix -- and stranger things -- are always out there. Additionally, Covenant status or the favor of powerful Kindred are powerful defenses if another vampire goes after you or your herd. The more contacts you have, the more likely one of them will give a shit if you're in it deep. They'll probably expect something in return, of course, but every vampire knows there's no such thing as a free lunch.

                                                My key view on politics in Vampire is that Kindred are nasty, territorial predators, and without a political framework to hold them into check, they'd never interact except to fight. I remember way back when I first heard there was a game about vampires in the works, and my first thought was "how is that going to be interesting?" Here we are 26 years later...

                                                Despite being a legacy of Masquerade, the politics are a lot more personal, or should be IMHO, than the previous conceit. VtM (and really, most of the oWoD's societies) was monolithic and rigid and oppressive. It was rife with eldritch conspiracies, ancient masters who could devour cities, and parallel caste systems. Requiem is much more personal and intersectional, and it has more to do with how you live as a vampire, rather than trying to survive the Jyhad. Conspiracies here are hatched in cloakrooms and around corners, rather than in antediluvian tombs or soaring skyscrapers, and it's too easy to catch up mortals in the schemes, who aren't the faceless Kine of old. Everyone matters in Requiem, as people, and even more so in 2E, and it's politics that will save your Touchstone from your rivals, not bloodyminded violence.

                                                    On the subject of population, I've had the experience of being in a game where there were too few vampires and it made things feel silly. I was playing a game of VtM in Toronto, roughly 1800's. There were fourteen vampires in the whole city. At the same time, we had a Prince, a Senshal, a Sheriff, a Scourge, and primogen seats for all 7 clans. There were more titles than untitled vampires. It felt silly when I claimed to be the primogen of the Tremere, I was the only one.
