          Honestly, it boils down to a simple question:

          Are they a character, or are they a cardboard cutout?

          Basically, let's say I introduce a black character who's a tough guy from the 'hood. Let's call him Jerome.

          Do people stop to comment on the fact that Jerome's black? Is he conspicuously the only black dude around? Then it's tokenism.

          Is that everything to his character? Could you replace "Jerome" with "black guy from the mean streets" without losing anything? Then you've made a stereotype.

          Is the fact that he's a tough black guy unimportant in the face of him being Jerome? Then Jerome is a character, and you win!

                          From an ST perspective, I follow the rule of "at least two." If I create a prominent character that's veering towards a stereotype, I need to create at least one character of the same race/gender/etc that does not display those traits as a stereotype. For example, in the last campaign I ran, there was Wade Perkins, a homeless conspiracy theorist; Bob Winchell, the mayor of the town; and Jonquil, a film student at the local collage. All were black, all fell into some stereotypes, but they didn't all fall into the same ones. There was variety, which helped avoid tokenism and stereotypes.

                          The other thing I do is try to make incidental characters that have different racial/gender/etc traits without playing into stereotypes as much as possible. This helps with the "at least two" part, as well as encouraging spotlight diversity. It can be easier to create named NPCs of different races/genders/etc, because you can see and examine them better; one-off characters created on the fly tend towards whatever the ST things of as "average" or play deeply into stereotypes (at least in my experience). So for one-offs, race/gender/etc should be mentioned, but more incidental than with a planned NPC.

                              3 of the last 4 characters I played were non-white, who had some major traits one would describe as stereotypical. Actually, I kind of feel like characters who are decidedly non-white need to have SOME major traits which are effected by it, especially because bigotry is a central component of modern culture, so it's hard for someone not to be affected, and because creating a minority character is an opportunity to explore some of that stuff. The important part of it though is to make them realistic characters, not charicatures.

                              - Richard Beanpicker was a black, ex-military body-guard who had the stats to effectively dual wield AK 47s. He was extremely tough and strong, dumb as a brick, and talked in a thick back-woods accent. He was also a born-again southern baptist, had the documents to trace all of his ancestors to when they were slaves, had a Bachelor's Degree in American Literature, was a bit of a Cosby-esque racism-denier, and was extremely competent and dedicated when it came to protecting the party. I didn't actually make him with the intention of him being black at first, he just ended up that way when I drew the picture.

                              - Scratch Sandwalker, lifted from a series I'd been writing, was a demure Navajo detective who dual-wielded custom-made 12-guage revolvers. She was mildly racist against white people, remarking to another character that "if you let 'em in your house, they'll start acting like they discovered the place." She also had this whole deal going with Coyote, and one of her merits was a relic bone-daggar which could affect spirits. She also dropped Peyote in a satanic rock-concert at some point, which lead to the crucial discovery that "This isn't a concert hall... It's a WHEAT THRESHER!" A large chunk of her character was rooted in the concept of being Navajo and Female (including the high Dexterity, which is an actual feminine trait), but a large chunk of it, like the leather-jacket and the revolvers was other tropes.

                              - Waijao the Traveler was a cross between several races in the fantasy setting we were using, but was effectively a chineese/arab in early-industrial America working as an ambassador/secret-agent for a highly collectivist nation. He was the party's socialite, and used a very foreign set of methodologies and philosophies in his work, outright poisoning people and using others as pawns because it was "the only ethical option". Because it was a fantasy setting, he was less "from China" and more from the result of "What if there was a superior, yet thoroughly alien culture?"

                              Interestingly enough, the first two had a lot of negative feedback from other players, not for what I was worried were stereotypes, but for what they DIDN'T personify. The black character got complaints every time he bucked the perceived character-stereotype of the pre-desegregation "dumb negro" by doing things like referencing the Scientific American he'd been reading on the plane and pointing out things that he'd researched in the night between the briefing and the operation. The Navajo character got held to traditions and stereotypes for completely different cultures, going so far as one player insisting that "her people were defeated, and therefore, by her own cultural standards, she is not a person" because of a supposed tradition which I looked up and seemed to come from a North-Eastern group like the Mohicans or Mohawks, which were a completely different culture. Oh, and the third guy kept getting called a brainwashed communist from a nation where everything was free, even though he outright stated repeatedly that he was getting paid "enough money to spend a whole year in a brothel at the end of this two month operation." I feel like my campaign group is kind of unknowingly racist though, and I'm the only one who realizes that, because I grew up the East Coast Megalopolis, but I now live in a conservative part of Florida, so it might might be that...

                              Another character I made recently but didn't really get to play began with me saying to someone on the internet that I could build a character off of the phrase "I am an Angry Homosexual": he was a mechanic who was moonlighting as a vigilante because Vampires had murdered his husband. He was specifically played without any stereotypes, going so far as to talk in a gruff voice and have a very masculine sense of aesthetics (the husband was the one with the fashion sense), and I only ever told the GMs (it was a club campaign) that he way gay, intending for it to be one of those things that you have to see the photos he keeps around or read a newspaper article to find out. The GMs started calling him "the gay guy" though, so that was kind of... yeah...

                              Originally posted by GibberingEloquence View Post
                              when do you think it is acceptable for a character to have a few stereotypical traits?
                              When you actually have a reason WHY the characters have those traits, because anything that is common enough to see a stereotype has a very good historical reason, but pop-culture, from a sociological perspective, is terrible about acknowledging that. I've seen people on the news (and I don't mean Fox) insist that Black People are some solid monolithic group which has somehow collectively decided to act stupid because they think it's "cool", completely ignoring 3 centuries of context. Hell, Bill Cosby was one of the worst racism-deniers in the popular media for the past 3 decades, and until he got outed as a serial rapist, any debate about racism would devolve into racism-deniers quoting him because "black people can't be racist".

                              It doesn't make sense for a character from another planet to talk shit about the police in "gangsta-patter" (I'm looking at you, Transformers), and it also doesn't make sense for someone who grew up in a nice suburb, but it CAN make sense if they're from the inner-city. Understanding concepts from street-philosophy like the THUG LIFE cycle or the NWA line "our art is a reflection of our reality" is CRUCIAL to playing that sort of character. There is endless information about the racism which is central to modern-day American culture, especially concerning the destructive relationship the black community has with police: if you have a black character who hates the police, it is ESSENTIAL to know where that resentment came from.

                              Sure, you can make a character who is just copying rap or "doing it to look cool", but you HAVE to realize that that is NOT where those behaviors originate. A good place to start that sort of research is to go watch a few episodes of The Boondocks, and understand what roles the characters are trying to portray, and that Huey Freeman is usually the one giving the context for the stereotypes, while Riley, Grandpa, Tom, and Uncle Ruckus are usually playing out subcultural bastardizations: understanding WHY the behaviors that Riley is imitating and Tom is shunning exist is essential.

                              The same goes for Islamic characters, especially ones who support Sharia Law or Terrorists: go read about the Koran from an academic source, understand that the popularity of Sharia Law is relative to how shitty a country is (99% supported in the shitholes of Afghanistan and Iraq, but only 10% in the modern democracy of Iran, despite Iran being between the other two), and understand that Sociologists consider the "Islamic Terrorism vs the War On Terrorism" conflict to be a single Meme born out of the combination of Nationalism, Interventionism, and American-Style Democracy. Whatever you do, do not portray Islamic Terrorists as "hating us for our freedom." That's way too simple. They hate "us" for bombing them in response to some of them bombing us for some of us doing a thing at some point because they did a thing in response to us doing a thing... Basically, there's been a conflict going for half a century, and neither side has been able to break out of Nash Equilibrium because popular opinion doesn't select for negotiation. If you're using Terrorism as a central element in any story, that view is not only far more accurate, but actually makes for a WAY more nuanced view of things. (then again, if they're terrorists in the US, you should actually consider making them Christians, as the vast majority of Terrorism in the US is Christian oriented.)


                              In short, if you're going to play a stereotype, do your research. Don't just toss it in because it's an easy signifier of race, actually understand why it's common enough (or at least, commonly believed enough) to BE a stereotype in modern media. Treat it with respect, because (as one of the previously mentioned local GMs found out when the other player from the East Coast exploded on him about his rants about "gangsta thugs") some of it might actually hit closed to home with people who know other people who embody those stereotypes. Even if it's something you really don't like, find a way to sympathize with it, because that's what makes it a believable trait and not a charicature.

                              Or don't. It's your campaign. You could glorify the NAZIs for all I care.

                                  Two points of clarification on RockstarRaccoon's post:

                                  1) You can't be racist against white people. You can be prejudiced against them and discriminate against them and treat them badly based on their skin, certainly. But racism involves systemic, cultural bias against a group, which white people don't suffer under (in the US, at least).

                                  2) Biologically, females don't trend to be any faster or more dexterous than males. They do, however, have a higher average pain tolerance, just as males have higher average upper body strength. Culturally, I haven't run into stereotypes of women being faster or more dexterous than men; it's trended more towards women being more socially adept, while men are better physically and/or mentally (at least in present-day US).

                                  That said, I agree about research, research, research. Get first-hand sources or works written by a person of the group you are aiming to portray, if you can.

                                                      Originally posted by AceOfAlmonds View Post

                                                      1) You can't be racist against white people. You can be prejudiced against them and discriminate against them and treat them badly based on their skin, certainly.                                                       Here's the thing about that, though. This definition of racism is relatively new and far from widely accepted or used. I agree with the importance of distinguishing between systemic oppression versus racial discrimination on an individual level, and that the former by definition can't happen to people in the position of power within an oppressive dynamic. But racism is such a charged word, trying to use it to mean the former when it has historically and most commonly been used to mean the latter is only going to create controversy.

                                                      I think it would be more valuable to acknowledge that there is a difference between the prejudice people in power experience and the systemic oppression people not in power experience, without correcting people on whether they use the word racism to describe prejudice or oppression, at least until the idea of prejudice and oppression as two different things starts gaining more traction outside of academia. Especially when the current dictionary definition of racism doesn't (yet) agree with the newer, more academic one. For the time being, all that policing the use of the word racism really does is spark flame wars. I don't think that was your intention, but I do think ZealousChristian24 is right that this line of discussion is quite likely to lead to people on both sides getting infractions and is probably best to just leave alone.
