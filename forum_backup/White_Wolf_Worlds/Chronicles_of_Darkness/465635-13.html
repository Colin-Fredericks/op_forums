                                            The metaphor that doesn't work is electron energy states one. But also the use of machine imagery on the whole is more thematic than metaphorical. It's the thing that links Demons to Angels to the God-Machine. It's a factor they have in common and that commonality primarily serves a thematic purpose.

                                            With respect to whether or not whatever aspect of machine imagery is actually a metaphor and whether or not it works depends on the authors' original intentions. IMO, the goal is probably to showcase a supreme entity that is so alien that its motives are unknowable. The fact that the imagery of machines is used as opposed to the more anthropomorphic representations that are more commonly attributed to deities and their minions (and their opponents) seems to support such an interpretation. If that was the goal, to showcase the alien aspect of the God-Machine and the continuum of things related to it through shared thematic imagery (i.e. angels, infrastructure, and demons), then that metaphorical usage begins to breakdown as soon as counter-examples to that alienness, such as the contents of your post, are shown. So taken in the larger context of literature, the machine is alien to us metaphor breaks down when we begin comparing it to other sources that are explicitly challenging the boundaries between our own nature and the nature of machines. Consider for example, the Matrix Trilogy, A.D. Police Files, Bubblegum Crisis, any of the various iterations of Ghost in the Shell (although Stand Alone Complex is probably the most pertinent), Ergo Proxy, Psycho Pass, and, of course, what you've already posted, among other titles. (And of course some of these titles actually appear as "inspirational material" so I'm thinking that the usage machine imagery is primarily thematic rather than metaphorical.)

                                            Now it may be the case that you believe the authors intention was something other than using machines as a metaphor for alienness (and it might be the case that the authors believe this themselves). That falls into the "all bets are off" YMMV category of argumentation.

                                            On the whole folks seem to have a penchant for seeing metaphors (and analogies) everywhere but that isn't always the work that any particular bit of imagery is doing at any particular point in a text.

                                                        It must be noted that God-Machine/Demon Developer Rose Bailey has gone on record saying that her primary source of inspiration for the God-machine was this sense of how people's entire lives, through the fault of no one, seemed to placed into certain ruts and forms, that a person's life was dictated from birth to death by the accidents of systems upon systems laid before, during, and after, that their successes, failures, swings of good fortune and ladder climbling, their falls of luck and casting from the echelons of the society-these things happen, not at the fault of any given person, but merely the order of reality. The pervasive sense, that these things that happened without purpose or malice, served something, some end, is a big point of birth for the God-Machine, and is in fact why the God-machine is actually not a machine, so much as an impossibly colossal sense of order, the presence of a plan, and the accompanying implication of a will, all balanced by the simple horror of a clock.

                                                        This is one of quite a lot of metaphors that make up the soul and skin of Demon the Descent and it's monsters, who walk the crossroads of flesh, machine, and spirit between the heavens of the divine and the earths of "merely beyond humanity".
