                            This conversation reminds me in a general way of Michael Moorcock's article "Starship Stormtroopers" which argues (from an anarchist perspective in 1978) that there's a lot of reactionary politics in fantasy and sci-fi genre fiction. Highly recommend reading it yourself and drawing your own conclusions, it's very thoughtful.

                            The part that sticks in my mind is when he talks about protagonists who fight on behalf of some institution or established rightful order. In the specific case of CofD games, I enjoy playing characters who are motivated by loyalty to their Order or Legacy or whatever social group, so this bit made me reflective.

                            There always comes the depressing point where Robin Hood doffs a respectful cap to King Richard, having clobbered the rival king. This sort of implicit paternalism is seen in high relief in the currently popular Star Wars series which also presents a somewhat disturbing anti-rationalism in its quasi-religious 'Force' which unites the Jedi Knights (are we back to Wellsian 'samurai' again?) and upon whose power they can draw, like some holy brotherhood, some band of Knights Templar. Star Wars is a pure example of the genre (in that it is a compendium of other people's ideas) in its implicit structure -- quasi-children, fighting for a paternalistic authority, win through in the end and stand bashfully before the princess while medals are placed around their necks.
                            Make of that what you will.

                                                Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
                                                I've always preferred protagonists tied to large organizations, either ones that aren't too morally grey or possibly using unpleasant means to achieve good ends. It gets really, really tiring to see every single church, military, and empire in nerd fiction be made out to be horrific systems of oppression with zero worth to them while all of the scrappy rebels and heretics are perfect heroes. Eclipse Phase threw out a decent amount of what could've been interesting setting material out the window in favor of making every hierarchy evil (the major political body is a cartoonishly evil alliance of hypercorporations who practice indentured servitude, and there's a backwards fascist American holdout that's the only bastion of hated Christianity in the setting and so blatantly awful that they read like a bad Starship Troopers parody) while making anarchism a literally flawless, utopian structure where every single person cooperates in the name of the whole, and it's the most glaring flaw in a universe I otherwise adore. Shadowrun is always a pain for me because I want to play a contractor every time.

                                                What I wouldn't give to see more on character types like D:tD's Integrators or the Conspiracies like VALKYRIE and VASCU that work to redeem systems or use their size and organization ostensibly to do the right thing. I'm so tired of the "structure bad, punk good!" that Star Wars seems to have made nerd gospel.
                                                If you spend enough time following corporations and their lobbying groups in the real world, you will note that a lot of what they do could be construed as cartoonishly evil, and this is a universe where some regulations do exist. That said, it would be nice if there were some corporations that tried to pretend that human goodwill was something they cared about/were in the business of expanding, but in a setting like Eclipse Phase where the corporations already won, actual altruism would be a weakness they could easily take advantage of. I never really read into Anarchs the way you did either, to me the good guys would be people who are yolked to these systems but still manage to find a way to help others, or otherwise subvert the negative consequences of their patrons.

                                                Whenever you think a corporation is being portrayed as a cartoonishly evil entity, just remember that there are buildings filled with workers making our stuff for next to nothing, with nets in place to keep them from jumping to their deaths.

                                                    Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
                                                    I've always preferred protagonists tied to large organizations, either ones that aren't too morally grey or possibly using unpleasant means to achieve good ends. It gets really, really tiring to see every single church, military, and empire in nerd fiction be made out to be horrific systems of oppression with zero worth to them while all of the scrappy rebels and heretics are perfect heroes. Eclipse Phase threw out a decent amount of what could've been interesting setting material out the window in favor of making every hierarchy evil (the major political body is a cartoonishly evil alliance of hypercorporations who practice indentured servitude, and there's a backwards fascist American holdout that's the only bastion of hated Christianity in the setting and so blatantly awful that they read like a bad Starship Troopers parody) while making anarchism a literally flawless, utopian structure where every single person cooperates in the name of the whole, and it's the most glaring flaw in a universe I otherwise adore. Shadowrun is always a pain for me because I want to play a contractor every time.

                                                    What I wouldn't give to see more on character types like D:tD's Integrators or the Conspiracies like VALKYRIE and VASCU that work to redeem systems or use their size and organization ostensibly to do the right thing. I'm so tired of the "structure bad, punk good!" that Star Wars seems to have made nerd gospel.
                                                    I think something worth considering in these situations is how there can be a kneejerk temptation to attempt to "redeem" these organizations and systems to achieve good ends because fiction seems unceasingly critical of them, but I'd like to posit the suggestion that, much like others are saying that they really do do cartoonishly evil things that looking at stories of people working within the system to derive. The systems really might just be flat out bad, if looked at from a position of principle, and attempted reconstructions which try to turn them into "necessary evils" of a sort are missing the mark a bit.

                                                    Maybe not by making them necessary evils per se, but rather they fail to question what they are a necessary evil for, just assuming they accomplish some greater abstract notion of good for society.

                                                    To illustrate what I mean, let's look at an interpretation of the CoD. Someone argues that the TCG does horrible things to monsters which might also be people, but they say it's for the greater good, because monsters are bad and pharmaceuticals are good. Those pharmaceuticals certainly can save lives, but they are still operating within a system where their monetary value takes primacy over their capacity to actually save lives, and it's very possible that the only lives that will be saved are the lives which can afford it. Does that mean that the system is fundamentally bad? Probably! But people might be upset, because then we have to ask, what is the evil necessary for? A bad system?

                                                    Not necessarily. That evil might be necessary to keep us, a particular group within society, comfortable. The money that Cheiron makes is part of the economy that we specifically inhabit, and it is people related, however loosely to us, who benefit from the wealth they acquire and the comforts it affords. There are people outside of that who just suffer, there is no benefit to them, and if one is strictly principled, it is just bad because of this. But it does make us comfortable. Is this a redeeming quality? Should it be?

                                                    Thinking about that, I consider the kind of protagonist who works for one of these big organizations. On the light, comedy side of things, we have someone like Ted, from Better off Ted. He works for an evil corporation. It's an evil corporation, quite unambigously lampooning all of those tropes, but Ted himself seems like a sympathetic character. Why? Because he is empathetic in his context. He does the best with what he seems to have, and those are traits we appreciate in someone. If we were approaching him strictly from principle, we might call him unethical, because he is still participating in a system of oppression by acting as a part of the company. We might even call him a purveyor of oppression through the work he does.

                                                    We sometimes do this! Other times, we are more sympathetic. Think about the good cop who bends the rules. Why do we like him? He recognizes the flaws of the institution, and tries to bend the rules to achieve the best result he personally can, and in doing that, he acknowledges tacitly the limitations of the institution, but the idea still that he believes it might ultimately be a good thing, or that its overall status is not a thing he can realistically change.

                                                    We might be sympathetic to that because:

                                                    1) those institutions which we might criticize on principle are critical or at least important to our personal standard of living and comfort (i.e. Western society built on the backs of others, the United States built on the backs of slaves, etc.) thus accepting them but still attempting reform within their context is radically different enough to appeal to us, but not so radical that it might adversely effect our status quo.

                                                    2) Because it feels real. Because it's what we all have to live with and deal with every day, and each of us is a part of and a victim of these many systems of oppression, and by making the best of it and trying to help people or do a good thing (whatever that may be) we are echoing the real, complicated part of our actual lives, but perhaps in a way which is more ideal than we can actually live (not everyone has the luxury, the ability or the courage to 'do what is right' when what is right might hurt us.)

                                                    So bringing it back to CofD, I think what makes groups like those we see in Hunter go from being just bad guys to being cool and interesting is that many of them represent real systems that we live and participate in, and by forcing us to try to enact change but still within our context, CofD gives you a story that feels raw, real and ideal, without actually being ideal. You don't have to give up your home and live in poverty, but you can still make other people's lives better, seemingly.

                                                    I think it's one of the things that helps make CofD feel more raw and real, and I also think it's the key to what makes groups like VASCU or VALKYRIE potentially interesting. It's not because they're actually good guys by some kind of objective, ethical or higher standard. They're probably not even good guys by a regular standard of ethics. Rather, they are playgrounds which create situations where you can be just good enough that you can make the decision to be good, which can ultimately feel more empowering because of how close it is to reality, which is that we all participate in things which probably are not good if you scrutinize them, but we try to do the best we can within that context.

                                                    This is long and rambly, but I think it's part of what makes CofD feel more "grownup" and is one of my personal favorite parts of Chronicles of Darkness.


                                                    EDIT: Put another way, Chronicles creates nuance not by suggesting that "both sides have a point." It just suggests that we take a more nuanced look at why we choose options which, strictly speaking, are probably bad, and what we do on the personal level to try to mitigate our negative impact. Or in some situations, how we choose to justify it.

                                                            Originally posted by Leliel View Post
                                                            Anyway, I was reading the Masquerade Q&A thread, when I read a post (I will not name names) that cued me into something about the nWoD/CofD:

                                                            It's best work came after the writers gave up on punk culture, and in fact started invoking things that are utterly anathema to punk culture (those within the system as heroes, outsiders often being ridiculous and delusional, the human craving for order not being a bad thing). So, I thought about this for a while, and realized that the only game I can think of that is proudly cyberpunk, Demon, portrays the guys who want to change the system so they can rejoin it (Integrators) as being hated for being moral consciences besides their apparent and obvious betrayal in the making, and part of the fear of the game being that you end up causing more harm than good by breaking the God-Machine. Both of these are pretty much fists to the nose of punks in general. Mage portrays the Silver Ladder, believers in a natural hierarchy of people, as big fans of the idea of responsibility and equality of opportunity, with the main problem with them being their worship of the self as an individual moral actor who knows better than everyone else.

                                                            So, I was wondering, has anyone else seen any post-punk and anti-punk elements they like or dislike?
                                                            Eh, for me it's kind of a toss up on the topic of post-punk good guys in the system being so much better. Better fleshed out/developed, yeah sure, but then again that's after all their early years of stumbling in making WoD. I like some (Promethean/Demon/Changeling/Werewolf/Hunter) Better than others (Vampire/Mage especially). I do see what you mean on the one hand, about especially doing more harm than good in their rebellion and/or not having much of a conscience, but that can also be seen as a similar stripe of black & white as the post-punk stuff in WoD also. I would say on the whole, I like elements of both more than one overall style. From the post punk era, I really dig the super factions in Masquerade and how the Anarchs are rebelling but still caught in the middle. Also how Mage explores the idea of the trade off between the dangers of elitism and carelessness if the Traditions win the unwinnable war versus censorship and thought control if the Technocracy does. For the anti-punk era, I feel that a lot of it comes from the combination of the writers having matured more and the scope being more of personal, internal horror on a smaller scale. I love how they make the packs in Forsaken fully integrated into not only their territory but the spirit reflection on top of having to deal with things like reckless humans or the Pure. But what I like most is how much more fleshed out and better explored mortal games are in ChroD. In WoD, mortals sometimes felt like extras in a movie.
