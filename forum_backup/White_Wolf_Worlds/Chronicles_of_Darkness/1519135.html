                  NPCs are fine. You have some folks who want to keep the flavor and theme of their core monster game strong and thus prefer to either not have other supernaturals in the world or reinterpret their nature in the context of the primary game, but everything remains perfectly functional if you don't.

                  The trick comes in when playing with player characters of multiple monster types. This isn't a full stop never-do-this (I've certainly played in multi-splat games), but it's a warning that you have to pay attention to what you're mixing and how that will impact the game. Vampire PCs, obviously, limit how much of the game can occur in daytime. It can be hard for a werewolf or a promethean to keep up in a game with mages, whose Arcana lend them enormous versatility in comparison. They may end up having to mostly sit out of scenes where the mages use their deep investigation abilities, which most splats have little to compare with. Mummy, of course, has the great challenge of aligning the Descent with the activity of other PCs. Each of the gamelines was roughly balanced within itself, but not with other gamelines. Mage wasn't designed with any intent to make sure that a vampire cabalmate could keep up and contribute evenly. That doesn't mean you can't have fun but it is a potential hurdle you need to understand is there.

                  Themes and genres can be relevant too. Demon is a spy game, and a demon is going to bring that element to any game she's in. Blindly putting her into any werewolf pack might not be a good idea, but she might be able to fit into a Werewolf game about Iron Masters fighting Claimed and idigam infiltrators in human society, and she's probably even a better fit for a Changeling game dealing with Huntsmen as everpresent faceless threats. Each game's rules aren't neutral about genre, so if you're mixing them you want to think about what impact that will have on the flavor of gameplay, to avoid a confusing melange that goes one way one second and another the next, and instead have ideas that complement one another.

                      First, you don't need werewolves in your vampire game or vampires in your werewolf game, etc. You can run a perfectly fine game with only a sngle splat type and their antagonists.

                      Second, most people aren't against multi splat games necessarily, contagion chronicles and dark eras both lean into multi splat. That said multisplat does have weaknesses.

                      First, multisplat brings balance issues. Some of these may be minor but some are not. If the second splat is being used for NPCs it won't matter much but if it's for PCs, you really have to be careful, nothing sucks more than having a character who is just better than you at your thing just because.

                      Second, it dilutes the theme. The Wolf Must Hunt, but Sin Eaters don't. Uratha try and protect the physical from the Shadow, the Sin Eaters instead worry about the Underworld.

                      Third, the Uratha operate a significant amount in the Shadow. Most other splats don't even know the Shadow exists, much less have a way to go there. Sin Eaters deal with the Underworld. The Underworld is hostile to all the living splats, and especially Uratha.

                      ​​​​​Lastly, multisplat games tends towards snow flakes, where everyone is special because of what they are rather than who they are.

                          Originally posted by trynmybest View Post
                          Why is splat-mixing such a taboo & why are so many people against it? It doesn’t make sense, to me anyway, for only one type of supernatural to exist in a chronicle regardless of which splat is being run.
                          Addressing that last bit separately from the general thrust of this post, because it's worth emphasizing:

                          Supernatural weirdness in general is all over the setting. Specific kinds of supernatural weirdness, like the types of monsters gamelines are built on, are not ubiquitous enough that they can and must be assumed to be present in every city one could set a game in. The ludonarrative arrangements of how a given type of monster gets by tends to mean they cluster together in places where there's a lot of the stuff that fulfills their needs, which means the vampires gravitating toward densely-populated cities aren't hanging out in the rural areas where people will notice that their neighbors have been feeling suddenly unwell, the mages jostling for real estate in the Haunted Hospital Capital Of The World aren't poking around the truck stop at the center of a successful county-wide monster purge, and the Sin-Eaters flocking to sites of tragedy are not hanging out in the places far from death and the dead. This has the advantage of allowing the writers for the corebook to not bleed wordcount on addressing the local changeling freeholds in setting writeups for Demon and Werewolf and Hunter, but it's also just how groups of people in the global human population tend to work, especially when those people are also inclined to secrecy by the nature of their conditions — compare organized crime, as a quick-and-dirty analogue.

                          Early 1e's difficulties in grasping "roughly a fifth of starting mages can teleport" notwithstanding, being largely societal games set in the modern world means most Chronicles of Darkness games are built under the assumption that traveling a substantial distance outside of the city in which you are living is usually for purposes that limit your engagement to the particular scene, chapter, or story that the trip is for. It doesn't really matter that the ruins you're investigating are on another continent if the furthest you stray from that area is your base camp or local lodgings, to say nothing of how an extradimensional Athenaeum being in the Shadow doesn't have more than a passing impact on the one scene you're there to retrieve research materials from it — to use a video game analogy, these places tend to be isolated pages of the map rather than being placed in the fuller context of an interconnected overworld.

                          And wouldn’t you have to mix splats to be able to have multiple supernaturals in the same chronicle since each one has a different power stat? Even if the players are all one type, vampire for example, wouldn’t you have to apply another splats’ power mechanics to have NPCs of another type?
                          There's lots of monsters in the Chronicles that aren't Kindred, Uratha, Awakened, Created, Lost, Bound, Arisen, Unchained, Begotten, or Remade. Most powers either just work or have a dicepool associated with it, there are two or three different general stat profiles to build with for any given creature or character if one doesn't just assign set dicepools for an abbreviated statblock, and NPCs use Willpower little enough that it serves as the go-to-fuelstat for anything that doesn't have a published supernatural template with separate juice to keep track of.

                          For much the same reason as video games reuse assets and limit the contexts in which certain tools can be used, it is more efficient for a game with as many potential details as Chronicles has to enable Storytellers to avoid having to invent new material from whole cloth every time and figure out how each thing interacts with every possible use case, because the most important thing for Deviant is that you can use the Deviant books to tell a story about people turned into monsters by human action working against the conspiracies that hunt them, not that you know what happens when a vampire ghouls a Linchpin under the assumption that subversion by Kindred vampires is a typical concern for elements of the Web of Pain who might never encounter a vampire of any sort.

                          The thing to remember is that the games aren't built under the assumption that a chronicle is a fully simulationist sandbox. Theoretically, the troupe is presenting a story of some kind, even if it's a relatively loose "here's how these characters trying to live in peace/build their power base/achieve enlightenment creates complications and here's how they deal with them" structure. The needs of the story determine what the characters run into — even if there's a pack of werewolves stalking the city limits, if a motley of changelings never leaves the downtown residential area or interacts with anybody affected by their hunts then the pack functionally doesn't exist as far as the story goes, and if the troupe does decide to steer the chronicle in that direction the Storyteller ostensibly has the time to figure out how they want to model a group of social werewolves in a game of Changeling: the Lost.

                          Mages and Beasts are outliers among the supernatural vis-a-vis seeking out different strangenesses — most monsters with societies carry forward the inclination from their mortal friends and family to not poke too deeply into unfamiliar occult phenomena. Those that do either have an occultist-type role like the Autumn Court or the Ordo Dracul or the Refinement of Silver or else do their prying through the lens of a specific pursuit like werewolves trying to stop a Wasteland from messing with their territory or Sin-Eaters investigating the origins of an artificial ghost.


                          tl;dr: Most of the characters and resources the PCs will know and be comfortable interacting with are going to be at least nearby to where they hang their hat, and most characters in general have enough on their plates already without getting enmeshed in a second or third level of secret society beyond the level of a business agreement not to dump their trash in the neighbor's yard. Not everybody is a scientist, anthropologist, or nonspecialized monster-hunter.
