            Two Merits I both like/dislike at the same time:

            Headbutt and Poison Weapon. They both have the same problem: Cool rules that I'm glad to see spelled out in the game, but are immediately gated behind spending Merit dots to just do a thing. Wanna headbutt somebody? Here's some rules for it! Awesome! That'll just be 2 merit dots' worth of xp to hit someone's head with your head, thank you. I understand from a mechanical-conceptual level that there's a "well here's how you do it without hurting yourself" situation, but...why have it that way? If you want headbutting in the game, just explaining how (with this merit's text) would be great, and enough. If you want a Merit for specializing in skull-to-skull shenanigans, maybe make something where the possessor is Hard-Headed and gets a bonus to the rolls instead.
            Wanna coat a weapon in poison? Again, here's how! But you need Int 3, Medicine 2, Science 2, and 4 or 5 merit dots spent. Oh and if you fail the application roll, now it's your problem. Which would be fine, if not for costing 4/5 merit dots, a couple skill/stat prereqs, and in-game whatever costs are deemed by your ST. Making it a general thing (and making acquisition/application easier/more certain) with a Merit would be an approach that resembles "anyone can, but some people do it WELL" more than the D&D 3e feat evoking feel of these Merits as they stand.

            Really cool ideas, though.

                                ***ONLY HUMAN***

                                These Merits are intended for use with Mortal characters, but feel free to add them to your other World of Darkness games.

                                Treat the Merits as benefiting Mortals, for example if a Merit would grant Armour against Bashing damage, this would only apply to unarmed damage as opposed to any Bashing damage a Vampire receives instead of Lethal damage due to being undead.                                 Something along those lines leaves open the interpretation for other groups or Storytellers about how the Merits work in their games and keeps the focus on Mortals for the rules and their intended implication. Everyone else who wants something different can take that guideline and extrapolate from there.

                                                I have heard of Iaijutsu, but I'm not really familiar with it, so I'm going by what you have submitted as to the "spirit" of the style. It took me a few reads and some thinking to follow everything, so my understanding might be flawed.

                                                Batto (2) has no limitations on the number of times it can be used, so it seems possible to put the weapon away and draw it again to "restart" the batto. I'm thinking this might go against the intent. I consider each distinct combat sequence a scene.

                                                Scenario 1 - two separate scenes
                                                Fight the guards at the door leading to the boss. Scene 1
                                                Walk through the door and fight the boss. Scene 2
                                                Scenario 2 - everything is one scene
                                                Boss walks out while you are fighting the guards and joins the fight or you enter while fighting guards and boss joins the fight.

                                                I think the intent is for batto to be used twice, if desired, in the first scenario, but only once in the second scenario. I could be wrong and it is fine to dodge for a turn so you can start up batto again for a long fight.

                                                Uke Nagashi (4) is the one I had trouble figuring out, but this is how I understand it.
                                                Spend 1 Willpower and add your weapon's damage rating to your defense.
                                                Then, for a turn, when an opponent attacks their attack is automatically negated and the damage they would have done is reflected back onto them.
                                                The attack negation and reflection can occur multiple times during the turn.

                                                This creates some issues. A logical issue is as your defense decreases after each attack an opponent's chance of damage is increasing, later attackers have it worse. Stronger opponents will be hurting more than weaker opponents, but the combat system isn't set up to compensate for that, more attack is countered by more defense not more health boxes. Normally, you can forego your defense which I think pushes this maneuver out of balance. You negate all attacks and everyone that tries to attack you gets damage reflected back on them as if you were defenseless, both combined is a bit harsh.

                                                I understand there is a limitation on how long this can be kept up, but this seems like a combat killer. You would have to assume that everyone is familiar with the style and won't attack when this maneuver is used. For that, you can just remove the damage reflection. The automatic attack negation pretty well covers you with that anyway. At least, if your defense was decreasing then it would make sense for close combat attackers to hit you to set you up for a ranged attack or something. Speaking of which, are we considering grappling moves attacks, at least the ones that don't deal damage? You might address this somewhere else in the book.

                                                I think addressing some issues would bring this back in line. I can only give possible suggestions since I'm not familiar with this style.

                                                1. Don't allow the user to forego defense when they are going to be countering an attack. It would make some sense for people to still attack, if only to reduce your defense.

                                                2. Limit the number of times you can negate attacks a turn. This isn't very elegant since the number is going to be subjective.

                                                3. Eliminate the damage reflection because, practically speaking, it won't come into play. Simple but doesn't seem to fit the intent.

                                                If I understand the intent here then I think the first suggestion, possibly in conjunction with the second, would keep everything more or less intact. This maneuver isn't quite game-breaking, but it does kill the fun. You could just as easily say "for 1 willpower nobody can attack you in close combat." Nothing wrong with deterring an action, but this feels more like eliminating a choice. I'm not down with that.

                                                Sorry for the length, but I had lots of rules to follow. I liked the concept and flair. I just don't think you got out what you put in.

                                                Note: I just realized that the counter attack is probably not meant to be automatic, but it doesn't change things for me. Don't hit and receive damage or don't hit and possibly receive damage isn't a huge difference to me. I never considered the damage reflection as a bad thing, just unnecessary in practice.
