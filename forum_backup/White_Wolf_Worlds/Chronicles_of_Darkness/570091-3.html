                    Well, are you yourself in any way non-normative by societal standards? Being part of a non-majority group can mean belonging to tight-knit community. People who also share some of your experiences or are at least empathic towards those things you have to go through. Sometimes groups like that can be a cross between family, support group, local community, maybe even professional network.

                    We're all human networks after all, and while you can reduce it down to "who I'm related to/who I work with/who I am friends with" you might consider how different aspects of your identity inform that.

                    Like, say you live in a particular neighborhood, and you're a trans person of color. It's very possible that you're involved in some kind of private community with other trans people of color, or you might have some kind of formal or informal group based upon that. Maybe this group isn't totally visible, because it's online, or done through a medium which prohibits people from gathering easily and becoming associated with one another.

                    So you're part of that community, and you get your Awakening, or your Embrace or whatever. That could force you apart from that community, and that community is, in all likelihood, very interested in figuring out what happened to one of their community members.

                    I remember when I was in college (I'm a bi male) we had special meetings of the people in the associated colleges that were also bi or LGBT+, and I met a bunch of my close friends through that organization.

                    It can be easy to discount how your sexuality or your gender identity might play a part in your social groups when it does so "invisibly" as can be the case when you're cisgendered or heterosexual, but they can be very important to people in a world that seems at times to be very hostile towards the very existence of people who defy heteronormativity.

                    ***

                    I guess a way to trim that all down is to think about how being LGBT+ might effect a character or an NPC's friends, family or livelihood, incorporating that isn't making it the focus but acknowledging how these things play a part in our lives.

                    Casual inclusion can be cool too, though. Having characters who are in some way non-heteronormative in your games where it's mentioned incidentally (The NPC casually mentions her wife, or her girlfriend in conversation, maybe the next link in an investigation into a missing person is the queer community they belonged to, etc.)

                    These things are parts of our lives after all. It's another way to create NPCs with those inner lives that can make PCs interested in interacting with them.

                            One question is how common is sex and romance in your game. If it is fairly common, and your game is modern, it is super easy. Have some NPCs be attracted to each other who are same sex. Done. All four of my games have had gay male and female characters come up, and 3/4 have had transpeople. The Majority of PCs in Balance of Shadows, Night Train Detective Agency, and Golden Eagle are somewhere on the spectrum, and Malibu Dream House is 50/50.

                            In a game where romance nearly never happens, it is harder. The simplest, least obtrusive way is have NPCs mention a wife/husband that indicates a same sex pairing...a shopkeeper mentions her wife called, and ally needs to have his husband do something for him. Understated, but indicating existence. A friend wants to schedule something around a date with a girl she has and really does not want to mess this up, or a different friend asks if he thinks a mutual acquaintance might be gay as he is shy/afraid to ask, if you want to be slightly more direct.

                            Transpersons are often easier, take an NPC, make them trans. Frequently, it is at least somewhat noticeable. But don't make a big deal of it. And don't try to use "subtle cues" either, that often turns out offensive. Just say something like "Beverly meets you inside the building, she is dressed in a red suit with a feminine cut, has long black hair and is trans. She is holding the briefcase and smiles when she recognizes you, but looks behind you to make sure you are not followed."

                            Gay culture can be trickier to include for people without much exposure to it. it is, fortunately, pretty easy to work in LGBTQs without having to touch on it too much. Unless you plan to look into it a great deal, I'd skip that part. Honestly I know young homosexual men and women who have no exposure really whatsoever to it with the greater acceptance of LGBTQs going on.

                            My caution would be to avoid stereotypes, especially negative ones. Predatory gays and lesbians is a big one, don't go with that. Don't make gay men effeminate jokes. Hard Gays and Bull Dykes are out there, but not every gay man or lesbian is one or the other. Transpeople trying to "trick" people is downright offensive. Try not to make every LGBTQ a criminal or sex worker. There are LGBTQ criminals and sex workers, sure, and including some is fine. But don't make them the majority, the majority of visible ones, or the first ones the PCs meet. Avoid the slutty/indecisive bi stereotype. Most bisexual and pansexual persons know what they like, thank you, and their libido is not necessarily better or worse than any other. Have some idea of the difference between transvestites, drag queens, and transgender persons. If you are unsure, just include transpersons who are a gender other than what is on their birth certificate and want to be treated as the gender they present themselves as, and leave out drag shows.

                            If NPCs can be LGBTQs without their whole personality being "gay" or "trans" or something, you are on the right track. They can be activists, but not every LGBTQ is.

                            Hope the above helps.

                                    Thing is, the less of a "model" you have, the best. Not just because there are a variation, but because this variation can run almost any experience you can imagine. Just mix freely how the character feel about each aspect of his identity, his body, his life.

                                    In my games I already did a lot of variations, both as ST and as player. Most of my favourite characters aren't cis. The secret is that there are no secret, just people.

                                    You can try starting to think of your character just as a standard char. Think about his sexuality first in the most common experiences: is he a virgin? Does he have heavy expectations? Problems in a previous relationship? Is he very sexualy active or think about sex just once in a whyle? Go ahead with questions that you could ask to anyone, those are the really important questions.

                                    When you're done, just add "quirks" you find interesting to this char and think of how they could interact with the other answers. In its core, he's a human being, like anyone else, even about his sexuality. Or gender identity, by the way. They're not the same thing.

                                    So don't ask. Just think, and if you have an idea, give it a try thinking about this char as a normal person at the core.

                                        Simply put, Trans people are people whose correct gender is not the gender they are born with, it is the gender they present themselves as. They just want to be treated as the appropriate gender and want to live their lives as that gender. That's pretty much transpeople in a nutshell, and they, we, are all too often treated very poorly. Here is a video from "Last Week Tonight" which discusses a great deal about transpersons and what we face in the world despite moving ahead. If you are normally averse to LWT, I can say this segment only deals with transpeople specifically, not politics outside the subject.

                                        Some people (including all transpeople) are genderqueer, which is to say they have a gender outside cisgender norms, but are not necessarily as straightforward as being a transperson. Some feel, for example, they are a third gender. Some are agendered. Some are genderfluid. It is difficult for me without as much personal experience to speak intelligently about them. I prefer to allow them to speak for themselves when possible.

                                        Drag Queens are a thing in gay culture (though not all are gay) who tend to be somewhat outrageous and theatrical. Drag Queens (usually) identify as male, their persona being a female and often outrageous for humorous or amusing purpose. There is not (usually) a real sexual component to them, though for some there are. There are a lot of caveats there, but I have known several Drag Queens (more than transpeople IRL, honestly) and not all were gay, and some had a sexual component to them (one was only interested in men in his persona, for example) but most were gay men with a persona they enjoyed being outrageous with.

                                        Tranvestites enjoy wearing clothes of the opposite sex, often for gratification but not necessarily. Some are Drag Queens, most are not. Some are just more comfortable in women's clothes but do not feel that they are the wrong sex. Like with some of the other parts of the Genderqueer spectrum, I feel less qualified to discuss them.

                                        If I misrepresented anyone in the above post I am sorry. Please correct me.

                                            In the end, any label put limits in something that is limitless, that's why I prefer by myself to avoid them. If you can think of a human experience in that regard, you can be sure someone out there is LIVING it. Like, you could have a "male born" character that only in his mid-twenties start to realise he never felt like a "he", and is almost thirty when can be sure, finaly, that she feels like a woman inside. But she's not sure if she wants to get rid of her genitals or something like that, she doesn't quite feel like a woman traped in a man's body, but instead that something is right with each gender, even if she's at least sure she's a woman. Oh, and she likes women, mind you. Add spice by making this char already married with a straight woman she loves, but doesn't have the slightest idea of how this wife of her will react if told that her husband actualy feels like a woman and wants to be one.

                                            Just let your mind play with ideas and with the limits of human experience. Then get the result and imagine a real, normal, full and complex person that just have this in place of expected identity or sexuality, but is otherwise whatever a common person could be. A genderqueer can be anything, even homophobic. You can even give to your character the SAME doubts about the issue you have yourself. Some do have those doubts and a thousand more. Some do not.

                                            You can them just use hose characters as normal people and watch your players react. It seems poor at first as an inclusive game, but believe, just to make those people apear as normal, with nothing to expect from them just for this, you'll both treat the question with respect and make your players and yourself think about it. And maybe your players will surprise you, be with receptiveness or prejudice, maybe not from them, but from the characters, and them all you have to think is how this perfectly normal person would react. And if you don't know how to react, just play the part and let the character not knowing, either.

                                            A next stage could be making relationships relevant in your game, an exercise you can and should start with common heterosexual couples. It can range from a passionate crime to simply have a character that need to account for his relationship when scheduling his week. An NPC can call the PCs to negotiate something in his house, where his familly can be used as background, and some of them can come to the fore and become relevant NPCs themselfs. Them you start to blend in some queergender content, and work from there.

                                            The closing of the circle comes when you add the reactions of the world at large. And that doesn't have to be allways the same way, either. Some people will find normal, even if they're not queergender themselfs. Some will find insulting. Some won't be able to understand at all, but are going to try anyway, with good or bad results. Some have their own theories about what those people realy feel and do. And them you just picture how the character will counterreact.

                                            Do that, and everything else will fall in place sooner or later. Odd places, for sure, but that's precisely the point. You don't even have to present "proper" gay culture in your game to present a gay culture. Just imagine people feeling isolated and looking for equals that can form a supportive social group, and let this group develop its own culture, that's pretty much it. You'll only have to remind your players its a fictional culture, not really a big deal in an RPG.

                                                        Supernatural Stalemate
                                                        What does this mean for Beasts?
                                                        Beasts come from all walks of life, their inner natures poorly understood by even the most learned occultists. The Begotten may not be wholly human, but nevertheless carry their childhood prejudices and assumptions forward with them.

                                                        A freedman Nemesis may fight to punish former slave masters; while a Confederate veteran Tyrant chooses instead to feed on the nightmares of Yankees he holds responsible for ruining his society. And the Ravager, whose childhood home burned in the war, is angry at everyone.

                                                        To a certain extent, this makes life easy for Beasts. Begotten, after all, hold themselves accountable to nothing except their Horrors. The racial and class anxieties in the South offer a delicious spread of fears to feed their Horrors. Even if an individual Beast does not necessarily possess any egregious bigotry, he’s usually not above exploiting such fears to get a good meal.

                                                        Observing how Beasts interacted with the South may offer a clearer perspective on why Reconstruction failed. After all, a Beast’s most emotionally (and ethically) fulfilling meal usually comes when her victim learns an important lesson. Without a sense of purpose or cultural unity, these lessons lose their meaning. A white supremacist might wake up to the pain she has caused, but a different man gets taught the perils of supporting black voting rights.

                                                        Community Ties
                                                        For Beasts, the closeness of Southern communities becomes a double-edged sword. Young Beasts who have yet to realize their full potential feel their isolation even more keenly, as they struggle to make the social connections which come so easily to others.

                                                        Though the Devouring brings self-actualization, a Southern Beast faces even more isolation. She lives in close, intimate quarters with those whom she loves but cannot entirely stop thinking of as prey. She may resist by declaring herself the protector of her community, refusing to feed on her neighbors and instead sating her hunger on those who have caused harm.

                                                        When Sue Ellen is raped, the hoodoo witch she goes to might very well be a Nemesis, who sustains herself for years by repeatedly punishing (an outsider might say torturing) her rapist. Other Beasts, though, thrive on the fear generated by racialized hate. Even for Beasts with little or no racial prejudice, few see anything wrong with putting on a white hood, if doing so satisfies their Horror. Yet, though Beasts often accept without question others of their own kind, some find exploiting racial hatred a bridge too far.

                                                        Such a commitment to community protection invariably comes with a price. The majority of Southerners live in small, sparsely-populated towns. This, to a Beast, means greater difficulty finding those who meet not only her Horror’s particular needs for feeding, but her personal standards as well. Eventually, the Beast misjudges her Hunger or goes too long without hunting, and her Horror compels her to feed on a loved one. Now the Beast feels ashamed, angry at herself for failing in her chosen role.

                                                        Even worse, her mistake may attract the attention of a Hero. Rounding up an angry mob to go take care of a threat isn’t difficult in the South, especially if the Hero can take advantage of racial animosity. Even if the Beast escapes, she must bear the burden of knowing she brought the wrath of the Hero down on her loved ones.

                                                        Some Beasts, then, make the painful choice to leave, to deliberately stay away from their people in order to protect them. Where can a Beast go and stay truly isolated, though? Even if a Beast moves away from her childhood home and settles in a different town or city, she still must face the risk she presents to her new community.

                                                        Sometimes a Beast gives up having a stable home in the mortal world altogether, and takes to the road. The traveling salesman or peddler might not succeed exceptionally well in the economically depressed South, but at least he has an excuse to be constantly traveling and meeting new people. As the South slowly industrializes, railroad or telegraph employees can also stay transient.

                                                        Other Beasts speak of small neighborhoods, rural towns, or desperately poor farm villages, where a Beast actually confessed what they were and still found acceptance. The name of the town changes with every telling, though. When a Beast arrives at this legendary place, they often find the story to be just a tall tale. They don’t stop hoping, though, and continually seek a place they might find true acceptance.
