    So Many Sidequests

    06-05-2015, 12:11 AM
    To be honest, what I'm going to get into here is a 2e or even World of Darkness problem in general. That said, I'm running a Vampire game, and Vampires have a few specific issues beyond what the rest of the nWoD has.

    And the issue I'm going to get into is, for lack of a better term, side-quests.

    That is, there is an awful lot to be done outside of whatever main plot you prepare for your players. There are feeding scenes (recommended at least once every session). There are Aspirations, which you, as Storyteller, are expected to work into the story whatever they are. Related to Aspirations is the political game that vampires are generally expected to be a part of. There's social maneuvering, which takes days or weeks to perform if you aren't just forcing doors every time.

    I run an online game, which admittedly tends to get less done in the same period of time, but we go up to eight hours on the weekend. Even with all that time, I just roll for all the feeding scenes. I don't feel like there's enough time for a dedicated mini-game every session. How do I make time for a project like "research this ancient artifact" without splitting the party and leaving everyone else bored while I handle one person's aspiration? How do you make time for politics? How do you get the players into the politics when they just flounder every time you try? How do you implement social maneuvering, when every other game I'm aware of (and even prior editions of the Storyteller system) encourage completing social interactions with some roleplay and a singular roll of the dice? Players generally seem to want to just get it done and move on to their main goal.

    I feel like my campaigns are just Hunter: the Vampire Edition because I can't figure out how to fit all these various systems and mechanics into a campaign. I end up just going for the same "present problem, hunt down problem, solve (usually murder) problem" setup that I would use in any other system. I'm not really satisfied with this. I want to use Vampire: the Requiem 2e to its full potential.

    Anyone have similar experiences or suggestions?

      I know it's easy to get side-tracked, that's sort of a price for these kinds of games.

      But I think part of the trick is having the character aspirations tie into the main plot. After that the politics can be, really, ignored or just happen without the players, providing they're not a part of the main plot or player aspirations. While feeding scenes are pretty important, there are times you can skip ahead and have some happen during the fade to black, 'You spend a couple weeks tracking down rumors of a dealer who accepts trade in blood, and while doing so manage to get some yourself.'

      The length of time involved with the social maneuvering system, among other things, has kept me from using it.

          I think this is a common complaint that many a GM has run into; I know I certainly have from time to time. Perhaps it's a holdover from players having a lot of their RPG experience playing games like Pathfinder and D&D, but the general format for many players is to barrel about your game world trying to murder-solve any problem they're presented with. Which is fine in some occasions but can become counter-productive if you find them wheeling out the same strategy to every problem you throw at them. Perhaps its also a symptom of the way our generation/s are perhaps a little too spoiled with immediate gratification type games and have little time for the hard work of deep role playing unless its shoved down their throats.

          In any case, you might try blocking off the possibility of a simple 'kill the problem' type approach and try layering the solution to the problem in a complex world political power players, many of whom might or might not have the influence, contacts, information, blood sorcery, that your players require in order to cobble together a solution. Prevent them from just killing a party just to obtain the plot 'point' they need to complete their quest. Make sure there are some terrible and potentially far reaching consequences if they should start pulling out guns, knives and claws. Force them to fill out their social rolls with in-character role playing before an NPC responds with anything useful. If they get frustrated, fine, let them get used to living in a world where it takes a little patience and a little hard work to get the rewards.

          Also consider having a look at their character types and aspirations. Ask yourself how someone might go about obtaining something analogous in the real world - what sacrifices or favours would they have to do get enough social credit to enable them to access their desired goals. Its all about the levers of power and who holds the keys to the kingdom. If they want something then they'll have to use their social smarts to get close to those who hold the keys to power. These people should want/need favours, debts, odd jobs, strange occult things, want the players to take part in strange rituals for no apparent reason, etc. But don't above all, let them just fight their way through any problem just get some reward - no matter much they whine out of character. In sum, embed parts of the solution in the social structure of your game and refuse and punish attempts to simply shoot a way to the prizes with nasty social consequences.

          That said, make sure your players can 'see' the way forward in any problem/quest. Often players resort to violence when they cannot see how their characters could progress or what their best course of action should be. You need to make sure that the ways forward are not too opaque and that there is somekind of trail or sign post to point them in the right direction. You don't have to hold their hands of course, but they need to be able to find a path in order to walk it. try to look at the game from a sympathetic point of view rather than as a D&D Gm trying to butcher his players with ever fiendish puzzles and traps (I'm sure you wouldn't anyway)!

              I wouldn't try to use every facet of the game, particularly ones that the players don't show much interest in. Don't include something because you feel required to, use it as a flavour when you have a good idea for it.

              The courtly politics can easily take a back seat, only really featuring when it connects to a specific plot. Likewise social manoeuvring.

              Aspirations and entirely personal plots could be played outside of regular session time, if the players prefer not to be the 'audience' for a while. This also allows the players to have their characters work in secret, if that's their preference - personally I prefer to have the players acting overtly and make sure they can keep OOC and IC knowledge separate.

              A good feeding scene every few sessions is better than a required one every session. Quoting Rose in the old forum archive: "If you're playing Vampire, and things start to slow down, ask who's hungry. Almost inevitably, someone will be running low on Vitae. Asking them how they look for food is a good chance to dig into their character's relationship with humanity, and give you a chance to complicate things."

Mages have their pet projects and experiments. Changelings seek out their fetches and try to "return home" so they're only Lost in name, not truth. Promethean is effectively a game built around Golconda seekers, so side quests are the whole point of that game.

                  Everyone has Aspirations - if its a problem in Vampire for your group, but not any other game, than its a curious thing, but I wouldn't think that's common. For vampire, aspirations tend to be political-social. In werewolf, they tend to be related to territory building or hunting, or, yes, political-socializing for above territory building. Same in mage, but with their own personal mysteries they're solving to study and grow in magic.


                  Anyone have similar experiences or suggestions?
                  Nofather actually hit it on the head, in my opinion. Tie everything into the main plot. You'll notice this a lot in movies and novels - if its mentioned at all, you can bet it somehow has relevance to the story. If there's a territory dispute subplot, feeding scenes feed into that - you spot poachers in your territory. Skim over the unnecessary parts, but do include things that are central to the story by weaving it all together.

                  Its all about making the player characters care and become invested in. Tie the political climber's needs into the main plot. The Ordo Dracul alchemist needs the heart of a diablarist... and one happens to be part of the main plot. Remember, everything that happens is a potential plot hook. Just make sure the fishing line is generally tied into the main plot's reel.

                  (Exception) There are times when the plot needs downtime, so don't tie everything in when its not dramatically appropriate.

                  That said....
                  I end up just going for the same "present problem, hunt down problem, solve (usually murder) problem" setup that I would use in any other system.
                  Perhaps this is the main problem here. If your games are turning into Vampire: the Vigil, perhaps you should work on some different main plots? This could be why you have such an issue with "side quests." Your players want to do vampire-y things, but it doesn't resonate with the plot you make? Maybe make the problem into an elder, for whom "murder-hobo" isn't the best answer.

                  Take Reap the Whirlwind, for example. The plot STARTS with you killing off the city's Prince. And now, you have to deal with the cluster!@#$ that results from the power vacuum. "Solve the problem via Murder" isn't really a solution here, not if they dont' want an entire city either trying to kill them, or pretty much killing off the entire city themselves.

                  Make it so that murder as a solution has consequences. This is the WoD, not D&D. Killing has negative ramifications - attention from police, potential hunters, friends and family seeking revenge (potentially through manipulating covenant status and allies), power vacuums, etc. Make a point of calling for that humanity loss if they're constantly killing, which can be manifested with hunting problems as people avoid the characters.

                          Yeah, but people come at games differently.

                          With some games a Storyteller has an idea about a certain situation, and wants to run the players through that plot. A problem with this is that they can often overlook the players Aspirations or personal goals. For others, the players come up with characters and they have a plan for that character, an aspiration that is something they really want the game to get into. A problem with this is if it's not fully appreciated by the storyteller, it can take things widely off track. I have a general rule, 'Do whatever you want, but if you're going to fly to Mexico, give me a lot of warning,' because I have not planned anything for Mexico, or Montana or Thailand, but it's entirely possible a character will decide they want to head off somewhere, likely someplace less drastic, based on a single line in their backstory that I overlooked. And sometimes storytellers or players just want to play, and don't have a particular direction where they want things to go, they're content letting the game open up in front of them. In this case you can have games with no-to-little direction, as even Aspirations might not be very helpful if they lean more towards mechanics, like, 'Get the fifth dot of Wits.'

                          So it really depends on who's involved, what they want, and a balancing act between it all.

                              Got a quick solution for you. Go on over to drive thru RPG and pick up Silent Legions. It's an old school Lovecraftian Horror game with a totally brilliant system for building mysteries. The short version is that you start with a resolution that ends up something like "A crime is about to be committed by the Enemy against an Actor of Friend in order to advance a Scheme. The clues are pointing towards this grim purpose." The actors, friends, schemes and enemies are all grabbed from your setting.

                              Then you generated a few investigation scenes that will lead to a that resolution, then generate an introduction scene that tells the players what direction they should head in, and finally a hook that gets them invested. Silent Legions also builds in other scenes to pitch the action up or down as needed. The great thing about this is that you can roll up the basic structure in about half an hour, and get a good 6-8 hours of solid gameplay out of it. Of course, you still have to stitch things together and know when to drop scenes or add them. But it gives a good direction without expecting the players to be 100% successful.

                                                          Some thoughts:

                                                          Tying Aspirations into your main plot is a good idea. You might also consider reducing the number of Aspirations characters have if you think your games run slower than average. You can also do Aspirations during downtime, as has been mentioned.

                                                          Changing the time scales for social maneuvering might also help you out. The defaults are meant to cover stories that unfold in steady installments over a fair length of time. I often do night-by-night stories that then have gaps of a few weeks or months in between them, and for something like that, you might want to change the scales.

                                                          Social maneuvering is also more for downtime and long-term projects than it is for stuff like sweet-talking the detective into letting you into the evidence room, which you'd still want a single roll (and maybe some Majesty) for.

                                                          Politics are a fun part of Vampire, but I don't think it's worth forcing it if they don't fit into your storylines. It sounds like you tend towards more procedural stories, so you could use politics more the way department politics play into some cop shows -- as a recurring but non-central complication in the story.

                                                          One feeding scene per session is approximate, and if your sessions are slowing down you might want to do them less often. As Craig quoted above, I find feeding useful to jumpstart action, and if your problem is that you've got too much action for your time frame, then doing it less frequently is totally fine. I don't recommend glossing feeding entirely, because it's where important parts of the horror and drama live.

                                                          As for players getting bored while other people's characters do things, you might consider handing off NPCs to players whose characters aren't in the scene. Whether you can do that with important NPCs varies with how much you're willing to let a player in on the plots, but it can work nicely. In one of my games, there was a recurring character that I ended up always handling to another player, because he actually played that character better than me.

                                                          For those of you who like Climbing the Ladder: the nWoD 2e corebook will feature a similar generator for missing persons story arcs. Think "who killed Laura Palmer?"
