                            The Sovereigns of Paris and the Score of the Spheres

                            While the groundwork for the courts was in progress when the Cycle's Bulwark was first enacted, they weren't completed for the first few performances. By the time they were, the expectation of the time was that the Married ruled the city of Paris. But mixing city politics with a ritual that demands sincerity became a problem, and one of the first in the handful of failures over the years came from the Engaged trying to perform the ritual to force a Union between Maiden and Woodsman in 1951 as changelings were dealing with the civil consequences of France going to war with Vietnam in the First Indochina War. The subsequent nine months were a disaster for the Parisian Lost as the Married and the failed Engaged tried to patch things up while the True Fae swept in and the Courts scrambled to arrange defenses, and despite the subsequent successful performance of the Bulwark, the populace declared something had to change. At this point, the Courts solidified as political entities, with each Court developing their own Sovereign who operated in a council for the maintenance of Paris's freehold. They were tasked with ensuring handling the management of the actual freehold, as well as seeing to the facilitation of the Bulwark's performance without influencing the people involved or the actual performance of it. They are to see that the Bulwark happens, but they cannot directly choose the people for it or the direction that it actually occurs in.

                            This sort of detachment from the Bulwark was a new thing for the Parisian Lost, with the Monarchs of the Seasonal Courts and the Captains of the Teeth-and-Forest Community being directly involved with the Bulwark itself, and while cycle and sacrifice did force their hands, they at least were able to steer the course of their Wyrd-granted blessings. It was clear the same couldn't be done with the Sovereigns, and yet the needs of the city often times clashed with the needs of the Bulwark. Furthermore, the facilitation created some complex issues for the Sovereigns. The Duels were quickly elevated from a way to keep the population busy and bring back some pride to a way of determining who could fill the Roles, and yet the Sovereigns couldn't force the duels into being a required thing of the populace, a thing to keep on schedule-not only would that defy the sincerity demanded from the Bulwark, but it opened a can of worms of moral and ethical dilemmas that risked turning the freehold into a dystopia. On top of that, any form of government is considered ideal when the people are content and conflict is low, but the Duels were effective at finding people suited for the Roles despite being sparked by conflict in the Freehold.

                            Sovereigns aren't idealist figures. The Courts can engineer their paragons, and the Bulwark will have its' pretty little icons. Sovereigns, on the other hand, very rarely have Mantle 5, and if they performed in the Roles, it's usually quite some time before they make the move for Sovereignty. They do strive to embody the ideals of their Courts-they have to, in order to win the trust of the courtiers- and they naturally have conflicts with one another as a result of following the differing psycho-spiritual philosophies. At the end of the day, though, the Sovereigns are more keenly aware of the practicalities of their positions, and are often more keen to work with one another in order to keep things running. The Sovereigns appoint the successors, and they look for practicality and flexibility alongside the virtues of their own Courts. A Sovereign who can't find a compromise is hardly a Sovereign at all. And so, the Sovereigns have devised a compromise with their circumstances and follow a secret creed: Contain the Conflict, Carve the Rivers of Fate.

                            Their secret to the threefold problem of minimizing conflict, filling the Roles, and directing the performance of the Bulwark is divination and manipulation, plain and simple. Enter the Choir of the Stars and the Score of the Spheres.

                            The Sovereigns employ a number of divination attempts, but they're cautious about employing people to do the divination for them given the sensitivity of what they do. Sovereigns who discover those with contracts and gifts of foresight watch and test them carefully, trying to weave them into a cast of seers loyal to the state. Those who they believe are willing to put the maintenance of Paris above all else are inducted into the secret council called the Choir of the Stars, there to sing Fate's songs, and chart them on the Score of the Spheres.

                            The Score of the Spheres is the grand achievement of the Sovereigns over the years. A potent divination tool, the Score is a star chart the size of a large round table, overlaid by infinity music scores. Using it can grant a +5 to mundane and supernatural divination attempts. Divinations received by someone using the Score or someone who has an Expression Speciality(Music Scores)* or Occult Specialty(Astrology)* can be added to the score by marking up notes that align with the stars on the map-people who used the map inherently know where to place it, while specialists must roll Intelligence+Occult OR Expression+Wyrd to divine the right place to mark it. Each divination added puts a die to the Score's pool to be used for either of it's special features beyond it's use as a divination tool.

                            Once a month, a Sovereign can use the Score to divine people with strong potential to fulfill the role and aspects about their life, using Wits+Occult+Wyrd+any amount of dice pulled from the Score's pool, with each success revealing either a person or an aspect of their life that strongly can be used to move them, such as their rivalry with the baker, their romance with their neighbor, or their passion to perform in the opera. Any Sovereign can divine as many people as they want and can afford on the day they start the first one, but must wait the month once that day is up.

                            A Sovereign can use the Score to cast a Contract on or [REDACTED]** anyone who has either been divined by the Score or added to it, anywhere, for a scene. In order to use this, a die must be spent from the Score's pool, and the Sovereign must spend a point of Willpower. Dice can be pulled from the score to augment any use of this map or to pay the cost of Glamour.

                            The Score clears itself with the completion of the Bulwark ritual, and constantly changes day to day, both in accordance to the shifting of the sky and in regards to the events of the city. The Zodiac shine prominently on it, and other constellations stars are constants, but there are also stars that never existed in the sky that appear on the Score. Divining what each star is can often mean a great deal for keeping Paris safe and prosperous.

                            With their resources, their skills, the Choir and the Score, Sovereigns find ways to gently push more prominent potentials together in such a way as to force a Duel, and more importantly push those they think should win into place. It's a delicate balance, but the Sovereigns believe that they can lead the Wyrd down the rivers they carve using the Score to push for certain results without violating the results. It's not perfect-the Score and the Choir have failed to get all the details before, and for being a clearly magical item, no one has yet quite been able to discover the drawback to it. The Sphere Score seems to be bound to the Bulwark as much as anything else, but even as useful as it is for sustaining it, the Sovereigns aren't quite wholly sure to what end it plays. It works, and it works well, and that is enough for now. They will Duel with Fate should the time come that the Score leads them to ruin. They might very well end up doing just that.

                            In the meantime, the Sovereigns run the city, using the Duels to sustain the Bulwark, resolve conflicts between parties, entertain the masses, and unite the Parisian Lost by Courting them one to another. The citizenry know that they push this system, but they live their lives unaware of just how much the Sovereigns of Paris rule all the aspects of their life.

                                            SIDEBAR: The Reach of the Score and the Grasp of the Sovereigns

                                            How far can the Score go? Pretty far. The Score is broken up into twelve sections based on the times of the Zodiac for the span of a year, and the Sovereigns can use the Score to divine and render their powers as the Score permits in terms of space. Of course, the Bulwark's resetting gets in the way quite often, but asides from that, the Score gives the Sovereigns a lot of latitude. It's spatial limits extend only as far as the freehold's territory in the Hedge goes and to the edge of Paris Metropolitan Area, but the temporal reach is neat.

                                            Still, reaching that far and keeping grip on the events is a hard affair to do. Changes in the present can completely change the context that caused a Sovereign to make the choices they did. For this reason, most Sovereigns keep their plans within the next month.

                                            SIDEBAR: The Lost Serpent

                                            What of Ophiucus, the thirteenth Zodiac? Good question. It's not present on the Sphere Score, but there are times where specialists feel like the conclusions they reach should land on a star that isn't there, and such calculations would work with the Serpent Constellation as a factor. Quite a few of the astrologers employed by the Sovereigns feel like the Score is missing, and that this is what partially causes some of the inexplicable failures of the Sphere Score. The Sovereigns don't have answers, or if they do, they're keeping tight lipped about it. Wayward who learn of the Sphere Score often look sideways at the Water Between, and wonder if Marcel took something with him to the grave.

                                            The Duels and the Courted

                                            Originally, the dueling system employed in the early days of the freehold were intended as a means of giving war-torn changelings an outlet for their addictions to the fighting, a way to sort of deal with the trauma head on, and a means of restoring grandeur to the identity of the Parisian Lost. Marcel Bellerose based the idea off of the narrative of the Court of the Sun King as much as the reality, and in fact he fabricated the idea that Louis the XIV approved of them himself(in truth, he outright banned the practice in his time-not that stopped the aristocracy or the Lost). In short, it was a balm on freehold ripped by World War II, and it was treated as such by being turned into something of a spectacle. Duels were to be publicly announced and arranged, and while resources were still being gathered in the early days, effort was made to make them as big and grand as possible within the limits of venue and finance. Participants were made into legends, conflicts were blown into grand and twisting narratives of rivalry and disgust, people were invited, food was served, etc. It was a violent affair, but it was also a well-played game to build community and spirit. And if it was bound to the Wyrd and the Bulwark, it was only in the spirit of both stories inviting improvement from conflict, and even then only in a loose sense.

                                            A lot has changed since then. The first, and most important, came when the Sovereigns solidly connected the Duels to the Bulwark as a way to refine participants into potential Roles. The decision to encourage courtiers to use the Duels to demand that others prove their exemplary nature tied into some of the other factors of the time, such as the population becoming less war-torn and more concerned with lawful justice. As a result, the Duels diversified and became less violent, thought sword and pistol retained their popularity as cinema and literature began exploring the romance of the swashbuckler. Still, while more people took the option of keeping their duels private, the spectacle of the event has become one of the hardest things to shake from the ritual. Whether a bartender's duel, a Hedge race, or a trial-by-combat, courtiers are still encouraged, by court and by populace, to make a duel at least publicly known for the benefit of community. In fact, several courtiers make a career out of dueling, earning popularity and money by putting on a weekly show-in fact, one changeling has set up a Patreon-like website dedicated to supporting "artists of conflict".

                                            Still, spectacle plays second to the use of the Duels as conflict resolution. Paris has law enforcement and due process, but the majority of the freehold's laws push for a duel to resolve most civil affairs. Grevious affairs such as conspiracy, rape, large-scale extortion schemes and things of the like are weighed on by the Sovereigns and their enforcement with grim severity, and all else is a matter for Dueling. Murder someone in secret and you'll be hunted down and made to pay dearly. Challenge someone to a sniper duel and win and the freehold officials will normally just grudgingly nod its' head. Fortunately, thanks to the rules of the duel and the Wyrd, duels to the death aren't actually that common. Most of the time, the outcome doesn't hurt anyone more than loss of pride and resources.

                                            The rules of the duel are fairly simple. The first is that any Duel must be agreed upon by way of [pledges, it's a thing with pledges]. If a [pledge] is removed at any point, the Duel is considered off. The second rule is that the Duel must reflect on the nature of the grievance and conflict. If the matter is one freeholder making another overpay for their apartment, expect the duel to involve money, housing, judgment, or other such things in the duel. The third rule is that a Freehold official must at least know of the duel, although several changelings ignore this one, and while the law enforcement of the freehold gets riled up, the Sovereigns never rush responses to such infractions. Nevertheless, an objective eye is expected to be kept to maintain a sense of fairness that the Wyrd sometimes overlooks. The fourth rule is that once the terms are agree upon, the duelists are not to interfere with the arrangement of a duel personally. And the fifth rule is that every duelist must have a second to prepare the field of conflict and to stand in the fight if needed, called the Courted(more on them in a bit).

                                            The rules also state the rights for challenger and defender. The Challenger, usually being the one with the grievance, gets to decide the form of the duel, within the agreement of the Wyrd on the second rule, and may also set the level of stakes for the conflict, though it must be equal on both sides in terms of the scale of loss. The Defender gets to decide on the location and the time of the duel. They must agree on an official to inform (if they bother with that), and agree upon the wording of the [pledge], with each side contributing (a common reason the Lost agree to the officials rule).

                                            In the early days, the seconds of duelists were expected to swear to the same terms(although admittedly, the same rules were not in place at the time), but things changed with failure of the Bulwark in 1968, running concurrently with the student riots. Faced again with the threat of True Fae and Huntsmen, a sardonic joke emerged from the community of courtiers no longer duelling, saying that if they gained anything from the freehold, it was a magnificent buddy system, as most courtiers turned to their seconds to watch their backs. Once re-established, the Sovereigns picked up the joke and turned into a new facet of the Duels. Their motives were to strengthen the community and ties as well as to create another way in which to filled the roles. With these goals in mind, they elevated the jokes of buddies and the old realities of seconds into the Courted.

                                            Strictly speaking, the Courted's role is not wholly different from seconds before hand, in the sense that they support the Duelists and are willing to stand in for them should cirucmstance demand it. But with their introduction, responsibilities of arranging the field of honor in preparation fell to them, as was, in general, just preparing the duelist in any way possible. Whether it was hiding weaponry around the field, stocking the bars, or making love to the duelist to ease their nerves, a Courted is expected to work hard enough in the favor of their duelist in preparation for the duel so as to create and strengthen the bond between them.

                                            The Courted, at the beginning of the duel, are required to do at least one act of preparation that involves a dice role, whether it an Intelligence+Brawl to give them point, Manipulation+Expression to psych them up, or any other combination that makes sense. The amount of preparation they performed before the duel gives them a bonus or penalty to this roll, up to +5 or -5. For each success the Courted gains, the duelist may use their highest Power, Finesse, and/or Resistance Attributes in place of the ones they'd normally use for the Duel. For example, if a Courted earns two successes with this roll, the Duelist could choose to use her highest Power and Finesse Attributes, or her highest Resistance and Power. The player can also stunt as though in a dream if the Courted is successful. Exceptional successes also allow the duelist to use their Wyrd as an equipment bonus to everything. Dramtatic Failure forces the duelist to use their lowest Attributes in all categories.

                                            A duel area that has one or more successful Courted attempts is still mired in reality, and yet takes on a dream like-quality, as do the duelists who benefited from their Courted. Everything is heightened, lays of physics seem to suspend just a little, and duelists seem to find just the right thing unexpectedly or pull out the right sort of twist. Duels like this always seem to improve someone in terms of their qualities for a Role, and it's no wonder the Final Duel always has this sort of effect.

                                            SIDEBAR: What About Dream Duels?
                                            Oneiromancy is a popular way to have duels-spectacle, privacy, and meaning can all be derived easily from it. So how do the Courted bless these duels?

                                            Their efforts are up to them to figure out, but the success they roll right before the Duel are added as a permanent dice pool modifier to all rolls. The Duelist just haw more potential to work with.

                                            A special exception exists if the Courted offer up their dreams for the stage. It's rare for this to happen, given the advantage, but it does on occasion work out this way. In these cases, a courted who succeeds on their roll makkes it so Environmental Actions made are always a teamwork roll, the Courted rolling the dice pool before the Changeling acts.


                                            One of the unspoken benefits to the Courted is the fact that they aren't quite bound by the [pledge] as the duelist. Courted tend to fight each other in various ways in the process of setting up and even during the duel as a way of psychological warfare. While torturing, brutally wounding, killing, or threatening any of these things or other similar actions in the middle of the duel is a quick way to earn a trip to the gallows of the guillotine, it can be heartening to look over and see your Courted wrestling the enemies ally to the ground successfully. And in the middle of this duel to prepare Duelists, Courted often display qualities of their own that incline them toward the Roles, giving the Sovereigns more people to pull into play should they need to.

                                            Motleys tend to emerge from duelists developing Courted for various kinds of events, and the freeholders of Paris are quite sure that few other cities have as many tightly knit groups. Still, not every day is a duel, and there's life beyond the Bulwark and it's maintenance.

                                                SIDEBAR: 1001 Problems but a Belle Ain't One of Them

                                                The city of Paris has a significant population of immigrants, particularly Muslims, and to say this has caused conflict in the past is a significant understatement. Paris is quite a secular city, and while the city is more open and welcoming towards American and other European travelers than expected, there's a troubled history with how they have handled people from African and Middle Eastern nations. This has built up on top of the city's significant history of ill-treatment of Eastern European and Roma people in the past, and Paris is home from everything to ill-maintained neighborhoods around Islamic mosques to outright slums housing the dislocated and lost.

                                                The Parisian freehold has less issues with race, national identity, and religion than most of their peers-Changelings have experienced a wider variety of things thanks to their Durances and are more open minded as a result. The freehold has been willing to graft in ideas and people to the positions and functions it fosters, and as newer minds come in, the boundaries that exist continue to break down. Where they aren't willing to compromise, however, is the Bulwark itself, and this is where changeling and mortal politics parallel.

                                                Immigrant changelings have always brought their freeholds with them and continued their practices as best as they can. This has always led to conflict with the Rose Courts, who have argued everything from these systems disrupting the ritual of the Bulwark to creating a deadzone that their Bulwark can't touch. The latest and most troublesome version has been the Scheherazade Freehold put in place, folding in the earlier Roma and Slavic practices into their court systems. Each side points at the failings of the other, the Scheherazade Courts point at the long gap of time the city is exposed if their Bulwark breaks, and the Rose Courts pointing out how often the opportunity for breaking happens with their own.

                                                The Scheherazade Freehold is not big enough to confront the Rose Freehold, and the Sovereigns tend to push for a "live and let live" attitude, making conflict with the Scheherazade one of the few non-grievous acts that receives a harsh penalty. Still, it is a significant enough thorn in the side of changelings that it becomes the vehicle for other issues that arise with the immigrant population, and small skirmishes happen yearly. Still, rumors in the last ten years have emerged of the Scheherazade Courts making allies with cults of the ancients ones that put the Rose Courts on edge, and there's some question of when and how the conflict of Bulwark will be dealt with in the coming years.

That and it's never a subject I want to ignore-Paris has had some really big riots over the subject in the past few years, and while I knew the reasons couldn't be the same, I wanted to ensure each game I potentially run in anticipation of the crossover game at least gives a nod to it. Plus I like the idea of an outside commentator on the somewhat rickety nature of the Rose Freehold in the vein of "The story is as much about the reading as it is the text".

                                                        But yeah, some of the stuff I've found in regards to racism and issues of religion in Paris has been chilling, and I really didn't want to ignore it even if I figured it wasn't going to be a major facet of the game.
