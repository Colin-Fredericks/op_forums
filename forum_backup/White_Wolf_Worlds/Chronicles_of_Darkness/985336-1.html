        They've also made some alterations to how damage is dealt in combat. It seems a bit more vicious. All weapons deal lethal damage now (though I think vampires downgrade lethal damage, so they'll have an even easier time slaughtering mortals), and weapon damage adds a flat bonus to your success rather than adding dice to your roll.

        That said, the new beat/experience system requires more bookkeeping but is actually pretty great because it rewards players for encouraging failures on the part of their characters.

                            Well, one of the problems with the convenient little condition boxes is that they're all at the end of the book, so when I look at disciplines, I have to be ready to jump to that section and back multiple times. I've never really felt the need for reminder papers in 1E. Not sure why, there were lots of things to take into account, but it never felt like much, honestly. Maybe it's the way my memory works, I don't know.

                            I understand the reasoning that it pushes players to get in trouble so they'll get more experience. Honestly, it's smart. That said, I find it a little artificial as means to advance plot. Players should be getting in trouble because their characters want to. It's their job to come up with characters who will be at least a little proactive and, more importantly, it's the GM's job to shape the story so that every character can find a decent motivation not to just pack up and leave (it also means setting up an engaging enough atmosphere and making the whole thing intriguing enough). It's difficult to do that consistently (and vampire never really encouraged the camaraderie that can make it easier in some other games), but every GM should aim for that, and every player should contribute a little to that goal.

                            I feel about the new experience system and the way it ties in with critical failures, tilts and conditions the same way I feel about social maneuvering. Functionally, it's smart, but I don't want my players to fail because they want more XP, I want them to fail because they made a stupid decision, or because they bit off more than they could chew or because they were really unlucky (if they do want more XP, they usually just tell me and we talk about it). The same way I don't want my players to think "ok, so I gotta open this guy's doors so I'll try forcing one and..." but "ok, I get the impression this guy thinks that way, so I should try to pretend like I agree with him for now, maybe he'll like me and spit out some information". Ok, the two social situations are pretty much the same, but one feels to me like some "roleplaying for dummies" thing. It would be a good system in a video game, but I feel an pen and paper RPG should be more organic. Physical combat needs a lot of abstraction, because it's not something anybody can replicate. Roleplaying gives us this opportunity to be very nuanced. The doors system should be the bare bones form of roleplaying, not something to follow all the time. Sometimes, "doors" don't matter, sometimes, the player will start talking and say one particular word or phrase, and that phrase alone will unexpectedly get the NPC's attention the way maybe a exceptional success would have. Maybe an elder doesn't like it when people talk to him informally, maybe the sheriff will laugh at the ventrue PC's face when they start adressing him as "sir" because he doesn't bother with formalities. It's better to let players find these things out via roleplay than letting them know they adopted the wrong tone after a failed roll.

                            Again, I think I see the point of those systems, and they do make sense. Guess they're just not for me. After all, I mostly discovered RPGs with Feng Shui, which had a super light system. Besides, the social maneuvering thingie can be omitted without losing anything basically except the fast talk debating style. Some of the other systems are a little bit more engrained into the mechanics, which demands a bit more work to ignore them, but so far, it worked.

Rather, I see them as a way to leave the characters' agency in the player's hands. 1e was chock full of powers that forced characters to take certain actions, especially in Vampire. In 2e, the majority of those forced actions have been replaced by Conditions that still allow the player agency in deciding if their character takes the action or not, but rewards them for taking it. Dominate being the notable exception, but even that gives the player a Beat as a consolation for having their agency temporarily suspended.

                                Interesting. That's... Not how I run Social Maneuvering. I use Doors and Impressions as a Storyteller tool, to help me track the progress PCs are making in socially manipulating NPCs. I don't present it in terms of "you have opened one Door, leaving the Seneshal with three more Doors and your impression is Good, so you will have to wait until tomorrow to try to open the next one or try to Force his Doors now." I describe the scenario in in-character terms, and the player describes their actions in in-character terms. The systems are tools to make it easier for the Storyteller to adjudicate those actions. And honestly? Players who are going to think in terms of mechanics instead of narrative are going to do so no matter what system you use. It's not my job to police how my players make decisions for their characters, my job is to describe the scenario, adjudicate actions, and describe the results.

                                This comes down to adjudication 101. If the reaults of an action are not uncertain, don't use dice to determine the results. If a particular word or phrase is sure to get an NPC's attention, don't even bother rolling, all that does is introduce a chance of failure where none should exist. Likewise, rolling dice when addressing the Sheriff who doesn't put stock in titles as "sir" to see how he reacts to it introduces a chance of success where none should exist. The Social Maneuvering system shouldn't come into play in these situations because you don't need any system to figure out the results of these actions.
