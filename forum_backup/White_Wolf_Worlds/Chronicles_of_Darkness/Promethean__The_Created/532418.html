    Some thoughts on the "Humanism vs Transhumanism" debate in Promethean.

    05-29-2015, 06:42 PM
    I recently read the comments on some blog updates for the Promethean 2ed and this was the first I learned that "humanism vs transhumanism" seems to have been a major, long-term debate concerning the Promethean game. This really caught my interest, as it's similarly been a big issue for my group. I actually made an account just because I was so interested in hearing different perspectives on this issue. I'll start by saying that I've always really enjoyed when the games offer multiple "alternative" truths and possible paths, rather than assuming things must be one way, but when they don't my group has never hesitated to alter things to fit our preferred narrative. I appreciate that the ability to do this is stressed in the blog posts, as well. We did, in fact, largely verge from the intended path of the series and, in doing so, I think it was one of the games we've most enjoyed playing. This fact makes me sad to see a lot of people saying Promethean is "unplayable" due to the "philosophical content", because I think it's a great concept that can easily be tweeked to fit multiple perspectives without a monumental effort.

    I never actually saw the complaint my group had with the Pilgrimage as one of a preference for transhumanism. To me, transhumanistic themes are more in-line with some Mage, Hunter and general God-Machine aspects. There are Legacies in the Mage material that certainly address transhumanism and, personally, I've always approached the Free Council as having a strong transhumanist slant among their majority. Some of the Hunter groups, like the Cheiron Group, Utopia Now or The Knights of Adrian certainly have strong transhumanist elements, but I feel like Hunter is especially thick with this philosophy. When it comes to Promethean, I would have only really even thought "transhumanism" when it came to certain technologically heavy backgrounds for the Unfleshed. Even then, though, the person created isn't and was never human.

    The fact that they weren't human and, yet, their only acceptable goal is to become so is what was unappealing to my group. Of course there are drawbacks of being a Promethean, extreme ones. The Wasteland is the one that can't really be ignored simply by removing themselves from human society, but a big focus on what's "wrong" with being a Promethean is that humans react negatively to them. The general feelings of my group were probably colored largely by the fact that we're all somewhere on the LGBT spectrum and some have alternative gender identities they've struggled with, as well as some of those same people experiencing the double edged bigotry sword of added racism, so a couple of our group members couldn't get past the idea that becoming human was just giving up what made the Promethean who they were in order to gain acceptance from their tormenters. There was no way they could get comfortable with the idea of a character that would sacrifice their inherent individuality (as the players saw it, at least) in order to appeal to the majority expectations. It would always come back as a reflection of the question in their mind, "If there was a way to undo the thing people persecute ME for, should I take that choice?". A game that in any way implied they should caused them discomfort and ruined their interest.

    However, we could recognize that this wasn't really the intended message. The Prometheans aren't facing homophobia or racism, even if the rejection they experience can pick at the insecurities of players that have experienced those things. We didn't feel the need to shun the material, because it actually had a lot of aspects that really hit home with us. The journey of these beings to overcome the external torments inflicted on them and find peace inside themselves was actually extremely appealing to us without the suggested endgame goal. Our alternative was that the endgame goal wouldn't be to become human, but to find a way to control the Divine Fire inside ourselves so that our characters wouldn't have to give up what they were, their memories, etc, and could continue on with their lives gaining acceptance for what they were and not only by sacrificing themselves to become something new.

    We focused our character Refinements on Mercurius, Stannum, Argentum, Cobalus and Plumbum. We made it a focus to study Pyros, but also the existence of other supernatural creatures, but not to see how it was lesser or more flawed than the existence of humans. We found an interesting theme to explore in our game was how supernatural entities and mortal humans were truly no better or worse than each other. "Humanity" wasn't just for humans. A werewolf, or vampire or even more "non-human" creatures could be kind, helpful beings that used their power to help others. We, as Promethean, could do that. Some mortal human Hunter archetypes claim to be doing good by slaughtering supernaturals out of hand, but their actions boiled down to bigotry through ignorance ending in murder. Some humans caused terrible pain for others without supernatural powers, while others gained supernatural powers and used them for good. Humans aren't good or bad and supernaturals aren't good or bad. Sapient beings can make a choice to live as they desire and giving up abilities that allow you to not only protect yourself, but others, from the horrors of the world (of darkness) shouldn't be an easy first choice.

    The problem for us was that it's the only clear choice presented by the books. Thus, we made a new path for our story. We first observed that the Qashmallim could effect the Disquiet and Wasteland produced by the Promethean. If they could do this fairly easily, why couldn't we eventually learn to do it? That would become the seed for us to develop a way. The end game would be acquiring the answer from an Arch Qashmallim, either through force or by convincing them that there was as much value in our path as that which most Promethean took. It assumes a lot about the Qashmallim and their own endgame that others might not like, but it worked well for us. While others may like the "human end goal" path, this ended up being a really enjoyable experience for us. Several of our group considered it to be one of their favorite campaigns to date, which is why I was excitedly checking the progress on the 2ed version.


    So, TL;DR version to help discussion:

    I was basically wondering how people feel about the idea that there are multiple "right" endgame goals for games like Promethean. It seems like a lot of people tend to take a strong right/wrong approach to the games where the purpose primarily stated in the books must be taken as golden. This results in either some refusing to buy the books if that bottom line disagrees with their view, or the other side telling those with complaints that they're wrong if they don't like it and should just find another game.

    Personally, I would like if there were more alternative paths, but I also understand that there's a word-count limit and the path they choose to focus on won't always be the one I would prefer, personally. In these cases, I've never hestitated to just go in another direction and slap together new rules to make it happen my way, if that's needed. Is that not something most people are cool with? Have many others changed things to better fit their ideal narrative, or do you always just work with what you're given or pass entirely?

      I have zero qualms with ignoring existing fluff and rules if it engages the players and makes for a good story. I could definitely see a refinement specifically based around surpassing the Promethean and Human state, especially seeing as the alchemy around which a lot of Promethean is based was supposed to be about perfecting humans in the first place.

      The fact that we're getting mortal alchemists in 2E should probably help flesh out transhuman ideas in Promethean, I think.

      EDIT: I also felt I should add, I was really drawn to Promethean for the same reasons I was drawn to Frankenstein. As a guy on the autistic spectrum, I could always relate to the Created, and I always felt that some of the Promethean state was (or could be interpreted as) sort of a metaphor for autism and other disorders. The thing about Promethean that I liked though, was that there was always hope. The Promethean could eventually learn to fit in with the world, and find a place where they could be loved and accepted. Hell, with the Athanors, they ended up having a slight edge over normal mortals. I don't think the idea behind the New Dawn is that you need to change yourself to fit in, so much as that fitting in is always going to be hard, but the rewards for toughing it out and finding a place in the world, whatever that might be, will be so worth it in the end. And even in the dark times, you don't have to be alone either. When you can't take the Torment, and everyone around you is stuck in Disquiet, you and your Throng can always rely on each other.

                      I think to me and my group, the drawbacks of the original goal aren't just that the Promethean has to give up their powers. That's sort of a similar state that Mummy ends in, with the freedom from the Judges (Apotheosis) requiring you sacrifice most of the supernatural benefits of being an Arisen. I don't know if that got as much friction as the Promethean issue, but at least it was never a problem for my group. The difference with Promethean is that it's not just about giving up supernatural abilities. It's about giving up your memories and the entirety of your being to become something entirely different than you are. The idea that you have to let go of power and ambition to reach a state of enlightenment is one thing. It's something that I think transhumanist enthusiasts would not like and I do think it's something that has good arguments on both sides. I wouldn't mind playing it, but I wouldn't mind playing paths that offer an alternative, either. That's not really the whole sacrifice of Promethean, though. The narrative basically asserts that Prometheans were created as something inherently wrong and that they won't be "right" until they give up everything (or at least very nearly everything) that they are in favor of becoming someone and something else entirely. I think that's a lot harder for some people to come to terms with than just sacrificing power.

                                      Personally, I recast the amnesia as the final obstacle that you must push through to truly achieve your goal. That is, you've physically become human; but you're not truly finished until your memories return.

                                      There's a dissonance between the fluff and the rules on this: according to the fluff, most of the Redeemed end up as amnesiacs; according to the mechanics, if your Final Azoth Roll starts at two dice, you've got even odds of getting your memories back; more than that, and it becomes increasingly certain that you will get your memories back — it's just a matter of how long it will take. So as I said above, I recast it in terms of how long will it take to get your memories back, rather than will you get them back. In fact, I house-rule the Final Azoth Roll into an Extended Action, with the goal being to accumulate enough successes to retrieve your memory — and in the meantime, any Exceptional Successes you roll gives you some sort of reward, such as immunity to Disquiet or some sort of Boost. You remain human; but the better you do, the closer you are to being the best that humanity has to offer.

They're reasoning, thinking beings with a wide range of individualized emotions and that is, by my definition, an identity in itself. The Promethean has already built a pretty complex set of beliefs and ideals by the end of their Pilgrimage. I feel that way more reasoning and self-searching is required of them to even approach near the end of the Pilgrimage than most humans probably do in their whole life, but it's not questioned whether the human developed an identity. It can be said that Promethean don't have a "true" identity, but that's not what's represented to me by their experiences by the philosophical terms I personally subscribe to when defining the terms of an "identity". I think identity is just the memories and experiences of a sapient being. They have that and they lose most of it by becoming human. I'm not saying that I don't think you're entitled to enjoy the narrative in the way you've presented it, but it's not for me, so I'm going to take another path. We're dealing with subjective philosophical concepts here, so it makes sense that people will verge on their interpretations of the material. Promethean are essentially whatever we say they are in the context of our own games, anyway. All that matters is that each group enjoys the story.

                                                      Prometheans are unstable things, in constant flux. The Pilgrimage is about achieving stability so that they can become a person, not a half-formed thing always a step away from dissolution. It isn't about humans being good or bad or supernatural things being good or bad. Ethics isn't the issue. Humanity is a huge spectrum of behavior and truths. You aren't giving up a unique identity to conform to some standard. Humanity has no standard. They are diverse and unique. Becoming human lets you become one of these diverse and unique individuals, too. What you are escaping is a half-life where the forces animating you compel you to change, to evolve or devolve, until you achieve stability or are destroyed. As I stated in another thread, Prometheans are on fire. You can't come to terms with being on fire and move on with your life. You either put the fire out or burn alive. Sacrificing your Promethean existence to be human is like a cancer patient abandoning that truth in order to be a healthy person. Very few would mourn losing their identity of "terminal cancer patient" in favor of the freedom to create a life around an identity they can choose and that won't destroy them.

                                                      Short version: The Pilgrimage is about striving to not be broken. Unless you believe that sexual identity is a matter of being broken, there's no analogy to draw.
