                        Originally posted by Okami View Post
                        The Thing) Ok, Your reading, whether its the sidebar or in general, seems to want a more nuanced take on the motivations of the Alchemists, Promethean killers or not.
                        I think that's a fair assessment of my stance. With the addendum that I understand and accept that there's not space to discuss non-Promethean-killing Alchemists (nuanced or otherwise). I don't think that means the discussion of the Promethean-killing Alchemists can't present a nuanced take on their motivations.

                        Originally posted by Okami View Post
                        A few things here, first I would argue the example NPC is a good one of someone driven by their desperation to do terrible things. She's a deeply damaged cancer survivor, and fears both re-occurrence of the disease and now other signs of death so deeply that she is now willing to torture and kill beings that aren't human to stop it. I think that's a good mix of 'your evil as all get-out, but I can understand what drove you to this'.
                        I agree, she's a fantastic example of the nuanced antagonists I believe Alchemists have the potential to be. The thing is, I don't think she's consistent with the way Alchemists are presented in the rest of the section. She's motivated by fear and desperation, not by hunger for power and hatred of the human condition. I agree with PenDragon that it reads like there are conflicting goals contributing to the writing, of trying to present Insensate Alchemists both as characters with believable motivations, and simultaneously trying to present them as irredeemable and unsympathetic monsters. I don't know if writer/developer conflict was the cause or not, but one way or another, it shows through that the text is simultaneously trying to present all Insensate Alchemists as humanity-hating madmen, and as tragic antagonists, and it doesn't come together in a coherent way for me.

                        Originally posted by Okami View Post
                        In terms of how you think it sets up a dichotomy I again took it as dichotomy only so far as the focus of this sections goes. The writing makes it clear there are

                        *
                        *bold and et. al mine

                        They just aren't discussed here. That, I think, is where you and I primarily differ. You view that discussion, the one left out, as the one that's more compelling and interesting, and want to see it, and think the book would be better if it had that instead, or in addition to whats there.
                        I don't agree that it does make that clear. It says that rational, sane Alchemists aren't discussed here because Promethean characters aren't likely to run into them. That implies that the Alchemists Prometheans are likely to run into must all be irrational and insane. That's the dichotomy, either they're reasonable people who don't hunt Prometheans, or they do hunt Prometheans and they're Vitriol-crazed fiends. It doesn't even leave room for Alchemists who are mostly rational but are driven to hunt Prometheans out of desperation (despite there being an example character who does just that), or Alchemists who haven't gone off the deep end yet, but have started rationalizing the behavior and are on their way to becoming full-blown Insensate. I don't need an in-depth discussion of potential motivations, I just don't want the text to implicitly try to shut down the possibility of nuanced Alchemist antagonists.

                        Originally posted by Okami View Post
                        I don't necessarily agree with this, and here's why, first I'll cover the 'instead' notion. I think it's important to cover villains seen as villains, so long as you caveat along the way that its not this black and white and simple. I think the book does that throughout, letting you know the somewhat simplistic picture they're painting isn't the whole thing, but it is an important and necessary part they need to cover. That leads to the natural response 'why not make the game better then and give us both? Tell us how they're villains but explore those grey areas, give that more nuanced view in addition to the villain-y picture we get wouldn't that make it better?

                        In short, I do think that's right, it would, so why don't I have a problem with it not happening and in fact seem opposed to readings calling for just that? One main reason; practicality. At the end of the day this book had a limited word count the writer's had to work with, and including more on the Alchemists meant sacrificing elsewhere. Given that this is the Core Book for the new Promethean line I think the focus needed to be kept where its at, on the Prometheans and how they see and experience their Pilgrimage and their Great Work. A more nuanced view would have been better in presentation perhaps, but would have detracted, due to word count, on the villainous perspective that the Prometheans themselves experience, and as you yourself pointed out has so many rich variations to explore it likely wouldn't have done itself justice. Hence the approach the book took and one I agree with. Keep the focus tightened down on the Promethean experience, as simplistic as that might be for these complex villains, but let the players know there's a wealth of gray for them to explore. That exploration though, we'll need to leave up to them until some other books come out perhaps.
                        I don't agree that leaving room for Promethean-killing Alchemists with more complex motivations would require more wordcount. All it would require is the text not undermining its own attempts to leave that room open. There's already a fantastic example character that shows Alchemists can be sympathetic and still be distinctly villainous. She just seems to be in direct contrast with the statements of "Insensate Alchemists all despise their humanity and are obsessed with gaining personal power." There's already a section contrasting them with Pandorans in that they can be (more or less) rational beings, suggesting that they make good tragic antagonists, and pointing out that this theme is made stronger when they don't start out wanting to kill the throng. But then the example it gives of such a "tragic antagonist" totally did start out wanting to kill the throng, he just pretended not to. There's already a sidebar explaining why there's simply not room to discuss Alchemists who aren't likely to kill Prometheans. But then it associates those alchemists with sanity and rationality, and the ones it does discuss with madness and obsession. I don't need a detailed discussion of all the possible reasons an Alchemist could have for abandoning humanity and murdering Prometheans. I just want it to leave the motivations a little room to breathe, instead of quashing the potential of characters who are both rational and antagonistic.

                        Originally posted by Okami View Post
                        Sorry for the wall of text, hope all that's clear. If you'd like to go into anything like the importance of the more simplistic villainous view as it is in the core book let me know, same if any of this is confusing or doesn't seem to add up, its a lot and I want to be clear.
                        No problem! I enjoy getting deep into these discussions. I actually agree with you that it's important to present villains in a straightforward manner at first, I just think it's equally important to leave the door open for more complex villains, and this section doesn't do that to my satisfaction.

                            Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
                            While I don't have anything meaningful to add to debate on whether or not the Alchemist section spends enough time on the "grey" areas or not. I see both sides. The practicality of what belongs in a core books vs. compelling things that would be fun to explore doesn't have a good solution.

                            I have to say though... as someone that's not neuro-typical... all the point complaining about "ableism," here have made me feel far, far, worse about my mental status than anything in Beast or Promethean 2e has printed. Sanity is not a mental health diagnosis, it's a legal ruling, and conflating them doesn't help in any way. It doesn't help writers create more nuanced games. It doesn't help larger societal issues. Sanity is an issue of being able to appreciate the consequences of your actions; which my diagnosis doesn't impact at all. It intersects of neuro-atypical issues, but they aren't the same thing. "Crazy" and "mad" are similarly not diagnostic criteria, or mental health conditions. Sane, crazy, and mad, are not in the DSM or ICD, because they have nothing to do with psychology, psychiatry, or actually helping people that have real mental health conditions.

                            I don't need defending against a game describing antagonists that have gone off the deep end in pursuit of an irrational path, with morally dubious methods, using terms that intersect with how poorly society discusses actual important issues. Nothing in the book has any relevance to things like the fact that every time I apply for a job, I need to decide if I want to opt to exercise my right to not disclose my mental health issues and possibly get the job but not have any assistance is (very simple) accommodations my employer could make to help me be a productive employee (and of course they're going to find out anyway, and living in a state where you can be fired very easily despite supposed protections "lying" isn't a great idea), or disclose and then risk not getting the job because I just gave them a subconscious reason to put me to the bottom of the pile regardless of my qualifications.
                            This is not an easy topic by any means, but it should be noted that this kind of argument isn't about, or even really for you individually. It's not even really about respecting the feelings of other people, although that plays a part. It's about how the prevailing representation of different groups in media can inform society's general attitudes towards that group. On its own Promethean's presentation of Alchemists isn't going to cause people to start conflating "mental illness" with "evil" and "sane" with "good", but it is yet another example of media reinforcing exactly that representation, which plays into a long running history of similar portrayals. This has real world consequences when it comes to any attempt to reshape how we conceive of these terms, and helps to inform the very subconsciousness biases you mention.

                            Alchemists as insane is also just boring, as reseru said in his excellent posts a few pages back. So there's that.

                                                My initial thoughts on alchemist rules:

                                                • “Alchemists do not store Pyros in their bodies the way Prometheans do, but in alchemical preparations that must be quickly mixed into other potions to activate them.” Great in theory. But in practice, storing Pyros externally leads to complications: you can set it down somewhere and walk away from them, and they can be stolen from you. Do they lose efficacy if you don't keep them on your person? And if not, what happens if you try to carry more than your Magnitude permits? Do the latest additions to your inventory suddenly lose their power?
                                                • As well, unlike a Promethean's automatic daily generation of Pyros, alchemical preparations that embody the alchemist's Pyros don't materialize out of nowhere. What do alchemists do to create them?
                                                • As for their Distillations: the fact that an alchemist's Distillations exist in the form of potions, powders, and the like implies that the effects are transferable — that is, if the alchemist hands a potion to someone else and they drink it, that person experiences the effects of the potion. As long as potions aren't extremely perishable, that's an extremely useful property.

                                                        I don't have much to say here, since I haven't read Promethean 2ED yet.

                                                        But I do want to throw my hat in as against the idea that the only Alchemists who should show up in a Promethian game are the Insensate Alchemists who want to cut you up for parts. I can think of obvious and unique milestones that you could fufill through friendly interactions with an Alchemist.
                                                        • Gold - Defeat an alchemist in a debate about humanism vs transhumanism.
                                                        • Gold - Learn why someone who has mortality would want to remove it.
                                                        • Lead - Learn why a human would see value in the Promethian condition.
                                                        • Quicksilver - Debate alchemical theory with a mortal.


                                                        Besides. A Created trying to learn about humanity from a mentor who's worldview is not humanist is something with precedent. I'm thinking of the awesome novel The Golem and the Djinn where Chava, a Golem, learns about humanity from Rabbi Meyer.

                                                        Humanism is often contrasted to religion, and Rabbi Meyer is obviously a devout man. He can also be seen as "transhumanist" in the sense that he wants to overcome human nature. Not by magically or technologically replacing parts of the human body. But by replacing human culture with the laws of God.

                                                        It's a bit of a stretch, but I don't think it's a stretch to breaking point. Especially if you subscribe to one of the varients of Humanism that includes atheism as a core tennent.

                                                        And if you haven't, read The Golem and the Djinn. It doesn't have a Wasteland or Disquiet, but aside from that it is the perfect Promethian novel.
