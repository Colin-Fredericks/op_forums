    Originally posted by Azahul View Post
    This is not an easy topic by any means, but it should be noted that this kind of argument isn't about, or even really for you individually.

    I didn't assume it was however:

    It's not even really about respecting the feelings of other people, although that plays a part.
    This is a problem. If you can't discuss things without respecting the feelings of the people you're trying to empower, it isn't going to work. Even if you're 100% correct in the need to reevaluate a passage in a text given its relation to a social ill, if you can't present that case with respect to the feelings of the people that are maligned by that social ill, the larger case is lost, because the inertia of social norms don't change based on rational logical argumentation (or we'd have progressed at lot farther and a lot faster than we have as a society).

    And again, my issue with how this has been brought up is that there's really two intersecting social issues at hand.

    First, there's the social issue of how we discuss people that do things that don't seem rational, or at least are incredibly jarring to social norms. The feeling that there has to be something wrong with someone that does something sufficiently extreme, and our extremely poor discourse as a society on how to communicate about it. This is what Promethean runs afoul of because it's talking about people that are doing extreme enough things that the text is asserting something must be wrong with them.

    Second, there's the ableism issue of how a neuro-typical society discriminates on multiple levels against the neuro-atypical members of that society; aka the real life stuff I was talking about.

    While, again, these two things intersect plenty, they're not the same. It's upsetting when they get conflated, regardless of the context, because I don't like it when people react to me differently because they're conflating them, and it feels exactly the same when people say that some of the CofD games are having ableism issues because they push a bit hard on mental instability. The conflation is frustrating, regardless of the intent. If the intersection is actually validly there, that's one thing (like the 1e Morality mechanics hitting that intersection hard by having immoral behavior directly linked to developing Derangements), but everyone seems to be on the same page here that this isn't the case for this topic.

            Originally posted by Dataweaver View Post
            My initial thoughts on alchemist rules:

            • “Alchemists do not store Pyros in their bodies the way Prometheans do, but in alchemical preparations that must be quickly mixed into other potions to activate them.” Great in theory. But in practice, storing Pyros externally leads to complications: you can set it down somewhere and walk away from them, and they can be stolen from you. Do they lose efficacy if you don't keep them on your person? And if not, what happens if you try to carry more than your Magnitude permits? Do the latest additions to your inventory suddenly lose their power?
            • As well, unlike a Promethean's automatic daily generation of Pyros, alchemical preparations that embody the alchemist's Pyros don't materialize out of nowhere. What do alchemists do to create them?
            • As for their Distillations: the fact that an alchemist's Distillations exist in the form of potions, powders, and the like implies that the effects are transferable — that is, if the alchemist hands a potion to someone else and they drink it, that person experiences the effects of the potion. As long as potions aren't extremely perishable, that's an extremely useful property.
            On the first point, it turns out that there's an official answer: try to carry too much Pyros on you, and you trigger a Firestorm. Ouch. Understandable, but ouch.

            Also on the first point, I'm thinking of saying that the Pyros-charged preparations that an alchemist makes to hold his Pyros tends to dissipate rather quickly if removed from his person. By “quickly”, I mean that it lasts for the rest of the scene; but if the materials are not in the possession of an alchemist for more than a scene, they become alchemically worthless. It doesn't have to be the same alchemist, mind you; logically, alchemists ought to be able to share Pyros by sharing the Pyros-laden reagents. I'm also thinking of adding a “Storehouse” Merit as an adjunct to Safe Place that lets you leave up to its rating of reagents without their efficacy fading or the concentration triggering a Firestorm.

            I'm also thinking that Prometheans should be able to retrieve the Pyros stored in a reagent, just as they can “retrieve” Vitriol stored in another Promethean.

            My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

                        Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
                        Not entirely. A core book has limited space, and especially for antagonists, stereotypes for the ST to quickly jump into are a good thing for the text as a game. The important thing for the Alchemist section is to not cut off the ability for more nuanced approaches (which to me is pretty clear they did not do); either by ST creativity or in future books.

                        If there's a 2e Alchemist book, or PtC 2e antagonist book with a major chapter on Alchemists, that doesn't go beyond those stereotypes? That's when everyone's interests are being poorly served.

                        As things stand now, the people who are not best served by the current text are people that want Alchemists to play a major role in their games besides being Insatiate antagonists; which is not everybody.
                        We're deep into value judgement territory at this point, so bear that in mind for the rest of this post. You may not think the Alchemist section cuts off the ability for more nuanced approaches, but the section does dedicate precious word count it could have otherwise have saved by explicitly ruling out specific types of Alchemists that, so far as I am concerned, would have made for excellent interpretations of Insatiate Alchemists. Rather than just use stereotypes as a shorthand to save on words, it uses stereotypes and then spends some of the saved word count removing options outside of those stereotypes.

                        It's not the end of the world. Like Charlaquin I'm fairly confident in my ability to write antagonists with more interesting motivations without the book's help. But rather than purely going beyond the source material it means that I'm having to ignore it. Not the best feeling.

                        Beyond that, there's the question of whether the stereotypes used are actually interesting. This is pure subjectivity, but the depiction of Insatiate alchemists as amoral, insane, power hungry monsters goes too far for me. They're pure black, which can be thematically strange in a world so full of (dark, dark) shades of grey as the Chronicles of Darkness. Those "pure evil" villains who do show up in other game lines are usually alien, inhuman creatures. So on a thematic level I find it a bit jarring, and as a concept I find it lacking. This is humanist game that is in a large part about the human condition, there is so much meat to dig into there in having a villain who is human. After all, if being a human is better than being a Promethean, and if a Promethean knows that and seeks to become human, how do they then resolve the question of whether their life, a Promethean life, is worth more than that of a human? What if it would be morally correct to allow the Alchemist to do what they want? Good writing can often create depth near as easily as make use of a stereotype, but the Alchemist section has an entire sidebar that seems to be apart reinforcing the stereotype and closing off the potential for depth and nuance.

                        And finally, there's the real life issues we've already discussed. As useful a tool as stereotypes are, it is important to understand where they come from and the kind of attitudes they entrench through their use. You may feel that "mad=evil" is harmless, just a symbol of our poor ability to discuss issues we find horrifying, but others hold stereotypes of that nature responsible for creating the unconscious biases that lead to everyday discrimination.

                                Okay, wow! I feel like it must have been weeks since this book dropped to read the back-and-forth of this thread, but's been DAYS! Some of us take this seriously... And I wholeheartedly approve! Not on to the points at hand, I have some thoughts that will – no doubt – feel positively dated in this context, but onward I go!

                                Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                Healthy Minds do not seek the Divine Fire.
                                This is the crux – and the truth – of the matter. The Divine Fire is NOT for mortals. Every demiurge who managed to create a Promethean was, to one degree or another (and sorry-but-not-really for the less-than-clinical-term) bonkers. That touch of madness is what made the process POSSIBLE, and it is part and parcel to the experience of the Divine Fire in all its forms.

                                Yeah, yeah, yeah, the terms for 'sane' and otherwise ARE all kinds of problematic, and while I won't go into that specifically in this post, please do know that I think all of you have done a brilliant job of unpacking, repacking, pack-packing and everything else around this. The point I would make is that the OP got it right when saying that this notion 'broke me'. THAT is what the Divine Fire does to a mortal, does it mind, body, and soul. The means of that damage is via alchemy.

                                Alchemy, in this case and for this game, is the exact same alchemy of the Promethean Transmutations, the slippy processes that are driven and fuelled by the Divine Fire. The same forces that cause Disquiet and Wasteland come with mortals attempting to harness the Divine fire, no matter if it be to wield the alchemical forms that come 'naturally' to the Created or to act as a demiurge. That is – in case it's not clear enough – ALL mortals who try to use this form of magic (by whatever name you wish to call it) are broken by the Divine Fire that is incompatible with a human soul. Prometheans, on the other hand, are NOT hurt because their Azothic sparks ARE as close as they come to possessing souls.

                                Alchemists – the ones we're discussing from the game – use the EXACT same Transmutations as Prometheans do, but these mortals use the various almebics and whatnot to make potions and elixirs as ‘external alchemy’ mentioned on p 117. The write-up could offer more info for clarity’s sake, sure, but it's all the same alchemy. The biggest difference, however, is that 'Pyros is not for mortals' (p 262). What's this mean? The book says they'll suffer persistent Conditions and Derangements (something that CofD does not have as a basic rule, by the way), but I would have liked to see more. The notion that a mortal imbibing or otherwise 'taking' concoctions of distilled Pyros for some benefit ought to come with some consequence, especially for THESE Insatiate monsters who take a devil-may-care attitude to what their pursuit may cause. Stains on the soul, physical changes, SOME sort of stigmata should come with such reckless use of forces that mortals are ill suited to manipulate. The fact that the mortals who DO use these methods become jaded, horrid, and less-than-sane (however one chooses to define or conceptualise that state) is what makes these alchemists different from the 'sane' ones who know better, and those changes might have been spelled out a little better in the book.

                                ...Of course, all of the above are MY thoughts, none of which are binding even in MY own games. I'm NOT saying anyone who disagrees is wrong so much as, you know, just not agreeing with me? Yeah.
