                    First of all, I get the feeling Jacob's been talking in response to something I said, because there's odd gaps and responses to things that aren't said. This is what we call a context clue, kids.

                    Secondly, there is such a thing as bad storytelling, both on the part of storytellers and players. The thing to remember is that bad storytelling need not ruin a person's or group's appreciation of the event-people have fun with bad movies, bad books, bad music all the time. The important thing to remember is that bad storytelling does not, by itself, facilitate the enjoyment, it's an impediment that people who have fun work around in spite of it. At a bare minimum, if it makes having fun hard to do for any number of reasons, it's bad storytelling.

                    Thirdly, the problem here is a misapprehension of the tool. You don't use a screwdriver to hammer nails, you don't put broccoli on pizza, and you don't use Instinctive Milestones to deny your players input, because that's not the function of them. Storytelling is a craft, and if someone insists on using a tool for storytelling the wrong way, that's bad storytelling.

                    Finally, it's important to understand what is and isn't bad storytelling because it's integral to the experience. It's enabling, it's empowering. Understanding of the craft allows storytellers and players agency in the discussion for their own enjoyment out of the game, it gives them the dialogue and awareness to actually be able to contend with problematic perspectives and assertions. When you say "There is no bad storytelling", you're telling people who are in groups that aren't providing for them to shut up and accept it. By creating the distinction and educating people in regards to it, you give people the ability to change and improve themselves, their environments, their lives.

                    "There is no bad storytelling" is an entropic concept. "There is bad storytelling" is a creative one.

                                            Originally posted by Errol216 View Post

                                            Yeah, no, if Wyrd's response was just "I don't like it, I'm not using and just gonna figure out milestones with my players," I wouldn't care. In fact, I actually assume that's gonna be the default attitude. I don't care about that.

                                            My problem is a misapprehension of the tools and their application, on two fronts.

                                            First, a failure to understand that you use a hammer for nails leading to an outright rejection of hammers is clearly and obviously making things harder on anyone involved, or at the least denying themselves something that will ease the process. The construction metaphor breaks a little apart, because I'm not about to say players can't tell a good story without Instinctive Milestones, though I think it's clearly as good a tool as Climbing the Ladder from Requiem or the Five Questions from Don't Rest Your Head-but it must be said that on that point it's all on the players, where as using it takes some of the weight off, which can certainly help the vast amount of people who are intimidated by and unsure of how to even direct a Promethean game.

                                            Second, a failure to understand that you use a hammer for nails leading to an insistence on an outright demand for the change of hammer design is clearly and obviously making things harder on everyone, both involved and not involved, and actually fucks with other people who are nowhere near the original problem, which is is the order of snafu for these kind of threads(Use your context clues). Simply put, the problem that is being harped on about doesn't actually exist. Instinctive Milestones work. If you don't like the tool and would rather use another one, that's a totally fine and fair argument, but you cannot blame it on the tool not working. The only way they don't work is when you grossly misunderstand the way the tool works, and considering that the methods of entry into this branch of the hobby all go by way of a notice of the tool box setting and endorsement to only use any mechanic as it serves your story, there's not a failure to educate and explain on the part of the makers. Simply put, it's user error. That's not a basis for a revision, to cry and demand the establishment should work in response to the opinions presented. To do so is to further demonstrate one's lack of comprehension.

                                            Worse, all of this sets the precedence for bad storytelling to happen. Good storytellers, both players and game-mastering storytellers, tend to be good readers with strong levels of comprehension and lateral interpretation, able to apprehend information in it's correct form and application as well a broadness to comprehension that allows one to shift the apprehension to different angles and perspectives. Put more simply, readers who can understand things as they are and can also bend and flex their understanding tend to be good storytellers. They know how to creatively and correctly Tetris all the information from the group and the books into, at a minimum, an enjoyable experience, carefully fitting together things together, wisely use some pieces correctly, wisely creatively using them in wrong ways, wisely not using them when it wouldn't work, and grabbing other blocks to make the full work. Bad readers, ones with poor comprehension, likewise tend to build bad stories, using poor construction techniques with the tools they have and even ignoring the tools that could best serve them, leading to a shoddy, slammed together piece that only stays in place by the strict enforcement of the maker's hands. Rigid only by force, flimsy without hands holding it up, a storyteller with bad comprehension can only poorly create something is at, the barest minimum, three walls that cover one's head, something that only barely sets a stage for the enjoyment.

                                            These are problems that need to be addressed. Hack away all you want, but know what you're doing first. You can't break the rules if you don't even know what the rules are doing first.
