            Glamourweaver's got it down pretty strongly, from my perspective. "Supernatural beings as a thematic stand-in for real-life oppression" is an inherently broken aesop, because there are legitimate reasons to be afraid of many supernatural beings.

            I mean, in all honesty, I more or less ditch the "personal horror" mojo in my ChrOD setting homebrews and go for something a little closer in feel to the WoD, as I'm an unabashed "Humanity, Fuck YOU!" supernatural fanguy. But I don't hate hunters as a concept because they have a perfectly natural and valid reason for existing.

            Vampires suck blood and can potentially drain humans dry just because they get too caught up in feeding to stop.

            Werewolves are predators with hyper-aggression problems.

            Mages, mentally, are closer to the Great Old Ones of Lovecraft than they are to humans and that's not even getting into their potential to cause huge destruction by complete accident.

            Faeries are an unholy amalgam of murder, rape, kidnapping and other crimes by their very nature, and even changelings can be so damaged from their experiences they can lash out unpredictably.

            Prometheans suffer similar bouts of hyper-aggression to werewolves and cause a literal spiritual pollution wherever they go - I don't need magical brain-whammying to object to having a leaky nuclear reactor camp out in my backyard, especially if if I know it may get pissed and try to punch my head down my neck if I politely ask it to move on before it irradiates my supply of drinking water.

            Sin-Eaters walk amongst the dead and so there's always that possibility that they'll side with the ghosts over the humans, beyond their general potential to just be a selfish dick and abuse their necromancy for their own ends... yeah, okay, this is a weak case.

            I could probably go on, but this gets my point across.

                                                            I think people have a problem with reading monster fiction as being 1-to-1 with reality, which is how you get the idea of the transgressive representation of the other in monster fiction being described as a "broken aesop." If the thing that it was doing was 1-to-1 with reality, there wouldn't be Disquiet, there'd just be fear & prejudice, and there wouldn't be a monster, there'd be <insert your favorite other here.>

                                                            If we accept the idea that a thing can represent another thing and not actually be that thing, then we're in the realm of the story. Disquiet as a mechanic is a literary device in the story of Promethean, and becoming human is not the literal, 1-to-1 transition of going from "being special or being Other" to being everybody else, but rather a moment of self-realization which allows one to come to terms with who they are and the baggage that they carry as a result, leaving that behind to live their life.

                                                            Maybe then, Disquiet going away doesn't represent some kind of literal 1-to-1 escape of prejudice, but rather can also represent how our inability to come to terms with ourselves can put us in situations where a home is no longer a home, a friend is an enemy, and we feel forced to leave things behind.

                                                            All of this stuff seems to rely on implicitly understanding that the fiction and the metaphor don't map exactly to reality and that a more substantial analysis than a surface comparison is more rewarding than just looking at the thing, comparing it to a piece of the puzzle, and then declaring it broken.
