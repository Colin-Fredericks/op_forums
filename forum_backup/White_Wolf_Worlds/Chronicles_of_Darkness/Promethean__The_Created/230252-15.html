                    And there’s some cognitive dissonance between that position and the fact that a Promethean can come to be when a natural disaster leaves a corpse in its wake which subsequently gets caught up in a Firestorm. In the case of these Extempore, there’s no act of desperation involved, because there’s no creator to act desperately. The whole premise behind the Extempore is that all you need to create a Promethean is a corpse that has been immersed in a suitable element and an infusion of Pyros; if a mortal can arrange for these conditions to be met, no desperation is needed.

                    In short, it’s not clear to me that Promethean 2e is going to present the task of the demiurge in the same way that 1e did. In fact, with demiurges confirmed as being much more of a thing in 2e than they were in 1e, it wouldn’t surprise me if “demiurgy” ends up being much easier to pull off than it was in 1e.

                    Which is not to say that elements of obsession and madness won’t be in there anymore; but I could see cause and effect being reversed between the two editions: instead of desperation being the key ingredient that enables a mortal to make a Promethean, a Disquiet-like effect gets directed at the aspiring demiurge and threatens to drive him mad if he pursues the task all the way through to completion. That is, the cause is Pyros; the effect is a mentally unbalanced demiurge — as opposed to 1e, where the cause was desperation and the effect was the spontaneous generation of the Pyros needed to animate the corpse.

                    Of course, it’s also possible that matters aren’t quite that clear: it may be that most acts of demiurgy is accompanied by a Firestorm, but that a Firestorm can sometimes be triggered by an act of desperation — so there may indeed be cases where desperation was the key that allowed the mortal to create the Promethean.

                    Remember also that we’re getting mortal alchemists as antagonists in 2e: these will almost certainly be presented as mortals who have developed techniques for acquiring and utilizing Pyros. Their antagonistic nature would fit naturally with the notion that handling Pyros is fundamentally hazardous to a mortal’s (mental) health. And if the generative act is essentially as I described above, then it would follow that most demiurges would be alchemists: it would be their alchemical aptitude, rather than desperation, that lets them perform the generative act. The act of desperation approach would primarily be for those rare demiurges who aren’t alchemists.

                    My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

                                                    On the desperation thing: I get it, and I see the attraction of the notion — to an extent. My single biggest problem with the notion is that if every single act of alchemy performed by a mortal is a one-time miracle that shall never be repeated, that greatly limits the viability of alchemists as ongoing characters. This is especially true if what mortal alchemy can do is defined by the Promethean Transmutations: in effect, you’re saying that a given mortal can use any given Transmutation once, on top of whatever other restrictions he has to contend with.

                                                    As I see it, alchemy powered by acts of desperation has its place; just not as the only thing that mortal alchemists can do, or even as the main thing they can do. That’s one reason why I was suggesting the possibility of Pyros-fueled alchemy as their mainstay, with desperation-fueled alchemy supplementing it by letting them transcend the limits of Pyros-fueled alchemy. And if desperation-fueled alchemy can only be done once, it really ought to be accomplishing something truly spectacular.
