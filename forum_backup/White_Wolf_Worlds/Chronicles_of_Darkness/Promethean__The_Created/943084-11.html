                                                    Originally posted by Geckopirateship View Post
                                                    I guess one of my main problems with "humanism" the way Promethean uses it is that I've never felt much of a connection to humanity or a feeling that it's a particularly wonderful state. The way defenders of the game describe what it's like to be a Promethean sounds like the way I feel a lot of the time. They talk about how wonderful it is that humans have stability and belonging, but somehow despite being human I never really wound up with anything like that.
                                                    I think the thing to look out for here is that Promethean is both exploring that place of being the other who doesn't have that sense of stability and belonging, and it seems to be about (which it sounds like is something you identify with) and its about finding that among people. It doesn't necessarily mean hunting city to city until you find the loving family that accepts you for who you are. It seems that Promethean is more interested in you learning about yourself and accepting who you are, the good, bad and ugly. That's the sense I get from the way the Pilgrimage is laid out. Learning about humans, acting like them and becoming one of them is more a process of self-knowledge mediated through experiencing the myriad of situations life offers.

                                                    Originally posted by Geckopirateship View Post
                                                    One of the reasons why I push the "ChroD should be about being a monster and coming to terms with it" rule so hard is because I identify much more with monsters than with heroes. I can't relate to enlightened superbeings, I can't relate to people who think humanity is just the greatest thing ever, and so on. That's why the massive focus on the Pilgrimage is such a letdown for me. Because it obliterates the reason why I loved Frankenstein so much, and why I related so strongly to the Creature.
                                                    I think Promethean's interest in humanity is as cognizant of the flaws as it is the things which are genuinely aspirational. Perhaps a better way to think of Promethean is to not understand it as a game about escaping monstrousness, or if you want to change it, into a game about accepting the fact that you're a monster. It's a game about accepting and coming to terms with humanity, and oneself. So while the metaphor of the monstrousness is present, it's moreso about personality identity than the state of being a monster.

                                                    It's not about becoming an enlightened superhero or even some platonic ideal of a person. It's about self-knowledge in the context of humanity, and that's reflected by the transformation from Promethean to mortal.

                                                    There's a particular thing you're doing here that I think happens a lot with fiction and monstrousness, others and self-knowledge. I'll try to think about how to articulate it, but I think the short of it is that there's baggage you're bringing to the language of the book that makes it harder to parse and consequently harder to enjoy.

                                                    I think it's a thing we all do, especially with the sorts of large metaphors and thematic spaces the Chronicles of Darkness explore. I don't know if it's possible to approach these concepts without the baggage we bring, so maybe a better way to do it might be to give it some time and come back to it? Obviously that's your prerogative, I just think it might be worth thinking about what biases you're bringing to the text, and considering how to work with/around those.

                                                    Like, as a queer person of color, there was a way I reacted to Beast that I think was really unfounded upon thinking about it, and I think a lot of people did similar, and because it touches a lot of sensitive buttons on the baggage we carry, it made it a bit harder to appreciate. I still see some things that I think are weaknesses, but looking at it again I think there are a lot of problems with the way people engage with Beast.

                                                    I think the same thing happens with some of the words used to describe Alchemists, where there's a knee-jerk reaction that makes it harder to appreciate, and I think it's worth considering that if one considers this a "failure" then it might be one that both we as readers and the text can both take responsibility for. Incidentally, this also goes for Beast since I mentioned it!

                                                        I'm thinking about something: a person earlier in the thread described the whole journey and theme of Promethean The Created as ultimately - orthogonal to the matter of humanism vs transhumanism - being easily read as the desire to surpass the circumstances of your birth.

                                                        Which strikes a cord, because when you think about it, transhumanism and by inclusion the want to improve ourselves and make our lives better, are very human things. But just because we define the first humanocentrically, doesn't mean we're the only intelligent beings the idea ought to resonate with. If the book was written with that interpretation in mind, we would likely have a different (less divisive) focus which wouldn't see a need to single out some Alchemists as obvious villains. In the end, I see many of them looking for the same thing as Prometheans; the latter have reason to idolize and pursue mankind, while the former have reasons and drive to surpass it as it is. But, as already commented on, it's very much a "the grass is greener on the other side of the fence" thing, isn't it?

                                                        Too bad the most reliable way for Alchemists to chase that goal is to grind up Prometheans. Which should be enough to promote conflict! There doesn't really need to be a fundamental clash of philosophy between them or an integral difference in the inherent value of what they respectively seek. The differing perspectives and incompatible priorities in what they're willing to do to achieve their goals ought to suffice to establish the Alchemists as complex but clear antagonists for the titular characters.

                                                        (Bear in mind, I'm sharing these thoughts having not read the book, which is why I haven't added anything to the discussion of what what the definition of antagonistic alchemists implies about people with mental illness; the criticisms that the wording is insensitive or insulting seem valid to me, though.)
