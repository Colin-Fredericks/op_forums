    This is part of my issue.     I'd be hesitant to call this kind of reading "subjective". It's correct, because I make no claim to my own objectivity, but the term is used in arguments like this in a way that seems to ignore the innate subjectivity of the written word. All language is metaphor.

    Now, as to how I got to this reading, the text is quite clear that there are two types of Alchemist, and that the division between them is sharp. It goes so far as to split the Alchemist population down the middle and assign one half its own label. These two groups are defined against one another. One group are explicitly called out as greedy, ambitious, mad, and hunt and kill Prometheans. The other group is sane, seeks knowledge, "knows better" than their insatiate brethren.

    Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
    Well... there is only one "kind" of common antagonistic Alchemist. They're just a lot more varied than I think they're being credited as being. Things like, "mad," "obsessive," "ambitions," "insane," "power seeking," and so on, can express themselves in nearly countless ways. So "one kind," doesn't translate into a lack of spectrum of characters.

    But that's not why Alchemists hunt Prometheans. As I already covered, Prometheans are a source of materials. That's it. What the Alchemists do with Vitriol has a large variety of reasons, which is separate from the quotes you're focusing on for the most part. It feel like this is a regular sticking point. The text shuts down getting Vitriol outside of torturing a Promethean to death, which feels like it's getting conflated with saying there's only one reason to want Vitriol... which isn't present in what's getting cited.

    Where does the text actually say that, "I want Vitriol to cure my kid's cancer," "I want Vitriol to live for ever," and "I want Vitriol to transcend the limitations of my human mind," aren't all different and valid reasons for an Alchemist to go down the Insatiate path?
    Honestly, I think this is where your own subjectivity is playing a part. I don't understand the distinction you're drawing between an Alchemist's desire for Vitriol and an Alchemist's desire to hunt Prometheans. You are describing a very specific attitude on the Alchemist's part, one wherein they see Prometheans as a resource to be mined. I don't think there's enough infrastructure in place for an individual to easily create that divide in their mind. If Vitriol could be bought at a supermarket then I could understand that mentality. When I want to buy a steak that doesn't mean I want to go out and slaughter a cow. But when you're as close to the source as an Alchemist almost necessarily is, those lines blur. The act of hunting is the task that gets you what you want. As you say, there's no alternative. The text assumes that you have to be insane to undertake that task, but for an end this valuable you could justify the means in so many ways. And to an extent I disagree that we're conflating the one mean of getting Vitriol with the one reason to want Vitriol. The presentation of those Alchemists willing to obtain it

    Now, I'll concede that there's variation in the expression of "mad", "obsessive", and "ambitious". But that's almost beside the point. The use of those adjectives restrict us from a wider pool to a smaller pool. Whether that pool is wide from an objective standpoint is besides the point. There is potential for violent, antagonistic Alchemists that fall outside rather mundane frame presented by the text. A far more interesting, relatable kind of antagonistic than whatever spin on the madman offered by the book.

    In real life humans kill for stupid, ridiculous reasons. Some of us maim and harm those closest to us for reasons that might be abhorrent, but don't necessitate that the perpetrator be insane. The book doesn't seem to understand that. It presents us with non-human protagonists who are hated by the world to begin with and seems to think only the insane would try to kill them for the vast reward they promise. It's almost cute, now that I think about it, adorably naive to the world at large.
