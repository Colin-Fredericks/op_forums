                Warning: I've only skimmed the thread, so much of what I'm about to say may already have been addressed. With that in mind:

                My initial impression of the Alchemists section is that it's not saying “all, or even most, alchemists are power-hungry horrors”; it's saying “most alchemists that Prometheans are likely to encounter are power-hungry horrors”. That's why it coins the term “Insatiate” (i.e., one with a hunger that cannot be satisfied).

                That said, it occurs to me that there's a perfectly viable role for non-Insatiate alchemists in a Promethean chronicle; and it's one that was touched on way back in Promethean 1e's Water of Life story: the alchemist as mentor. Similar in some ways to the role that Elpidos are designed to fill, but with a much more human face and less of a deus ex machina aspect to it.

                Granted, they're playing with fire; but by definition, the non-Insatiate alchemists do so with due caution. And unless they're also Redeemed who chose to retain their memories, they're probably well-meaning bumblers who know about as much about the Pilgrimage as an Extempore does, and are likely to be as much a hindrance as a help. But these are plot fodder — reasons to incorporate them into a chronicle rather than to dismiss them.

                                    Luckily, the Alchemist section doesn't seem to be so bad as to make me outright uncomfortable the same way Beast did.                                     So I thought about this a lot last night at work. To summarize:
                                    • I agree that Alchemists should be presented as primarily an antagonist, but their portrayal as unsympathetic villains does the game a massive injustice.

                                    Good villains are sympathetic.* It inherently makes them complex which means, in a sense, they get more word-count, but that's a weird aside. Sympathetic villains make us want to play them so we can explore their perspective.

                                    Seers are no bullshit evil because they sold their souls to cosmic symbols of tyrannical oppression, but they're Mages just like us. We're one step away from that. Centimanni are monsters except they've given up hope on the Pilgrimage which, hey, is really tough. They're disillusioned and bitter, but we're just like them. We're one step away from that.

                                    Alchemists are in a weird spot. Unsympathetic villains do have their place but 1) irredeemable mortals in the CofD are pretty non-existant and 2) Pandorans cover this niche anyway because they eat your face. Alchemists are human, you know, the thing every Promethean wants to be. They deserve nuance, they deserve moral greyness. They should show that the human world Prometheans desperately want to join has a wide spectrum of morality. Their motives are such a so-good-how-did-I-not-think-of-them-first that it's shocking they're written like a brick.

                                    How is it that Alchemists written as being crazed by lust for the Divine Fire is okay but it's not okay for Beast's Heroes to be driven to murder by a Beast's Astral rampages?

                                                    Okami: So, after reading your post, I went and did the same. Read through the Alchemist section, skimming a bit at parts, but trying to develop my understanding within the context of their presentation in the book. Here are my own thoughts.

                                                    On the whole, I really like the section. But I do wish the presentation of Insensate Alchemists' motivations was a bit more nuanced. There's a lot of focus on the hatred of the human condition, but precious little dedicated to the fear that drives that self-hate. Or the ambition not just to abandon humanity but to improve upon it. I am down with Promethean's humanistic themes and understand why it would want to portray such inherently transhumanist characters in a negative light, but I feel the message would be considerably more powerful if the deeply human drives that can turn people towards Insensate Alchemy were made clearer.

                                                    The Alchemists and Their Prey section almost does what I'm looking for when it talks about Alchemists as tragic antagonists and points out that the theme is made stronger if the Alchemist doesn't start out wanting to kill the throng. But then instead of exploring the possibility of an Alchemist villain who goes from sympathetic to Insensate over the course of play, it presents an example of an Alchemist who wanted to kill the throng all along, but pretended not to at first to earn their trust. That's not a tragic antagonist, that's a one-dimensional antagonist pretending to be sympathetic as part of his nefarious scheme.

                                                    The other part I didn't care for was the Great Work sidebar. I think it comes from a good place. I understand and appreciate the attempt to explain that while not all Alchemists murder Prometheans for Pyros or Vitriol, the ones that are most likely to play an important role in a Promethean game do, so that's what this section is going to focus on. The core idea behind the sidebar is great. My beef with it (complaints about prepetuating the stigmatization of mental illness aside) is that it sets up a dichotomy between cautious, rational alchemists who don't kill Prometheans and therefore aren't covered in this section, and power-hungry, Vitriol-crazed alchemists who do kill Prometheans. It doesn't leave room for otherwise rational, cautious alchemists who are driven to kill a Promethean out of desperate circumstances, or those just starting down the slippery slope towards becoming Insensate, nor for corrupt extreme-transhumanists who would have no compunctions about killing Prometheans but lack the opportunity, or the means, or those who's fear of the power Prometheans wield just barely outweighs their hatred of the human condition. The classification of Alchemists who kill Prometheans as Insensate and the attempt to explain why only the Insensate are covered in this book is appreciated, but the lack of nuance in the analsys of what constitutes an Insensate Alchemist is disappointing.
