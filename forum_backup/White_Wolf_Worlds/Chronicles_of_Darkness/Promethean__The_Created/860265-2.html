                                            Completely off-topic at this point. So feel free to TL;DR.

                                            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                            My problem is a misapprehension of the tools and their application, on two fronts.

                                            First, a failure to understand that you use a hammer for nails leading to an outright rejection of hammers is clearly and obviously making things harder on anyone involved, or at the least denying themselves something that will ease the process. The construction metaphor breaks a little apart, because I'm not about to say players can't tell a good story without Instinctive Milestones, though I think it's clearly as good a tool as Climbing the Ladder from Requiem or the Five Questions from Don't Rest Your Head-but it must be said that on that point it's all on the players, where as using it takes some of the weight off, which can certainly help the vast amount of people who are intimidated by and unsure of how to even direct a Promethean game.
                                            Actually this is just a bad metaphor. There are many types of hammers many of which are not intended for use on nails at all. Persistent employment of it is at best a good example of slipshod writing and at worst an indication that someone lacks a modicum of imagination.

                                            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                            Second, a failure to understand that you use a hammer for nails leading to an insistence on an outright demand for the change of hammer design is clearly and obviously making things harder on everyone, both involved and not involved, and actually fucks with other people who are nowhere near the original problem, which is is the order of snafu for these kind of threads(Use your context clues). Simply put, the problem that is being harped on about doesn't actually exist. Instinctive Milestones work. If you don't like the tool and would rather use another one, that's a totally fine and fair argument, but you cannot blame it on the tool not working. The only way they don't work is when you grossly misunderstand the way the tool works, and considering that the methods of entry into this branch of the hobby all go by way of a notice of the tool box setting and endorsement to only use any mechanic as it serves your story, there's not a failure to educate and explain on the part of the makers. Simply put, it's user error. That's not a basis for a revision, to cry and demand the establishment should work in response to the opinions presented. To do so is to further demonstrate one's lack of comprehension.
                                            The main problem is that you're making a great number of assumptions with regards to the "tool." In this case the "tool" is comprised of some string of text. Unfortunately text is not a high-precision tool. It's not as simple as using context clues and exercising comprehension to apprehend some purpose. I think that perhaps you're assuming that writers and readers share the kind of relationship that storytellers and players do but this isn't the case at all. Writing is performative process of encoding intentions. Reading is a performative process of interpreting intentions. Unfortunately the semantics of any given string of text (or more properly the semantics of any given sentence, or paragraph if you prefer, that is referred to by a string of text) is ambiguous. The majority of human beings take this for granted, however, interpretation and thereby comprehension is a highly idiosyncratic process unique to every individual in part based on their background experiences and knowledge. To expect that all people will arrive at precisely the same understanding from some given text is to expect too much. Language is a very tricky animal. It isn't as reliable as you think it is. For example, consider the following sentence: "Flying planes can be dangerous."

                                            A better metaphor for the "tool(s)" in this case is to liken the situation to a reference interview. The "Five Questions" play the role of the interviewer's questions and the shape of the Instinctive Milestones play the role of the interviewer's response to the answers given to those questions. With this metaphor it's much easier to see that the "tool" in question is actually two tools -- telling and listening (which in various circumstances we might reduce to a singular tool called collaboration). Successful use of these tool requires skill on both the part of the storyteller and the parts of the players. The storyteller needs to be good at listening and the players need to be good at telling.

                                            The fact is that storytellers and players are not innately "good" at these things. Listening and telling are skills that take time to develop and the precise nature of "good" falls across a rather broad continuum. A storyteller who is excellent with one group of players might be terrible with another group. The same thing is true of players. The important thing to acknowledge is that the situation isn't as simple as X is bad storytelling and Y is good storytelling. In some circumstances X may be good and Y may be bad.

                                            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                            Worse, all of this sets the precedence for bad storytelling to happen.
                                            I'm sorry this isn't a court of law or a tribunal. No precedents (for bad storytelling or otherwise) are being set here. Mainly you're arguing for the sake of arguing. And at this point, so am I.

                                            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                            Good storytellers, both players and game-mastering storytellers, tend to be good readers with strong levels of comprehension and lateral interpretation, able to apprehend information in it's correct form and application as well a broadness to comprehension that allows one to shift the apprehension to different angles and perspectives. Put more simply, readers who can understand things as they are and can also bend and flex their understanding tend to be good storytellers. They know how to creatively and correctly Tetris all the information from the group and the books into, at a minimum, an enjoyable experience, carefully fitting together things together, wisely use some pieces correctly, wisely creatively using them in wrong ways, wisely not using them when it wouldn't work, and grabbing other blocks to make the full work. Bad readers, ones with poor comprehension, likewise tend to build bad stories, using poor construction techniques with the tools they have and even ignoring the tools that could best serve them, leading to a shoddy, slammed together piece that only stays in place by the strict enforcement of the maker's hands. Rigid only by force, flimsy without hands holding it up, a storyteller with bad comprehension can only poorly create something is at, the barest minimum, three walls that cover one's head, something that only barely sets a stage for the enjoyment.
                                            Sorry it takes more than reading comprehension and knowledge of tools to tell good stories. One must also know one's audience. Frequently knowledge of audience is much more important.

                                            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                            These are problems that need to be addressed. Hack away all you want, but know what you're doing first. You can't break the rules if you don't even know what the rules are doing first.
                                            The thing is we can (and do, as evidenced by these "discussions") quibble about what the rules are doing.

                                            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                            PS: By the way, speaking of miserable reading comprehension, it seems that, in an effort to illuminate the wisdom of "There is no bad storytelling" on me and others, the person trying to do this seems to have missed the note where I basically said I can't read a damn thing they're saying because I ignored them a long time ago. So, you know, I'd figured I'd be nice and blunt about it.
                                            Who says I'm counting on you reading this? (And obviously, you must be reading something or you wouldn't have bothered with such an oblique personal attack.) It does always amaze me how personal you make your stake in these arguments. I'm sorry if you're offended by my not taking an orthodox approach to the text but the nature of semantics permits me this leeway (plus as a storyteller I know my audience, i.e., my players, way better than you do). If people aren't interpreting what you've written the way you intended then you might consider if the problem is not with the reader but in fact with the writer. Grammar rules might be normative but I'm afraid that things like reading comprehension are idiosyncratic.

                                            Closing thoughts, you might try reading a little Frege, Grice, Russel, Chomsky, or Smith (on Chomsky) sometime. Reading comprehension doesn't actually work the way you think it does. After all, what did "Flying planes can be dangerous," mean again?
