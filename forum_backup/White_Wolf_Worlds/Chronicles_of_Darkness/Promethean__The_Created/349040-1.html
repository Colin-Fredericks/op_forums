            Originally posted by Michael View Post
            I don't like it. It makes cosmic forces seem far too human.
            I love it for this exact reason.

            I don't care much for cosmic horror in and of itself- the idea that I should be cowed by an infinite and uncaring world has always struck hollow for me. So having cosmic forces demonstrate themselves as being prone to bouts of humanity not only makes them more erratic and fearful, it just avoids a trite trope altogether.

            Two of my favorite examples of this trend in nWoD are the Idigam and the Shan'iatu. These guys should be poster children for cosmic horror, but no, they get personal, and show all the signs of their circumstances in very human terms, and they are more frightening for their warped intimacy than their alien distance.

                    It's not about cosmic horror it's about the sublime. The idea that humans are ultimately just part of a far larger universe, and it doesn't revolve around us.                             ...And?

                            I haven't caught precisely what you're getting at, but I fail to see how that's lessened because Pyros is directly linked to human behaviors. Discovering that stuff is alive in some strange sense doesn't lessen the mystery, or the fear; to invoke a different media, it's like lyrium in Dragon Age; already the stuff was one of the most dangerous and intriguing materials in the setting, it both driving people batty and being the ultimate source of magic. In Inquisition, we incidentally discover that it's actually alive when we realize how the more dangerous red lyrium is formed-namely, that it's ghoul lyrium, and nonliving things can't become ghouls. This actually makes lyrium more mysterious, because apart from that, we know nothing about the stuff. We can assume the stuff grows given how aggressively red lyrium will infect and colonize everything if given half a chance, but apart from that? Nothing. Could be our equivalent to something Lovecraftian, could be something entirely incidental.

                            Pyros is much the same way. It lives and thinks, but we humans are not privy to its mind or its functions. It could love us, because we feed it...but the existence of Extempores implies that it doesn't actually need us. It could also be that "thinking" isn't a great description of what it does, it just acts like a thinking thing because we don't understand its nature well enough (see; nature, every just so story, ever). It's just as likely that it's not so much humans it supports to much as sapience; again, sapience feeds it. To put it bluntly, nobody really knows, and ignorance is only bliss in that you can't see the thing about to kill you.

                            (That, and given such a fundamentally humanistic game as Promethean, cosmicism seems rather counterproductive to all the other themes. There might be cosmic horror elements, but making that a focus seems...self-defeating).

                                        Originally posted by Dave Brookshaw View Post
                                        One of the things Wood Ingham did in his qashmallim section of Pandora's Book, which I read about twenty times before writing them up for 2E, was point out that there's really no such thing as "Elpidos" or "Lilithim". Or for that matter Elpis or Flux - it's all just Pyros. The choirs and orders were made up by alchemists and Prometheans to try to classify what they were seeing - no qashmal ever calls itself "a greater Lilithim" or whathaveyou. Or, for that matter, no qashmal ever calls itself a "qashmal" - if you ask one what it is, it'll just say "A Messenger" or "an Agent."
                                        I really like how this invites questions about the qashmallim's relationship with the God-Machine's angels and the Unchained.

                                        On a side note, why do the books always italicize qashmal and qashmllim? Is it because they're words from another language?
