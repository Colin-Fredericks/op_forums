    Cipher Writing and my Bleeding Headache

    10-11-2015, 02:20 AM
    Okay so I am currently running my first game of DtD and I have got to say this book gives you more than plenty of homework to do. Between designing Infrastructure with very little in the way of guidelines, designing a cipher with very little in the way of guidelines, and trying to keep up with my players it is becoming a total headache. I can't seem to understand any of it. Are Interlocks combinations of Concept or mechanics? I don't want to buy a whole new book in order to understand these essential but somewhat unclear sections. I also do not want to give up on running the single most interesting venue for World of Darkness I have found.

    But holy heck this is becoming such a headache. Like I have to design 3 fully original powers for each of 7 players in a sandbox game each who wants to do something unique.

    I could really use some help here from someone who understands the whole thing better.

      Hmm. Let's see how I can explain it.

      Infrastructure and how to use it

      Infrastructure has a lot of vocabulary that makes it sound more specific and detailed than it is. I tend to like the God-Machine Chronicle book's explanation of projects and infrastructure better than the Demon corebook's, because it does a good job of laying it out with clarity and transparency as to what means what and why, but you don't necessarily need that book. Infrastructure is just a way of thinking about the God-Machine's operations in a way that's easier to manage than just the abstract concept of a world conspiracy. It asks you to think of those operations as several projects, each of which is just "a weird, cool or threatening thing to challenge your ring of demons with," in the form of "a thing the God-Machine's forces are trying to do, and the people and stuff that are being used to do it."

      The classifications of concealment, defense, command and control, etc. are not important and are just described to give some examples of what the God-Machine might need some projects to do. You never actually have to decide whether you'd call a given project "concealment" or "defense" or "elimination," and in-world there's no fundamental difference between them. They're just descriptive categories to sort things with.

      What does matter is the infrastructure, the tell, the linchpin, and the output.

      The project's infrastructure is just whatever the God-Machine is using to do it. This can be anything you want that makes sense. A factory with a hidden room staffed by doppelgangers of the normal employees, a single hunter angel and a bunch of stone monoliths across the city that it can track prey by, an old boys' club of machine cultists with money. What matters is this is the step where you take "abstract conspiracy threat" and give it a face you can look at, talk to, or punch in, so that the players have a tangible means of targeting and dealing with the enemy.

      The tell is how the players notice the God-Machine doing things. It signals them to get involved and helps them know where to look. I don't think the book really refers to this by any given name, but God-Machine infrastructure consistently manifests in some outwardly weird fashion that stands out. A project operating out of a convenience store might stock contemporary magazines in dead languages. That factory might have weirdly specific work shifts, and the doppelgangers might occasionally pass by in the halls. Concealment infrastructure is assumed to be in place as a handwave as to why people aren't constantly noticing these indicators of something wrong: there's some project around psychically preventing them from noticing. Demons and stigmatics are immune. They don't know a project instantly when they see it, but there are these visual triggers that function to say Plot This Way →.

      The linchpin is there to remind you that no project is invincible. Sometimes it's obvious what the ring could do to shut down a project, like killing the hunter angel or shutting down the cult. If you come up with a neat, weird project that's harder to know how to interact with, like sigils written into the city's traffic patterns that prevent people from accessing a facility in folded space, the linchpin asks you to think of a failure point the ring can find out about and target, like some extra machinery installed on the computers running the city's traffic lights. Break in, identify the devices and remove them, and you've disrupted the project. Linchpins are also good for projects plugged into human society, like if the God-Machine is controlling the local Department of Motor Vehicles or something: you could have a physical linchpin somewhere in a related building that's essential to maintaining control, if you want to avoid giving your players a no-win choice of either letting God do its thing or slaughtering a bunch of innocents who don't know what it is they're actually working for.

      The output just sets the stakes. The matrix used to invoke it sets your deadline: when the God-Machine achieves Arcane Thing A, it's too late and Arcane Consequence B results. If the ring loses, now they have to deal with the output. If the ring wins, the output quantifies their achievement: it lets them name a specific bad thing they've prevented from happening.

      It's just a flowchart for designing game constructs. Everything else is window dressing and aesthetics. For those, urban legends and conspiracy theories are good inspiration to draw on. Mothman phenomena, alligators in the sewers, electronic voice phenomena, chemtrails, all that stuff resonates with Demon nicely.

          I waited until the end of my first story before I I started writing my players interlocks. And then let them roll before the first game of the 2nd story. I did this because I found it easier to write based on how the players and their characters developed, how they reacted to certain stimuli and what their aspirations shape out to be. At least the general theme among them.

          Mr still A medical doctor who as an angel ravaged the world with disease wanted to create bacteria more ferocious than he could as an angel. He was given interlocks that allowed him to adapt to his surroundings and become something the god machine couldn't reject again. I love a well played integrator.

          Mr blaze sought to bring my down the machine that enslaved him. His sometimes reckless approach caused him to forge a path of deep mistrust and paranoia. Even fit a demon he'd never allow himself to be caught unaware. And so his interlocks gave him the eventual ability to connect threads of aether to their source. He could discern the way infrastructure was connected to One another by following the aether and essence trails.

          So then. Since the interlocks are so unique then build them as a way to assist the players on their personal journeys to hell. The interlocks are made to over come the same obstacles the demons create for themselves by chasing their goals.

          Now I follow a personal pattern for interlocks so this is conjecture, at least everything beyond them being a personal fusion between two embeds that make a progression.
          My first power is always something that's mild. The character can activate it as an instant action with out a dice roll. It's always a very specific circumstance.
          The 2nd interlock is a little more open, requires a roll.
          My third interlock will require a roll and have a cost of either willpower or aether. And should be nearly always useful whenever the character is pursuing a goal they normally would by their own volition.

          The cypher. Basically gives you enough rope to hand yourself with. A key phrase that will push you towards. Something. But also makes that a something left to the character's interpretation. The great part is is that when done right the player will go off in their own direction with it and all you have to do is roll with how're they're tackling it.

              I'm very glad I broke my reply up into two posts because I was three paragraphs into Interlocks when Firefox crashed and scrapped everything I'd written. Rewriting what I had before is annoying, but I would have been super pissed if I lost all that infrastructure stuff with it too.

              Designing the Cipher

              I won't lie: this one's going to be more work for you, especially with the size of your group. Seven players is a lot to wrangle at once; I think the three-powers-per-player convention was written more with groups of around five players in mind.

              First tip: you don't need to have the entire Cipher written out in advance. You can, but everyone has their own way of approaching it. The way my ST does it is to just keep notes on the broad shape of each character's Cipher: which four Embeds are going to be their Keys, a broad idea of their final secret, maybe some ideas for how the Keys connect, but no Interlocks written. Then, when a player locks in a Key in play, he tries to find time in the week before the next session to sit down and write that player's new Interlock. You're not going to have people jumping two Keys ahead in one session.

              Second tip: take to heart the passages in the book encouraging you not to sweat the details. Writing a Demon power is an art, not a science, and you have an advantage because you are just working with a group of seven friends. The powers in the book are written with a more careful eye because they're written for the consumption of an audience reading a hardcover book, an audience large enough that the writer has neither the time nor the opportunity to sit down with them all, share his intents and ideas behind each power, and work out any problems they might have using it. Once the book is printed, the words are etched in stone. Yours aren't. If you write a power, it gets used in play, and you or your players decide you don't like it for whatever reason, you can go back and change it. If a question comes up in play about how the power interacts with a situation you didn't write rules for or think of, you can make up something on the spot. If you have to do that multiple times, and what you make up one time doesn't match what you make up the next, again, if your players are having fun and unbothered, you don't have a problem. Just keep your players engaged and have a dialogue with them to make sure everybody has fun. That's something you should do in any game.

              Figuring out where to start

              Conceptually, there are several ways to approach sketching out the Cipher. It's down to personal preference. You could pick out a sequence of Keys, looking for Embeds your player is likely to want to use or find helpful, and then backfit Interlocks that seem like they bridge each pair of Keys, and write out a final secret that seems like it kind of reflects the Keys that led into it. You could start with the final secret, writing out a little pithy koan that speaks to the character arc you anticipate for the story of a given player character, and then try to draw a conceptual line of three Keys between the First Key and the final secret, and then fill in the Interlocks between those. You could take it sequentially and improvise each step as it comes, looking at the First Key, coming up with a cool Interlock power you think your player would like that relates to it, and then picking a second Key that also relates to that Interlock, repeating until you decide on a final secret that seems like it flows out of what you wrote before. None of these are bad approaches. I personally tend to start with the final secret, fill in the Key blanks, and then write Interlocks into the spaces between those, but that's because I tend to do a lot of stuff in a single sit-down burst, so I pick an approach where I'm writing the whole thing at once.

              For an individual Interlock, look at the two Keys between which it's situated (or one Key, if you're writing in that order), and think about the ideas they play with. Play word association with them; think about stuff that sounds kinda similar. You don't need an Interlock to do something the same or similar to either of its Keys, as long as it sounds like there's some kind of connection there. A Key about knowing how to use an object can lead into an Interlock about knowing how to manipulate a person, especially if its other Key is about interacting with people. A Key about making people forget each other could lead into an Interlock about keeping them physically separated, or preventing one from affecting the other. A Key about physical healing could lead into a Key about mental or emotional healing, or repairing an object, or even reversing time.

              Mechanically, start with an Embed. Look at the list of Embeds in the book and copy their format. Description, dice pool, roll results. You can use them as examples of roughly what Demon powers do in general; Interlocks at their base are comparable to being like unique custom Embeds. You can break the rules with Interlocks in a way you don't with Embeds, if you have an idea that seems neat for it. You can have Interlocks that call for compromise checks like Exploits do, or Interlocks that cost Aether to activate, or Interlocks with nonstandard dice pools, or Interlocks that don't have to roll any dice to be used, or even Interlocks that don't need to be activated but just confer a passive enhancement to something the demon can do, permanently. You can do all or none of those. You can also just keep it simple and do something a hypothetical normal Embed could do. Works fine.

              Think about your particular player character: their play style, their personality, and what they tend to enjoy doing in a game. Give players who like doing lots of investigation and preparation powers that help them ask more questions and put stuff together. Give players who like flying by the seat of their pants powers that are useful spontaneously without much setup or planning. Give players who like looking at their sheet and coming up with weird solutions powers that are a little awkward but potentially very broad. Give players who like being awesome and cracking heads powers that help them crack heads and look awesome. Similarly, have their dice pools use traits that the player either already is invested in or wouldn't feel ripped off or deterred by having to increase.

              Don't be afraid to be as vague as you need to be; remember, you, the writer of the power, are also the person who will be making all the judgment calls on what it can or can't do. Just make sure the player has some kind of guiding idea as to what it's appropriate and useful for.

              Go wild with them. They don't have to be subtle, they can be weird or sideways or direct or outlandish. They just have to do something that relates vaguely to the Keys they use, and something that, when you get to know the player character using the Interlock, you have a little "ahh" moment. Ahh, it's an expression of that guy's nature. Now it all makes sense. He would do that.

                  As an example, here's the completed Cipher of the character in my game, with a little explanation as to how it ties together.

                  Shift Consequence ▶ Freeze Assets ▶ Mercury Retrograde ▶ Common Misconception
                  True invisibility is achieved by omnipresence. Hide in the pattern and you can be found, but the pattern itself is invisible.

                  Snow is her ring's Otacon. She hangs back, hides at base, looks up a lot of information, does a lot of hacking and tricking. She's mischievous and impulsive and very emotionally attached to her Cover identity. She likes to mess with people, but she's averse to vulnerability; she doesn't like to put herself at risk. Her Key Embeds use her Skills as a "face" for her ring, which is heavy on bruisers and fixers, and revolve around hiding out, cheating consequences and fucking with people remotely. Her final secret bridges her need to engage with the world as a part of it, her fear of discovery and retaliation, her active anonymity, and her humanist, lax approach to the Descent. It may imply that one day she could hide from the God-Machine by hiding inside it.

                  Financial Security
                  Shift Consequence ▶ Freeze Assets
                  Costs one Aether. Unrolled reflexive action. When Snow stands to lose a dot of Cover, she may activate this Interlock in order to shift the psychic stress off onto her own assets, instead temporarily losing a number of dots of Merits associated with the Cover in question equal to the penalty on the compromise roll. These Merits return at the end of the story. Compromise Conditions are still applied.

                  Financial Security cheats some of the rules: it has no roll, it costs Aether, and it's not an active power so much as an enhanced reaction to the demonic condition. It's also something that works for our group, but might be disruptive in some other games, depending on how strict the threat of loss of Cover is, or how plentiful the Aether supply. Its relations to its Keys should be apparent: it dodges out one metaphysical consequence by trading it for another consequence based on temporarily freezing her assets. It functions to help her keep her social life separate from her fight against the God-Machine, as long as she has stolen goods and connections to "cash in" to fuel it.

                  Cat in the Bag
                  Freeze Assets ▶ Mercury Retrograde
                  Manipulation + Larceny. Instant action. Introduce a new fact about a transaction Snow could interact with, so long as it doesn't contradict what is known. Extreme changes may require an expenditure of Aether, and repeated attempts to alter the same transaction cost one Aether each.

                  I'm one of those players who likes to look at their sheet like a toolbox and find unconventional solutions. This is a power designed to enable that. It has strangely restrictive requirements: it only works on "transactions" or exchanges of value or goods, it needs to be something Snow already has some kind of practical means to affect without this Embed, and it is better at altering reality and having an effect on the world the less Snow knows about the event she is altering, because everything she learns through research locks in facts she can no longer alter. At the same time, within those limits, it's extremely effective, and can be used to reroute or derail shipments, or to arrange for a needed item being sent somewhere to conveniently get lost near the ring. The Aether clauses were later added to make sure it doesn't get too outlandish. In relation to its Keys, Freeze Assets alters the ability to transact or exchange, and Mercury Retrograde causes misunderstandings. Cat in the Bag causes malfunctions in transit during an exchange.

                  True for Today
                  Mercury Retrograde ▶ Common Misconception
                  Wits + Subterfuge (resisted by Resolve if targeting a person). Reflexive action. Tell a simple lie and roll for compromise (+2 if no human witnesses are present). The lie becomes truth for one round in combat or thirty seconds out of combat. Truth then reverts.

                  The final Interlock is the most obviously potent, in this case, and is Snow at her most blunt, using her power to just outright bluff. Her Cipher progression reflects the fluidity of assets and the value imposed on them, so the final Interlock just outright takes what is believed and makes it what is physically true. It's more like an Exploit than an Embed in terms of how gross its effect is, and has a compromise risk to counterbalance it. Mercury Retrograde causes misunderstandings in communication, and Common Misconception affects how easy it is to do something based on hearsay and beliefs, regardless of whether the beliefs are normally true. If you can confuse someone's understanding, and make their understanding then take hold, then you can basically just bullshit your way through anything if you try hard enough.

                      The Demon Storyteller's Guide, which devotes half a chapter to the Cipher, should be coming out fairly soon. There are some good hints in this thread already, here are a few more...

                      Ciphers can be built forwards or backwards. Forwards is basically taking it a Key at a time, picking a appropriate or cool Embed and making an Interlock to suit. Backwards means starting with a final secret or a cool power and making the Cipher to fit. You can make very thematic Ciphers or Ciphers that are just cool powers strung together; either is fine. Stupid Loserman's covered this so I won't go on further.

                      Personally I'd ask your players a few questions about their demon - the circumstances of their Fall, why they chose their Agenda, where they see it taking them, their conception of Hell, that sort of thing. Imaginative answers to these questions have created some of the coolest Ciphers I've seen/designed, which included a demon who ate things/people out of reality (and could even use them as temporary Covers) and a demon who could control events like a director (his Interlocks are literally Casting, Directing and Editing).

                      Heck if you can get some answers from the players (or if you just know their basic character concepts), throw 'em on here and I'm sure we'll throw out some ideas for you.

                      With such a large number of players, here's another alternate solution. If they're somewhat creative or experienced, get each 'em to make the Cipher of the player to their left (with ST oversight and editing). It saves a lot of work, preserves a lot of the secrecy and also gets your players invested in the PCs of other players.

                                      Loserman has a good breakdown, but let me toss in my 2 cents.


                                      First of all, ignore the Big Words. Occult Matrix is just a fancy name for "magic ritual." Infrastructure is just another Big Word for the people, angels and magic components used in said ritual. I like comparing the aether sense to more traditional demons "sensing holy magic." Its like D&D's "detect Good/Evil" magic abilities, but without the actual baggage of Good/Evil. What you do is really simple. Want to have the God Machine try to cast a magic ritual to take over the city's subway system for some crazy plan? Start including odd magic components, sensing something off, and strange cultists wandering around.

                                      Like Loserman said, Linchpin is there just to make sure that every Infrastructure can be beaten - the writers just wanted to make a point that God is not invincible, that demons can win. Honestly, my players have figured out any number of ways of stopping the God Machine's plans without using a formal Linchpin, so I don't even bother using one anymore. Just play it by ear, and make something up if players get lost. Really, these are actually basic concepts that are pretty much part of every game line, but the way its presented is very confusing and overly formal.


                                      Now, as for Cypher. I describe Cypher as being the Demon equivalent to Bloodlines or Legacies. Special powers for specific niches that players want to occupy. The only difference is that, instead of giving us example ones, DtD requries each player to have a custom built one. So, what I do is actually (again) play it one step at a time, not the whole thing. First, communicate with the player over what direction he wants to see his demon move in. Then figure out what your first ability should roughly be. I'm actually much more open and less secretive than the book suggests, and work openly with the players on what they want for their powers, mostly making sure its balanced and let them do the bulk of writing. I would do the same for custom Legacies/Lodges/Bloodlines, so I see no reason to be different here.

                                      As for Interlocks? They're powers, flat out. Thematically appropriate powers that follow a concept, yes, but still powers. Keys are basically just prerequisites - little different than Devotions in VtR, or the prereqs for each Legacy level in MtAw.

                                      For example, lets say we have a demon with a succubus concept. Pick two Embeds that are thematically apporopriate for the succubus concept, and write up a power involving some kind of dream-hopping ability, or something that revolves around invoking hedonistic desire. You have until your player goes through the four questions, so take your time actually writing it out. That's the hard part, having to balance abilities, but I generally shove the load onto the player's hands, especially if you're working with 7 of them.
