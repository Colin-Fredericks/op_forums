                                                    I read the review of the first chapter. Skimmed the thread too. Here's what I have to say.

                                                    Keep in mind I have only ever glanced at the Genius fansplat (the tone of which I found quite defensive), so my comments are in regards primarily to your review.



                                                    First, a triggering element: in your description of Genius's approach as scientific smut (for lack of a better summary) you claim that Philosophy is the cornerstone of the humanities and thus is not a science. I feel the need to defend Philosophy in this regard.

                                                    Philosophy is the purest science; it is the approach to the universe which allows the formulation of questions. Additionally, all humanities are ALSO sciences; the division between the two 'disciplines' is rather arbitrary and is used primarily to create dueling senses of superiority (Science majors believe they're smarter for not pursuing humanities, and humanities majors believe they're smarter for not pursuing sciences).

                                                    In order of scientific purity (from most pure to most applied), scientific disciplines may be categorized:
                                                    Philosophy -> Logic -> Mathematics -> Physics -> Chemistry (and Geoscience) -> Biology -> Psychology (and Computer Science) -> Anthropology (and Sociology) -> Engineering (and Theology) -> Philosophy

                                                    Logic is applied Philosophy. Biology is applied Chemistry, which makes it applied applied applied applied applied Philosophy.

                                                    That Philosophy is both the start and end of the line of scientific purity isn't a mistake, either. All science relies [EDIT: How did that 'p' get in there?] on a leap of faith (the assumption that we have the ability to question) which in itself is an act of Philosophy. This creates an interesting logic loop; one science being true results in all sciences being true, but we can't definitively confirm that one is true. Each scientific discipline builds on an initial assumption which is taken to be true (it is the foundation of the implication, a core tool in proving the boolean state of a phenomenon). This could perhaps be the reason the writers of the fansplat pursued science in the fleeting manner they did; if you cannot make that initial assumption all that remains is the void. They may have come to such a conclusion subconsciously and simply failed to present the idea clearly. As it was an amateur production instead of an officially sanctioned product, I'm inclined to give leniency in this regard. For those clamoring for the book to BE official, though, you are correct that this topic should be addressed more clearly.

                                                    TL, DR (This section): Philosophy is science. Humanities are science. All science requires an initial assumption, which may or may not be true.



                                                    Second (regarding the conversation in this thread, and this is just a personal pet peeve of mine): Every time this sort of conversation comes up, everybody goes on and on about Genius, Princess, and Leviathan. Those aren't the only fansplats! Demon the Redemption was the second to get a PDF treatment, and Embers had a full book (albeit not a great one) WAY before Leviathan did and before Princess was even on the map. That Demigod one has been on the top of the page for months now too, not to mention Pathogen and who knows how many others. These other fansplats have more hours of labor logged and more contributors than Genius, which has remained relatively static since its initial release (save the recent drive to push it into the second edition ruleset). It's like "This is the first shack ever, isn't it great?" "They're building a skyscraper over there." "Isn't this little shack great?". In that sense I suppose I agree with you; I don't get the hype.

                                                    I feel that discussions like these need to be kept within the confines of the splat being examined. This review is about Genius. The conversation should stick to Genius. Maybe if players stopped trying to compare fan splats against each other their creators wouldn't be pressured into making their own material compatible with fanon and the quality of the resulting efforts would improve.

                                                    TL, DR (This section): To people responding to this review; stick to Genius. Don't compare to other fan lines, since each fan line is designed to (and must) stand on its own two feet.



                                                    Third, car engines ARE demons! (Sorry, needed some humor in here)



                                                    Fourth, in defense of a fan book not having pictures, I can relate. A picture says a thousand words, and if the picture is wrong for the book the words it says can contradict the message the ACTUAL words are attempting to convey. Onyx Path Publications are fortunate to have astounding supernatural artwork. Conversely, fan projects are forced to scrape the barrel of the lead's talent (generally writers aren't great illustrators, so this resolves the work to stick figures and/or doodles) or shell out hundreds of dollars on professional artwork (a subject which not only complicates the legal situation of a fan work but which is also difficult to justify considering fan works can never make back the fiscal losses generated by such purchase). Walls of text with no breaks are hard to justify, but a picture of a grinning guy with crazy hair whose limbs are disproportionate and whose background defies three-point perspective will chase off prospective audiences before they even read the text. Genius at least recognized that risk and chose to let the words do the work.

                                                    Reading about your call to go to the internet and find it, nothing is more generic than a bunch of public domain open license artwork. It might improve the quality in the short term but would become a point of contention as the project ages. Learning skills, on the other hand, is a good process. My own artwork improved greatly as I practiced and acquired tools to make it more consistent. Granted, they're still cartoons, but the act of working on a fan project improved my own marketable skills.

                                                    TL, DR (This section): Fan projects don't have an art budget. Bad artwork can clash with theme. So no art may have been a good thing.



                                                    Fifth:
                                                    So it's not impossible to do a game of mad science. You just have to stick to it and be willing to work to justify things as science. Maybe when you use Mania to build Wonders, you enter into a state of reverie where your information processes these arcane physics absurdly fast, and in the flow of inspiration, you build or exploit branches of science hundreds of years ahead of where we're at. When you snap out of the reverie, you've defied current understanding, but you don't know how except for bits and snippets of information-nothing that makes sense yet, but you know it has place in the models. But because you were caught in the act of doing, these ideas are often just done, without the scrutiny needed to ensure these things can last or work perfectly. Bam, You can maintain the game as is while still talking about it in terms of future science and the manifestation of questions of "What if?".
                                                    This. Is. Awesome. If that was the pitch Genius had made, I would have read the whole book instead of briefly skimming and dismissing it.

                                                    And that's probably the one of the big problems Genius had in it's writing. In Ascension, all you had to do was bullshit, and reality did the rest. In Genius, that framework of "clap your hands if you believe in Tesla's Death Ray" doesn't exist, and the game had no idea whether it wanted it to be there or not. Was it science or was it the magic of belief? No one knew. I'm not even sure they thought about it.
                                                    This would have been cool too, and can easily be framed in the scope of science. Extradimensional reality doesn't have to follow the established (and accepted) ideas of physics; an alternate dimension might have entirely different physical laws. Genius could have tapped into that by having inspiration rip dimensional tears, temporarily letting these alternate physical laws into the World of Darkness. The result is the same: a clap of the hands summons a bolt of lightning. There is--however--nothing magical about it. The phenomenon can be explained within the confines of science... just not by the genius exploiting the phenomenon.

                                                    Fan project leads can be divided into two categories. There are those who have an idea for a mechanic (or character type) and try to build a world around it. And then there are those who have an idea for a world and try to affix mechanics to it. Individuals rarely have the ability to fill in the missing component on their own; that is why projects like these (both professional and amateur) employ teams to bridge the gaps. Weakest link mentality doesn't apply here; more voices means more feedback which means a better result.
                                                    As far as I am aware the production of Genius was entirely from the hands of a single individual. Gauging by its presentation he was a "mechanics first, story later" type of guy. That could mean the gameplay is very engaging but the plot is not. Since the review has only progressed through the first chapter that may be the case, which would mean many people enjoy Genius in SPITE of the setting rather than BECAUSE of the setting.

                                                    TL, DR (This section): One guy on a project can only play to his strengths. Setting may not have been his strength. The issues found may be a result of your own strengths, and can be fixed by opening the project to team iteration (as it is currently).



                                                    Sixth:
                                                    Second off, the predominance of failure doesn't build to anything. This is actually a big point in science itself, as scientists spend a majority of their time trying to do something to get a result and coming back with a negative. This isn't a problem in that field, because science operates on the "I found a thousand ways not to make a lightbulb" principle, using the failures to help refine an answer that will inevitably remain, and finding ways to use the negative results in interesting and useful ways. Genius emphasizes it's failures, but never posits that that failure might serve a point, that it builds into a climatic point. Instead of telling a story of failing upwards into violation, it maintains a steady flat land of failure. And that's disappointing, because as a narrative there's a lot of cool to be found in failing upwards, and if I believed Genius did anything interesting with it's mechanics, I would be excited at the possibility of failing as a way to earn Inspiration Experience to possibly make a Wonder stick in the same ways Prometheans can calcify their Alembics.
                                                    Perhaps it's not an indication of "Mad" scientists but rather "Bad" scientists, but an inverted approach to the scientific method might be a more engaging premise.

                                                    You START with a success. The first experiment results in a favorable outcome. But it was a field experiment. You try to recreate the results and try to put all of the variables in order to produce the phenomenon again, but the second time it fails. And it continues to fail. There's a variable in the mix that isn't right, and the "Bad" scientist can't find out what it is. Convinced by the anecdotal fluke that the foundation of the theory is correct, the scientist rants to peers and is decried as a quack.

                                                    And they are, because they aren't eliminating scenarios which break their original hypothesis. Instead they are trying fanatically to convince others they are right, regardless of the clearly limited practicality of their results (which clearly require absurdly specific conditions).
                                                    Again, if the game had presented itself like that I think I'd have given it a more thorough examination.

                                                    TL, DR (This section): Starting with success and then peppering the 'lockdown' of the scientific method (experiment, observe, revise) with failures might have been a better approach to plot graph structure. The game starts to slow down? Throw in a success in another discovery, seemingly building off the first but impossible to reproduce. That's some Hadron Collider style frustration there, with quantum entropy's low particle count making nature unpredictable.



                                                    Seventh:
                                                    Well, apparently Geniuses go insane really easily, I guess. Disregarding the ableism for now, the problem is that the book doesn't really sell this problem too well. It never feels like a consequence, namely because it doesn't posit a reason why it happens. Maybe if I knew that the longer a Genius sits there not building wonder, the more and more his brain breaks, that'd be something. Hell, that would be cool, building Wonders is literally a stress relief mechanism, done to tire and weaken the connection to Inspiration for a while for some peace and quiet. But as is, Geniuses just kind of go insane because they go insane. It is bound to Inspiration, but it just feels like a thing that happens, much like the rest of this game.
                                                    This gave me a neat idea, built around my own issues with insomnia. Usually when I can't sleep it's because I have an idea in my head I need to get on paper. If I try to table it it gives me a headache. I become uncomfortable holding the idea in its ethereal form.
                                                    It could be interesting if a Genius didn't sleep. Instead, they work on wonders. Making a wonder is thus the only way for them to regain Willpower (because hey, no sleep).



                                                    Eighth, it's really hard to write a thorough response in a popular thread (More funny, sorry. The replies keep flowing in)



                                                    Ninth:
                                                    See, the reason that your "large, oppressive organization" enemy type that is often the playable antagonists is not also your "individual or small group of radically deviated perspectives with radical, unusual powers" is because the idea is that the "radically deviated perspective" is so unique and personal that is can't work in a larger harmony-in fact, that's why it's so strong, because it is unique and personal and a surprising one-hit KO in terms of shock value. Large organizations need to agree on something, and people who are invested in their own personal mythologies to the exclusion or all else don't really work in structured organizations. You might have Marauders running the Technocracy(although it's actually the Nefandi, yes, I know), but the Technocracy is not made up of insane people because insane people don't really work together well. The same goes for every other pairing of that sort you might imagine.
                                                    I agree. These Unmada sound more like a protagonist archetype, actually. If a player character believes machines function more effectively when they're coated with powder blue enamel (something which might actually be true in the weird bubble of science they've created), that's a cool thing. But a larger social construct isn't going to build up around that notion, especially if the individuals comprising that construct believe green, pink, and yellow are better respectively.

                                                    And you're right, the hand-waving for it is painful. If the Lemuria came across some universal truth their individual realities should have collapsed into this consolidating black hole. They should all work from the foundation of one order of physics; one set of physical laws. They might not be the ones widely accepted by the general public, but they should be consistent. In fact, perhaps that should be their goal. If the reality-bending power of a wonder requires understanding of the science behind it (one which a normal person cannot comprehend), should not the goal of the Lemurians be to induct everyone into their numbers? Reveal the secrets to everyone? Burn the unbelievers? If reality is solipsistic, popular opinion should be their goal. And if they truly are mad and wicked, force should be the means to that end.

                                                    But I must diverge from you here. While these Lemurians could represent some unified truth between them (like minds are more productive) they should not be so world-encompassing and universal. There should be conflicting "one truths" that different factions raise as their banners in their war of zealotry. These antagonists should not be a single force but many, fighting themselves as much as the player base.

                                                    TL, DR (This section): I agree, it sounds like the Lemurian Unmada and the Genius should trade boots.



                                                    Tenth (I made it to ten?), I have to disagree about your interpretation of that one quote from the book. I think you may be overreacting, partially due to building stress from disagreements with the text and potentially due to other factors. While I agree the writer seems to have gotten the Lemurian and the Genius turned around (the wrong one's the organization, the wrong one's the focus), I don't believe the statement is advocating for oppression. It is calling for leadership.
                                                    Service is not slavery. A soldier has to follow orders and may even have to die doing so, but the soldier is not a slave to the commanding officer. He/she entered the service willingly, is entitled to an explanation of the reason for the orders if something goes wrong, and is waived liability to the commanding authority if that happens.
                                                    If the antagonists of this fan splat were switched (Lemurians have an agreed consensus on reality and force it upon everyone, whereas a Genius sees the potential for that consensus and rebels against it), you might not have even tripped on that paragraph (even if the wording was identical).



                                                    Eleventh, I thought writing points as I read the review was a good idea. I probably should have taken notes and organized this better. Oh well, at least it's chronological.



                                                    Twelfth... I've reached the end of the review for the first chapter. So I guess this is the TL, DR for my whole response.
                                                    ...That's a hefty responsibility. Hmm....
                                                    Philosophy IS science. Actually, all thought is science.
                                                    [Readers, not review] Threads about a fansplat should stick to that fansplat (for instance, this thread is about Genius and should stick to Genius). Don't cross-compare gamelines.
                                                    <3rd point is a Joke>
                                                    Bad and generic artwork can do more harm than good. Be careful what you wish for.
                                                    Projects assembled by one person will have weaknesses which team projects will not. Genius appears to be mechanic-oriented and not plot-oriented.
                                                    An initial success followed by an experiment unable to be replicated (Anecdotal Observation, false hypothesis, failed experiment) might be a better format for Genius character plots than "Everything fails".
                                                    Geniuses could suffer perpetual insomnia, recovering Willpower through the construction of Wonders. Mechanics to drive connection to plot and power work well.
                                                    <8th point is a joke>
                                                    Lemurian and the player characters should change function. Players should be the Unmada, while the Lemurian have made a consensus on reality and intend to force it down everyone's throats. Also, the Lemurians should be more fractured organizations; reduce the scope of their influence and add infighting.
                                                    Service and Slavery are different things; there is a difference between willingly agreeing to follow someone and being forced to do so. That difference should be emphasized: the Lemurians force others to think the way they do whereas a Genius invites others to think the way they do.

It doesn't need to last forever.

                                                            It also ties into one of the best speeches by a scientist ever.

                                                            Originally posted by Pale Blue Dot by Carl Sagan, 1994
                                                            The Earth is a very small stage in a vast cosmic arena. Think of the rivers of blood spilled by all those generals and emperors so that, in glory and triumph, they could become the momentary masters of a fraction of a dot. Think of the endless cruelties visited by the inhabitants of one corner of this pixel on the scarcely distinguishable inhabitants of some other corner, how frequent their misunderstandings, how eager they are to kill one another, how fervent their hatreds.

                                                            Our posturings, our imagined self-importance, the delusion that we have some privileged position in the Universe, are challenged by this point of pale light. Our planet is a lonely speck in the great enveloping cosmic dark. In our obscurity, in all this vastness, there is no hint that help will come from elsewhere to save us from ourselves. [...]
