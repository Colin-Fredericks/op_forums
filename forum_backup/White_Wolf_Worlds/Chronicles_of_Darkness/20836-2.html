            Started working on major revisions; I'm going to be producing sections piecemeal and then releasing snippets (well... chapters) for examination. This is one among many developmental strategies I'm borrowing from Onyx Path (this being the blog-esque feedback style) to finally finish this project!


            Anyway, I was thinking about what ExplodingFrogs said in the previous discussion about customization of True Form. One of the big problems with Degree of Divinity as written is the mandatory physical characteristics. It directly contradicts the declaration that an Oroboros' divinity is self-defined. So I came up with this:

            Currently: Tail, Teeth, Size, and to some extent Scales are traits that have been absorbed by Degree of Divinity.

            New: At character creation, the player selects three Ablutions to be DoD Ablutions (this is separate from the Ablutions that receive dots). These three are always manifest at a level equal to the Oroboros' current Degree of Divinity. In other words, they are traits that begin to express themselves as the Oroboros manifests other features.

            For example, one player might pick Wings, Claws, and Venoms as DoD Ablutions when constructing a Coatl.

            Benefits to this change include greater system flexibility, especially during character creation. It also makes it easier to remember what DoD actually does (every five points manifest, get a point manifest in each of your DoD Ablutions). It is not without drawbacks, though, the two I can think of being really strange-looking dragon combinations and terminology confusion (I suppose we could come up with a separate name to call these DoD Ablutions to prevent getting them mussed with regular Ablutions... maybe Divinities?).


            TL, DR
            DoD Ablutions (or Divinities) are Ablutions that the player has flagged as being characteristic of their dragon's true appearance. Rather than being used like powers, they are always manifest at a level equal to the dragon's current DoD.

Given the themes of the game, these seem like good things for the system and setting to encourage.

                            Some further elaboration on how the dream-world might work, and also some ideas on Dragon's Integrity/Honor/Mandate/whatever and Willpower recovery mechanics.

                            Mythic Reality

                            So I think that a good name for the dream-world might be the Mythic World, or maybe just the Mythos. Fits with the overall symbolic/archetypal/abstract feel.

                            At the lowest level, it functions as Dragon's form of supernatural senses: the Ouroboroi's answer to Mage Sight, Auspex, what have you. It's symbolic perceptions overlaid onto the ordinary world: a murderer's hands drip with the blood of his victim(s), someone driven by greed has coins for eyes to symbolize their materialistic view of the world, bureaucrats are literally shackled to and by the institutions they work for, and so on. Naturally, supernatural phenomena are represented as well. This doesn't allow the dragon much in the way of actual influence except insofar as their newly-granted insights give them more information to work with.

                            To enter the higher levels, a dragon must be in True Form. They can't enter in human form or partially transformed. On the other hand, doing so costs absolutely nothing; they can do it anytime, anywhere...provided that they're willing to take the risk of assuming True Form first. In the higher levels of the Mythos, symbols and concepts overtake reality; the dragon vanishes from the ordinary world upon entry.

                            Everything in the higher levels of the Mythos is an abstraction of a concept, idea, or archetype that touches on the the things of the ordinary world. These abstractions can be interacted with to produce real results. This won't result in buildings being torn down or anything (unless the dragon somehow manages to implant that notion in a construction company), since the abstractions are a product of living beings' thoughts and feelings, but the effects can be profound nonetheless.

                            For example, killing a businessman-abstraction representing a particular corporation might lead to the loss of that company's "spirit", its zeitgeist, its "corporation-ness"--whatever it might be on paper, it's not a corporation any more. The people composing it just don't have any sense of shared identity or loyalty or purpose. They're just a bunch of guys who happen to work in the same building. It might recover, developing a new direction, a new "company spirit". (And anyone aware of the loss--as the abstraction's killer almost certainly would be--would be in a unique position to shape this new vision.) Or it might just fall apart, regardless of how well it was doing before.

                            Or forging an alliance with the abstraction of a neighborhood could leave all the people in that neighborhood feeling vaguely well-disposed toward the dragon, even if they've never met. Perhaps even somehow...obligated. (Or perhaps expecting some kind of obligation from the dragon. That could be interesting.)

                            Or compelling fear and worship from an appropriate abstraction could start a cult centered on the dragon. (Or even an inappropriate abstraction: imagine a cult composed of, say, all the used car salesmen in the area...)

                            The point being, messing around with abstractions affords dragons widespread social influence, but very little in the way of fine control, especially at the individual level. I also think that perhaps this should be their only form of supernatural social-fu: it gives them a unique niche, and fits in with the themes of rulership and divine right. A king commands nations, not individual peasants.

                            Even if they have other social tricks, I think they should be variations on that general theme. Dragons are primal, elemental forces: their powers should have a simple, direct, forceful feel to them, without much in the way of complexity. Influencing crowds with raw charisma and blinding terror? A-OK. Subtle, one-to-one social manipulation? No way.

                            Anyway.

                            The Mythos has varying levels of abstraction. The higher you go, the more archetypal and universally relevant the abstractions you're dealing with become. For example, you might be talking to an actual businessman at the lowest level, overlaid with appropriate symbols of the kind of person he is. Then you shift to True Form (ignoring for the purposes of this example the logical consequences of turning into a fucking dragon mid-conversation) and enter the next level of the Mythos. Now the businessman you're talking to is an abstraction of his entire branch office. Go higher. Now he represents the entire company. Higher still. Now he's all corporations, everywhere. Even higher. Now he's the very concept of Big Business, or Commerce, or Wealth.

                            How high you can go is determined by your Furnace. This is mostly so that starting dragons can't become King of the New World Order after a few interactions with suitable archetypes. (Although abstractions have game traits, and archetypes that big-time would probably be biting off more than said starting dragon could chew. Still and all.)

                            To leave the Mythos, you have to pass through all the levels of abstraction between you and the ordinary world--you can't be chatting it up with Death Personified and then pop instantly back into the real world.

                            Distance in the higher levels of the Mythos is a function of conceptual relation rather than spatial separation. Take the office-businessman from our earlier example. The building you're in could represent the corporation as a whole; going into another room and exiting the Mythos could find you in an office of the same company on the other side of the country. It can be a handy way to travel. Bearing in mind, of course, that you're in True Form when you're in the higher Mythos and can't assume human form until you leave. You need to be careful about what's on the "other side". ("Breaking news! An honest-to-God firebreathing dragon materialized in front of the Eiffel Tower this morning. More information as soon as we figure out just what the hell is going on.")

                            Also, traveling long distances through the higher Mythos can provoke dreams of a dragon's True Form in the individuals touched on by the concepts they pass through. This becomes more and more likely the higher the level of abstraction, if only because of how many are touched on by major archetypes. These dreams may or may not convey a vague sense of the dragon's intended real-world destination.

                            Usually, only concepts and archetypes held by currently living (or, well, unliving, in the case of vampires and lucid ghosts and such) beings are present in the Mythos. The Heart is an exception to this rule; every Heart contains the symbols and archetypes not only of the present bearer, but of all Antecedents as well. It comprises a small Mythos unto itself, called the Treasury. A dragon enters the Treasury automatically upon (non-Final) death, but the Treasury may be accessed as with any other part of the higher Mythos, with one exception: even dragons with Furnace too low to enter the higher Mythos can enter their own Treasury.

                            At a sufficiently high level of abstraction, all Hearts are connected by the archetypal Dragon's Heart. They are no different from any other Mythos phenomenon in this respect. This means that a dragon with sufficiently high Furnace can visit another dragon's Treasury, and from there emerge into the ordinary world at the location of their Heart. This may or may not be a level attainable in the normal course of play. (That is to say, if there's a Dragon equivalent to archmastery, it may be restricted to such an evolved template.)

                            Mandate, Majesty, and Monstrosity

                            Dragons don't have Virtues or Vices. They're not human any more, and the keystones of their psychology have changed as dramatically as their forms. Instead, they have Majesty and Monstrosity, the glory of the divine god-king and the rapaciousness of the tyrannical god-monster.

                            Majesty is the nature of the authority a dragon holds. It's how they relate to their subjects. It doesn't have to be a specific position--it's more about the style of authority than the particular role. Do they bark out orders like an army drill sergeant? Do they have advisers? Do they lead by example? Or by raw charisma and force of personality? What persona do they try to project, in their role as leader? What kind of Province are they trying to build? What role do they want their subordinates to play? What principles of rule do they strive to uphold?

                            When a dragon lives up to the principles of their Majesty in a meaningful way, they gain 1 Willpower. When they take significant risks to do so, they earn a full Willpower refresh.

                            Monstrosity is how a dragon abuses their power. It's the indulgence of dark hungers, the exercise of authority to TAKE what they want, and damn the consequences, the clawed boot stomping on a human face, forever. Do they bully subordinates? Do they take protection money? Do they demand worship? Do they literally get away with murder?

                            When a dragon abuses their authority in their characteristic fashion, they gain 1 Willpower. When they do so in an extravagant, destructive fashion, they earn a full Willpower refresh. (What I mean by "extravagant" and "destructive" is stuff like demanding exorbitant tribute or human sacrifice or executing incompetent subordinates or making their subjects genuinely fear for their lives. Banal corruption like taking bribes and kickbacks or blackmail earns 1 Willpower; you need to get into full-on Evil Overlord territory for the full refresh.)

                            Dragons don't have Integrity or Morality. They have the Mandate instead. Mandate breaking points don't change from dragon to dragon like Integrity does; the Mandate is something that all dragons must uphold as part and parcel of their divine right to rule.

                            However, Dragons might still have their own set of "five questions". These would be tools to help figure out the details of Majesty and Monstrosity.

                            The roll for a Mandate breaking point is Resolve + Presence. A dragon experiencing a breaking point isn't struggling to maintain mental clarity, but rather to show their worth as a ruler.

                            The following circumstances modify breaking point rolls:

                            Acting in the best interests of one's subjects and/or Province (+2)
                            Mandate 8-10 (+2)
                            Punishing a traitor (+2)
                            Eliciting awe/reverence/deep respect from subjects (+1)
                            Inciting terror in enemies (+1)
                            Inflicting retribution for disrespect (+1)
                            Mandate 7-8 (+1)
                            Upholding established laws, promises, agreements, etc. (+1)
                            Mandate 4-5 (-1)
                            Inciting terror in own subjects (-2)
                            Mandate 2-3 (-2)
                            Unnecessary violence (defined specifically as "given this particular goal/set of circumstances, you could have gotten what you wanted without resorting to violence.") (-2)
                            Mandate 1 (-3)
                            Selfish, arbitrary commands (-3)
                            Allowing one's subjects and/or Province to suffer through inaction (that is, you could have done something about it, but didn't) (-4)
                            Undermining rightful authority (-4)
                            Acknowledging a mortal as superior (-5)
                            Allowing disrespect from a mortal (-5)
                            Breaking laws, promises, and agreements you set yourself (-5)
                            Causing widespread chaos and disruption (-5)

                            Breaking points should fall into one of the following categories:

                            A dragon is a Ruler. A dragon is In Charge. If the dragon's authority is undermined? That's a breaking point. If they allow disrespect and insubordination to go unpunished? That's a breaking point, too. If it's clear that no one respects the dragon or recognizes their authority? Breaking point.

                            One who rules under the Mandate is as much representative of the state as person. They must conduct themselves as such. If the dragon breaks the laws of their Province, whatever those may be, that's a breaking point. Even (especially) if they established those laws. Breaking their word is a breaking point. Exceeding their rightful authority (somewhat nebulous, but basically: whether formally or otherwise, the dragon is recognized to have authority in their Province, but even the most unrestricted authority has a point at which they overstep their bounds) is a breaking point.

                            A ruler's purpose is to maintain peace, prosperity, and order in their domain. Anything that significantly disrupts the day-to-day functioning of the dragon's Province is a breaking point, whether or not they were directly responsible.

                            Things that are not breaking points:

                            Violence, destruction, and killing are not, in and of themselves, breaking points. If it falls under a dragon's authority and won't disrupt the workings of the Province, they can do it. (In fact, failure to commit violent acts could well be a breaking point--say, in the case of blatant insubordination, or extreme threats to the Province.) Violence-related circumstantial modifiers will tend to make the breaking point roll worse when it does happen, but it doesn't actually cause them.

                            Selfish and immoral behavior. You can be a completely awful person and still be a good (for certain values of "good") leader. In other words, gaining Willpower from Monstrosity is not automatically a breaking point. Again, though, the relevant circumstantial modifiers will make breaking point rolls a lot more likely to fail when they do occur.

                            Breaking the Masquerade. (Or whatever term Ouroboroi use. You know what I mean.) This isn't especially relevant, because it'll usually fall under one of the breaking point categories by default (probably "disruption in the Province"), but for whatever it's worth, it's not a Mandate violation in and of itself. The secrecy of the Ouroboroi is strictly a social construct.

                            Eating another dragon's Heart. This isn't a breaking point, because there's no roll. Any dragon who commits such a heinous act immediately loses a dot of Mandate.

                            Ideas for Conditions resulting from breaking point rolls:

                            These don't have mechanics. They're just ideas and flavor at the moment.

                                                    Sorry to double-post, but I managed to get those ideas on Edicts I mentioned above in something vaguely resembling order, and I think they're more or less presentable now.

                                                    So like I said, I think that Edicts should based on a handful of primal forces. The dice pool should probable be something like Furnace+Willpower+Edict, or something similarly evocative of how they come from raw power and divine right inherent to being a dragon rather than any mortal Skills or Attributes.

Although that has the downside of making low-Mandate dragons inherently less powerful, which doesn't seem right; there should be advantages to upholding the Mandate, yes, but the temptation of forbidden power as a trope is rather undermined if breaking it leads to weakness. Hmm. Maybe the higher of Mandate and Furnace? That would make flouting the Mandate seem more and more appealing as the dragon grows more and more powerful, which plays well into the metaphor of dragons-as-rulers. The young, ambitious, and idealistic prince who becomes more and more jaded and corrupt when he sits the throne himself...)

                                                    Anyway.

                                                    The Laws an Edict grants should be fairly simple and direct, although powerful. They're not subtle, at least not in the sense of being delicate tools wielded with precision and care. (They can be subtle in the sense of being hard to detect; it's just that their actual effects are more like being hit with a sledgehammer than being chipped away at with a chisel. If that makes sense.)

                                                    Greater complexity can be achieved by combining Laws, both within the same Edict and with Laws from other Edicts.

                                                    I have a few ideas for what some of the Edicts might be, and how combining them might work:

                                                    Edict of Heaven: The name refers to the concept of Providence, "Heaven's Will", the idea that if Heaven wants a thing to happen, this is a thing that must be. It's the edict of fate and fortune, omens and prophecy. It works a bit differently from Mage or Changeling-style Fate, though.

                                                    When you give a prophecy with the Edict of Heaven, you're not foretelling the future. You are declaring--commanding, even--that "this event is a thing that is going to happen."

                                                    And it is so.

                                                    The world makes it so. How is not up to you. If you say that there's going to be a bridge over a particular river when next you need to cross? That bridge is going to be there. It might be a fallen tree. It might be a construction project that just finished when you show up. (Or even isn't finished, but the bridge is done enough for you to get across, and that's all you need.) A spirit could use Influences to freeze the river's surface in that spot, for whatever arcane reasons spirits do anything. Could be an enemy luring you into a trap. (You never specified that the bridge was going to lead to anything good, after all.) Beavers could have built a dam. Debris could have floated, drifted, fallen, and blown into that spot over time until it accumulated into a mass that could support your weight.

                                                    It doesn't matter how it happens. One way or another, that bridge is going to be there.

                                                    Mechanically, this works by encouraging events that help the outcome you've chosen, that move toward it, and hindering events that oppose it. By default, this is stuff like circumstantial modifiers to rolls. So the construction crew might get bonuses or even automatic successes on any rolls to make, implement, and overcome obstacles to building a bridge on that particular spot, and penalties or even automatic failures--maybe even dramatic failures!--for trying to bring the project to a halt or move it elsewhere. Anything influencing the crew would likewise benefit or suffer--funding flows like the river they're bridging, supportive lobbyists are incredibly persuasive on this one point, obstructive bureaucrats are effortlessly bypassed. And this applies not just to the construction crew, but to anything and everything in the world that might result in there being something bridging the river when you get there.

                                                    Obviously it would get boring fast if the Storyteller had to roll for every random force or entity that has the slightest impact on your goals, so unless this affects significant characters he can usually just handle it in the narrative. (Unless you can come up with a system to abstract all of that down to just one or two dice rolls, in which case go for it.)

                                                    Combining other Edicts with the Edict of Heaven allows other "incentives" and "penalties" to be applied, as appropriate to that particular edict. You can also use the Edict of Heaven with another Edict to change the time and place a given Law will take effect--effectively, you're telling the world "at thus-and-such time, at this place, under these circumstances, this Law shall take effect."

                                                    Edict of Earth: This is the Edict of the physical world, the mortal world, the earthly world. It allows control over matter, natural forces and disasters, and the world we live in in general. It does not allow control over living things, except insofar as it can impact them via weather, radiation, kinetic force, and suchlike.

                                                    Laws of the Edict of Earth normally have a relatively limited range of effect. This can be expanded by combining them with the appropriate Edicts.

                                                    Edict of Eternity/The Eternal Edict: This is the Edict of time. As the name implies, it doesn't allow much in the way of time travel or other cross-temporal shenanigans; it is much more centered around preservation and stasis, exempting things important to the dragon from the ravages of time. It also has some influence over age and transition--you can age things into dust or make changes to them that would normally only come with lots of time (like fossilization). Basically, if time is a river, then the Eternal Edict lets you see it clearly and halt its flow or speed it up, but not to go back or instantly jump forward.

                                                    The Eternal Edict can extend the duration of other Edicts' Laws, assuming they have one and it isn't already "permanent". It also allows for "skipping ahead" in a Law with progressive effects over time.

                                                    Edict of Blood: This is the Edict of living things and the connections between them, both biological and metaphysical. The "tree of life", blood relations, inheritance (not Inheritance, dear god) both genetic and material, bloodlines, interactions, obligations, oaths and promises, bindings. It generally does not allow direct control over living things but rather over these connections--thus a dragon doesn't mutate the local animals or control their bodies like meat puppets, but alters the ties of predator and prey and parasite to twist their normal behavior and send plagues upon his enemies. He doesn't mind-control humans, but rather binds them in oaths and laws and contracts.

                                                    The Edict of Blood can allows other Edicts to target living things through these connections and relations. It can also allow them to spread from their original target through these connections--a contagious blessing or curse that ultimately affects many more than the dragon could easily target himself.

                                                    There are three more Edicts that I have more vague ideas on. They're really just a set of themes associations, some of them very shaky. I haven't yet come up with even a vague outline of how they'd work out in play. I'm not even sure if they're worth using. See what you make of them:

                                                            They shouldn't. Edicts are a core power, an inherent part of being a dragon. A splat's factions are social constructs and thus don't get to control access to traits and abilities inherent to the splat's condition.

                                                            That is to say, you can't be a dragon and not have a Method. That's just plain impossible. You're a dragon because you have a Method. That's why it makes sense that Method determines what Ablutions a dragon favors, something that could be extended to Edicts. (Or not. We could just as easily go with favored Edicts being either a thing that doesn't exist because they all cost the same, or something the individual player chooses during chargen independently of Method or Philosophy.)

                                                            You can, on the other hand, be a dragon and not be part of a Philosophy. They're a social construct that a dragon can choose to be a part of, or not. Thus the benefits of Philosophies should be unique to each Philosophy. Which means non-members can't use them. Which means they can't be universal abilities fundamental to the nature of a dragon, because how the hell would they control access? Even if it was something that wasn't immediately instinctive, sooner or later someone outside the Philosophy would figure it out.

                                                            Look at Vampire. Invictus Oaths, Carthian Law, Coils of the Dragon, and the variants of blood sorcery aren't Disciplines. They aren't part of a universal, standardized system of powers that all vampires get access to and even the most exotic variations run on. Each is effectively a distinct subsystem of its own that only vampires of the right Covenant get access to. (There are mechanical similarities to the existing systems, chiefly because they work and the writers are sensible enough to not reinvent the wheel, but also marked differences.)

                                                            If Philosophies are to grant any special powers at all, this is the model they should follow. Maybe the Imperators, with their emphasis on direct autocratic rule, have some kind of weird Hoard-based magic where the symbols of luxury and power are translated into actual wealth and authority. Maybe the Idols are particularly skilled at manipulating their subjects' attitudes towards them via the Mythos, or can "draw down" Mythos abstractions to become the embodiment of an ideal, with appropriate social effects.

                                                            Another approach is the way that Demon: the Descent's Agendas work. In Demon, following an Agenda gives you a permanent, constantly renewing Condition that earns you Beats for stirring up plot and/or trouble in an Agenda-characteristic fashion, and can also be periodically resolved for a useful Agenda-relevant bonus. Since anyone can go and see them in the free Kickstarter preview, I think it should be okay to reproduce them here:

                                                            Condition: Prepared for Anything

                                                            A Watcher is always planning and preparing, analyzing edge cases and counter measures and
                                                            performing statistical analyses — all in her head. Other demons are frequently impressed by the
                                                            Inquisitors’ preparedness for events they believe nobody could have predicted, but which the Inquisitors
                                                            would point out was a simple matter of statistics.

                                                            • Beat: The Inquisitor gains a Beat when the character poses a question to one or more members of his
                                                            ring that leads them to reconsider or change their course of action. “What if we try going in through the
                                                            roof?” or “How did the angel know we’d switched cars?” are possible examples.

                                                            • Resolution: The character can make a leap of logic, connecting disparate clues into a revelatory truth.
                                                            Resolve this Condition to gain information from the Storyteller about how the current situation — how
                                                            two plot threads tie together, what a good next step would be, or something that the character saw but
                                                            just didn’t connect until now. If no such element presents itself, the player can instead resolve this
                                                            Condition to gain a +3 on any Mental Skill roll (Embeds and Exploits included).

                                                            Condition: Angel Empathy

                                                            While most Unchained emotionally distance themselves from their angelic past to some extent,
                                                            Integrators don’t. Instead, many spend at least some time actively trying to remember what angelhood
                                                            was like. For this reason, devoted Idealists have greater insight into the psychology of the angel than
                                                            other Agendas, which they may call upon to their advantage.

                                                            • Beat: Take a Beat when the character puts the ring at risk or makes the ring vocally or actively
                                                            suspicious of his motives.

                                                            • Resolution: The character can place himself in an angel’s mindset, understanding its actions and plans
                                                            by his own memories and analysis of its behavior. Upon resolving this Condition, the player gains a +3
                                                            bonus to any roll to evade, outwit, persuade, or learn the bane/ban of an angel. This bonus does not
                                                            apply to combat rolls made against angels, however.

                                                            Condition: An Eye for Disorder

                                                            A talented Saboteur quickly picks up a keen eye for the seams where the world is stitched together and
                                                            knows just what to say or do to make the houses of cards around her collapse.

                                                            • Beat: Gain a Beat when the character draws attention to herself by destroying, unsettling, or
                                                            destabilizing a system. The attention doesn’t have to be from the God-Machine or its agents, nor does it
                                                            have to be immediately dangerous, but it should at least be inconvenient or cause some conflict.

                                                            • Resolution: The character knows just what to do to turn harmony into dissonance. This may be just
                                                            the words needed to start a fight, what buttons to press to set off alarms, or where in the machinery to
                                                            toss her shoe to cause it to start tearing itself apart. After performing an action intended to create
                                                            chaos, the player enjoys a +3 bonus on the relevant Skill roll.

                                                            Condition: I Know Someone

                                                            The character is a member of the Tempters and probably an associate of one of their secret societies.
                                                            Through this membership, he has access to the Decadents’ extensive network of friends and favors
                                                            owed, giving him threads to pull all over the city.

                                                            • Beat: Gain a Beat when the character delegates a task, talks someone into taking a risk on his behalf,
                                                            or otherwise avoids getting his hands dirty directly.

                                                            • Resolution: The character knows someone everywhere. With a single phone call or a word in the right
                                                            ear, the character can gain instant VIP treatment. He enjoys a +3 bonus to a Social roll where VIP status
                                                            would be a significant benefit, such as being admitted into an establishment that caters to a restricted
                                                            clientele, cutting red tape, or similar.

                                                            Getting Beats from Agenda Conditions (including by resolving them) provokes a Cipher roll as well. (The Cipher is...well, it's complicated, but suffice it to say that every demon is on a personal journey of self-discovery, and gaining Beats from involvement with demons, angels, and/or the God-Machine can provoke a roll to see if they've had a moment of insight, allowing them to get information from the Storyteller about the next step in that journey.) I'm not sure how significant this is to balancing Agency Conditions: are the Conditions enough on their own, with the Cipher rolls being a "bonus", or are the Cipher rolls a big part of what makes them "worth it"? Because in the latter case, Philosophy Conditions (should they be implemented) would need something extra. Maybe a persistent effect just for having the Condition?
