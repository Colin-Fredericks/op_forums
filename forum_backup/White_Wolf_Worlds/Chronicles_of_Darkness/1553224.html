    Splat Banes and Changing Them

    08-16-2025, 06:38 PM
    Overall, Aggravated damage is far rarer in CoD than in WoD, and that's generally a good thing. But for the most innately resilient splats, there are Banes, weaknesses that let common people deal Aggravated to them, bypassing most of their (un)natural resilience. Those are specific methods, so far, so good.

    But it really annoys me that three of those splats have Fire as a Bane: Vampires, Prometheans and Mummies. For one, it gets repetitive. It doesn't necessarily makes a lot of sense, either. But what's worse, it's a considerably accessible resource, one that makes causing Agg to those things particularly easy.

    So, I'm torn between exchanging this to something else, or simply ditch the Fire Bane entirely. For Prometheans in particular, for Mummies second, but even for Vampires. Any thoughts?


    Just to clarify some things, let me delve a little bit on the rationale behind and against the Bane of Fire for those creatures:

    Promethean: the main reasons I can think of for fire being a Bane in Promethean are first that Frankenstein's Creature in the original novel fears it, and second because in most movies he dies in a fire (usually started intentionally to kill him). But none of those have anything to do with him being particularly weak to fire, he fears it in the book because he got burned once and really didn't like it, thinking of fire as "duplicitous". Most other inspirations for Promethean have nothing to do with fire at all, or similarly not in a "weakness" concept. Aside from it's name being related to the "Divine Fire", I see nothing to justify this weakness among the Created;

    Mummy: sure, they're literally desiccated corpses wrapped in linen, plus oils and stuff. But from a thematic perspective, burning mummies comes from two sources: first, grave-robbers sometimes did it because that was the easier way to get the treasures wrapped in the mummy. They didn't do it always, though, because mummies were valuable by themselves for making medicine and pigments. Second, there's a very popular joke by Mark Twain, that a lot of people takes seriously, in which they used mummies as fuel for trains. Overall, I don't see any of this as really contributing thematically to a game about gods' serving immortals with extreme but short-lived power;

    Vampire: while the weakness to fire may look traditional in the game, in literature and folklore things are complex and varied. Vampires that burn easier and more completely than mortals do exist, but vampires that are particularly resistant to fire are just as common. More frequently fire is mentioned as one of the few things that can permanently kill a vampire, but how easy it is to do so isn't stated. Frequently burning a vampire in those stories means dowsing them completely in flammable liquids or some other method that requires at least as much effort as immolating anyone. And sometimes it must be holy fire, blessed in a very similar way to holy water. There are certainly thematic reasons for this Bane, especially fire as a cleansing element against the unholy, but it still seems unnecessary if it isn't really contributing to the game in any other way.

      Fire is notable for a couple of reasons:

      1. All three monster gamelines that have the bane in question primarily concern beings that are undead (or at least made of dead bodies and/or powered by an explosively volatile substance) with a corruptive effect on their environment (even the Arisen, while not possessed of anything like the Predatory Aura or the Wasteland, spread Sybaris to those who enter their Tombs). In the absence of unusual circumstances or variations on their conditions, two of them are explicitly magically preserved and the third doesn't age even if they still count as alive. All three are inhuman enough to be comparable to wild animals, feral humans, or dread gods better left to sleep outside times of great need, and more than once within these games fire is equated by metaphor with humanity's mastery of the world beyond itself, with vampires and Prometheans losing or painstakingly gaining proximity to humanity over time and the Arisen being set apart from life and humanity by their status as enshrined instruments of the Judges to bring back (but never create) vessels of Sekhem across the devouring ages.

      2. Fire is a volatile consumptive force that requires care to be taken to avoid having it spread out of control of the would-be monster hunter attempting an unscheduled cremation. Attempting to leverage fire as a weapon runs the danger of being trapped in a burning building or forest fire, or, on the other end of things, running out of fuel or losing one's fire source without a way to reignite it. Even without these complications, fire is a highly visible way of dealing with something the size of an adult human person and can lead to difficult questions in a setting where people don't seriously talk about monsters in mixed company. Fire is life and pain and transformation, and its ability to slip the leash of humanity makes it a tool of lesser resort compared to vessel weapons or other supernatural weapons or simple overwhelming force.

      It's also worth remembering that these gamelines are not being used to represent generic phenomena that can be used for every possible variation on the theme of "vampire," "mummy," or "artificial being," and that being "repetitive" is not a primary concern for monsters who are designed to be played in games where one of them is the main type of monster being engaged with on benign terms — Requiem offers a lot of variety and the tendency of the Blood to pool and run together makes its overcategory of vampirism a big tent, but the Kindred are not the only vampires in the setting and even they don't all necessarily share the same qualities; Prometheans likewise are such odd ducks that the corebook includes an explicit statement in its introduction that every rule described in it is a tendency that may have strange exceptions (as expected for constructs powered by a force that can literally explode into storms of mutagen just from staying in one place for long enough); and as paradoxical nukes attended by destiny-steered cults to cosmic demon-gods the Arisen are far from the only game in town if you want a different flavor of sorcerous lich to play with.

      There's also the matter of the fire weakness lending extra weight to those powers that involve it from these splats. Resilience and the Coil of the Ascendant make a Kindred vampire notably more dangerous in the face of the one reliable source of aggravated damage they can be confronted with (to say nothing of elder Devotions like Spontaneous Combustion enabling them to directly set someone on fire with a touch), the Ignus Aspiratus Alembic of Vulcanus Transmutations is the domain of Prometheans who've spent at least a little time in an advanced Refinement, and the Arisen tools for wielding or protecting themselves from fire are Utterances (which variously produce an effect that remains within the control of the mummy, allow fires to be snuffed out, work at sufficient range to be no cause for concern, explicitly render the mummy immune to their effects, or displace the mummy from the phase of reality affected by the flames), each of which also implies a minimum Sekhem rating for their effects to work, or particular Affinities (whether the Ka Affinity that also protects against other forms of aggravated damage and environmental effects or the Alchemist Affinity representing their guild's methods of self-perfection). Even without treating parts of these as "air-breathing mermaid" powers, they have weight in the two gamelines whose monsters' biggest drawbacks are the most significant to their ability to participate in human society (and crossover) and the gameline whose powers are most dramatically apocalyptic/disastrous (and who has the tightest narrative operating timeframe and relatively few other weaknesses beyond a subset of the artifacts their default gameplay loop revolves around).

          Originally posted by monteparnas View Post
          But what's worse, it's a considerably accessible resource, one that makes causing Agg to those things particularly easy.
          I generally handle this is by making access to weapons that can easily manipulate fire much harder to acquire by explaining it within setting reasons.

          Vampires have enough pull in governments, businesses, that dragon breath rounds and similar ‘’easy to use’’ fire weapons are both highly illegals and hard to find.

          They are not accessible without having some really special contacts. Fire rounds are things that even hunters can struggle to get a hold on.

          I also make them harder to craft, by having the knowledge required to craft those being actively suppressed by all covenants worldwide. Vampires do not want humans, or other vampires for that matter, having access to weapons that can easily kill them.

          While not completely realistic, I think it helps deal with the highly efficient fire-based weapons.

          I mainly run vampires, but I think it can apply to other splat as well, especially if they share the same world.

          Now sure, things that can generate a fire still exist, but they are a bit harder to use if the target is fighting back. A blow torch is not easy to use in the fight.

          I wasn’t even aware that mummies and Promethean had weakness to fire (to be honest, I’m not super aware of game lines outside VTR and CoD),

          Originally posted by monteparnas View Post
          So, I'm torn between exchanging this to something else, or simply ditch the Fire Bane entirely. For Prometheans in particular, for Mummies second, but even for Vampires. Any thoughts?
          I personally don’t mind the fire weakness too much with my previous restrictions, but if I were to change it something I would probably do it this way :

          Vampires are classically weak to a few things : Sunlight, running water, Garlic, Silver, salt, etc. At BP 1, a vampire could be weak only to sunlight. At BP 3 a vampire could gain an extra bane chosen from among a list of classical vampire weakness. Each extra two points of BP after could add an extra weakness. You could also use some of the supernatural banes present in the book.

          I don’t know how it works for other splat, but it could probably work in a similar way since they have a power stat.

          My idea is not without a downside, however. I run a game with a book log of over a hundred NPCs. Adding extra weaknesses to all kindred would be tedious and could be difficult to track in the long run. Fire has the advantage of being universal and simple.

          If you simply wanted to use another substance, I’m not sure what to suggest. Maybe something that’s more in line with the splat origin?

                      Originally posted by Algarik View Post
                      The legality totally depends on which country or states.
                      You're focusing on outright bans, the thing is that even where they're legal they are regulated, usually heavily so. And I am speaking about the US, the most armed country in the world, with the second loosest regulation (Yemen has actually even less restrictions on guns).

                      Dragon's Breath aren't really ammunition in the usual sense, and aren't regulated as such. They're incendiary devices made for use with a shotgun. In the way they really work, they wouldn't even use firearms rules in the game, and they're not regulated as ammunition, they're regulated as incendiary devices that pose serious hazard. By the way, they're not used by the military, either, at least not for shooting things. They're strictly incendiary devices, used to put things on fire in a somewhat controlled way.

                      What that means for the game is that you don't need to create contrived fictitious laws to impede their use, you can apply real-like laws, just a bit more strict. People who wouldn't have permission to buy or use high-end incendiary devices won't have permission to buy or use Dragon's Breath. And any use of Dragon's Breath is likely to attract authorities, because it leaves a lot of telltales and traces that are relatively easy to detect, so using them and not getting caught isn't that easy. In practice, you'd have a very similar level of impediment for acquisition.

                      Also, they shouldn't work as firearms. Instead, I think the best rule for them is to treat as a "portable bonfire" of sorts, nothing more. No roll should add to the damage, instead a roll should be made only if there are things around the user don't want to burn, because it's really easy to burn the wrong thing with it (up to and including the user).

                      But then, that's me. Your take actually has a final result very similar to how things work for real. As for your players, I can understand, but also have a very different take on the problem, so there's nothing more I can help with.

They can collectively decide to tackle one of the coterie member's current task, but they can't split, players that won’t participate in a coterie action are not given extra action.
