                    I will give my answer as bold after each section:
                    Originally posted by Peraxis View Post
                    When I say WoD and CofD are two separate games I mean in the sense that before the name changed, in some ways nWoD was just seem as a new revision instead of a completely new thing if you didn't know anything else. That you would assume it was just like going from d&d 3.5 to 4e. Given that my first game with the system was VtM (not sure which version) in 2009, my view is limited about the very early days and how it was.

                    And the thing is that such view is wrong, plainly put. It was an error, maybe, to present the setting as nWoD, but it was meant as an entirely new game line from the start. What's as going from AD&D to D&D3.X to 4th Ed is the movement from 1st Ed WoD books to 2nd Ed to Revised to 20th Anniversary.

                    The arguments they gave me is that you can't even remove it easily from the Diamond Orders with how ingrained it is into their histories/writeups. As for the spells part, I suspect this is more of an writing appearance issue as the book makes it look like the pre-written things as the bulk of magical knowledge a mage can learn. That only the most powerful mages use Creative Thaumaturgy, as there is 100 some pages of spells, then some other magical things for another 50 pages and then comes the bit about CT at the end of the chapter (in 1e anyways).

                    Yeah, maybe was that.

                    Perhaps an example: Say a character is chasing someone down some stairs. The Character wants to jump down the flight and possibly close the gap. Assuming the Character has the skill dots reasonably to do so, would you require a roll for this? Some ST would because by the rules it would. Some ST would say no, you don't have to roll because of the skill dots, and you just do it. Actions like this are probably minor compared to more social things. If someone can roleplay lying, in a believable way, would you still make them roll for it?

                    While I agree that not really dice doesn't mean more/better roleplaying, I think in some ways it forces you to try to RP better because you don't have dice backing up your described actions.

                    As for paying 60 bucks for a book for...it depends on the person. Personally I own 7 random MtAw 1e books that I have never used. I would probably even say barely even looked at. I really only got them because WhiteWolf had stopped publishing the books and I liked the game(I know you can get them all PoD now, but the non-full bleed pages, no page enders just is a bit of a turn off to me)

                    Ok, first, your example doesn't exemplify any distinction between systems. It is still on the ST. Second, it isn't necessarily better. Dice aren't rolled because it is needed, they are rolled because it adds tension. Maybe rolling dice in such a scene will only make we loose time, but maybe it will add tension and drama because of the chance of failure, even it being low. The narrative and the group's mood is the only parameter to say if rolling dice is a good idea. Third, depending on the group I would certainly let roleplay take care of everything. And depending I would never let roleplay substitute rolling dice, because sometimes players who aren't good as actors or at social interactions want to play a PC who is. Unless you're using RPG as a tool at training actors, players shouldn't be ever punished for not having personally an ability the character have.

                    Forth, being forced to RP to cover for system failures isn't a feature for the system, it is a bug. cWoD have rules for everything CoD have, anyway, they just aren't that good. If a good system is one that forces you to Roleplay because it lacks rules for social interactions, you should play AD&D 1st edition, or D&D Basic. By the way, more people roleplayed a lot in those systems than most hardcore WoD players would ever accept.

                    I know it is more of just a matter personal dislike of how things are presented and it can easily be changed to be more like 1e as needed and any actual issues (like people taking conditions for the beat gain) is a matter of roleplay styles as you said.
                    I think you're minding it far more than you need, but suit yourself.
                    ------

                    @Uxas said that oWoD combat was long and many dice rolls. I don't deny this. I think this is why I was always told that most groups avoided combat as much as they could because it was so long and drawn out. Or maybe it is part of the 'game system tacked on' bit. I don't know. My experience with oWoD games is very limited.
                    It was just because it wasn't revised. Forget about this thing of 'x tacked on it', this take on system won't take you anywhere. Even because with 3 changes on edition the game had enough room to develop the system, they just didn't. I also doubt completely that it was intended for discouraging combat, more probably it simply sounded realistic for the developers at the time (it isn't). And if it was intended as such, it is a poorly choice. Encouraging lateral thinking by making default response be covered by bad rules, seriously? This is obnoxious.
                    -----

                    Back to the topic at hand as I didn't mean to have such a vague sounding post originally (sorry about that...), I don't think there is going to be an official crossover outside the translation guides. Anyone who wants to do a crossover can/will and those who want no one touching their preferred game line will be able to stay within it without fear of official designers trying to link things.

                                Originally posted by Peraxis View Post
                                I see WoD and CofD as two separate games. I have had one person describe WoD as 'Story with a game system tacked on' and CofD as 'A game system with a story tacked on'.
                                I would describe them as different games with different ideas, though I prefer CofD personally.

                                I mean, the only WoD games that I can still get myself to care about would be Wratih: The Oblivion, which I have only played once and Mage: The Ascension, in which I only care about the Technocracy to be honest.

                                CofD's system is more elegant and basically superior in every way, instead of having to think about required successes, difficulty, penalties and bonuses whenever I want any kind of roll (and I have to make like 3-4 every combat turn...), I only need to ask myself "what's the penalty?".

                                CofD resembles the First Edition of the original WoD to an extreme degree, filled with mysteries and possibilities and new ideas that were slowly exhausted and eaten up by the metaplot as time went on.

                                Whether that is a good thing or a bad thing is up to you to decide.

                                Originally posted by Peraxis View Post
                                I have a number of friends who hate MtAw because of the Atlantis backstory and the appearance of being more spellfocused (instead of just saying what you want to do with the dots you have). And I have a feeling that this is a true statement, more so when you look at CofD 2e.
                                It's also wrong.

                                I used to love Mage: The Ascension.

                                Back when it was released, I could shittalk on Awakening all day for not doing X or having removed Y or whatever, and I would often do so.

                                But then Secrets of the Ruined Temple came out and I got caught up in all the mysteries and weirdness of Awakening, I found that the gnostic themes resonated deeply with my own religion (considering that I am Gnostic, this should surprise no one), I liked playing a refugee, fearing for his life and wearing a fake name in a prison-world overseen by harsh gods who could barely care about what went on below. I loved the feeling of danger as I investigated the shadows of Awakening, as I questioned existence itself and asked whether any of this was even real, I loved the symbolism and occultism and esotericism everywhere.

                                And most of all, I love the fact that casting a spell is actually possible without three hours of arguing followed by two hours of whiplashes for daring to light a fire with magic. Ascension had a system for casting spells, it had one in First Edition, in Second Edition, in Revised Edition and it still has one in M20, this system consists of throwing a bunch of successes into effects and it very quickly got immensely broken.

                                In Revised, the most balanced of them all, I could have a constant effect that reduced all Difficulties to 3 and went on forever and ever and ever and I could just run it as "well im lucky u see". Sure, the Storyteller could declare it as vulgar, but that'd be a storyteller decision, and one that is easily fixed by simply casting such a spell as conditional so it triggers in specific circumstances and I suddenly just get a stroke of luck when necessary.

                                But that's all balanced and okay, because if I got more than ten Paradox, I might as well rip my character sheet in two due to the extreme harshness of Paradox and the fact that the game named Mage incentivizes you to use as little magic as possible.

                                Meanwhile, Awakening had an actual functioning system that would let me cast spells and be an actual modern-day sorcerer. My first Awakening character could bend space and the minds of men, he could step through walls and move himself along the hidden lines of reality that no one else could see, and he was awesome. Our cabal was filled with symbolism and hidden ideologies and agendas and the political landscape was a breath of fresh air from the Ascension War. It was a labyrinth of smoke and mirrors where everyone could be an enemy and we could trust no one, where every Pentacle mage could be a Seer working for the Enemy in disguise, where even Sleepers could be puppets on the strings of unforgiving gods and their cruel servants.

                                And in that moment, I fell in love.

                                Originally posted by Peraxis View Post
                                There have been a few sessions (in CofD 1e), where my group didn't roll a single dice. WoD I think could support this type of 'RP first, game second' style more than CofD 1e could. (I'm not sure how 2e handles, but given that bonus/penalties have been coded via conditions/tilts, I don't know if it would work out very well.).
                                I'll be the first to tell you that Conditions are annoying to keep track of, but I mean come on they are literally the same as just going "you're sick and must make a Stamina + Resolve roll every month, success abates the disease while failure causes it to worsen. Dramatic failure makes it terminal and exceptional success causes it to disappear." The difference is just that they have names now, and are baked into the core of the system instead of being stuff you make up halfway through.

                                Originally posted by Peraxis View Post
                                On the topic of conditions/tilts, it wasn't until these were created for 2e that I understood some people's hate for what was nWoD over oWoD. That oWoD was doing its thing to be a roleplaying system and that nWoD was moving more towards a rollplaying game (that D&D seems to focus on in some groups).
                                The most intense roleplaying session I've ever had was in a game of DnD 4e, which everyone calls "a game designed as an MMO" while some of my most boring game have been oWoD's shitty four-roll combat system.
