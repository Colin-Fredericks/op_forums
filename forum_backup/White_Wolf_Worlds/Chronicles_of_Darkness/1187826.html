    Hard Magic and Soft Magic in Chronicles of Darkness

    02-20-2018, 10:09 AM
    Howdy. As I'm prone to do, I was binging storytelling videos on youtube right as Hello Future Me posted his analysis of Hard and Soft magic systems. My first thought ran to RPGs (Werewolf the Forsaken specifically), thinking that since this was a tabletop game, the magic system must be diamond hard. As I watch more though, I realized that while there are hard rules in every gameline, within those rules there is lots of latitude for making new powers and abilities, which is a characteristic of a soft magic system. Since different gamelines may have different characteristics of each magic system, I thought it may be interesting to open it up for discussion.

    How hard or soft is the magic system in each CofD gameline? Do they vary from game to game?

          When you create or improvise a new effect in a soft magic system, you do it because you're a magic user and magic can do anything.

          When you create or improvise a new effect in a hard magic system, you do it because your understanding of the rules of magic tells you it should work, or you found a loophole in existing rules (possibly with a heavy cost involved) or even because your superior understanding tells you the thing everyone thought impossible is actually possible if done the right way.

          As such, all magic systems available for player use in CofD (and most those antagonist-only as well) are hard magic systems. The only exceptions are the entities which explicitly operate at plot level, generally limited to Rank 6+ ephemeral beings and some big cosmic beings such as the God-Machine, Principle, Exarchs/Oracles and such.

                                      Edit: Sorry, slow to type apparently. Got ninjaed on some of this, but I'm feel a bit lazy about rewriting it all.

                                      Well... no... the Hard/Soft divide Second Chances is basing things on is not a binary, but a spectrum. The videos in question use Harry Potter as an example of something that's very much in the middle. The Potterverse looks solidly hard magic on the surface (wave a spell and speak the incantation, thus X happens), a deeper look shows a lot of soft elements as well, because the audience never really knows why Potterverse magic is the way it is, and how to slot in all the exceptions that aren't explained. For example, some people in Potterverse are parcelmouths. Why? Nobody knows. It's just a thing that happens. This is clearly soft magic. The Potterverse magic is primarily accessed in hard magic ways, but is effectively soft at its core as for inexplicable reasons (beyond authorial intent) those rules get broken all the time; leaving the true capabilities and limitations of magic unknown.

                                      Most (though not all) RPGs do fall on the hard side of things, simply because designing a game set in a magical world otherwise is incredibly difficult. The CofD is definitely on the hard side of the spectrum, though not 100% as the general setting of the CofD allows for plenty of soft magic elements (esp. in ST controlled major NPCs).

                                      To the original question, the major player characters, regardless of flexibility are generally all equally hard magic in design. The best sources of soft magic for players are in the minor templates and Hunter: the Vigil. A lot of Endowments are very soft, because Hunter's limitations allow the game to follow the "Arthurian" approach to soft magic pointed out in the videos. Just as we need no real explanation of how Excalibur works, came into being, or why in any given telling of the King Arthur Story, Endowments can be very vague, because the players are only going to be dealing with the specific power or handful of special items they'll get.

                                      Similarly, I expect Deviant to be a CofD game actually on the soft side of the spectrum because - again - it doesn't really matter. Renegades have the powers that they have, so they don't need a larger hard framework to explain it all into.

                                                  It’s also worth noting that Sanderson was talking about writing, not games. A game requires a certain degree of hardness in its magic mechanics in order for the players to effectively manipulate them. But that doesn’t need to have any bearing on the way the magic is written within the story.

                                                  Using the CofD line I am most familiar with as an example, the magic of vampiric disciplines is pretty rock hard as a game mechanic. The player knows exactly what their character is capable of. They know there are 10 standard disciplines, each with 5 levels of mastery, and the specific effects of each level, plus two forms of blood sorcery, which follows its own set of rules and each has rituals with specific effects, and coils of the dragon. As players, we all know exactly how to execute all of these magical elements within the rules of the game. But from a writing standpoint, is it really all that well understood? In novels and short stories set in the Requiem universe, the rules of the magic system are often quite a lot softer. It’s generally understood that vampires’ powers fall into the broad categories of manipulating minds, manipulating emotions, manipulating perceptions, enhancing physical abilities, and blood magic. And it’s generally understood which clans are most known for which kind of magic. It’s generally known that older or more experienced vampires can do more impressive things with their powers. But it doesn’t get talked about in the same terms as it does in the rules. Vampires don’t think of Disciplines as one of 10 specific collections of powers on a 1 to 5 scale. They don’t think of Vitae costs in specific numbers. Heck, it’s said that in Rome, the Julii didn’t even realize they were using magic, they just thought people followed their orders because that was the natural way of things.

                                                  So, I think it’s very possible for magic systems to be hard in terms of game mechanics and soft in terms of narrative. This is one of the ways that games differ from books as a storytelling medium. In a game, a magic system has the luxury of being as narratively soft as it wants, while still being able to solve problems without breaking Sanderson’s First Law, because unlike a novel, a game can communicate to its audience outside of the narrative, through its mechanics. The audience can understand perfectly how the magic works mechanically, while not having a clue how it works narratively, and generally won’t feel cheated by its ability to solve problems.

                                                          Let’s set CofD aside for a moment. Look at Star Wars. The Force is extremely soft as a magic system. Sure, we might know a few common elements of force power - telekinesis, mind tricks, and sometimes shooting lightning if you’re evil. It also has some very vaguely defined and inconsistent perceptive abilities involving searching or reaching out with one’s feelings. Apparently it’s an energy that surrounds, penetrates, and binds the universe together. Except when it’s microorganisms in your cells. And it has a will of its own. Except when it doesn’t. Not the softest of magic systems, but generally pretty soft.

                                                          The force doesn’t stop being a soft magic system when you make a Star Wars video game and attatch it to a bunch of systems, with force meters, and light and dark side points, and specifically defined powers with specific effects within the rules that govern the video game. We, as players, understand that these systems are necessary abstractions of the soft magic system that is the force, required to allow us to manipulate it within the rigid system of rules that a computer is capable of understanding and executing. Likewise, when you play one of the various Star Wars tabletop RPGs, the hardness of the game mechanics governing force use are understood to be an abstraction, and not a precise representation of the rules that govern the Force within the fiction of the Star Wars universe. That doesn’t change the fact that the Force is a pretty soft magic system overall.

                                                          Again, we’re trying to apply a concept that was invented for analysis of written fiction to the medium of games. Magic hardness and softness as Sanderson described it has to be understood in terms of the narrative, not the mechanics, because he was writing about narratives.
