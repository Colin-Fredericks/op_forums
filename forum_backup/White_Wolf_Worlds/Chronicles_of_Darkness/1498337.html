    Chronicles of Darkness as Action Horror

    11-29-2022, 03:53 PM
    Chronicles of Darkness' combat is very deadly and realistic. People die pretty fast, and fights are generally treated the same way they would be in reality. Healing is also very limited, especially for mortals, and surviving a fight could put you out of action in weeks. This is all to help simulate the horror genre and feeling of a horror protagonist.

    Combat in Chronicles of Darkness is very strategic. If the players are in combat, they should have done massive preparations beforehand that will make combat be heavily in their favor. Even then, combat should be the climax of a story from what I understand, or at least something fairly rare with perhaps some exceptions.

    But there are many horror genres, one of which is action horror. In this case, I'm thinking of things like Resident Evil, Silent Hill, zombie fiction in general, From Dusk Till Dawn, The Mummy, Army of Darkness, Fear, and Condemned: Criminal Origins. In this genre, the protagonists get in a lot of fights and often have to fight through situations where they don't have a lot of time to prepare. Fighting their way out of a zombie-infested lab for instance. In some cases, the combat is constant. A lot of action horror, particularly video games, rely on healing items. But action horror movies generally don't.

    So, I have two questions:
    1. Can Chronicles of Darkness emulate or be altered to fit the action horror genre?
    2. If it can, what are some of the alterations would you recommend for that purpose?

    To clarify, I'm mainly talking about mortal or hunter games. Making Werewolf: The Forsaken action horror is pretty easy, and the same can be true of Deviant and probably a few other games as well. Though, advice for other gamelines could work too.

      Random hack ideas to make combat less deadly, but still exciting.
      1. Give the entire group a bonus health pool of "close calls" which can be spent one for one to reduce damage. Effectively an extended health track, but shared by everyone so any injury is felt in tension by everyone at the table. Has the benefit of encouraging communal resource sharing, though once the pool is empty everyone becomes equally squishy which may incur some narrative dissonance.
      2. Treat the health bar as a buildup til injury, with each heath-box representing a cut, bruise or near miss. Once a player's whole track fills up they empty it and take an apporpriate tilt, which follows them for the foreseeable future. This lets you simulate the sort of gradual injuries action heroes often take, slowly taking on my physical disadvantages. Perhaps you set a maximum number of tilts equal to a character's Stamina, though TBH I don't see a reason for it, death is a bad motivator. Downside is the whole health system gets wonky when you throw in Supernatural Splats and healing, though not in an unbearable way. NPCs also need a wholly different method of injury tracking, since combat would take ages if they were ask hard to knock down as PCs.
      3. Borrow from the consolations/complications in the Storypath system. Players can reduce harm by voluntarily taking narrative complications instead: The monster's claw doesn't cut your artery, but it does destroy your smart phone. The witches curse ages you cosmetically, but does not incur physical harm, now you look like a horrific zombie until the curse is lifted. You escape the car crash with only scrapes and bruises, but now you are without a car and the flames have announced your location to all the gargoyles. Etc. This requires the most creativity, since the Storyteller has to constantly be coming up with relevant complications scaled to the situation, but it's the most exciting when done right. Arguably more exciting than normal injury mechanics, since all the 'injuries' have clear cut narrative weight. Again though, requires a storyteller with above average improvisational storytelling skills.

          I think you kind of answered your own question: look at the more action oriented (or action friendly) supernaturals for how they make action-horror more viable. Though the 1e Mirrors book had a template for more exceptional mortals that were meant to handle a bit more of the action horror side of things if you weren't going full supernatural.

          Action horror is also a pretty broad range. Just looking at the Resident Evil franchise, there are entries (esp. the earliest) where you don't need to change any of the CofD rules to handle those stories, as they're more survival horror than action horror, and getting into too many fights is not a good idea vs. avoiding the. Some entries are more of Tier 3 Hunters or the Mirrors exceptional mortals rules (or both) so you can do some of the more badass stuff in the last RE games or a lot of the supplemental fiction without losing some of the lethality. And some entries would probably require going full Deviant to make work (Alice from the RE movies being a prime example).

          The answer to your questions very much depend on how much action tropes are going to weigh against horror tropes for any adaptation. A lot of zombie action-horror stuff can be handled with just making sure the players make characters built for the task. Making something that can handle the big end fight of From Dusk Till Dawn which is really just action with supernatural enemies at that point, is a lot more work to get the CofD to do well.

                          I've swiped a few things from Savage Worlds for use in more action-y games. These are meant to allow the non-combat characters to have more agency in a fight.
                          Additional Combat Options: Tricks and Support

                          This system is something I'm cribbing from Savage Worlds, because it's a fairly elegant method for handling all manner of ad-hoc actions a character might try to even the odds in a fight, either to aid your companions or hinder your foes.

                          Tricks

                          A Trick is almost anything a character does to make things more difficult for their foes. Tricks include embarrassing an opponent, throwing sand in his eyes, staring him down with a steely gaze, or anything else a clever player can think of to rattle his enemy and put him off-balance. To perform a Trick, the player describes the action and works with the ST to determine the most appropriate skill to roll for it.

                          Dice Pool: One Power or Finesse Attribute + One Mental, Physical, or Social Skill vs. an appropriate Finesse + Resistance combination.
                          Target: One foe within Close or Short Range
                          Action: Contested

                          Roll Results

                          Success: You embarrass your opponent, throw sand in their eyes, stare them down, kick them in the jibblies, confuse them with technical jargon, or anything else you can think of to rattle your enemy and put them off-balance. The foe gains your choice of the Distracted or Vulnerable Tilt.
                          Exceptional Success: You inflict the desired Tilt, plus an additional Personal Tilt appropriate to the action (defaults to adding both the Distracted and Vulnerable Tilts to the target if nothing else is suitable).
                          Failure: Your trick fails to achieve the desired result.
                          Dramatic Failure: Your foe turns the tables on you. You gain the foe's choice of the Vulnerable or Distracted Tilt.

                          Special: Modifiers might include cover, certain Conditions, situational factors (using a slur you know the foe reacts strongly to), and possibly Equipment Bonuses. Using the same or similar action repeatedly quickly grows less effective; subsequent attempts at the same Trick in the same Scene count as Successive Attempts and suffer the appropriate penalty.


                          DISTRACTED
                          Personal Tilt

                          Description: The character is briefly irritated, angered, pained, confused, or otherwise unable to put their full concentration on what they're doing at the moment.
                          Effect: The character suffers a -2 penalty to their next Instant Action. If they would suffer this Tilt again before they've acted, they gain the Vulnerable Tilt instead.
                          Causing The Tilt: Tricks, environmental factors, sudden surprises.
                          Ending The Tilt: This Tilt lasts the end of the character's next turn.

                          VULNERABLE
                          Personal Tilt

                          Description: The character has briefly left themselves wide open and struggles to defend themselves against their foes.
                          Effect: The next action taken against the character gains a +2 bonus. If they would suffer this Tilt again before they've acted, they gain the Distracted Tilt instead.
                          Causing The Tilt: Tricks, environmental factors, sudden surprises.
                          Ending The Tilt: This Tilt lasts until an action is taken against the character or the end of the character's next turn.

                          Support

                          This is an alternative to the Teamwork rules, allowing for more flexibility. If you want to help an ally with a task, and you can think of some method of doing so with a Skill not normally associated with that task, you may attempt a Support action instead of a normal Teamwork action. You and the Storyteller should be creative when making Support rolls. You might use Survival to find useful herbs to help a Medicine check, or use Science to calculate a Tokoyo Drift to aid a character's Drive check, or provide general encouragement ("You can do it!") with a Persuasion check.

                          You can use a Support action to narratively affect an opponent, but the only effect it can have is aiding an ally's rolls. If you want to "trip up" a feral vampire to help a friend make a Weaponry attack against it, the character you're aiding gains the bonus but you don't actually inflict any Tilts or Conditions on the vampire. For that, use a Trick instead. The advantage of using Support over a Trick in this manner is that the roll is not Contested.

                          Dice Pool: Any justified by the narrative.
                          Target: One specific Skill used by another character.
                          Action: Instant

                          Roll Results
                          Success: You add +1 die to the character's next roll with that Skill within the Scene. To add more than this, use a regular Teamwork action instead.
                          Exceptional Success: The character resolves the Inspired Condition on that roll in addition (the roll is considered an Exceptional Success on three successes instead of five, and they gain a Beat)
                          Failure: Your attempted Support does not aid the character.
                          Dramatic Failure: Your attempted support backfires, inflicting the Distracted Tilt on the character.

                          When the cat's a Stray, the mice will pray...

                              Here's some other ways to make the PCs more like action protagonists:
                              • Don't roll to stay conscious at full bashing; just let them keep going so they can deal out more damage (alternatively, flee if overpowered).
                              • Don't use the bleeding out rule as-is. Instead, let them bleed out at the end of the scene if they don't receive basic medical attention before then. Makes things a lot less lethal.
                              • Reduce healing times. How much depends on the pacing of your game, but bashing should rapidly disappear after each combat, and lethal should be able to at least be reduced between combat scenes unless they're back to back. Allowing successful first aid rolls to remove a small amount of lethal damage can also be used.
                              • Apply less Tilts that reduce the PCs effectiveness. More effective PCs means more focused damage on monsters.
                              • Only apply the above suggestions to PCs to make them stand out against NPCs.
                              • And most importantly: Don't design too dangerous encounters and antagonists. You could reduce damage output, make the antagonists more survival oriented (i.e. they try to flee instead of trying to kill the PCs), and/or make them threaten something other than the PCs' health. If done properly, you could make the antagonists seem threatening without deadly risk to the PCs even if you don't change any actual rules.
