            One of the ideas I have used for an Asian American changelings was the idea of Cathayans. These are Burmese, Cambodians, Chinese, Japanese, Koreans, Philippines, Vietnamese, and Laotians that were made the Changelings of WESTERN True Fae. Through these characters, I hoped to explore the long, historical connection between the East and West. This ranged from the colonization of Asian nations by Western powers, mass immigration from China and Japan to the United States during the 19th century, U.S. occupation of Japan after WW2, the Vietnam war, and the swarms of Asian students that came to study at major US universities.

            I also hoped to touch upon historical and folklore that touched upon this connection like Madame Butterfly. During the Tokugawa era there was a Youkai used for anti-Christian propaganda. It was a mantis that told god the names of people who did not pray correctly to god so they could be killed. My idea is to make this youkai actually a Western racketeer that kidnapped ordinary Japanese for Western Gentry to satisfy their craving and fascination with Asian culture. However, I have forgotten what this Mantis Youkai's name is.

            Does anyone know it's name?

                    Here are some more ideas about the Cathayans.

                    The Cathayansare more lost than even most changelings.

                    1. When the Cathayans escapes Arcadia and the Hedge, rather than ending up in their homeland, they find themselves in the West; Europe, North America..If they are lucky, perhaps Australia or Russia.
                    2. Because they spent their durance in service to one of the Western Fae, they have developed the mannerisms and social perspectives of Western culture. On top of that, they now speak English, French, or German better than their native language (Cathayans get the bilingual or trilingual merit for free) As such, they are treated with hostility and suspicion by Eastern Changelings.
                    3. When a Cathayan returns from the Hedge, they are one of the few types of changelings that LOOK different than their original appearance. This is because their mask and mien now reflects Western biases about Asian peoples and cultures.

                        I am very fond of the Cathayans, because I enjoy using them to show the strong historical connections between the East and West. I built the different Cathayans based on the historical relationship between the U.S. and different Asian countries. I also use the Cathayans to touch on Western bigotry towards Asian Americans and Asians in general.

                        Here is my description of Chinese Cathayans.The irony is that, because imperial China limited the Western barbarians to Canton, the changelings of the West very quickly discovered that they could access the Western hedge in western districts. Just as Western mortals showed a fascination with the China, so was this fascination reflected with the faerie creatures of the West. Changelings from England had come to China, hoping to trade for tea, spices, and porcelain, while changelings from the United States were lured by the Chinese market and a fascination with Chinese cultures. Even the Gentry/True Fae developed the same curiosity and interest their former slaves possessed. For that matter, Chinese men would sale to California to take part in the American gold rush or tried to make their fortunes in U.S. farming and building the Transcontinental railroad. In this way many Chinese found themselves falling into the nets of Western Gentry. Yet in the East, Western businessmen found themselves frustrated by their limited access to the Chinese market and lack of interest in Western goods. So, it was the changeling courts, who first smuggled Opium into China for greater access to the Chinese market and racketeers from the American South who used Opium to lure Chinese men, women, and children into the slavery of Western Fae. After the first Opium War, thousands upon thousands of Chinese were given to Western Gentry for they had gained a taste for Chinese women as their consorts and Chinese men to build their kingdoms in Arcadia. This steady stream was slowed to a trickle, when the Communists took over China and closed it to the West. Though a slow yet steady stream still came from Hong Kong. When Nixon reopened China and Chinese began coming to the West as students and then workers in the United States and Europe.

                        The highest concentration of Chinese Cathayans can be found in California, though there are some extremely old and powerful Chinese Cathayans living in solitude throughout North America and Western Europe.

                        Relations Between Chinese Cathayans and the Western Courts: Most Chinese Cathayans join the seasonal courts, THOUGH THEY USE Chinese themes for their season and practice old Chinese traditions associated with the seasons.

                        Relations Between Chinese Cathayans and Chinese Courts: The changeling courts of China treat the Chinese Cathayans with hostility and suspicion. They regard the Cathayans as corrupted/tainted, and no longer true Mandarins yet not truly Americans, either. Ironically, they still consider them Chinese and under Chinese law. The Western Courts have tried to use Chinese Cathayans as a bridge between them and the Chinese courts, yet this does not go well.

                            Japanese Cathayans: Because the Japanese very quickly adapted to Western technology and political structure, from the end of the Tokugawa period to the beginning of WW2, there was never the steady stream of Japanese disappearing into the slavery of Western Gentry like other Asian societies; Burma, Cambodia, China, India, Laos, and Vietnam. Though a handful of Japanese disappeared each year. This DID NOT protect the Japanese who immigrated to Hawaii, Europe, and the mainland United States, with several thousand disappearing into Western Arcadia from 1868 to 1911. After the devastation of WW2 and the United States occupied Japan, the chaos allowed many American racketeers to capture and ship thousands more to Western Arcadia. Japanese students also made tempting targets for US loyalists and racketeers. After the year 2000, the number of Japanese disappearing into Western arcadia has become a slow but steady drip, drip, drip. Although the number of Japanese immigrating to the US to study or work has shrunk, because of Japanese media, there is still a strong interest in Japan from Western Gentry. While it is dangerous, there are organizations of US racketeers posing as western businessmen in Japan to kidnap Japanese girls and women to be consorts for the Western Gentry.
                            The largest populations of Japanese Cathayans can be found in Brazil, Hawaii, California, Oregon, and Washington, though Japanese Cathayans can be found in Canada, Mexico, and Europe. Interestingly, the US population of Japanese Cathayans is disproportionally large in regards to the Japanese American population. This is due to a combination of the longevity the wyrd provides and some Japanese Cathayans have not officially become US citizens even if they have lived in the US for decades or more than a century.

                            Relations Between Japanese Cathayans and Western Courts: Like Chinese Cathayans, most Japanese Cathayans join the seasonal courts, but with Japanese themes and rituals. Interestingly, Japanese Cathayans tend to be more traditional than most modern Japanese. This is especially true in Hawaii.

                            Relations Between Japanese Cathayans and the Japanese Courts: Unlike the Chinese, the Japanese and Japanese Cathayans get along pretty well. However, the Japanese courts do not consider the Cathayans true Japanese anymore.

                                Please, please, do not use the word "Cathayans" here. This is an archaic word used during a period when Europeans knew little about what was going on in central and eastern Asia, derived from the Khitan of northern China and misattributed to other territories of China. Referring to people from Japan or Korea as Cathayans is like referring to Peru as "the East Indies" or to the Spanish and the Irish as "vikings." Korea in particular has a history of resisting foreign invasions, including by the Khitan specifically. It is kind of rude to call them Chinese people.

                                I am just very uncomfortable with this idea of "Cathayan Lost" as explicitly in-world stereotyped peoples in general, and it really distracts from the evocative myths you can draw on that might reflect East Asian interpretations of something like the Durance. Why does Urashima Taro have to run around in cosmically imposed yellowface? He's already got enough to deal with guarding the treasure box that contains the three hundred years that passed on Earth while he was in the Dragon Palace under the ocean depths. If you want to explore themes of human prejudice, do it by having humans show prejudice, not by having the universe itself single out Asian people and displace and literally otherize them.

                                                    Originally posted by Madhatter View Post
                                                    I chose the term Cathayan, because it was the term the Kindred (Vampire: The Masquerade) used for the Kue-jin from Kindred of the East. I should have done more research on the term.
                                                    I figured that's where it probably came from. It was a weird pull when Kindred of the East did it, and one of a lot of things Kindred of the East did that were, if not outright offensive or insensitive, then clumsy at best. It is not a good model to aspire to.

                                                    I'm not sure exactly what you're trying to do in this thread: brainstorm ideas for a fanbook you're writing? Pitch structural elements for a game you're going to play in? Just spitball cool ways you imagine the courts of the Lost might manifest in the countries of East Asia?

                                                    My advice would be to stay grounded, and to write what you know.

                                                    Stay grounded: Don't write material about Asian Lost, write material about Asian Lost. One of the strengths of Lost as a game is the wide breadth of ways in which the world of the fae manifests, and that variety isn't going to be diminished in other parts of the world. Another strength is Lost's potential to focus on relatable human drama. People are people, not just manifestations of the culture they live in. Go anywhere and you'll find rebels, hotheads, the meek, the cold, technocrats, traditionalists, pragmatists, the self-sacrificing and the selfish. Cultures are just circumstances these people find themselves in and navigate. Consequentially, writing a sub-template about specific types of Eastern Lost that manifests differently from Western Lost is missing the universality and the relatable humanness of the concept. Alternate Court structures don't hit this problem nearly as much; a different court structure is simply a different framing in which these human dramas play out, deriving from local historical circumstances and whatever bargains have been struck with patrons who might deal with the Lost. Avoid being monolithic, though. You're not going to have "these are the courts of Japan" or "this is the ruler of the Japanese Lost" any more than you have "these are the courts of the United States" or "this is the reigning leader of the American Lost." The corebook already contains examples of how the seasonal courts are not universal. New Orleans has the Traders' Courts and Ipswitch on the New England coast has the Courts of the Tides. Changeling society is locally focused and has a lot of variations.

                                                    Write what you know: If you don't know what life looks like for a person living in Japan enough to portray an image of it, either don't write about it, or do research until that changes before you do write about it. If you feel more comfortable writing about the Asian-American or Asian-European experience, write that. A first-generation immigrant is going to have a very different outlook from a third or even second generation mixed-race Asian-American who was born in the States speaking English from childhood. If that's the story you're writing about, the court structure overseas probably doesn't have a lot of direct relevance. Even a first-generation immigrant who moves to the West before suffering the Durance isn't going to know anything about how the Lost organize themselves in their home country until they either start corresponding online or take a trip back home and encounter them.

                                                    Just in general, it's a good idea to step back and clearly identify for what purpose you're writing. When you have a clear idea of whether you're writing "game elements for use in a story in an American city with a large Asian demographic" or "sample courts and characters in a domain of Lost in modern Shenzhen facing the reawakening of a Wild Hunt long forestalled and forgotten," it's a lot easier to narrow down resources to draw research from, to outline particular topics you should address and elucidate on, and for others to provide you with advice and ideas on where to go, as opposed to the vague and broad prompt of "Changeling in East Asia: ideas?"
