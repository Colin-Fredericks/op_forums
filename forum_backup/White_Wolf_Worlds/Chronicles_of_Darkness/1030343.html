      Originally posted by wyrdhamster View Post
      Sheriffs in USA are limited law enforcement leaders on very small units.
      They're not necessarily small units, and while limited in some places, they have full authority of the law in most. It's going to depend a lot on the state.

      So by the Status dots system they should be like Status (Police) 5 for local town - as they are the peak of local law enforcement. But this does not sound right, somehow to me. On first thought I would rather point Sheriff as something like Status (Police) 3 figure.
      The sheriff of a small town would be an elected official, and the highest ranking law enforcement officer in the county, so he'd certainly be more well known than 3, and his authority would certainly justify a 5. In a small town the only one who would vie with the sheriff would be a mayor, not counting unique cases. It's also worth noting that, as an elected official, the sheriff can't normally just be fired. A chief of police or police commissioner, both appointed positions, could be.

      Also, deputy sheriffs are about as 'uniforms' in larger cities - i.e. Status (Police) 1, am I getting it right?
      Yes. The sheriff's deputy would usually have a larger area of jurisdiction. Police jurisdiction lines tend to be pretty small, while a sheriff's department watches a whole county, sometimes a state.

      And also do not 'bigger counties' - with proper precincts and Police Chiefs - are over jurisdiction on local Sheriffs?
      Not really. It's going to depend on the situation, and the place.

      You should check out Tales from the 13th Precinct, I'm pretty sure it gets into this.

              Status describes your affiliation with a certain organization or social structure. This has however not to be an official existing group like a police department but can be ajusted to reflect actual dependencies. For a small town, let's call it Springfield, I would advice to not define the organization as "Springfield police" but rather broaden it to something like "Springfield authorities". This could look something like this:
              1 dot
              Lower employees of the town: gardeners, administration workers, tourist information guides etc
              2 dots
              Sheriff deputies, secretary of the mayor
              3 dots
              Higher administrative staff, like the treasurer or something close
              4 dots
              Sheriff, members of the city council
              5 dots
              Mayor

              Note that the 1 dot merrit usually doesn't give you any form of authority in the given organization. On the contrary it makes you easier to be pursuaded by persons in that given organization with a higher status. You do get the benefit of access to facilities and of being known, though. Therefore the example 1 dot police status from the core book lists "paid informant" and not the lowest actual police officer.
              If you do want to limit the status only to the sheriffs department, I would go something like this:

              1 dot
              Secretary, cleaning personal
              2 dots
              Deputies
              3 dots
              -
              4 dots
              Sheriff
              5 dots
              -
              You don't have to use all the dots of the merit. Since the sheriff solely decides on employing and firing staff etc and has usually some political sway and respect in his town, I would rate his position still with 4 dots, while deputies may achieve a third dot if they have accumulated some experience and respect from collegues and the sheriff.

                      State police wouldn't be local police, they wouldn't be responding to something a local sheriff would, usually.                                   You're not really getting it right, so I will try to clarify using the usual case in the US.

                                  The FBI, DEA, ATF, and other "men of letters", also referred to collectively as "Feds", have power everywhere in the US but only deal with national laws. The state police have power everywhere within their state and only deal with state laws which include murder, rape, robbery, and the usual crimes of modern societies. However, the state police give up power to sheriffs and police in most areas when they exist. State police typically patrol highways and state parks. A city should have worked out a contract to employ a police force so their police have power within the city. Outside of such cities, a sheriff has power for a whole county. A sheriff is an elected official. A police chief is typically appointed by a mayor or city council which are elected officials. In all cases, the state police train sheriffs and police unless there is an academy in the area.

                                  This effectively means that within a city the police have power, outside a city the sheriff has power except for state parks and highways where the state police have power unless someone commits a federal crime then the Feds come in. Some places will not have police and/or sheriffs so you keep moving right in that case. Example:

                                  If you commit murder in a city the police will investigate. If you commit the murder outside of the city then the sheriff will investigate. However, if you commit the murder on the highway even within a city then the state police will investigate. If you commit murders in multiple states then the FBI will investigate. If you commit 100 murders within a single state across multiple cities, counties, highways and parks then you will have multiple sheriffs, multiple police forces, and the state police investigating. None of them have to help each other but common sense dictates they would. The FBI could do nothing about it. The counter to that is the police, sheriffs, and state police can always ask for help from other law enforcement agencies.

                                  If you mean Status (law enforcement) then the Feds would be at the top, the state police would be next to them, sheriffs would be in the middle and deputies and police would be on the lower end. If the Status is defined to the local areas then the the police chief would be at the top for the city and the sheriff would be at the top outside of the city. This is assuming an urban area. Rural areas would not have police. Wilderness might not have sheriffs either, but they might have police for a city trapped in the wild.

                                  If you are within a large city then I would exclude everyone but police for simplicity. The fact that it would be sheriff's deputies delivering court orders is not worth bringing into the rules. If the city is smaller, under a million, then put the sheriff at the top and the police chief under the sheriff. Forget the state police as they are largely irrelevant unless there is no police or sheriff around. You would not have police for rural areas so the sheriff is still at the top but no police chief. Sheriff's departments have ranks just like the police do with captains, lieutenants, sergeants, and detectives.

                                  County
                                  Sheriff
                                  Ranked (sergeant, etc.) Deputy
                                  Detective
                                  Deputy

                                  City
                                  Police Chief
                                  Captain
                                  Lieutenant/Sergeant
                                  Detective
                                  Patrol officer

                                  Hybrid (maybe) for medium population area
                                  Sheriff
                                  Police Chief
                                  Ranked officers/deputies
                                  Detectives
                                  Patrol officers and deputies

                                  or

                                  Sheriff/Police Chief
                                  High rank officers/deputies
                                  Low rank officers/deputies
                                  Detectives
                                  Patrol officers and deputies

                                  If the setting was for an entire state and you want a player to have such status then that would be a mess. See example above.

                                                          Diggs described the basic arrangement pretty well. It can actually get pretty complicated depending where you live. I live in the Florida state capital and there are easily over a dozen different law enforcement agencies operating here with varying jurisdictions. For example, there is the city police and the county sheriff that work basically like Diggs described. Then there is Florida State University and FAMU that have their own police departments with enforcement powers on their respective campuses. There are the capitol police who have jurisdiction over the capitol buildings. There is the Florida Highway Patrol and various other state level enforcement agencies with their own jurisdictions (Fish and Wildlife; Agricultural Law Enforcement; Alcoholic Beverages and Tobacco). There are even some school districts (K-12) in Florida that are big enough to have their own police separate from their city or county police.
