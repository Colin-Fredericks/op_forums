Slasher mentions "serial killers and slashers" separately throughout and the Conspiracy introduced in that same book draws a distinction between slashers and serial killers as part of the fact that they specialize in the investigation of serial killings:
                                Much better.

                                Okay, so my bad, Slashers does make a clear distinction between the two terms. Though if I may mention, most of the distinction is made by the fact Slashers don't necessarly fit the exact definition of the serial killer because they don't necessarly follow the same modus operanti, not because they are somehow less mundane or less human. Admittedly that's a bit of a nitpick, but I think it's important, because again, part of what made slashers scary as Hunter antagonists was the fact they could be mundane and were still more human than any supernatural. Dehumanizing them, especially in the contest of this new Code, just turn them into another kind of monster to kill. Don't get me wrong, Slashers should be monstrous, but not in a way that allows hunters to say "well, that's not human, I can kill it just fine."

                                But let's move on and get back to what this thread really is about-- namely the Code.

                                As pointed out by Monica, the Code actually did exist in 1E, just with very different mechanics, so I suggest we go check how it worked here and see how it compares to the 2E playtest version.

                                On a note, I noticed that the very first thing the appendix on the Code tells us is that Hunters shouldn't make morality rolls on regular basis:

                                Simply put, if hunters are held to the same Morality ratings as non-hunter humans, a Hunter: The Vigil game has the potential for the characters to degenerate into low-Morality lunatics in a very short span of time (and that may very well be a game you want to play). If characters are forced to make degeneration rolls for killing a vampire, werewolf or other monster, as they would for killing an innocent human, a dedicated hunter cell is quickly going to find itself making degeneration rolls on a regular basis, and the chances are high that the Storyteller will be spending more time herding the derangement-swamped characters as they rampage across the game setting than actually running any sort of scenario.
                                But admittedly, the new Integrity system in Chronicles of Darkness makes it much easier to get success than with the old Morality one, and hunters now have the benefit of Touchstones, so you could argue it's not that much of a problem anymore. Plus Breaking Points, in contrast with the old degeneration rolls, can actually have a positive effect, since you get Beats from them. So moving on.

                                So based on what I read, Hunters in 1E start out with the same morality system than regular humans (which makes sense, because, you know, they are regular humans, at least they start out as such), and the Code is an option allowing them to modify it so they will be able to kill monsters without suffering degeneration rolls/breaking point every five minutes. The book actually offers three alternative methods to help:

                                The Code: The Vigil changes a person. “The Code” (below) details an alternative set of game rules that can be used to adapt a hunter’s Morality away from the human standard and into a Vigil-specific Code over time. While this system has its own drawbacks (social penalties and hunt-specifi c derangements known as Tells), it works well with a kill-heavy game, allowing characters to continue their hunt long after the normal Morality system would have sent them spiraling into madness.
                                Monsters Don’t Count: As discussed earlier in the book, hunters may not identify superhuman monsters as human. Storytellers may take this perspective into consideration, ruling that assaulting or killing monsters doesn’t count as a Morality sin at all, and not requiring hunters to make any sort of Morality degeneration roll for actions taken against a monstrous foe. This ruling skews the Morality scale in a major way, and Storytellers who utilize it should be cautioned that it lifts one of the major weights of consequence of hunter characters’ actions, but may allow for a more “heroic” game.
                                It’s Not Like They’re Really People: Slightly less extreme than the Monster’s Don’t Count theme is the idea that, while taking a sinful action against a monster may be bad, it’s nowhere near as morally damning as taking the same action against a normal human. This Morality variation gives characters bonus dice (+1 to +3 depending on how inhuman the monster is) whenever the player rolls for degeneration for an action related to that monster. A Storyteller might give a player a +1 bonus to a degeneration roll for an action taken against a seemingly human magic wielder, +2 for a sin against a humanoid lycanthrope, and +3 extra dice when making a roll for slaying a human who’s already dead (vampire, ghost, Reanimated, zombie).
                                Do Nothing: The Vigil is a morally perilous journey, and holding strictly to the standard human Morality scale only emphasizes that danger. Appointing oneself judge, jury and executioner is a path fraught with risks — physically, emotionally and spiritually. By forgoing any of the options that soften these dangers, players may have the opportunity to address a broad variety of moral issues themselves: where do you draw the line between duty and obsession? How does the punisher avoid becoming as bad as the criminal? And, finally, when the body count rises, what separates the hunter from his prey?
                                As you can see, the players are given multiple alternatives depending on the type of hunter chronicle they want to play, which already is more open-minded compared to what we saw in the playtest, albeit admittedly more chaotic too.

                                In 2E, the "Do nothing" no longer is much worth, since the new Integrity system allows you to customize what triggers a Breaking Point or what doesn't, and Hunter already have Inner Breaking Points seperate from this system anyway, while the "It's Not Like They're Really People" has been removed entirely.

                                Meanwhile, the "Monster Don't Count" has been made integral to the system, albeit with some nuance it somewhat (you still get breaking points the first time you torture a monster for example). I would like to point out this is the option the book advises NOT to use, precisely because it means hunters can now slaughter monsters indiscriminately and face no consequences regarding their sanity. So already back then, the game was suggesting hunters should not be allowed to kill supernatural beings in mass without risking their Integrity in the process.

                                Now let's focus on the actual Code, AKA Option number 1, in the 1E Book:

                                The mechanics of the Code are deceptively simple at heart. As a hunter progresses on her Vigil, her obsession with the hunt pushes her, at various points, to take actions that are a sin on the normal Morality code. At these times, called Trigger Points, the player may decide that her character’s Morality changes, and one of the “sins” associated with the normal human Morality rating is changed to an edict that applies directly to her particular Vigil. The original “sin” is no longer a part of her Code; it is replaced by a new sin, just as limiting, but more specific to her experiences and goals. Further along in the Vigil, another Trigger Point may be reached, and another of the hunter’s Morality sins might be replaced by a Vigil-specific mandate, gradually customizing the basic tenets of not only how she behaves, but also how she views the world around her.
                                For a hunter to deviate from that common recognition in a single “sin” is enough to start to crack the façade of normalcy. [...] In terms of game mechanics, each deviation is represented by a cumulative -1 Social penalty (to a maximum -5 dice) when dealing with other mortals. Every time the hunter’s Code shifts away from normal human Morality, interacting socially with other human beings becomes more diffi cult, until, at later stages, the hunter is likely to be viewed by nonhunters as a monster himself.
                                Each shift also brings with it a crack in the hunter’s own psyche — regardless of drive, a hunter is, at heart, human himself. Thus, the same obsession that drives him away from the rest of humanity also warps him internally. This damage is represented by the accumulation of hunt-related derangements. Each time a tenet of the hunter’s Morality Code shifts away from the human norm, he automatically gains a derangement specifi cally suited to the shift in ethics that has occurred. These derangements, as detailed below, differ from standard derangements in that they manifest only in relation to the hunter’s Vigil.
                                So, to summarize: in 1E, the Code essentially was a set of rules representing a Hunter gradually warping his own Morality code compared to most people's standards in an effort to cope with the horrible things hunting monsters makes him do on regular basis without losing too much of his Integrity. Which is done at the cost of alienating himself from normal humans (hence the social penalty) and damaging his own sanity.

                                Now, I see how that system no longer works for 2E, seeing how the Chronicles of Darkness' new Integrity system makes morality something subjective and no longer goes with the idea humanity commonly accepts some values. But personally, I do distinguish a major theme in this Code-- that going around killing people, monsters or humans, is still bad, and while you can lessen its impact with these rules so your character won't become a NPC, it will still mess you up and alienate you from humanity.

                                With all that said, I will let you guys judges. How do you think the 1E Code compares to the 2E one, and vice-versa?

                                        Thank you, Satchel, for your review of my example. Perhaps I should have been a bit more detailed with it, but I have a tendency to be a bit briefer in my ramblings when responding by phone.

                                        Ultimately, I'm not saying that humanity should be seen as inherently inferior to supernatural creatures, or that hunters that feel that way should be seen as normal or healthy. I'm saying that since some hunters draw the line of what constitutes a person in different places, the mechanics of the Code should allow for that kind of adjustment instead of treating it as though those arguments between hunters about what constitutes a person are mere words hiding the fact that they all agree. I don't think that taking the merciful approach should make a character more likely to gain the Merciless Condition than someone who is merciless to begin with. I also think that breaking points should relate more to a hunter's world view on an individual basis, rather than having them be universal with the possible exception of one. If Mr. K says that all monsters are horrible and irredeemable creatures that need to die, he should be disturbed if he sees a monster engage in selfless acts, as he becomes haunted by the fear that maybe these creatures are people, and that he's been acting as a murderer rather than a protector. I want hunters to question their actions regardless of philosophy, and I want zealots of the cause to be in greater danger of losing themselves.
