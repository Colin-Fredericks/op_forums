    General (newbie) questions

    07-31-2025, 01:45 PM
    Hello, everyone.

    I'm a long time Werewolf: the Apocalypse game master (since the mid 90s), but recently I got my hands on some 1e Chronicles of Darkness books, including the Core "blue book" and Forsaken 1e Core Book.

    I just loved Forsaken and found it, in many ways, superior than Apocalypse - but don't get me wrong, I still love Apocalypse's lore, with all its quirks and idiosyncrasies. What I don't like are some of its themes and moods which I find extremely outdated and stuck into the 90s zeitgeist. But I digress.

    Anyway, I have a few questions regarding Forsaken:

    1) As I said, I've got the 1e Forsaken core, but that's the only W:tF book I have now. How does it compare with Forsaken 2e? Which one do you think is better, and why? And should I stick with 1e or get the 2e core book?

    2) According to he book list I found, 1e has dozens of sourcebooks, much more than 2e. Are Forsaken's older sourcebooks still compatible (rules-wise and, particularly, lore-wise) to 2e?

    3) One of the things I loved in Apocalypse was that the storyteller could make the chronicle as heroic, hopeful, and epic as he wanted. Forsaken visibly works on a smaller scale (at least that's the impression I got from reading it). But, since CoD is much more of a toolkit than anything, is it possible to make it more heroic, with the PCs as the unabashed good guys of the setting?

    I guess that's all for now. Thanks in advance for your answers.

      A good amount of 1e lore is still compatible and Forsaken has the smallest 1e library of the "Big Three" gamelines that weren't conceived as limited runs, but some major general ludonarrative changes in 2e:

      1. Werewolves have to Hunt in order to maintain their spiritual stability — like many monsters in 2e's change from Morality stats to Integrity stats, Harmony is no longer a purely up-down ladder of sins that you spend Experience to increase your rating it, instead having breaking points toward the Flesh (numerically higher ratings) and toward the Spirit (lower ratings), and Harmony (or 10-Harmony) is the dicepool werewolves use to Reach across the Gauntlet. Harmony too high or too low makes Death Rage easier to trigger, and higher Primal Urge makes it last longer. Instead of suffering Essence Bleed that risks them falling into slumber, powerful werewolves need to hunt more often to avoid losing Essence and gaining Harmony (eventually locking them out of the Shadow until they rebalance, possibly by eating humans).

      2. Werewolves can reproduce amongst themselves — Ghost Children are no longer a consistent phenomenon and the Oath of the Moon's commandment that the Uratha Shall Cleave to the Human is instead both a more general commentary on the need for socialization and general maintenance of Harmony and an underpinning of the fact that the default werewolf pack is now more than just the werewolves that make up its hunting party, including Wolf-Blooded aides and mortal periphery.

      3. We as readers of the extended booklist now know more than modern werewolves about the origins of the Uratha — the particulars are still obscured, but we know Father Wolf wasn't a spirit and Pangaea/the Border Marches was a border dimension between Flesh and Spirit where Pangaeans (eldritch beings that were pillars of the world like animals and the seasons — some of them implied but not outright stated to be the origins of the Hosts) dwelled until Urfarah's death destroyed or irrevocably sealed it away (its replacement with the Gauntlet being ambiguously concurrent or subsequent to this). Werewolves were metaphysically adapted to/native to Pangaea and their troubles with keeping balanced since the Sundering is partially due to it being unreachable since some time in the last 7000 years — pre-Sundering werewolves are discussed in the earliest part of the Dark Eras books, right on the edge of prehistory.

      4. In addition to the Moon-Banished and Earthbound like Gurdilag and the rest, Night Horrors: Shunned by the Moon details another odd out-of-context problem for werewolves in the form of their predecessors the Geryo, a collection of unique hunters Father Wolf created in yet more primordial times who were locked away in Pangaea (transferred to the Gauntlet in the Sundering) due to being too destructive for their job as hunters and enforcers of the boundary between worlds.

      5. The changes to the Ephemeral Being mechanics between 1e and 2e mean, among other things, that more powerful spirits don't have a stronger mechanical incentive to anchor themselves in the Flesh (Essence bleed drains 1 point per hour regardless of Rank), but they do tend to be the more likely ones to escape, since Manifestations are no longer Numina and Rank gives a scaling number of free Manifestations.

      6. It was a little more implicit in 1e, but 2e's supplement The Pack is fairly up-front about lodges being both the connective tissue of wider werewolf society and the more widespread source of morally/ethically iffy behavior from werewolves who aren't simply part of a pack with some questionable alliances.

      Speaking of that last part, while nothing strictly stops you from playing the Forsaken as unabashed good guys, they are no-longer-human killer demigods who sometimes flip out and kill stuff and who nevertheless occupy a spiritual landscape where they aren't starting out as the biggest fish and their main liaison with the Shadow (the pack totem) imposes a stricter Ban the more powerful it is, to say nothing of the fact that nasty stuff creates nasty spirits, nasty spirits tend to be useful for controlling nasty stuff (including other nasty spirits), and spirits tend to need at least nominal payment to do anything (with low payment backed up by threat of violence being a tricky road to hoe at the best of times). All that, of course, is before getting into the cultural sense of werewolves who believe their ancestors killed their god, broke the world, and took over his job for good but unenviable reasons; Forsaken is pretty up-front about necessitating a certain baseline of gray on the black-and-white spectrum of narrative morality.

      Resident Lore-Hound

          1) I'm broadly a bigger advocate of CofD 2e over 1e, so take that as a bit of bias. The core rules are just generally improved, the werewolf specific rules pop better, and the game drops a bunch of elements that were included as nods to Apocalypse but really didn't add to Forsaken. If I had to pick one thing that really sells 2e over 1e for Forsaken? The pack rules. Forsaken packs can directly include Wolf-blooded (as in they can add dots to the pack just like the full werewolves do), and mark normal humans as part of the pack, which carries over into rites and powers that can expand past the PCs into their close circle of friends and even into a large organization. I feel like this helps replace some of the Sept level elements of Apocalypse with a human-werewolf mixed social unit that's large enough to have internal differences and conflicts, but still small enough were most people know most other people.

          2) Rules-wise? Not so much. Not impossible for some of them, but anything related to character traits and so on is going to require at least some CofD mastery to translate from one to the other. Lore-wise... very book dependent. While 1e's Territories is a bit redundant with 2e's The Pack, Territories still has plenty to offer regarding how to think about how the Uratha think about their territories and what they might face if they intrude on others'. The over-all looser approach to Lore in CofD also helps smooth over adapting the older books because there's less set-in-stone lore elements. 2e dropped the whole "werewolf spiritual incest" stuff that discourages werewolves from having relationships with each other, which is a lore change and some 1e books to assume that's still in place, but one of the reasons 2e dropped it is because it's not important to Forsaken. The Uratha aren't a dying race. Werewolves being spiritually punished for getting intimate with each other doesn't really add anything to Forsaken's themes broadly. So unless you're reading a 1e thing where that's a big part of a specific plot hook... it's not hard to just ignore that it was a thing in 1e.

          3) Forsaken is already a fairly "PCs are in the good guys" camp setup; and doesn't need a whole society of werewolf faith to justify it. The Uratha do things normal humans don't understand, and spirits don't like, and can still flip out and rage spree, but you can't have heroic characters with some darkness to overcome. The Pure might have some points about the flaws in Uratha philosophy, but they're not doing themselves any favors by being brutal supremacists. Balehounds are outright evil. There is some tragedy to the villainy of the spirits, spirit-influenced, and Hosts, as they're somewhat forced by their natures to do what they do, but putting the world back in balance is in the large scale is the only real way to break a cycle of constantly fighting them.

          There is a 1e book - War Against the Pure - that goes into fairly decent depth with scaling up the game. Brethren Wars (aka when the Forsaken and the Pure start fighting on a large scale instead of smaller pack focused conflicts) are part of the lore of both editions. So it's very possible to do grander scale games in Forsaken. The default assumption those is that those stakes are more rare once every century or two massive conflicts rather than how things always are. For me, that's actually to the benefit of more epic games. The stakes feel more important when they're rare. If every few years there's a conflict as impactful as WWII in your setting, each conflict stops feeling so special and "big." A longer ebb and flow of conditions before that kind of violence explodes helps make it feel like you're part of a unique and major conflict.

                          While there are some valid issues with some of the Beat system (though really I'd argue it's mostly a side effect of the books letting Condition creep become a thing and Conditions feeling like the most important aspect of the Beat economy even though the base Beat system offers lots of ways to get Beats besides them), they're really just XP as events happen to the characters rather than at the end of session as a lump sum.

                          I think there are additional perks (but they're not necessarily linked to Beats as is, other games do similar things with their own advancement systems), the big one being that Beats are far more player facing. That is, most Beats stem from player agency (either directly, such as choosing for a negative Condition to go off, or indirectly such as choosing to get into heavy combat and taking a lot of damage for it instead of finding a different way around a problem), whereas the old WoD XP guidelines were ST-based assessments of how things went for the player and the character (it is a very rare problem since most STs don't last long as such if they're this heavy handed, but I have been in games with STs that were very open in their critiques on who did and didn't deserve a good role-playing XP).

                          Even as the ST, there's a certain amount of ease when advancement boils down to: did the players do the things? Cool, hand out Beats. Rather than having to say, "well did Bob's PC really learn anything for that XP reward? Was Alice's PC ever really in danger for that XP reward?" after every session (and more at the end of the story). Not to be too simplistic about things, but D&D (and derived games) is the king of the TTRPG world... and it's been doing the whole, "PCs do X, they get XP and loot" method in various ways since the beginning... Beats aren't really that different in the end (it's just what the challenges are that the PCs have to overcome).
