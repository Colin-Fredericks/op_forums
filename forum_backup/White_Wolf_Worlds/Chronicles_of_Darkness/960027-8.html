        What about buyer's remorse, where the characters voluntarily step into power and come to regret their decision in doing so?
        Geist and Vampire vaguely touch on this, but both the Bound and the Kindred could be considered as being under duress when they make that decision. A game where the metamorphosis is completely voluntary and not a matter of self-preservation places blame immediately on the character: there is no scapegoat onto which the weight of responsibility may be deflected.
        That's one of the thought spaces the fansplat Dragon the Embers tried to fill (This fire can burn for eternity! Be like the fire and you'll live forever. ...You ate the Heart? Great! Oh, by the way, we're out of fuel to stoke the fire), and it appears relatively untouched by major templates (Vampires can blame their sire and the Bound can blame their Geist).


        As for stranded aliens, I think there's room there to emphasize the difference between ethnocentrism and cultural relativism. If you have a community of extraterrestrial organisms living on Earth, there is more opportunity to explore their differences with humanity (beyond biology or tech, as social structure can be explored) than with a single alien organism who crashed or escaped from a military facility. The game then becomes Dian Fossey living among the gorillas; characters are forced to live with an 'inferior' species and cleave to their cultures for survival (despite holding the opinion those practices are backward or downright savage) and come to realize there's something to be learned from that way of life and maybe it isn't so primitive after all.

        There was a television show "The Neighbors" which approached this concept from a comedy angle (Larger alien presence than "Third Rock", but that same sort of culture shock model. The Zavronians referred to their life on Earth and the lack of their technology and comforts as 'camping'), and the movie "District Nine" looked at it from the horror perspective (Alien refugee camp devolves into a slum, with the aliens having forgotten how their tech works within a generation). It also explores the trials of immigration, particularly in the early 1900s when families would have to abandon their language and hide their traditions for fear of reprisal if they were perceived as 'foreign'.

        Alternatively, what One Vorlon said about Earth being the Wild West of the galaxy would be equally intriguing.

                                                            Other possibilities for the "monster story" avenue of looking at the question...

                                                            We've had creatures who need humanity to feed upon, or for whom ties to humanity are a way to progress (Promethean) or resist degradation (Vampire and Deviant especially), but none where it's central to their power and nature. I had a vague idea (mostly as a Mummy hack) of a "Ancestor" monster psychically tied to a family/community/cult/other definable group, with the breadth and depth of ties determining the scope of their abilities. That one was mostly about community, duty and politics - but it could be approached in other ways. The Phoenix fangame presented creatures that would make good mentors, or "builders" of something in the human world.

                                                            We haven't had a line that's explicitly about more than one type of creature (the preceding idea touched on this in that I imagined Ancestors varyingly being humans who inherited the role and will pass it on, humans who use their ties to render themselves immortal, ghosts who live "in" their people and possess or manifest when they are needed), or undead who "live" in preserved bodies becoming animate when needed). I've remarked in the past how I would like a 2nd edition of Geist to actually be about sineaters, wraiths, and revenants. Depending on how broadly we define them, djinn could do this (marid, ifrit, zar, and ghilan are certainly more diverse than the x-splats of existing lines).

                                                            And we haven't had a line where the creatures are a new phenomenon; I mean really really new, like Hunter the Reckoning new - one where the player characters will be at the forefront of learning the scope of their metaphysics, inventing their powers, and defining their culture. Geist touches on the last, and Deviant probably has a little of it, but it's not been a big deal so far.
