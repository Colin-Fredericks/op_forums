            Eonight (aka Overshadow)

            This used to be a good town. Hard to believe, I know. Broken windows, crumbling brick, the circling cops who come here purely to inflate arrest statistics, I get it. But back when I moved here, around 05, this place was nearly perfect. It was poor and filled with petty crooks, but not the drug dealers. I would ask what changed, but it's stupid to ask the questions you know the answer to. To be frank, people lost faith; after we discovered the charity's drug problems, the mayor's infidelity, the regular tantrums of the local PD? Everyone here gave up, started soaking in their own misery. Gangs came here to sell deliberately brief escapes, and here we are. Even the smog's oppressive now.

            What smog? Yeah, you can't see it in the daytime, but night? It's like everything bright in the skies was eaten. Feels like it too-except it doesn't seem empty when you look up. Like whatever ate the stars was still there, sleeping off its meal. In any case, I'm getting the hell out of here; place is driving me to drink again.

            Darkness is not humanity's ally. It's not our enemy, necessarily, either, but it's never our friend. We simply aren't evolved to cope with the existence of real dark; as diurnal omnivores that, barring our technology, sit squarely in the middle tiers of the food chain, humans have evolved an advised aversion to the concept of darkness; it contains things that, in older times, would like to eat us, and in more modern times, hides the activities of far worse, and far more familiar predators. Because of that, the dark has long become associated with sin, and the sense of being lost spiritually as well as physically.

            Was it this, humanity's fear of the evils in the dark, that provoked the genesis of the eonights? They're certainly similar enough to spirits that they might be spawn of the Shadow, inseminated by the Essence released by billions of sapient minds, but there's several things that don't add up about the overshadows in that case. For one, they don't seem to communicate in any language, let alone understand First Tongue. While obviously intelligent and capable of long-term planning, all anyone's been able to get out of them is emotional states, and in fact they may not understand the concept of verbal communication at all (a hungry eonight transmitting its desire to get a potential opponent away from a new feeding ground will implant the sensation anger fear greed hunger defensiveness ambition in said rival, for instance). For another, their diet is awfully varied-not the oddest thing for spirits, but if they were magath, one would expect eonights to have some variance in their appearance and behaviors rather than the default. Most of all, however, eonights don't actually seem to exist in Twilight, at all. They can discorporate, but when manifested, all overshadows are in the strange, half-immaterial state that seems to be their trademark, with not a single iota of matter in any Twilight frequency anybody human (or superhuman) has reached in recorded memory.

            In any case, it doesn't matter. Spirit, creation of spirits, or something stranger, eonights are a threat to anyone unfortunate enough to be within their territories, for an eonight is both the dark and the predator-and a very efficient predator at that. For the overshadows have no fangs, no spears, and no need of any. Eonights make their prey kill their own lives, and then feed on the wreckage left behind.

            Eonights are enormous creatures, with the smallest able to cover an entire standard football field if they ever decided to land. Were it not for their unique relationship with tangibility, it's almost certain they would have been hunted to death long ago-but it's an odd person who realizes the eonight is a creature at all. From below, an eonight resembles nothing so much as a starless black night sky-because that's essentially what it is. From below, eonights simply do not exist as physical beings, simply patches of living darkness that are as intangible as actual spots that lack light. Flying above the creature (easier said than done, hard as that is to believe-eonights can increase altitude very quickly, so one would have to surprise or somehow negotiate with it) reveals the overshadow for the truly strange being it is. From above, an eonight is a living field of black, reptilian skin, with blank white eyes and bony protrusions that appear sporadically and with little pattern here and there. It's quite physical, too; more that once has an eonight dwelling in a subterranean lair tricked opponents into getting trapped above it, then crushed them against the ceiling (thankfully, outdoors overshadows can't do the same trick with suffocating people on their backs-eonights hate the cold and relative airlessness of higher altitudes). It's even possible to walk on one with little difficulty, though that may draw the wrath of the symbiotes they attract-eonights have no problem with other eaters of negative emotion hitching a ride, including spirits of the concepts they naturally provoke.

            Eonights are cunning, intelligent eldritch parasites that attach themselves to small communities and isolated wanderers, materializing out of whatever strange dimension they dwell in at day overhead. Sometimes they will manifest in underground locales or even large artificial buildings, but there usually is not nearly enough space or sapient prey to feed on in that case. It's a subtle manifestation, usually even more so due to the eonights instinctive shyness. Having an eonight up above isn't harmful, inherently-up until someone looks up, usually due to the quiet mental suggestion of the eonight or its symbiotes, and notices the lack of any light in the night sky or the abnormally dark ceiling. This seems to forge an internal link between the unknowing victim and the eonight's alien mind, and with it, its full powers. Eonights seem capable of actively modifying reality around one of its victims, all with the express intent of forcing them to confront a particular personal demon; an alcoholic encounters ever more sources of his unhealthy indulgence, an arachnophobe a seemingly endless parade of spiders in her existence. At first, these encounters appear utterly mundane-the drunk keeps on making wrong turns that lead him to bars, the arachnophobe only finds the eight-legged beasts in dark and unused spaces where spiders normally live. As the eonight gathers more prey and grows more confident, however, the demon grows ever more blatant and forceful; now the water tap dispenses wine, a tarantula crawls out of nowhere onto the head of her boss. Ultimately, the goal of the eonight is to goad its victims into destroying their own lives, if not as an inherent aspect of their personal demons, then as an incidental side effect of trying to get away from them, feeding on its victim's shame and despair (the arachnophobe is known to attack the spiders instinctively..). If it can eventually drive a victim to suicide or erode an entire community that might provide a support network for its victims, so much the better-in fact, that's why eonights tend to have symbiotes, they use passengers both to assist in their cruel manipulations and to work on a larger scale without tripping their instinctive shyness. The one reprieve is the day-eonights despise sunlight and will discorporate back to whatever alien plane of existence spawned them, taking their symbiotes with them (strangely, the sapient symbiotes never seem to recall anything about this place. None of the sane ones, anyway). But once an overshadow has decided on a territory, it will come back. Over and over, spreading self-destruction in its wake.

            There's only three ways to get rid of an eonight. The first is light; as beings that are, in fact, shadows from beneath, dispersing that dark severely injures the physical part of the overshadow. This is probably the best way to do so, as it's the only real way to surprise an eonight-and surprise is critical, as eonights are fully aware of this weakness, and send their symbiotes to destroy high-power lights in the area (flashlights and other minor lights shined at the eonight just tickle them-it's not enough to actually reach the physical part of the eonight and therefore injure it). Thankfully, overshadows seem to be cowards-if one is injured enough (lethal or aggravated damage in their last five health boxes), the eonight will flee and not return to it's old territory for at least a decade (it bears grudges like nobody's business, though-if the persons it blames travel far enough away from its old stomping ground, they often find it waiting for them, often underground where the hated sunlight will not reach). Another is to use their Bans against them-if too many people notice the eonight's sky at once, it will panic and flee Earth's known universe. In fact, the entire feeding strategy of the eonight is to avoid this-if somebody has noticed the overshadow before, they can no longer assist in scaring it, and the eonight abuses the fact that it only needs a period of about fifteen minutes to "denature" the prey. An eonight uses its symbiotes to distract all of the sapients in its territory until it's good and ready for all of them to notice it, and as mentioned, they prefer to lair near less populated areas anyway, only moving into urban areas if they can get a significant of the city-dwellers to notice them while isolated from the rest of the city. The third and final, method, killing the eonight from above, is not quite as insane as it sounds (the overshadow can't discorporate with hostile passengers unless it's to avoid sunlight or fleeing massive crowds), but it's a big monster with a lot of health and armor that only needs to wait out its attackers-really, the only good idea with a direct assault is to distract the eonight long enough for schemes one or two to be pulled off.

            And even once the overshadow is gone, the damage remains. Just because a patch of living darkness caused it doesn't mean it goes away.

                New Dread Power: Court of Shadows

                If all Horrors were solitary, asocial beasts, the Chronicles of Darkness would probably have happier stories. Unfortunately, the Horror with this Dread Power, though perhaps not capable of pretending to be a member of normal society or even human, at least understands others well enough to realize help in terrorizing the innocent and bringing ruin to the world is probably appreciated. For one Willpower, it can forge a psychic link with another with a similar enough nature to the Horror to emphasize with it. The subject gains an instinctive understanding of the Horror's nature and what it can do for them, should they swear eternal fealty to it. Acceptance gives the subject some form of supernatural power themed after their new master, at the cost of developing perfect loyalty to the Horror for as long as it lives (exchanging one of their current Aspirations for "Serve my Master" in the process), unless the Horror sees fit to release them.

                Special: When Discorporating into its native dimension, the eonight takes its Court of Shadows with it. No sane member recalls anything of it except for a warm darkness, and indeed do not age while there.

                Bane: Lights bright enough it hurts the eye to look directly at cut through the physical dark of the overshadow like knives.

                Ban: The eonight cannot Discorporate voluntarily while non-symbiotes are on its physical back, and must Discorporate to avoid sunlight or when more than five people notice it over a thirty minute period, fleeing the area for the next decade if they do. Since this isn't a voluntary process on the eonight's part, this ignores the first type of Ban, and indeed, overshadows like to fly up as high as possible before dawn when facing opponents on its back (this, by the way, is around 12 stories for most eonights).


                Night-Touched Murderer:

                Oh me? I just like walking at night. It makes me feel...less alone. So, how you'd like to be less alone with me, just for a few minutes?

                Description: Not all nighttime predators are those who just need to eat. One Robert Stuart, for instance, used to cut out articles on the various missing person cases, highlight the tearful, terrified comments of their loved ones, and paste it them on the barrels he stored said (now mummified) missing persons in; reading those comments, and then observing said loved ones fall apart from afar, really brightened his month. Unlike most serial killers, though, Robert's egotism never escalated to needing fame for his murderous activities, which is why he was never caught, and why he interested the distant intellect of an eonight drawn to the dilapidated community where he hid himself. Stuart finds the nomadic lifestyle and living accommodations of an eonight symbiote to be quite to his liking, and he has embraced his role as his "friend's" chief enforcer, making sure nobody who ever sees the eonight ever gets the chance to tell too many people about it until the overshadow is good and ready, much less ever threaten to hurt it. He's not a combatant, by any means (in fact, his crimes were partially motivated by his slight physique and natural cowardice driving him to affirm his power over the world), but he's an excellent blackmailer-and if his friend has any say in the matter, there's no small amount of weak points he can find. He still kills people, though-in fact, he's upped his game ever since becoming a symbiote. The cops can't catch someone who literally vanishes during the day, after all.

                    Storytelling Hints: Eonights have the same role in stories as they do by simple dint of their physical existence; living atmosphere. From the moment one parks itself over an area, the darkness.both internal and external becomes a very real thing. A story about a place menaced by an eonight is a story of creeping corruption and oppression-the shadow falls on a community, and as it worms its way into the hearts of its prey, it begins to slowly break them down, followed rapidly by the community; as leaders and pillars of trust fall to their magnified flaws, people lose faith in the concept of virtue, which leaves them ever more vulnerable to the overshadow's predations. Those who strike back against the creeping eonight quickly find it brings its full wrath against them, using Madness and Terror to twist perceptions of reality even as it uses its Influences to actually twist reality, while its symbiotes manifest and serve as the iron fist to their master's velvet glove. A few people will even succumb so utterly they willingly submit to their eldritch drug dealer and become the newest additions to the eonight's Court of Shadows-the hangers-on to the ultimate eldritch bully.

                    Still, despite its power, an overshadow is still a bully-which is to say, a coward. While it is only required to flee from too many people seeing it at once, even the beginnings of serious, light-induced injury often provokes one into Discorporating as fast as it can. It generally comes back afterwards, but a night free from the eonight is a night everyone within is assaulted by the constant provocation to give into their darkest urges. Thus, while the mood is one of creeping, overwhelming menace in any story where an eonight figures heavily, the theme is ultimately hopeful; the darkness can be turned back, it can be beaten, even destroyed...but the path there is long, hard, and treacherous.

                    Some games that fit with an eonight antagonist are:

                    Changeling: Eldritch, mighty monsters with more communicative minions who seek to force others into a form they don't want, but could still be called "them"? Changelings find this all horribly familiar. An eonight may not be the personal tormentor a Keeper was, or actually that obsessive (an eonight has an excuse-it needs to eat somehow), but the cold, utter amorality eonights show is worse in some ways; to them, vivisecting the bonds of trust and virtue that hold people together is nothing more than preparing a nice, juicy steak, and what direct malevolence the eonight lacks is more than made up for by its symbiotes. Beyond matters of ideology, though, eonights are especially threatening towards changelings themselves; even beyond the fact that they are a race of trauma survivors that have often turned to self-destructive means to deal with the memories and aftereffect of their Durances to begin with, overshadows quickly figure out that changelings, above all else, are terrified of going back to the point of it regularly overcoming their altruism. There's a reason many Huntsmen refer to a placed menaced by an eonight as a "Traitor's Ground."

                    Vampire: Kindred find eonights horribly familiar too, but in a way even more disturbing than changelings; an overshadow's habits often resemble their own when securing a feeding ground, and for the same exact reasons. More to the point, the symbiotes' life with the overshadow often remind vampires of ghouls to their domitor, but unlike ghouls, symbiotes always had a choice in this life. Add that to the fact that eonights don't spare vampires from their hungers (and really, frenzy is an excellent way to provoke shame-via-actions), and one has all the reason for any sane Prince to call a blood hunt on the darkness. And indeed, the Sanctified have many records of squabbles with eonights...along with theories on relation to another race of semi-tangible beings from a realm of utter darkness, one a certain clan of Roman vampires became all too familiar with...

                    Werewolf: Uratha Harmony is a precarious balancing act, one that frequently requires deliberate transgressions against both sides of the lycanthropic nature to stop their rage from devastating the world around them. A better race of prey eonights could not ask for, and thus a hunt of an overshadow is a mutual one-the pack tries gather enough light to mortally wound the eonight despite its best efforts, while the pack resists the mental assault of the eonight as it attempts to imbalance them-generally towards Flesh, for the convinience of the overshadow stuck on the earthy side of the Gauntlet, and because it deprives the Uratha of their potent shapeshifting. Of course, eonights are anything but blind to the spirit-and there's not small amount of Fear, Shame, and Guilt spirits who would enjoy traveling with them for the free meals...

                    Beast: The Begotten are the closest to the eonights biologically and mentally. Indeed, more than one of the Children has theorized they're a related species, perhaps an elder sibling born from the Dark Mother that adapted to feed on fear of the self, or even the shards of an old and mad Beast, so distanced from anything human they have become a living Lair. The trouble is, eonights don't seem to understand the concept of a purpose behind their hunger for shame; they eat everything, including that which destroys the self rather than refines it, a constant confrontation with the dark side that almost invariably ruins rather than allow the two halves to reconcile and question why they are driven by these impulses. Thus, while Beasts can sense the kinship, they usually end up deciding that, regretfully, overshadows' inability to communicate or moderate their hungers makes them a threat that must be destroyed. Usually-more than one Tyrant, Ravager, and Predator has dedicated their life to developing a way to explain to the overshadow that they can emphasize with more than their symbiotes. And for every one of those, unfortunately, there's three who just decided that the ruin the eonights leave behind them is a wonderful crop.

                        Demon: Despite their nature (or perhaps because of it), the Unchained are just as likely as the mortals they hide among to fall prey to an eonight's hunger. Many of their secrets deal with things they are ashamed of, things that can inevitably compromise them if they're not careful enough. Those who call themselves Integrators are especially vulnerable, as their greatest secret is their desire to return to the very God Machine that their comrades fight against or flee from. Like Changelings, Demons value their freedom, and their paranoia over being killed or captured by hunter angels and Stigmatics can force them to take drastic actions that can harm themselves and those around them. The eonight who chooses to prey on a Demon exploits this fear, subtly pushing him to break his cover by burning his mortal connections and leaving him isolated. Of course, it may even be hounding the Demon at the urging of the God-Machine.

                        Geist: Even with all the perks of the Bargain, a Sin-Eater's second chance isn't a completely clean slate. Aside from having to deal with the unfinished business of not only his Geist, but a multitude of restless shades, the Bound have to wrestle with their own unresolved issues, especially when the circumstances of their first death make it impossible. It is these internal conflicts which bring an eonight to plague a Sin-Eater, who eventually becomes so out of touch with his Geist that it might choose to sever the link in search of a new host. On the other hand, the Bound may end up on an overshadow's trail in response to the ghosts of its victims desperately begging for closure.

                        Mage: Sometimes, the things we claim to be most proud of can end up becoming our greatest shame. This can be especially true for the Awakened, who - for all the power they have at their fingertips - are still fallible mortals, at heart. More than a few are resentful of their enlightenment even as they push the limits of their Art. Naturally, the ones who stand to fall the hardest are Banishers, Mages who outright reject their own magic to the point that they become a danger to any and all members of a given Consilium. It is these damaged souls that attract eonights the most, and they only wreak more havoc as their worst fears and deepest shames are supped up by the overshadows like sweet nectar.

                        Mummy: For the Arisen, ignorance is hardly bliss. They have many things to feel ashamed of: the erosion of their Memory, the inability to craft the relics of their Guilds, and the seeming irrelevance of their place in modern nights. Such sentiments, coupled with the effects of Sybaris on those around them, cause the Deathless to shine like beacons to the eonights' hunger. And though most are easily disposed of, those who have the fortune to prey upon a Mummy well into his Descent have more opportunities to feed by hindering his goals. Naturally, this often means sabotaging the Arisen's efforts at claiming a relic for their dark masters, whether by aiding its competition, or (worse) destroying it. Either way, the reaction this causes is an exhilarating rush for the eonight, just as it's a flash of despair for its prey.

                        Promethean: The Created have a difficult existence. Their Pilgrimages toward the New Dawn are fraught with a multitude of hardships and tragedies which expose them to the worst of Humanity. Disquiet and the Wasteland only worsen matters, as eonights are attracted by the emotional fallout unleashed by such phenomena. The stifling apathy of the Nepri and the lustful daydreams inspired by a Muse are just as savory to them as the unbridled rage of the Wretched and the alien compulsions that manifest on those near the Ulgans. Ultimately, it's the shame and fear Created have towards the Great Work that are the figurative cherries to the feast they present to an eonight. Can I really become human? Is it even worthwhile? Why even try? These are questions that push a Promethean to the depths of despair, and the eonight will eagerly speed the process along. After all, one in Torment after failing a crucial Milestone (or, worse, the crucible of the New Dawn itself) is the greatest banquet of all.

                            It's that time once again to start the vote. This series theme is "Karma".

                            Starting off with humanoids. once more:

                            Stormbairn: It's a special sort who hurts children. The very meat that composes humans rears back in revulsion at the idea, this violation of a core imperative to protect the young. As with most transgressions, though, on the other side lies supernatural power-but not for the abuser. Stormbarins are children between 4 and 13 years old (generally closer to 4) whose pain and anger has awoken a fey force of catharsis within them, one that drives them to dance the pain away, releasing all their sorrow in a mad frenzy set to the tempo an unseen drum. It actually helps, given how said dance releases said anger quite literally, emotional turmoil becoming meteorological turmoil-when stormbairns frolic, the typhoon joins in.

                            Tattlers: Serpents have an odd dual symbolism. On the one hand, they're taken to be the symbol of liars, but on the other hand, they're also viewed as fonts of hidden wisdom. Tattlers, humans with the tongues of serpents, say the former is a desperate attempt by humanity to convince itself the latter doesn't exist; people fear truth, because it's harder than lies. Even before they grew out their tongue and venom, though that was never the case for tattlers-for them, hearing lies is physically painful, When they learned they could force people to give them the unvarnished truth with a quick taste...well, is it any surprise that most have dedicated their lives to spreading serpent venom among anyone who dares speak untruth?

                            Wereshadow: Not all vengeance is just. For a certain, strange group of people, vindictiveness is a way of life. Half of it, at any rate. They don't take out their grudges as humans, where they could be easily caught. But when one has the ability to turn oneself into a living, two-dimensional silhouette...well, there's not much that can stop you.

                                                Stormbairn

                                                Wanna play with me? Daddy said dancing makes it stop hurting, and now that Mommy's gone, it hurts even less!

                                                The innocence of children is a mixed blessing for all involved. On the one hand, innocence is purity in its, well, purest state; not only is the spirit unsullied by darkness, there has been no chance for that to happen to begin with. On the other hand, innocence is ignorance-and ignorance of others is all too frequently the source of cruelty. Children can be mean, spiteful little beasts, but out of a lack of understanding rather than malice-which also means they are capable of profound viciousness when actual anger enters the equation. Anyone who has dealt with bullies knows this-but the old school specials about bullies being fundamentally scared and unhappy kids taking out their internal turmoil on the world isn't wrong, though blaming their victims for being focuses of that certainly is. Children who are angry and sad release that anger and sadness into the world.

                                                In the Chronicles of Darkness, that sometimes isn't a metaphor, and anyone unfortunate enough to be nearby when the stormbairns release their sorrow in their mad dances find just how mighty a force the anger of babes can be.

                                                Stormbairns, also known as "ire muses", are symptoms of a well-hidden truth about the CofD, the hidden occult nature of humans; everyone in the world at least has the potential to perform supernatural feats if that potential is fanned and fueled. Of course, it's also a truth that the fuel is generally painful or a double-edged sword. In the case of stormbairns, what awakens their dance is trauma and pain-almost always the kind inflicted by adults. The frustration and despair felt by those emotionally abused by people the latent ire muses should be able to trust, to the point of losing their grip on what is real or not (a good baseline: to awaken as a stormbairn, a child must lose at least three dots of Integrity to breaking points caused by abuse either by authority figures or explicitly permitted by authority figures) awakes a fey force within them, a force of freedom, dance...and storms. Nobody's quite sure why this happens, and why it's always storms; perhaps the emergence of latent occult abilities is influenced by nurture rather than nature, perhaps it calls to some great spirit of storms with a soft spot for children, perhaps it's vengeance from the divine on the world that would abuse its weakest denizens. Whatever the case is, some of the most horrifically abused children of the world develop a strange trait; about once a month at the most, often less as the new stormbairn ages (generally coinciding with the new moon, but not always) they will feel a compulsion to find an external source of music, whether it be windchimes, a drum, the local concert hall, or even a smartphone with music apps on it, Should they be successful (and they usually are, even if they have to make the instrument themselves from scrap), they mentally not where it is, and wait for nightfall.

                                                As night comes, a strange transformation occurs over the stormbairns; their features grow indistinct, almost as if they were made of mist, and their eyes catch the light like an animals, allowing the ire muses to see in the dark perfectly well. Most startling, their hair turns the the stark white of clouds, and they develop a cunning, feral personality. This new persona is more than clever and focused enough to outwit all but the most extreme bonds placed upon them, and should there be any other stormbairns in the area (and unfortunately, there often is-the kind of environment that produces one often makes more), their first priority is to free their sibling. The ire muses then make their way to their instrument, which begins to play without any human input; wind chimes begin to ring a surprisingly complex melody, drums vibrate without being struck, smartphones activate even without power sources and search for file with a strong beat, and the concert hall's instruments animate and begin an impromptu concerto. With the sound of music in their ears, the stormbairns begin to dance and play-all to the backdrop of a growing and violent storm.

                                                The intensity and size of the storm varies, growing in power as the number of ire muses and dynamics of the song (ie, what the instrument is physically capable of doing) increase; two siblings frolicking to a dusty old set of windchimes may just provoke a rainstorm that may swell a river past its banks, while there's rumors that a dozen slave children playing to the set of fiddles kept on their prison ship triggered a hurricane. Whatever the case is, the storm is always chaotic, wet, and destructive..though not to the local wildlife, which becomes oddly docile and playful for the duration of the dance, often joining in with the stormbairns during their frolic. More pertinently, they become extremely vicious if anyone actually tries to hurt one of the white-haired children, swarming any fool who thinks to harm one of the ire muses with an almost suicidal bloodthirst. Just trying to stop the dance before the stormbairns cause any natural disasters doesn't provoke this uncharacteristic protectiveness-but trying to catch a stormbairn is a borderline Herculean ordeal. Besides the fact that even a crippled child becomes superhumanly fast and agile as a stormbairn, the world twists to enable their escape-fields the ire muses run across stretch into infinity, trees their climb become slippery skyscrapers, alleyways fold into twisty mazes where gravity varies from passage to passage (thankfully, stormbairns don't like keeping this up-their pursuer apparently giving up will return the world to normal). If nothing else, forcing the music to stop works, but the storm is generally most intense around the source. The revel of the stormbairns is not easy to stop once it begins.

                                                In any case, once the dance stops at daybreak or someone forced the ire muses to stop moving in some other way, the world and the children rapidly return to normal. The storm disperses, animals resume their normal behaviors, and the children have little memory of being a stormbairn, only feeling well-rested and with a lot of their inner anger sedated, with a subconscious sense of kinship with the other stormbairns in the dance (in game terms, a child waking from a stormbairn episode refreshes all Willpower, just as if they had fulfilled a virtue). It rarely ends there, though-an instrument used in the dance of ire muses (especially if it's been invoked more than once for the purpose) usually carries a bit of the fey, tumultuous power of the storm, becoming a Bygone or other item of power with influence over extreme emotional states, youth, and/or the weather. This holds true for the children, too: while former stormbairns may or may not relapse (and none 14 and over has ever joined or rejoined the stormy frolic) it's not at all uncommon for them to develop a touch of the strange about them, gaining some amount of psychic or other mystical ability as they pass puberty-a few even transform into full supernatural beings that have been focused on in gamelines. Unfortunately, what doesn't change is their home situation-while sometimes, the storm does serve as a wake-up call to the figures in a child's life that they need to clean up their act, usually all the abuser sees is a snotty little brat that ran away. Even if what the stormbairn was doing during their episode comes out, it's a sad truth about abusers that they instead convince themselves the ire muses are to blame for their condition, not the abusers, no matter how obvious it is the storm was fueled by their own cruelty; it's easier to view the world that way, even as their continuing brutality provokes another manifestation of the stormbairn's powers. Ultimately, often the only way the abuse stops is for the abuser to be one of the storm's victims, and realizing they murdered someone they, it must be admitted, often wanted to die, only gives more trauma to fuel the ire muses' dance.

                                                And as the new moon dawns, the storm dance begins again...

                                                    Stormbairn Stats:

                                                    Stormbairns are built like normal starting characters, often a few dots left off Attributes and Abilities to represent them being, well, younger children. Normally, they exist as perfectly normal, albeit deeply unhappy and often prone to delinquent behavior to release their frustrations, with a depressing amount being orphaned "street children." As mentioned in the Description, however, on certain nights they undergo a radical shift both physiologically and mentally, becoming white-haired, extremely athletic dancers with a feral, wild cunning and a playful disposition unaware and uncaring of the destructive power unleashed by their storms. All of them develop the following changes to their Attributes. which can exceed 5: Intelligence +2. Wits +4. Strength +2, Dexterity +4, Stamina +3, Presence +2, Composure -3 (to a minimum of 1), with derived attributes such as Health adjusted accordingly, and the Athletics skill at three dots. While in this state, stormbairns count as Horrors with a Potency of 4, with no Integrity-while in dormant form ire muses are capable of moral reasoning and are as sane as any other child, in white-haired form stormbairns are more forces of nature than playing children, unable to understand the consequences of their destructive power yet utterly at peace with the chaos that follows their music. All develop the Dread Powers Influence (Storms ***), Prodigious Leap, Maze, and Reality Stutter, and two Bans: They must play within a two mile radius of their instrument, and they must not be held still by an external force for more than three turns. Breaking either Ban causes the stormbairn to return to normal and remain so until the next dance, if it happens at all. Recognizing an active ire muse as their mundane identity is a Perception roll with a flat -3 penalty due to their vague features, with circumstantial bonuses to reflect closeness to the child.

                                                    Example Stormbairn: Molli O'Keefe

                                                    Quote: Call me that name again. I dare you.

                                                    His name is actually Molan, but everyone calls him Molli, pronounced "Molly" due to him having a cough when he introduced himself to the class and boys not having anything resembling good wit. They still do, but only by his "friends" and adults to his face-everyone else has long learned that Molli's anger puts kids in hospitals. He's the fourth grade equivalent of a mob boss; he does the really big and naturally violent bullies' homework, they protect him from mockery, with prejudice. It doesn't make him happy, but it does ensure he at least escapes from the mockery at school; his home situation was worse, even before he came up with his protection racket (which he doesn't get is a form of blackmail, not bribery). Born the middle child to a manic-depressive, emotionally abusive father and a negligent, overworked mother Molli's been the verbal punching bag of his family since he was born, a minor birth defect making him unsuited to most physical sports unlike his brothers-not to mention his relative androgyny. He channels his inferiority complex into his studies and burgeoning talent as an engineer, but that never seems to impress his athletic siblings-and actually seems to inflame dear old Dad's jealousy, causing the abuse to go from merely cruel into outright monstrous, forcing his other children to actively gaslight Molli after the bookish boy made a passing remark about his father's tendency to revise reality to suit him. One of Molli's burly guards noticed this, and being informed of just how willing his father is to sabotage his "girly" son's life was the catalyst for Molan to live up to his given name and become a stormbairn. He's been this way for over a year now-and the property damage his dances have caused has inflamed family tensions in his part of the city, giving rise to at least three other ire muses, with one of them bringing a violin to replace the handmade bangle Molli was using. Already, the city has been flash flooded twice-if more stormbairns join the dance, who knows what devastation will be unleashed?

                                                            Finishing up:

                                                            Storytelling Hints: Fair warning, stories involving stormbairn are dark, due to the fact that they invariably involve the subject of severe child abuse, and its consequences. Stormbairns are living representatives of the truth that monstrous behavior breeds monsters, in the most literal fashion possible. As exemplified by Molli, not all ire muses are innocents, but they are all the very embodiment of undeserving, the weakest people in society crushed by people who should be their protectors and teachers, with nothing but an amoral and often unhelpful cosmic vengeance to tend to their wounds. Thus, the overall mood of a stormbairn story is one of destroyed innocence; the brutal tearing away of comforting ignorance to reveal the ugly truth. An ugly truth that, usually, ends up destroying even more of the nearby world than the storm dance does-but given how it drove at least one child into episodes of divine madness, perhaps the world needed to die. The theme of a stormbairn story is the purifying storm, the primal chaos that simultaneously wipes away the old system and leaves the foundation for a new, better one; social services get involved, the abusers in a family are forced to seek help or leave, and abusive orphanages are slated for improvements as they're repairing the damage from flood anyway. But change is painful, and at first blush, always more painful than the way things are-but the way things are created the stormbairns to begin with.

                                                            Generally speaking, a story with a stormbairn antagonist is less about investigating and stopping the monster so much as figuring out why the monster is here; while it may be interesting to have a bait-and-switch once or twice where the troupe is initially led to believe the stormbairn is more of a generic "demon seed", the very point of the ire muses is that they aren't evil by nature or even that much by nurture; sooner or later, the players are going to get wise or end up hurting a mostly innocent child by mistake (though if it gets that far, make sure your players are okay with the subject matter long before it reaches that point, and in fact it's a good idea to raise the issue of whether the troupe will be okay with the subject of child abuse before running the story). Rather, the intended investigation is why the children are becoming ire muses in the first place, and correcting the error before the city block ends up being destroyed by the storm dance. While there is the potential for action scenes (catching the awakened stormbairns or shutting off their instrument during the dance is a wonderfully chaotic scene with the added wrinkle of the players desperately trying to avoid hurting the kids-if not on principle, then because of fear of being swarmed by the dancers' animal protectors), the story of an ire muse is a somewhat sedate, intellectual one where the problem not only has to be found, but fixed to the point where the anger that catalyzes the dance is calmed, at least to the point where the stormbairns don't feel the need to let out their overwhelming sorrow into the atmosphere. This is a difficult task, not the least because it usually doesn't have a simple solution or can be punched without negative consequence for the characters or the ire muses (though, if the reasons for the stormbairns are monstrous enough, the players might not care-in that case, tying someone to a copper pole in the middle of a raised swimming pool with a note taped to their forehead filled with New Atheist mottos on the new moon is a death method that doesn't leave any trace evidence on the body, and suitably fitting).

                                                            Games whose themes naturally harmonize with stormbairns are:

                                                            Beast: The Begotten, upon meeting a stormbairn, often find themselves wondering if they're looking at future siblings, future Heroes, or even something completely unrelated to either born from the Primordial Dream. The urge to protect children is so deeply ingrained in the human brain that it would not surprise the Children that hurting them so severely would trigger a similar reaction to a cancer antigen in the deepest levels of the collective unconscious, briefly turning said children into Kin in order to destroy such an obviously defective and hazardous member of the species while repairing some of the child in question's psyche. Unfortunately, like Heroes, this mechanism isn't as good as it could be; the storm does not know how to distinguish the root cause from the rest of humanity, or even if its violence is the best solution. But it does serve a key portion of autoimmune system most admirably; alerting creatures who can distinguish and apply more nuanced solutions to the problem, with great relish. Nemeses and Tyrants in particular mark a stormbairn's catalyst for special treatment, but it's a rare Beast who doesn't relish the chance to feel righteous while feasting-or take their young cousins under their (often literal) wings.

                                                            Changeling: Honestly, the Lost are protectors of abused children on principle; even leaving aside the fact that changelings have generally been there, there's few better ways to cement Acuity and sharpen Clarity than to confirm that you are the person who ended the cycle the Keepers tried to begin. Stormbairns add the extra motive that so long as they need to dance, the freehold's home is going to be slowly ground to waterlogged pieces. Frankly, changelings darkly relish the chance to calm stormbairns' rage; it feels natural, and righteous. Still, that does raise the question of where the dance comes from-ire muses are so similar to changelings that most of them suspect a link. Given how both storms and wrath are associated with the heat of Summer, most suspect an ancient curse on human inheritors of the Gentry, but a few others suspect the Others themselves, in one of their characteristic attempts at "being nice" without actually considering if their help was in their intended beneficiaries' best interest.This is mostly idle speculation-but a few militants have developed ideas about weaponizing their storms for use against the ultimate abusers and their forces. Which of course, means they need to be kept as they are-or created...

                                                            Mage: What is this strange force that creates and drives the ire muses? Storms have too much of a long and universal symbolism with chaos and change for the Awakened to believe there is no link between their symbolic meanings and the stombairns. But did the stormbairns create the symbolic association in the minds of adults, or did the symbolic association, combined with the wish of a child to destroy the order that gives them pain, create the stormbairns? It's all a very lovely mystery that mages would love to examine more-and thus, it's a sad truth that many wizards willfully blind themselves to the reasons stormbairns exist in favor of examining their dances, either blatantly ignoring the abuse that catalyzes them or pulling a series of victim-blaming platitudes out of their asses in order to make their own experiments seem palatable. Needless to say, this can provoke a far more metaphorical storm when other mages, especially Guardians (who tend to see the storm dance as magic endangering others and that which must be muzzled, and in their investigations usually realize that the stormbairn isn't doing it intentionally) realize what is going on; nobody likes child abusers or their accomplices.

                                                            Hunter: Most hunters, thankfully, are squeamish enough about hurting a child to make damned sure that the ire muses are actually evil or not really children before declaring them their new targets, and that usually uncovers that the stormbairns are at least provoked by nurture rather than nature. While most are too suspicious of witches to really think that such an obviously chaotic and destructive power is anything other than bad, they quickly recognize the "monster" isn't the aggressor in this situation, it's a kid lashing out against the world that hurts them. Most cells then start using their influence (along with favors in their Compacts and Conspiracies, if they have them) to get the kid a decent home under watch from the hunters-which is generally enough of an improvement that the stormbairns loose the urge. Of course, some hunters are not particularly good people, and take out their own difficulties on the Vigil on their families-which occasionally creates an ire muse or two. Generally, this either causes one of two reactions; either the hunter sees what they've done and cleans up their act (which often involves retiring), or (more frequently-such severe abusers are generally incapable of change) goes deep into denial. It's not their fault, it's the fault of the supernatural which corrupted their child-and their cell which defends the demon that replaced their child, and their spouse who let it happen, and possibly their child's teacher, and...
