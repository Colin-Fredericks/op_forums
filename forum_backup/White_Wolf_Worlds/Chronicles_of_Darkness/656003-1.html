    Yeah, the ST could have a True Friend abducted to direct a player towards or away from a certain action. The player can save the True Friend and do what they want or do what the abductor says. The ST never puts the True Friend at risk of death. If the player decides to not worry about the True Friend because the ST won't kill them off then they are gaming the system. The ST should say okay but you lose the merit and the former friend hates you. That was the choice the player makes. The player put the True Friend's life at risk rather than the ST. I have to think the wording was "kill" rather than "endanger" for a reason.

                        If you, as an ST, are actively thinking of ways to "get around" True Friend and push the very limits of what you can do without the player being able to point at the rulebook and call foul, you need to take a step back and re-evaluate the relationship between yourself, the rules, and your players. If things are becoming antagonistic, you need to identify the reason why and talk that out with your players. Maybe the True Friend merit is inappropriate for your table. I know in a Changeling game I ran, I asked a player to not take that merit for the express purpose that I didn't want them to feel like any of their connections were "safe" and I felt it undermined the tone I was going for. We talked it out for a bit and came with a compromise that made everyone satisfied.

                        In short, if your PC is taking True Friend, challenge yourself to find ways to make that relationship interesting and stressful without pouring acid on someone's face. In fact, I'd say that in general you should explore ways to entertain yourself that doesn't involve pouring acid on people's faces. It's just polite.

                            I think the acid on the face was hyperbole, but I agree with the sentiment there.

                            It was never about getting around True Friend. The problem was stated as a player gaming the system by using the no kill element as a means not to role-play. A True Friend should be at risk by virtue of having a close relationship with a player character that has enemies. The fact that the ST should not consider killing the True Friend without the player's permission creates a "safe" environment. When a True Friend is kidnapped, the player knows they can do something about it that won't lead to the True Friend's death. If the player refuses to do anything the they have forfeited the relationship. Taking it out on the True Friend is convenient for explanations, but really the point of the ST should be to let the player know they gave up the merit by their choice.

                            You see the scenario in movies. The protagonist's little buddy is kidnapped to coerce an action without any defined threat. The friend is held until the protagonist does what they are told. The protagonist can do as directed, save their friend, or get help. The friend is never truly in danger in a meta sense and depending on the circumstances even in the story there might be no danger. All this is well within the rules. A player would be wrong to take the merit to prevent such things.

                            I think touchstones should always get that treatment. Others might disagree. That would be the point discussions should be made. Players shouldn't take the merit to try to limit story ideas short of *bam* your friend is dead, now what? If a player doesn't want the kind of story where player character friends are put in danger that needs to get worked out without merits.

                                        Originally posted by Diggs View Post
                                        I think touchstones should always get that treatment.
                                        I've seen this sentiment a few times in this thread. I'm going to have to be one of the people that disagree.

                                        The Touchstone shouldn't be untouchable. There are aspects of a vampire's story that you can't really examine any other way than be threatening or even killing the Touchstone. It highlights how tenuous their connection to humanity is. Their death lets the group examine how the character reacts to that kind of deep loss. How they act without a leash to restrain them. How they might try getting another one, or if they even bother.

                                        Abusing this can lead to problems, surely, but as a once-in-a-campaign trauma, it can be very potent to lose a Touchstone.
