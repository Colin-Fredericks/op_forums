                  This first part needs another pass quite badly, possibly with an outline in mind. As-written it goes into too much detail on too many elements with too many names before it actually gets to anything of consequence, which is poison for a creation myth even without stapling Dreams of Avarice's second creation myth into the middle completely divorced from the context of the first one.

                  Like, I look at this and I cannot tell what the basis of the cosmology is, because the priority seems to have been naming or re-naming as many major cosmological figures from ChroD and WoD as possible and assigning them dominion over various groups of entities.

                  Mummy is clear that its take on the cosmology treats Suffering as foundational, Mage puts Truth in a central role for its metaphysics, Beast treats the various forms of Hunger as an unavoidable and fundamental part of the world, Changeling inhabits a setting where agreements make the world go 'round, Demon does likewise with an impossibly complicated set of arcane laws, Promethean centers change and transformation in its magical thinking, and so on. Elements recur across gamelines, like blood, sacrifice, cannibalism, treachery, alienation, identity, the significance of humanity. and more, but the stories they're used to tell are informed by the characters we're hearing them from.

                  I do not know what this creation myth is for, because it is crowded with allusions to entities that show up several steps after the creation myth and doubling up on different creation myths in sequence and seems to be more interested in abbreviated reference to events in more detail than we would need to hear for a big-picture story and less detail than we would need to hear for a character-focused story.

                  Emptiness exists before presence and hate ostensibly exists because the first thing to exist wanted to be hated, but instead of any form of exploration of why Darkness wants to be hated or hate and spite being such a central element of this mythology it's pretty much just there as Darkness's thing disconnected from anything except the things it makes. Without cross-referencing Ahriman in the rider at the end, what is the reader/listener supposed to take away from this Genesis?

                  The two things that come to mind are that this seems like a classic case of "I would have written a shorter letter, but I did not have the time" and that it would be prudent to figure out how to pace the presentation of information. Is it necessary for the Fae to be accounted for at the beginning of the world? Do we need to be told about the demon-king of Annwn and the Seventh Watchtower when things are just getting started? Is there any benefit to jamming the Triat into the cosmology while also stripping out half of Mummy's primordial gods, whose children are still included with dialogue lifted wholesale from their own mythology?

                  Like, it's valid to just want to kitchen-sink everything into your setting, but if you want to write a unified mythology it behooves you to construct it as a set of stories or at least a set of themes that hang together in an appreciable way. Most of what I'm seeing here is geographic narrative peppered with introductions to divinities from obscure parts of the universe, and making those characters relevant is the job of stories that come after the creation myth, where they can be introduced as they become relevant.

                      So- first, thanks for the feedback. Second, I want to clarify- making this look all like a hot mess was, in fact, intentional. I tried to mimic the "rambling of a madman" as a certain choice. Now, it may not have been a good choice, but it was a choice nevertheless. I wanted to use a style which was unclear, confusing, and that has all kinds of references to things which are not clear. I can't actually point for "what was the purpose" behind this document beyond "I felt a strong urge to write it". So yeah, it may feel compressed and unclear, but it was a choice- and I may revise it if it wasn't a good one. Now that this is out of the way, let's address some of the specific points which you brought out-

                      Originally posted by Satchel View Post
                      This first part needs another pass quite badly, possibly with an outline in mind. As-written it goes into too much detail on too many elements with too many names before it actually gets to anything of consequence, which is poison for a creation myth even without stapling Dreams of Avarice's second creation myth into the middle completely divorced from the context of the first one.

                      Like, I look at this and I cannot tell what the basis of the cosmology is, because the priority seems to have been naming or re-naming as many major cosmological figures from ChroD and WoD as possible and assigning them dominion over various groups of entities.
                      In terms of priority, the "first chapter" is meant to establish the world- so it doing a lot of name dropping and putting things in place was done because it tried to encompass the creation of the whole setting. Theoretically, other chapters are to build over the world as establish in here and take it forward. In terms of "what is the defining core of this cosmology", is to define reality as being created from a struggle between Light and Darkness, where each tries to assert dominance by making more and more aspects of reality. The acts of creation as stated in here are meant to represent that conflict, with each new creation is made as part of the struggle between the two entities.

                      Emptiness exists before presence and hate ostensibly exists because the first thing to exist wanted to be hated, but instead of any form of exploration of why Darkness wants to be hated or hate and spite being such a central element of this mythology it's pretty much just there as Darkness's thing disconnected from anything except the things it makes. Without cross-referencing Ahriman in the rider at the end, what is the reader/listener supposed to take away from this Genesis?
                      My main inspiration for this piece was Genesis, and yes the first line is taken from the book. However, choosing Ahriman as a name for the Darkness was indeed a very poor choice from my part, which I have changed now. I wanted to make the origin of the Darkness unclear and starting from it without explaining its motives of origin- and in a way, the Darkness and the Light are active forces in terms of making things which make other things, which in turn create more and more, etc, etc. Again, I took some inspiration from Genesis, but I assume that it works better in the Old Testament because God is a singular entity which makes whole of the world, while here each entity makes another entity which makes another. That, however, was also part of the message I wanted to convey- that the sources of creation make other creations which make others in their place, growing more and more distant from the world, like how the multiverse grows further and further from the creator gods. Whether or not I managed to convey that message is, again, another story.

                      The two things that come to mind are that this seems like a classic case of "I would have written a shorter letter, but I did not have the time" and that it would be prudent to figure out how to pace the presentation of information. Is it necessary for the Fae to be accounted for at the beginning of the world? Do we need to be told about the demon-king of Annwn and the Seventh Watchtower when things are just getting started? Is there any benefit to jamming the Triat into the cosmology while also stripping out half of Mummy's primordial gods, whose children are still included with dialogue lifted wholesale from their own mythology?
                      This part, I think, suffers mostly from this (at least in concept), being "part one". Again, those beings are being defined in here because I plan (ideally) to make them important later. The Seventh Watchtower is to have actions in a later part of the setting, as in here it represents the force behind (the vast majority) of the Lower Depths. Same from Annwn, and I have (in my head) plans for the Triat in a way which to adapt them into the setting to a degree. In terms of the Mummy gods, some of them are actually there, in a way- Amun is contained within the Light, for example, while others are planned to show up later. The line of dialogue which I used is just one that I really liked, and I wanted it to be a reference for Dreams of Avarice. Some gods have been, however cut out, with the decision for which enters and which doesn't depends on what I find interesting. So in this mythologies, things which may be important in the mythologies of other gamelines may get cut, while things which are minor are planned to get a larger role. So yeah, in this mythology, Annwn (for example) is much more important than it is in canon, while Geb is not so much, and the Triad are there because they need to be there for later.

                      Also, I should probably state what is the role of the gods in this mythology- Each of the gods represents a form of supernatural entity and, usually, a certain cosmological realm. Gaea is both the spirits of Earth and Hisil as a whole, Luna is the Moon and the core from which shapeshifters are born, the Triat are there because I plan of providing a setting adaptation for their Possessed creations without simply folding them into one of the current monster types. The Triat, however, are not meant to serve the same role they do in WoD, hence why I am putting them in a far more distant place in the setting, and while they have less influence over the cosmology they still have some.

                      So, yeah, you are correct in a way about it feeling like a "geographic narrative", because the point of this chapter is to provide the geography- and this is their relevance, in a way. The "characters" in this document are meant to be "geographic", as they are far less human and are more in relation for the protogeni which were birthed by Chaos than characters, where Tartarus and Nyx and Chronos are the principles behind the world personified. Gods from later generations, however, should be more character like (again, it is not like I'm too experienced in writing mythology, but that was the line of thought I had in mind while writing this piece)

                      At any case, thanks for your suggestions, and while I'll think about how I may improve this text, feel free to share any other thoughts you may have about this subject.

                                                      Well, it is a complicated question to answer- the thing is that while you could claim that they owe allegiance to their creators, that is not really true in this cosmology (as it is the case in many mythologies). In fact, you have a multitude of hierarchies which don't necessary answer to one another, as you have numerous entities of more or less equivalent power. While the Light and the Darkness rule supreme, they are also considered to not be very active (this is why they make their children, so they would act in their place). If we were to divide the many powers of this setting into groups, the Primordial Gods are definitely among the more powerful ones- but a fair share of them have also been deposed by newer generations, so old age is far from the only criteria. However, the Judges and the Nameless Gods are, with no doubt, found among the "gods of Earth", and as such their domain includes the life of earth, which puts them "below" cosmic entities such as the Tapestry or the Triad. Does it make them lesser? Maybe in the great sum of things, but on Earth the gods of Earth with no doubt rule supreme. That's why Gaea have a higher Rank than Luna and Helios in the Shadow- because if Helios were to manifest himself within Gaea, he enters her domain, and is now metaphysically below her.

                                                      So now that we establish that there are numerous hierarchies (which is why, like, the Chthonic Gods of the Underworld do not bow before the God Machine and vis versa), and that those Ranks are relative to one another (a spirit powerful in the Shadow of Earth would be much weaker if they manifest themselves of Pluto, for example), we can give a answer of some kind. First, the Judges are among the Gods of Life, which means that Sekhem and its manifestations is the aspect of reality falling under their rule. They are also a God of Earth, so their rule on Earth is considered to be very powerful, and a Primordial God, meaning they are ancient (in this context, older than humanity). All of that puts them close to the top of their own respective hierarchy. That puts them only below Fate, the Devourer, Life and Quest. The other Nameless Gods, however, are born from the Judges, and as such are put below them in that hierarchy. Now, while the Judges are technically below Life/Atum and the Quest, they are closer than those two cosmic entities, and are much more concerned with the matters of the world. Not only that, you may also say that while the Shan'iatu have failed during their Usurpation, it did gave them the tools to submit their power over Life, making them superior to Atum, while the Quest still resides outside of the Law. Fate, being unconcerned with the matters of Earth, so while they can't defy Fate they can do whatever they want as long as it does not violate Fate directly, and even then the younger generation of gods (like the Shan'iatu) can defy Fate, which is why the Judges rose above Life during Irem. That leaves the Devourer, and as we all know, the Devourer is very much above the Judges.

                                                      So In short, yeah, they are very much "high" simply out of being close of Earth, them having age and power and them willing to use it. Basically, they are the third or fourth most powerful power in all matters related to Sekhem- so as good as the leaders of their pantheon, comparable to the Blood, the God Machine or the Exarchs.
