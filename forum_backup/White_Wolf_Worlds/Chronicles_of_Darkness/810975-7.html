    Tier lists measure a classes potential, not necessarily what each and every character belonging to that class is going to achieve. Wizards are Tier 1, but that doesn't mean they can't be built terribly or played in a way that they are less useful than a Tier 3 or Tier 4 character. Wizards excel at being high intelligence casters and are Tier 1 based on the assumption that they're building towards what they're best at. That doesn't mean there isn't some 20 strength 12 intelligence wizards running around out there tossing around cantrips while punching people in the face. The same applies here. If a tier list says that something is a Tier X Y, then it's listing that whatever it is has the potential to be a Tier X Y, not that it will always be that regardless of build.

            Alright, assuming we try to construct a tier list, what's the metric? D&D's tier list works partially because of the game's base assumptions; that everyone is going to form a party and go adventuring together, encountering roughly four or so challenges each day. So how do we define CofD? Is it a game of "Visceral drama and personal horror"? "Phantom threats and savage fury"? or is it about "awe inspiring truths and deadly hubris"? Or is it some other splat's pitch phrase? The answer: it depends on the splat and storyteller.

            So how do we tier, in a way that is even mildly useful, the splats?
            • We could go the D&D route: raw problem solving ability. But while that works for D&D, where everyone is expected to be a buddy-buddy genocide squad opposing the DM's vicious schemes, it doesn't exactly reflect how CofD is played. Notably it makes no account for story, character and motivation which are essential to the Storytelling system.
            • We could go with the oft proposed five point statistic grid. Breaking each splat down by how their skill in each category. But that'll be comparing apples and oranges. Each splat has their own exceedingly different way of approaching every scenario, and it's hard to call one universally better than the other when the tiers can flip the instant the scenario changes.
            • We could break down each splat by how they play into other splats games. Which, IMO, is the only truly viable option. But that creates ten different tier lists, more as splats are added, and at that point why bother tiering them at all? Why not just write a really indepth guide to crossover addressing each splat?
            In the end we return to the question, what good is this doing? Because I've seen what the old tier lists do, and that's generate pages of bitching about Mage supremacy, imbalanced starting dots, and the (supposedly) tragic absence of Arch[Insert Splat Here]s.

                            I'm going to assume good faith here, so I'll propose a potential tier system for crossovers that doesn't misinform or overlook player choice.

                            The answer is actually simple: You don't list the splats in tiers, you list their mechanics.

                            So instead of Stealth: Tier 2: Vampire, you state Stealth: Tier 2: Obfuscate (Vampire Discipline). It will then become implicit that if the vampire character in your chronicle does not possess that Discipline, they will not be Tier 2 in Stealth.

                            The tiers would then be divided according to mechanical capabilities. Off the top of my head, I could see a tier in every major subsystem (Investigation, Chases, etc.) and then a subdivision of the main core systems (combat, social manouvering, etc.) and then we can include some common plot elements, to see what mechanics are the most suited to deal with them (for example, character imprisonment, a murder mystery, mortals discovering the character's true nature, etc.).

                            Would it be a very long and diverse tier list? Absolutely. Would it be very useful for crossovers and general Storyteller learning? Damn right.

                                Let me pose this question: Why tiers? What do tiers offer that a comprehensive breakdown of splat aptitudes and themes does not?

                                Because the only advantage I'm seeing is that it's concise. Like a cooking cheat sheet, someone can look at a tier list an develop an immediate idea of what goes well with what. Of course the tier list doesn't tell you that Vampires can't do shit with the Shadow, and a Werewolf / Vampire crossover that fails to address this issue will be plagued with dissonance. But they create a very compelling illusion of order. And people love order.

                                Sure you could expand it, address the X and Y of each splat, compare and contrast Disciplines with Embeds, but the more words you add the hard it is to grasp at a glance, and the harder becomes to put into a tiered order. In the end a detailed enough tier list is really no better than just reading the effing core books. Hence I am unconvinced of their utility.
