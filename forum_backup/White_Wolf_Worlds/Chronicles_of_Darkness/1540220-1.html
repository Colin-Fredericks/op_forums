        Alright, now that I've had a chance to spam the link everywhere, let's look at the Guillotines.

        Concordats, at least from a design perspective, are meant to be task-oriented, and oh boy do the Guillotines have a task. Carthians don't choose violence as a matter of course (at least officially), but when they do choose violence, they don't screw around.

        The Guillotines are who the Carthians call on when they face an existential threat or an enemy vile enough that the only answer is Final Death. They "charge" themselves with the blood of their comrades and gain terrible power for a short time to achieve this purpose. Does this make them ticking timebombs who might just diablerize the entire local Movement if pushed to the edge? Definitely, probably not.

        So, here are the Guillotines! Feel free to ask about the book or let us know what you think! And please consider leaving a star rating and/or a review! It's super helpful for word of mouth. I'll be back tomorrow with another excerpt!

        Guillotines: Blades of the Revolution

        I’ll make it quick.

        Executioners have a certain mystique. The black-hooded figure might belong more to fiction than reality, but he conjures a palpable aura of fear, of the ultimate authority of the government. While long used to make citizens submissive, during the French Revolution, executions turned into a symbol of the people’s justice, and the infamous guillotine became famous worldwide. The vampires who bear its name perform a similar function: They bring justice to the most wicked Kindred — and only the Carthian Movement is objective enough to decide who’s deserving of such punishment. Of course.

        In its early nights, the Movement comprised, in its vast majority, young vampires, barely a few decades removed from their mortal lives. This issue persists to this night, with the covenant’s membership skewing younger than most others. Unfortunately for them, age often means power in the All Night Society, and not just the abstract kind. When the Revolution stormed the streets of Paris, the guillotine became a symbol of equality before the law, killing all in the same quick and efficient way. But vampires faced a different challenge: While a mortal king dies as easily as anyone else, a prince can throw would-be assailants through brick walls or move at the speed of thought. This required some of the lateral thinking the Firebrands would later become famous for.

        More extreme wings of the covenant first advocated for diablerie as a quick route to power, but such voices were in the minority, and their experiments led to catastrophic results. What eventually bore fruit was a stopgap measure: selecting willing Kindred to feed on the Vitae of their fellows in large amounts, not draining any single vampire but drinking well above what one could normally consume. After trial and error, this enabled the Blood of the strongest one to adapt, allowing them to temporarily increase the ease Vitae could empower their physical forms. Drenched in gore and bringing equality to the masses, this survivor and their successors named themselves the Guillotines.

        The Executioners bring weight to Carthian threats. Their identities are often unknown even within the covenant, a secret weapon to be deployed only against the highest-profile targets. Despite their name, they rarely perform public executions, as capturing and keeping powerful Kindred on ice is both difficult and dangerous. Rather, they strike their targets where they ought to feel safe, employing a mix of Protean and daring to access them.

        The Guillotines pride themselves on avoiding needless pain in their duties: A strong killing blow to the neck or head is their preferred method of delivering Final Death. They often leave a sign of their presence though, like the Carthian crest or the image of a guillotine, so the masses know who delivered justice.

        Using the Guillotines is a last resort, as their methods pose several issues. Sooner or later, most become addicted to Vitae, and either blood-bound to most covenant members or in thrall to a single one, tangling them in complex emotional webs and reducing their loyalties to the Movement itself. And a Requiem of gorging on Vitae and murdering powerful vampires does little to keep the Beast in check, so most Guillotines who survive for any significant amount of time end up victims of powerful banes, less and less able to relate to mortals. The thought of a Guillotine draugr, driven to ruin by a hunger for Vitae they can never satisfy, is enough to keep even the most fanatical Carthians from calling upon them too easily.

        How we serve

        When an enemy of the people must fall, we’re the ones to answer the call. We gorge ourselves on the blood of our fellow Firebrands, and for one night, we revel in the kind of power others wait centuries to obtain. The look on the face of an ancient terror when it’s felled by someone a fraction of its age is one you’ll never get enough of.

        How we dissent

        Being a weapon can be frustrating if you remain sheathed for too long. Our duty is to the Movement, and it picks our targets, but staying inactive for too long can feel… itchy. And how quick our leaders are to forget us once they’re the ones in power. They should do well to remember a blade cuts both ways.

        Concordat Origins
        • “To arms, citizens!” echoed the cries of the French masses. In the nights of the Revolution, as the name E. B. Carth and Contre les vampires patriarcaux spread like wildfire and before the Movement that bears his name came together, a few desperate neonates chose one of their own to become the living weapon of their revolt, a blade that would cut down those who had dared violate the rights of the people. He carved a bloody path through the nights of Paris, eliminating tyrants who thought themselves unassailable for centuries. Le Guillotineur, as he was called, remained active throughout the days of the First French Empire and disappeared shortly after, but he had several apprentices and inspired many more. However, the fact that no record of his Final Death exists is of concern to some. How dangerous could an elder Executioner be?
        • It happened in a Brazilian city around the dawn of the 20th century. Some say Rio, or São Paolo, while others say it was somewhere far less important. It started as a beautiful example of Carthian revolution, the destruction of the entire Sanctified ruling class and the founding of a new order. But it didn’t go according to plan. The new “Colonel” styled herself a military dictator and enforced her rule through a brutal militia. She became crueler and more twisted with each passing night, to the point where vampires felt they had less power now than ever before. Something had to be done, and a small coterie of neonates uncovered the means to become Executioners. They fed on each other, forming a tangle of Vincula, and unleashed their revenge on the leaders who had betrayed the Revolution. Suspicion and murders only enhanced the Colonel’s paranoia, and the Movement crumbled around her, with the Invictus moving in to fill the gap — with their first official action burning the Executioners to ashes. Liberty or Final Death, indeed.
        • The Executioners are older than the Movement, or even guillotines for that matter. Ever heard the expression “avenging angel?” They’re a gift from God. No, they’re not from the Sanctified, either. They predate Longinus, with mentions of them in obscure rabbinical texts and Sumerian tablets, if you know where to look. Only the Revolution can call upon them because it’s the only covenant that fosters true community. Firebrand opinions about religion vary (like everything, some might say) but Carthians are the only Licks who consider the group, not just the individual. So, when things are dire, God — or Gaia, or the Curse, or whatever you want to call it — chooses some to become instruments of retribution. Sure, they have to hasten the process with Vitae, but what deity doesn’t ask for a little sacrifice? Everything has a price… and the Guillotines might not yet know what it is for their particular gift.
        Nicknames: Executioners, Butchers (derogatory)

                        Originally posted by Primordial newcomer View Post
                        My typical questions coming up

                        1. Favorite and most difficult section to write for the book? Along with your favorite antagonist?
                        The first chapter was a challenge, because it was mostly storytelling and player advice, and it's tricky to do those well without being dry. As for antagonists, I really like the Commonwealth of the two schisms (even though I wrote the other one, haha), and the hidebound are probably my favourite of the horrors, though they're all great.

                        2. Was there any big changes made during the process of creating this book? What aspect were you looking to emphasize, and what were you looking to not acknowledge at all (or view of the covenant you wished to dispel? Though I feel you already answered that with the popular "good vampires" belief around the carthians)
                        This one went through some growing pains. What I had in the outline is a lot different from what we ended up with, especially that first chapter.

                        Aside from dispelling the "good vampires" misconception, I wanted to present the Movement as more ideologically diverse than they sometimes get credit for. I think the closer they are to fitting into a particular human political ideology every time, the less interesting they are. That meant countering the stereotype that they're always left-wing in human terms. I also very much wanted to do away with the idea that the Carthians are just the Requiem version of the Anarchs.

                        We didn't really avoid talking about anything, I don't think!

                        3. Favorite covenant?
                        Definitely the Ordo Dracul, followed closely by the Sanctified and the Carthians sharing second place. I really like the Invictus, but I think in my mind they're kind of default vampires, which isn't really true, but that's just where my brain slots them in. I like the Circle of the Crone, but they're often my last choice as a player.

                        4. how will this book influence Circle of the Crone? What lessons have you learned making this book that will directly effect the next? Basically, what I'm asking is will you approach the next covenant books differently after the learning experience of this one?
                        I suspect the Crone book will be patterned off this one pretty significantly, since the Acolytes are the other "progressive" faction in the progressive/conservative–secular/spiritual dichotomy that the covenants have going (at least the ones that aren't the Dragons...but we'll get to that). That probably means that the Invictus and Sanctified books will form their own pair, and then the Dragons get to be on their own weird Z-axis.

                        In general though, I think I'm going to make sure I'm happy with the outline before I dig too deep. Looking back, it was a little underbaked, and that led to the book taking longer than I wanted.

                        Also as an aside, I realize you're not alone in this book, but i dont want to bother them with the same questions unless theyd like to answer
                        I don't think most of the authors are on here, but they're welcome to answer anything relevant if they come across this post!

                                                    Originally posted by Primordial newcomer View Post
                                                    In order to reconcile existing within the Carthian Movement, you have seemed to given them somewhat of a "red scare" theme. Is this intentional? I quite like it, especially since it doubles as a pun.
                                                    I didn't really go out of my way to play up the Red Scare. Rather, that happened pretty naturally. The original in The Danse Macabre isn't aesthetically styled after stalinism, but it has all the hallmarks of it: Authoritarian socialism with enforced ideological purity. It's Soviet at its absolute worst, and I was directed to lean into that. So how do I turn that into a group that could believably coexist (if by necessity rather than choice) with all the other Covenants?

                                                    Simply put, they just can't be open about their true nature and goals. In the biggest break from the TDM version (other than turning them into a schism of the Carthians rather than a conspiracy intended to replace them, but that's the premise of including them in this book) I made them careful, because they absolutely need to be in order to survive as a group. They hide, they infiltrate, they recruit through sweet offers of their perceived utopian society. I don't think it's surprising that McCarthy and Hoover (and others) envisioned the Soviets to do the same.
                                                    Besides, its already their MO regarding human society. It's kinda natural they do the same in the All Night Society.

                                                    2nd, how do you (or how would you) intend to run them in your settings in terms of scale? In general, is it fair to assume they have a fair amount of small experimental cities set up, or are even these so few as to be special edge cases each?
                                                    It really depends on the needs and tone of the game. If it's a paranoid game of politics and intrigue, the Commonwealth might be a big regional power hidden just out of sight, but with probably at most one pilot city for the coterie to stumble upon, ticking perfectly (like a time bomb). A game where the players play Citizens, the entire game might be set in one of them. In most other cases, the Commonwealth might not have any pilot cities, but rather have similar attempts to "live" openly in gated communities or reveal themselves for fully infiltrated organisations as proof-of-concept.
                                                    But in most cases, it doesn't really matter how many of these cities the Commonwealth has set up since most games of Vampire doesn't feature a lot of travel. And with the top-down hierarchy of the Commonwealth, it's unlikely most people involved in one of these cities would know about any others, if they exist.

                                                    3rd, unlike the AOA, the Commonwealth has quite a lot of mechanical exceptions from the Movement while still being part of it. Was this something you had in mind from the start, or was there more to it.
                                                    That's the thing. Is the Commonwealth part of the Movement? It's certainly born from it, but the line between them is intentionally blurred. Whether or not they are separate (if still largely overlapping), the Commonwealth has a very strong identity and hierarchy which definitely sets it apart from (the rest of) the Movement. Which is why a few Merits got some modifications to reflect this. For example, Casual User grants Haven because that's one of the things the Commonwealth explicitly intends to provide for all Kindred once they take power (as their societal duty, so no need to suspect ulterior motives).

                                                    Which is also why Commonwealth Law differs so much from regular Carthian Law. Instead of imposing effects on a domain (as most Carthian Laws do), they empower the individual Citizen to the role they've been given by the Commonwealth. Why/how this works like it does, and whether Commonwealth Law can even be considered a type of Carthian Law or if it's its own thing, would be interesting to explore, but alas, word count. (Originally, the direction was to use Carthian Laws if I wanted to give them a covenant-type advantage, but I quickly got the go-ahead for the current concept of Commonwealth Laws.)

                                                    4th, how was the experience re-introducing this group to the game?
                                                    It was fun to turn these guys from gonzo global conspiracy to a more grounded version while still maintaining their extreme goals. I immediately decided on making them competent and insidious, as opposed to the half-baked fanatics that their own writeup in TDM repeatedly states are idiots. After that (and ignoring the blood orgy thing (though it kinda has a successor in one of the Commonwealth Laws)) the rest mostly just fell in place, including things like organizational structure which TDM mostly skipped (most likely because you're expected to run them like Carthians).

                                                    The process was actually quite similar to the Sta-Au which I updated for Bloodlines: The Ageless. Also a group that should've self-imploded in their original version (imo), that I focused on grounding while still maintaining the extremist factor that's a core part of their identity. (Hmm. Is it possible to be "typecast" as a writer? After just two books? )

                                                    Oh, and who made the art for it? I love it
                                                    You'll have to ask Yossarian for that, I'm afraid.​​
