    Changeling Powers (Old Man Yells At Cloud Edition)

    04-23-2015, 04:51 PM
    Wrote this over a bored and speculative lunch hour. It is too long, and too meandering, and hasn't been edited. Also, it doesn't take into account spoilers or previews, because I've yet to actually read any of them. Feel free to correct any assumptions or already-denied speculation made in that direction.

    ...

    So, Changeling - like the rest of the nWoD - is up for its second edition. The game's not without its flaws, but I suspect most of its changes will be a case of rearranging pieces rather than reworking the game board. Just the name tells us that the writers have picked up on how difficult it was at times to use the True Fae, since their antagonism often felt like the binary sort afforded a sleeping dragon - giving more focus to (and reasoning behind) their huntsmen,
    turncoats and mercenaries would be a great move.

    Anyway, I’m making this post purely to muse on Changeling’s “powers” while vaguely hoping no-one notices how long it’s been so I so much as skim-read one of their books.

    The game has a fair spread of powers - along with your innate Changeling abilities, personal Seeming/Kith powers, and various supernatural Merits, you’ve got Contracts (Goblin and otherwise), Pledges, Tokens, Court widgets, the privileges of Entitlements, and so on. It can seem like an eclectic mix.
    It wasn’t until relatively late in my time playing Changeling - which was itself, sadly, still quite a while ago - that I realized most Changeling powers (well, all of them, but some only in the abstract, ultimate sense) derive from the same basic source. I don’t mean some in-universe source like Glamour or Arcadia - I’m speaking in terms of concept.

    This is especially disguised by how broad Changelings are, as a splat - along with covering practically every goblin, ghoul, and gorgon in terms of aesthetic, they’ve got one of the widest - or perhaps, most disparate - range of powers of any splat. Only Mages rival them, and their approach to power has far more surface coherence.

    ...

    What their powers tie back into is, essentially, Pledges. This might have been obvious to some, but for me it was a tricky deduction, in no small part because my group’s reaction to the pledge system was a mixed combination of “viciously abuse” and “bitterly shun”, with little middle ground.

    Contracts are oaths sworn to the narrative nature of whatever the Contract is about. The “catch” that lets you use a given clause without spending Glamour isn’t some extra bonus - it’s how the clause is supposed to work, the tit for its tat. Tongues of Birds and Wolves is part of an oath sworn to the storyself of beasts, where creatures will speak in exchange for a name - burning Glamour to do it without that catch is you cheating like the filthy elf you are. Pipes of the Beastcaller can be used to ask an animal to guard your home for free, because that’s the boon you received in exchange for whatever service won you the third “dot” of that Contract - using it for anything else requires Glamour, because you’re greasing the wheels.

    I wouldn’t be surprised if non-fae entities could make use of clauses through their catch. Hypothetically, anyway, if they learned how - god knows it’d be difficult. Being a Changeling “just” gives you a direct line to learning them via Courts and Clarity, as well as a supply of “fuck you I’m a fairy” fuel to ignore the catch. I wouldn’t be surprised if only Changelings and True Fae actually got to spend Glamour in that way - every other fae creature can sign up to Contracts, but they have to follow the rules. Hell, maybe it’s just Changelings, a result of their hybrid nature, and even True Fae need to lawyer around those clauses rather than just outright ignoring them.

    Maybe that’s what makes Goblin Contracts so particularly vicious - it’s impossible for even Changelings to blast through them with Glamour, so their catches (which are nasty) always come into play.

    This applies to other things, too. Courts are old, potent group pledges with big concepts like Seasons or Directions or Humours as their guarantors. Entitlements have a fairly obvious relationship with that sort of oath.

    None of this is a huge revelation, but in second edition - which includes Demon’s twisted take on “for the low low price of YOUR SOUL” - I’d kind of expect pledges to emphasise this aspect of themselves for the sake of differentiation if nothing else. Less bargains, more geasa. I’d also probably expect the “rewards” for Pledges to move further away from concrete material effects. Focusing more on things like “good luck” or “a bit of Glamour” than “dots in Merits”. Hell, Pledges would be really easy to make into Conditions that offer Glamour instead of Beats (or in addition to, I suppose).

    You can extend this even further. Clarity is at the heart of Changeling. It’s about the imposition of order and stability on your world. Really, in terms of equivalent “human” systems, it’s closer in nature to 2e’s Integrity than 1e’s Morality. Except, well…

    It’s about the imposition of rules. Making the world make sense. Drawing borders around your life, devising little rituals that keep you safe and sane. This is how fantasies are built - rules burning possibilities out of the frothing, formless madness. Iron wards off fairies. Knock three times, or the witch will eat you. If you keep your bathroom clean, the dirtlicker will stay away. Without rules, the True Fae would be unable to exist as discrete entities. Those rules may be self-imposed, but they define who/what/when/why/how each otherwise-shapeless is, to their benefit and penalty alike.

    The lower your Clarity is, the closer you are to the mindset of the Unshaped, where “rules” are self-imposed for the sake of advantage or amusement. Where laws are nonsense, the world fundamentally doesn’t make sense, stability and attachment are jokes, and nothing you can do will ever keep you safe, because there are no rules.

    The higher your Clarity, the closer you are to the mindset of a Pledge, where rules are everything, however arbitrary. The kind of little ceremonies often adopted by those suffering from trauma or OCD (oh man I wonder why that comparison might come to mind) to ward off their personal devils, internal or otherwise. To keep them level. Routines and rituals. Patterns and pledges.

    That these rules don’t necessarily have to gibe with human common sense is another matter, mind.

    If someone else breaks THE RULES and they can’t be punished or corrected, that hurts Clarity. For someone at Clarity 10 who’s kept up close ties with human society, it probably grinds their gears less to see someone jaywalk if they get hit or scared by a car. They ventured into the dark forest, out of the proscribed area, and they were chastised appropriately. The world is turning appropriately. Everything makes sense.

    Of course, breaking your own rules and routines is much worse.

    Is that healthy? Not really. But 2e seems more comfortable with moving “Morality” stats into a spectrum rather than 10 = Buddha, 1 = Satan. Going full-on Killing Joke isn’t particularly healthy, either. There’s no perfect solution to your problems.

    I honestly wouldn’t be surprised if (real actual not-avatar or whatever we’re calling it now) True Fae had Clarity as their “health” stat. If they let their identity become too blurred, if they compromise their own rules and narrative too much, they’re weakened or dissolve entirely beneath the Wyrd, the weave of squint-and-you’ll-invent-it perceive patterns that float to the surface of Arcadia’s formless chaos.

    Perception is how we make sense of the world, which is without that context a meaningless froth of energy. It takes a sapient perspective to lend that chaos the sense of discrete objects and forces. This is how the fae enter our world. It’s why they seem like chimera or funhouse distortions - their forms and behaviours are ultimately built by components confirmed by human thought. They have to be, or they wouldn’t be able to interact with our world in any meaningful sense. It’s where the need for rules comes from.

    The idea “this is just a form you can comprehend” is thrown around occasionally - but for the Unshaped, it’s the only option. In body and mind and narrative. You won’t find something so Lovecraftian that it sends people insane because they can’t grasp one iota of its alien logic - not lurching out of Arcadia. “A form you can comprehend” is the square peg that lets the True Fae slot into a world that we’re determined runs on quadrilateral rules.

    …

    Another thing worth considering is the structure of Contracts. At the moment they’re closer to Disciplines or Gifts than things like Arcana or Manifestations. That works pretty well at the moment. Certainly, it offers a few advantages - groups of powers linked by theme are a pretty good way to communicate the story-nature of a contractor, since you can include different “steps” in their story and tie them together by requiring they be purchased in order. If the story of the fairy “chemist” includes being a snake-oil salesman who can sell whatever ointment or tincture he likes to the gullible, that power can be slotted right alongside powers to do with brewing or distilling or whatever.

    The downside, of course, is that someone who wants to play the crazy fairy chemist is then locked into also having to buy a power for flogging piss with ink as a hair-restorative. I’m someone who enjoys stuff like that, and the impact it can be intuited to have on the setting, but it’s hardly a universal position, especially in games where xp is limited, and (with beats) tied directly to overcoming hardships in-game. Few people enjoy having to go over speedbumps before they can start developing the skills they actually want. That’s the downside of powers that are not linked purely by utility.

    Dropping the “must buy in order” aspect and allowing clauses to be purchased individually is an option, but at that point the teeth of linked clauses has been pulled, so why even bother with it? In that situation, there’s basically only two benefits to claiming a link between the various dots of the Darkness Contract; to make it easier to read, and to make it easier to establish “favoured” powers that get an xp discount based on your kith or court or whatever. And in the latter case, it seems like it’d be simpler to “decentralize” favoured powers, so that rather than Fairest favouring Vainglory, Reflection and Separation, the powers that comprise those Contracts are tagged as being favoured by Fairest.

    Another option is just splitting off the powers altogether. A power system like Mage, where lots of individual “powers” are presented under broad umbrellas, feels too coherent for Changeling, which has a power system rooted in lots of individual contracts with finicky stuff like “Mirrors”. Pretty much the only workable category-system I can see would be the six Seemings… which could be interesting, though it sort of rules out powers favoured by multiple types of changeling.

    Each “Contract” being an individual power bought on its own would have the downside of breaking up coherent little story-trees that tell players “how [thing] works in Arcadia/stories”, but the upside of flexibility.

    ...

    Second Edition has so far been pretty consistent about scratching a little deeper when it comes to the “root” of previously-established splats. I’d expect more elaboration on the nature of Contracts, in line with this.

    Are they a special sort of long-lasting Pledge with a story-nature of a thing? Are they something carved into the actual narrative substrate of the rules that define each Changeling? Do they exist independent of any Changeling’s relationship with them, more like skills that can be learned than deals that are made? To what extent is there a difference?

    Perhaps the most interesting direction to spin off that sort of exploration is in how you “learn” a Contract. I don’t recall much detail on that in 1e, so now’s probably the time to move in on it.

    If I want to develop the Contract of Stone, do I need to hunt down the avatar of Stone, or find a legal intermediary, or intuit the fine print of its clauses from the lines of strata in a quarry, or find a suitable stone elemental, or perform some kind of abstract magic negotiation involving an embarrassing dance and thirty distinct pieces of quartz? How does this work?

    Setting aside the possibility of fae or older Changelings “signing me in” for a fee, are there situations where I’ll need to pay an entry cost just to get the Contract - enact a narratively-appropriate doom on a Pledgebreaker to open up the Contract of Omens, for instance?

    Obviously you’d want to try and keep these distinct from Werewolf Gifts, which are also bundles of themed powers acquired from concept-driven entities, albeit ones rather less nebulous and story-bound than whoever the other party is in Changeling Contracts. In practical terms, there is definitely a difference - Werewolves learn to use their spirit-power to do spirit-things from spirits. Changelings are given backdoor access to the buttons that make the world’s stories go beep boop (in exchange for fulfilling their end of the clauses - or the aforementioned bribe/strongarm of Glamour). That aspect of their power therefore seems more external.

    Maybe draw a bit more on the relationship of Courts and Entitlements to specific Contracts? It always felt more appropriate for Courts to influence the development of Contracts, as opposed to Seemings. I guess that’s supposed to represent which storyselves are already woven into the Changeling’s altered body and soul… but it almost seems like the logical progression from there is that Contracts learned after the durance should alter the Changeling’s mien, too. Well, I suppose there’s an element of that with Court Mantles, if you take “Courts are a kind of mass Pledge/Contract”.

    ...distinct models of Contract are developing in this section of the ramble alone, which if anything suggests that maybe they need to be defined more - at least to the extent of in-universe knowledge.

          This is what's mentioned about gaining Contracts in 1e;

          Some Contracts are open to all changelings: The "common" Contracts of Dream, Hearth, Mirror, and Smoke. Other Contracts rely upon seemings or Courts, and their powers are more dearly gained by those not of the favored group. Along these lines, Contracts are not generally something that a changeling strikes himself with something else. Rather, most established Contracts have been formed by a body of Fae or changelings. When a player buys a Contract for a character, that represents the changeling engaging his right to "accept" any one particular level or clause of a Contract to which he's entitled via citizenship. For instance, a Contract of Smoke available to all changelings would probably be something they were entitled to accept by virtue of being changelings, as they fell into the category of potential "party of the first part" when the Fae took them in and infused them with Glamour. Other Contracts are more specialized. Thus, when an ogrish patron made you a changeling with an Ogre seeming, you became eligible for the specialized seeming Contracts that were struck by the Ogres in particular. A changeling of another seeming would also be eligible to invoke an ogrish Contract, by the Byzantine ties of fae blood, but the more distant connection makes it more expensive to "initial the clause," so to speak.
          So, I always saw Contracts as things that a Changeling willingly accepts themselves to be a part of, due to being entitled to do so. It looks like Contracts were spun either by Fae and changelings can take advantage of them, or a specific group of changelings make other Contracts (the Seeming ones and perhaps others) seperately from the True Fae.
