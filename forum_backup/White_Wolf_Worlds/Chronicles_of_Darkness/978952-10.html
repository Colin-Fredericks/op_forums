    Originally posted by Resplendent Fire View Post
    Right here: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8fhpXrrLBsI

    About 20 minutes in, there's a question about the old vs. new World of Darkness. Shane DeFreest makes a comment to the effect that the World of Darkness was designed as a world first and then a game, but that the Chronicles of Darkness was designed as a game first, and that this is a flaw. I'm not sure what to think wrt that, given that I generally like ChroD versions of games more than CWoD versions. Martin Ericsson's response is a bit kinder to ChroD.

    I'm not trying to pick an argument or anything here, but I am wondering what people think about the commentary.
    With all due respect to Shane, the facts, in terms of the design history at work, are the opposite. The classic WoD games were planned by name, but were designed sequentially, including their core setting concepts. Like, when VtM was released Mage's Consensus was not a thing. Changeling wasn't even the name of the game and had no core concept. By Revised the editorial line was to put the line you were working on before the idea of a cohesive setting. 2nd tried for heavy integration with mixed results.

    CofD was designed to support integrated games in a common setting from the beginning, right down to discussions about how different types of characters interacted socially and supernaturally.

    However! To be fair the solution for CofD was to amp up the level of mystery, the number of unanswered questions, and to take a light touch, so those efforts were invisible. The funny thing is that when, say, Mage: The Ascension says something about the CWoD setting and Werewolf contradicts it you end up with a lot of loud interactions because fans get on about it and creators have to deal with it. When the problem comes solved out the gate, it doesn't draw as much attention.

        Anyway, having worked on WoD material back in the old White Wolf as well as what is now called CofD, I suppose my main insight is that the process of keeping the WoD going for over a decade the first time around imparted a number of lessons and insights that I cajoled and inferred out of people I worked with, and which I learned directly, myself. I'll be interested to see how many of those lessons were learned by other people. There's a tendency in game design on established lines for folks to come in and decide their predecessors didn't know a damn thing and they're totally going to fix everything. Then they make the same mistakes. (Incidentally, Storypath, which I designed the mechanical spine of, is meant to be specifically hardened against this problem.) I think this is probably inevitable and even part of the creative process, but it still means you can expect a rough ride as the team finds its feet.

                    Originally posted by Omegaphallic View Post

                    Facinating, that thank you for the design comparison and the rebuttle.
                    Well I'm sure Shane has more complex thoughts than that quip. One if the interesting things about the WoD is that it's design was pretty jazzy, in that folks fluidly solved problems with whatever was on hand instead of building things off of a strict structure. Like, the Brujah add their lore and setting beats to VtM but they were also, intentionally, totally a way to play a Fighter-type character. That gives it an organic feeling that adds to immersion, so a "game" solution also contributes something to the world. The fact that WoD systems and concepts are designed to address action in the world means that even after all this time I can still design WoD stuff about twice as fast as CofD stuff, as I learned working on some Mage and Vampire books after maybe a decade away from that setting. So even though I don't agree with the account of how this stuff was made, I do see it as more of a statement of focus -- though again, I'd say that work like the Seers of the Throne really was about the world, not just clockwork for the game machine.

                                                    Originally posted by Omegaphallic View Post
                                                    &quot;The fact that WoD systems and concepts are designed to address action in the world means that even after all this time I can still design WoD stuff about twice as fast as CofD stuff, as I learned working on some Mage and Vampire&quot;

                                                    Why does CoD stuff take longer to design then WoD stuff, like what are CoD's systems and concepts designed to address in comparison?
                                                    In WoD you have many more switches to throw and less of an overarching game design mission. CofD has some specific principles you need to obey every time. Some folks forget, but this is generally the case. For instance, in WoD I can monkey with the target number, play with accumulated successes in a fine-grained manner, and use opposing dice pools in a freewheeling way. In CofD the target number is fixed in all but one game, opposed rolls are not as common because of the principle of keeping the number of dice rolls to a minimum, and there's a general desire to avoid best options for things, even if representing the world becomes less of a direct thing. So if I want to design, say, some rule for a flying kick, in WoD I just do it with whatever will produce a result that feels like it will output a flying kick in the world. In CofD I have a smaller set of tools and of course, model Merits to compare to.

                                                    I don't think any one way is the best, but I will note that WoD comes from an era immediately after the "realism" focus of the 80s and In the infancy of the genre emulation trend, and when the idea was that rules were ways to describe how things work in the world with a dramatic edge, and that one would, when necessary, use them as benchmarks for streamlined and freed form play, and never as RAW. Contemporary game designers often don't understand or like these principles, and they don't work when you're not playing with a group that has built a high degree of mutual trust, so this has fallen by the wayside to the point where even though many fans get it and like it, you may never see these ideas embraced again. I think it deserves another look, but given that MET is basically anathema to this in its current form, where it is designed to moderate between antagonists in a PvP context, I'm not sure we'll see it from WW either. CofD is designed to be fast and stable, though it requires a level of abstraction that makes the semi-free form option trickier.
