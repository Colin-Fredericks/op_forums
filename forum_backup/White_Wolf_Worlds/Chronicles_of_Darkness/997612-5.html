                        I think I need to re-evaluate my original opinion of Genius: the Transgression. It been a loooong couple of days since I first discovered Genius and was drawn in by the cool ideas it presented. That was before the cracks began to show, it was a more innocent time back then. And yet even then there was one thing about Genius that made me worry, and as I delved deeper into that abyss it only grew and grew until now what few bright points I could say about Genius have been all but drowned out. And that is such a shame, because I still believe Genius could be a good game. It won't, but it could. So....in a manner of speaking I truly did get to experience the core premise of Genius. Discovering a whole new vista of reality, only to be crushed and broken on the ground like Icarus because that vision of a better Genius will never come to pass. But more on that soon, first I need to cut the melodrama and give some credit where credit is due.

                        To all those who have posted so far. Those who have provided their own critiques about Genius, those who provided engaging counter arguments in favor of Genius, those who provided insight and corrections to above mentioned critiques and counter arguments. I thank you all. If it wasn't for this dialogue I fear that my time spent thinking about Genius and how it could be better would have been completely wasted. Right, let's get to the heart of this one last time.

                        I mentioned in my original post that Genius is one of the most stifling games I've ever seen. I original though this was because it clung to stubbornly to the trappings of Mad Science, until it was later pointed out in a counter argument that the rules themselves lend for a lot of wiggle room. My fears were abated for a time, but that stifling feeling never really went away. After exploring more and more nitpicks of mine in an attempt to reach the root of this frustrating feeling I had I ran across a part of the source material I hadn't read before. This is when I fully understood why Genius felt so stifling and frustrating. Genius the Transgression has a constant subtext that's reinforced in all of it's terminology, mechanics, aesthetic, themes, and writing. And it's no more apparent (besides maybe the infamous "No one's in charge" section that I foolishly gave the benefit of the doubt too) than in the section about the Clockstoppers, Genius the Transgression's version of the Autumn People from Changeling the Dreaming...oh how foolish I've been.

                        Genius isn't trying to be Mage the Ascension, Genius is trying to be Changeling the Dreaming. Or more specifically: Genius suffers from a subtext of pride & pretension.

                        Now to be fair, Changeling the Dreaming learned from it's subtext and greatly improved from it's 1st Edition roots (With the exception of the Autumn People, but that was their Time of Judgment phase so they're optional.) But Genius has yet to do so, there is still an unearthly level of pretension surrounding being a Genius and how their culture views the world. Mania (creativity energy) exists in all humans, but only academia generate enough of it for Genii to draw upon via "science talk." Regular humans disrupt Mania in a Genius' inventions and forces the poor misunderstood souls to hide that part of their life from anyone outside of the Genii community. Both good and bad Genii truly believe that humans can't lead themselves without being guided by one or more of the Genii, even the insane yet still superior somehow Lemurians. When foolish non-Genii make astounding discoveries about reality it literally creates contradiction paradoxes that not only invalidate the discovery but normally go about trying to destroy the world unless the beneficent Genii step in to save them. The horror of the game is supposed to come from the Genii realizing that their own superior intellect is the most horrifying thing in the world because it will either inevitably drive them tragically mad or the universe will never appreciate their genius and ensure every miraculous super-device they will into being is crushed by the mundane plebeians or their own extraordinary hubris. And of course, the greatest danger to a Genii outside of themselves and the chaotic universe where there is no meaning without their own brilliance, is the Clockstoppers: humans who's minds don't have a single creative or original though in their heads who literally suck up and destroy all Mania around them as they seek to make a world without scientific and technological advancement...

                        ...*sigh*...


                        Originally I said that I liked Genius the Transgression. Well, that's not the case anymore. And the tragedy of that is that I know the above isn't what the game is trying to be about. Genius wants to be a game about trying to make a difference in a world of darkness, and the struggles of that endless battle. I was shone that by those who love this game, and I want so much for Genius to be that game. But the subtext is there: Genius has nothing but utter contempt for those who don't consider intelligence to be the defining quality of a human being. And that means that all of the author's veiled distain is targeted at people like me. I am not an exceptionally intelligent person, heck I have trouble doing mental math. According to the game Mania doesn't overflow from me, and likely if I touch an invention I'll ruin it for someone who's superior in every way to me. It hurts reading a game like that, it's depressing seeing a world like that. If that is what being a Genius is like then I don't want to play from the perspective of one.

                        And that is all I have. Just bitter disappointment and melted wings.
