    Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post

    It's a bit of an unfair question though, because the audience isn't a monolith. I think forums do tend to skew things though. When I play with my normal RPG groups, or even back before COVID and I was a convention regular, there isn't nearly the same level wanting something non-horror. Forums tend to amplify negativity towards the core premise (not just of CofD) because you get a mix of people that like something enough to want part of what is being offered but want to change other bits, and older hands that have played to core conceit enough times to start wondering about tinkering with it for novelty.

    The old "Superheroes with Fangs" thing with VtM when talked about online could make it sound like the vast majority of people only played VtM for the dark power fantasy, but VtM is still a horror game all these decades later, and is still selling despite dark power fantasy games being around if people want that more.

    From a marketing perspective, it doesn't even really matter if some of your customers don't really want a horror game as long as your product is close enough to what they want that they'll buy it and cut out the horror parts.

    The CofD isn't being soft-cancelled because there aren't enough people buying it to be financially viable. Between the people that want it for what it's trying to be, and the people that buy it because it's close enough to what they want that they'll put in the effort to fill in the gaps, it sells as a horror franchise. Hell some percentage of sales are probably just people that buy anything Onyx Path does. From some of the crowdfunding reward tiers that get taken, it's clear there's a few whales in the community.

    I think it's worth thinking about something were the horror is more optional if Onyx Path were to serious consider their own product to replace the profits from making CofD books, but I don't think some reoccurring forum complaints are enough to scrap the horror focus completely by themselves.
    I find this sensible, even if I find it hard to shake the question (I've been softly asking it since 2015, so some habits die hard).

    Still, to bring it back to my overall point, the idea of OPP doing it's own take on Chronicles(/World) with the serial numbers filed off does still invite a question about core assumptions that might do with a little bit of challenging, and that's more interesting a question that the rehash of "What should a Chronicles 3rd Edition look like" this thread was stealth ("stealth") going for at any rate.

        Perhaps an approach to the question that would be most fruitful to dig into: What kind of horror should a potential new product aim for?

        With a mix of pros and cons, the *oD games have gone with an approach of a very vague horror themes in the general setting, and relied on the specifics of each line to highlight specific types of horror more effectively. This stands in contrast to games like CoC, Unknown Armies, and Kult, where even with plenty of character options, the core horror premises are kept more constant across niche play options within the wider games.

        I would personally argue that's one of the big things that makes *oD special and enduring, but it's always left some of the splats feel off kilter. Mage in both has always suffered in it's more subtle "the PCs are supposed to be assholes" style of horror. Exalted also has similar problems. There's nothing else exactly like Exalted, and a lot of people get Exalted even though they really want something else, and the different kinds of Exalted each highlight specific themes and approaches to the concepts of epic heroes. At the same time, some Exalted were pretty dialed in from the start and haven't really had more than mechanical updates, while others have struggled to find their "best" place over multiple editions.

        But it does feel like a lot of contention in the community does stem from the lack of cohesion at a high level of the game with the expectation that individual games will define things more thoroughly. Even with the idea the WoD was "gothic-punk," and CofD was "gothic," that really was about Vampire as the other games never seem to feel beholden to those concepts. Exalted does do better here, because even a game of mortals in Creation still holds to the idea of playing heroes in an epic story. What's a high concept like that, which would result in a more thematically unified set of games where what the more subtle or nuanced monsters are easier to interpret because of it?

            You do understand the function of mythology, right? Like, the things that are objectively true about the setting are largely in the present:

            The Supernal describes the Phenomenal (imperfectly, thanks to the Abyss, and, as Dave pointed out so many years ago, the Forces Arcanum describing electricity doesn't mean electricity is made of magic) and the Exarchs rule the Supernal through their subjugation of many of its remaining denizens.

            The Begotten have a metaphysical connection with the vast majority of supernatural creatures (when they get their powers from being merges with a supernatural being from the "conceptual being who resonates with things to do with that concept" category).

            The Arisen were made through the Rite of Return and are supported by cults to the Judges (and the rest of their history is for them to find out at their peril)

            Werewolves are not-quite-Flesh and not-quite-Spirit and have drives to hunt and maintain territory that have always led many of them to interpret other supernatural creatures through a lens that lets them justify acting as though they're something Uratha should deal with (for which Sin-Eaters and Storm Lords will probably always have some rocky ground to cross in meeting).

            Demons and changelings have big divine monsters after them, the fears and hopes around which lead many of them to interpret other supernatural creatures as being related to those god-monsters as additional threats to be tackled or avoided or as vectors for aid to be courted or exploited (if not both).

            Hunters who haven't managed to catch enough of a break to go into "remission" have a chasing-the-supernatural problem, and vampires have sires and Prometheans have genitors and deviants have progenitors (or at least circumstances that made them what they are), and so on.

            If your players are demanding "Supernal Symbology" in the Phenomenal otherworlds they're asking for nonsense, but also the analogous phenomena there are to be found in the Incarnae and Celestines of the Shadow, the tableaus of the Underworld's Lower Mysteries, powerful and deeply hidden facilities, and the depths of Arcadia and Duat. Secret-levers-of-the-world stuff has gotten more PC-accessible in 2e

Everything can be kept separate from each other or have excuses made for their interaction, it's just that Mage and Beast are specifically lines about exploring the supernatural and learning about supernatural creatures and so their toolkits facilitate that and their in-character mythology supports them engaging in those pursuits.

                I feel like the horror/superheroes with fangs dichotomy is a false one. A big part of the appeal to me is the most obvious one: you get to be the monster. You get to do things that are horrifying, but you don't necessarily experience the horror as you would in other "horror" media since your character is often the source of it. I think this is actually quite unique, even when other RPGs let you play as a "monster" they often make it so that you are really just playing a hero wearing a monster outfit.

                This also affects the social dynamics around the table in interesting ways. Legend of the Five Rings can be just as political a game as Vampire, if not even more so. However players don't expect the other players to backstab them at the first opportunity in L5R (at least not at my table), and they don't fondly reminisce about the time one player character betrayed and diablerised another. Other CofD games don't necessarily have this element, but most of them do share the feature that awful things are going to be done by your character and happen to your character over the course of the game. I might expect my packmates in Werewolf to have my back, but I don't expect them to act in a way that would be considered moral (except by other Werewolves). Exploring the odd needs of these characters, how they square these realities with their human moralities, or exploring the odd blue-orange moralities that they end up adopting instead are all compelling pieces of these games.

                                        Pretty interesting thread - I've personally appreciated the thinking being expressed here. One thing though, which addresses the title of this thread if not the rest of the OP itself, is the unalterable fact that Paradox owns both WoD and CofD. We don't, so if we were to create a new urban horror or fantasy game line, we wouldn't be able to basically do the new edition of any of the games under those two brands. We wouldn't be able to have a group of vampires called the Grujah (or even the Malkovians!) who are real hot tempered, or a magic-using group called the Beers of the Throne. So anything we'd be creating would have to be something new. Which is not to say that we couldn't explore the same or similar themes, structure the game world in ways that we know work for horror gaming, or use every bit of the experience we've gained after decades of creating WoD and CofD projects, not to mention learning from creating those new projects where we have come close to horror - as some folks have mentioned up-thread.

                                        As for system design, we wouldn't be using yet another version of the old WW systems, we'd use our own game system: the Storypath System. In fact, I'd imagine we'd use something like Storypath Ultra, the newest iteration that we'll be previewing at the GAMA Trade Show at the end of the month, and that folks are seeing hints of in the World Below teaser blog these last bunch of weeks. The World Below uses Storypath Ultra, and while it is a fantasy game, it is pretty dark (no pun intended with the setting being underground) and we're learning a lot about how Ultra delivers gaming that allows the system to complement the setting.

                                        Anyway, hope I haven't derailed the conversation, just wanted to illustrate some of the realities we face if, sometime in the dim mists of the future, we went ahead and got back on that horrific horse.
