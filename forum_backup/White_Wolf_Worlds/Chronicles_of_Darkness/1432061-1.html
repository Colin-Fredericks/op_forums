                                        Oh hey! A Splat Tier List Thread. Been a while since I've seen one of these.

                                        When approaching things like tier lists it helps to consider where this concept comes from, and the OG tier list: Dungeons & Dragons 3.5e. You can read a lovely breakdown of said system here: https://1d4chan.org/wiki/Tier_System

                                        It's a bit of a read, so the short version is: The Tier list sets out to measure each class's ability to overcome challenges when played by an experienced player in a traditional D&D module with a DM who is not explicitly trying to ruin their day. And to it's credit the list does a pretty good job at that. However, one must observe the considerable number of caveats under which it operates. The Tier list assumes that everyone is of the same skill level, has the same objectives, and most importantly is playing the same game.

                                        That last one is important, because the various splats in Chronicles of Darkness are NOT playing the same game. Each splat is designed to play it's own game, with its own unique themes and challenges. Demons are embroiled in a scifi spy thriller, Changelings a dark faerie tale, and Mummies a tragic battle against time. Sometimes these themes overlap nicely, for instance Demons & Mages both play fairly nicely in their shared opposition to cosmic oppression. But even where they do crossover the 'balance' may be off, like in the case of Deviants & Changelings. Both are on the run sinister forces hellbent on stripping them of their humanity, but Changelings have the means to laugh off the mortal threats that plague Deviants while most Deviants are woefully ill equipped to contend with the reality bending powers of the Fae. Thus we come to the ever present question of where do these splats crossover, and the answer tends to be: Combat.

                                        I have some theories on why this is. Many players are coming from a D&D background, where combat is the crux of the traditional dungeon crawl. There is also the fact that combat is easy to abstract, due to its systematic nature and ease of imagining. Of course CofD has other systems too, Chases, Investigation, Social Influence, but these tend not to feel as dire in the abstract, and tend to require at least a smidgen of a scenario to bind them to. Combat is simple, exciting, and decisive. It's easy to say "Ha, vampires can grapple good and move fast, so the mage can't react!" and with the plethora of options present in every splat book one can generally flex a bit of their splat preference into their decision. But most importantly, it's cool. Action scenes are fun to watch and fun to imagine. It's also why combat is a bad measure of strength, because in CofD combat is supposed to be scary. CofD is a horror game in which violence is a horror element. That's why forcing yourself on others (physically, magically, etc.) is a threat to most morality stats. It's why down and dirty combat exists, and why even potent combatants in CofD can die relatively quickly.

                                        Now, combat tirade aside, we return to the question of how to tier the splats. The easiest answer is: "Don't", but that's thread capping, and thread capping is lame. Arcane presents a more elegant answer, which I quote in part.

                                        Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                        As the conventional wisdom goes, it pays to think about the "power ladder" more by the way their power is part of the themes and atmosphere of the game. Design wisdom would probably group the main gamelines like this:

                                        Top Tier: Mages, Mummies, Demons
                                        Middle Tier: Vampire, Werewolf, Promethean, Sin-Eaters, Beasts
                                        Bottom Tier: Changelings, Hunters, Deviants

                                        ...
                                        But if we want to go by the OG 3.5 list read on, just remember to take it all with a grain of salt, because 3.5 and CofD are entirely different beasts. Also recall that the Tier list is meant to address a splat in the broadest possible terms, so just because a specific build of Vampire can jump a starting Mage does not mean they will be afforded a higher Tier (see combat tirade above).

                                        Tier 1: Mages.
                                        Tier 1 is defined as "capable of doing absolutely everything, often better than classes that specialize in that thing". That's Mages, with the right Arcana they can do just about everything anyone else can do, and a host of things no one else can do. They are much better at fighting conspiracies than Deviants, they can survive in the sun where Vampires would die, and people don't inherently try to murder them like Prometheans. No other Splat has a Mage's range of effective options, and range of options is the only thing the 3.5 tier list cares about.


                                        Tier 2: Demons, Mummies
                                        Tier 2 encompasses the D&D classes with access to insane, raw power that is capable of eclipsing lesser flexibility. Demons & Mummies are the only splats with insane bursts of superhuman power baked into the splat. Honestly even among the two Demons stand a head and shoulders above, with options that simply erase challenges and no baked in power decay. But the 3.5 tier list cares not about power decay, peak power is what it evaluates and Mummies have downright silly power out the gate.


                                        Tier 3: Werewolves, Vampires, Sin-Eaters, Changelings, Beasts
                                        Tier 3 is for classes "capable of doing one thing quite well, while still being useful when that thing is inappropriate". All five of these Splats have a host of innate abilities that set them up nicely for most situations and access to unique abilities in the form of Gifts, Disciples, etc. that let them shine in a variety of areas, but lack the nukes Demons and Mummies can grab out the gate or the sheer outrageous breadth of Mages.


                                        Tier 4: Prometheans, Deviants
                                        Tier 4 is "Capable of doing one thing quite well, but often useless when encounters require other areas of expertise, or capable of doing many things to a reasonable degree of competence without truly shining." Deviants are the former, Prometheans the latter. It should be noted that these are also the splats for which fear of mortal threats is a key theme, so it should come as no surprise that they end up on the lower end of a scale meant to measure how easily a character can brush off challenges.


                                        Tier 5: Hunters
                                        Tier 5 includes characters who are "Capable of doing only one thing, and not necessarily all that well". It shouldn't come as any surprise that the splat designed to confront monsters more powerful than themselves would end up tiered below the monsters they are supposed to hunt, should it?

                                                            I would like to propose a system based on 8 ratings. First lets start with the simple ones, based on the 3 standard attribute types:

                                                            Physical
                                                            Mental
                                                            Social

                                                            These cover most basic challenges. Next we will look at ones that are more subjective, but still play an important role:

                                                            Volatility: How likely it is to experience a fluctuation in that splat's power level. A vampire chooses to lower Blood Potency, so they are low volatility. A mage chooses to over Reach for all its worth, which is more common than a vampire. But its still sporadic, so mid volatility. A mummy doesn't choose to Descend, so high volatility.

                                                            Variancy: How big the difference is between the splat wielding its full power is from their standard. A mage with time to prepare has high variancy, since they can have imbued and enhanced items, tass, bonuses, etc. A demon going loud or a recently risen mummy embody high variancy, so does a deviant at full power. A werewolf giving into Death Rage has medium variancy, since they can wield the garou form's power for a bit and with more precision.

                                                            Combat: How quickly the splat can neutralize their opponents (immobilize, kill, knock out, turn in their favor, etc). The value is simply how well they could be expected to emerge when caught in single combat while fully healed, with their personal resource pool full (Aether, mana, Essence, etc) and in neutral ground.

                                                            Information Gathering: How easily can the splat figure out what is going on. All the raw might and careful use of their power won't help much if they don't understand what is going on.

                                                            Requisitioning: How easily the splat can be expected to secure or procure the resources and contacts they require. A vampire has the blood bond, Disciplines to support this and any favors they can procure, so very high requisitioning power. Mages have their Order archives, which leads to a medium. Werewolves need to barter or hunt for each fetish, which leads to a low.

                                                            Lastly, each of those is rated on a 5 dot basis, with the general meaning outlined as follows:

                                                            • (Frail): The splat has notable difficulty with this aspect, probably having penalties associated with it. Promethean's social rating are a great example, since their Disquiet makes interactions more difficult.

                                                            •• (Average): No great focus, but no great penalties. A splat at this level is about as competent as a mortal with equivalent training. They can be more competent than a mortal, but its not their specialty. Mages fall in this category for physical, since their bodies are still quite mortal, even when they have a few healing tricks.

                                                            ••• (Gifted): The nature of their supernatural condition supports this pillar in a minor way. Geists are at this level due to their natural ability to perceive and understand ghosts, which is specialized, but gives them more options than most mortals.

                                                            •••• (Skilled): This splat's default supernatural powers support this pillar in a major way. Demons with their perfect ability to lie are amazing at social, vampires due to blood bond. Vampires, werewolves and prometheans are amazing at physical. Mages are masters of investigation and changelings are great at mental due to oneiromancy and pledges.

                                                            ••••• (Superb): The splat not only has default supernatural powers that greatly aid in this pillar, but the available suite of powers lets them specialize in them.
