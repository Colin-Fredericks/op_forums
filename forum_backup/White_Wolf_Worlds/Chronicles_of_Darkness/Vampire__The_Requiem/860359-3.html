    Originally posted by WHW View Post
    And I think this is working, because some of my very shy friends are finally starting to play socially "competent" characters, simply because *it's not their social skill that is checked* to succeed.
    I'm going to sound terrible, but if I'm being honest, it has never been fun for the group when my socially incompetent friends try to play socially competent people. And no, this is not because I'm a heartless bitch that won't let them succeed because they can't talk. No, even when compensating and just letting them roll for everything, it doesn't work and it isn't fun. When the group is trying to immerse in their characters, having one guy constantly unable to do that, not talking in character for the most part and just throwing dice at the problem, it's very disorienting. It's also way less effective, even when 100% rolling every social interaction out, because people who are "shy" or whatever the nice word is for not being good at social interaction, don't just have trouble knowing what to say/how to say it, they also have trouble knowing when to talk. It's one thing when the group has a clear goal in mind and a social path to victory is obvious--then they're fine. But people who are good at talking can talk their way out of a lot of situations where it would never even occur to a "shy" person that talking is an option.

    And that's the funny thing about comparing being shy to being out of shape when it comes to roleplaying games. The 98 lb. weakling who can't lift a cat and the 300 pounder whose only exercise is lifting food to his mouth have no trouble at all using their physical skills to solve problems. No matter how unathletic someone is in real life, I've never seen a player whose character can climb, jump, swim, or fight well fail to utilize those abilities when the opportunity arises. Even if one can't breath after running down the hallway, they show no hesitation in parkouring up walls or off roofs, punching their way out of bad situations, taking ridiculous called shots, etc. People who suck at physical activity still understand how physical activity works, they just can't actually do it. But, in my experience, people who suck at social activity suck because they don't understand it.

    It's never been fun for the table when socially awkward people play the party face. Not once, whether I was the GM or not, no matter what special precautions the GM took to help them. However, I have seen a few socially awkward people become no longer awkward from playing roleplaying games where they had to talk to get things done. And I've seen even more that, as themselves, are a social disaster, but immersed in their characters, are smooth talking social masters.

    But this is all based on a different kind of gaming agenda, I believe, a different type of immersive roleplaying, so, if it works for you, that's awesome. It just doesn't for us.

    Originally posted by WHW View Post
    To provide some context for my mini rant: I don't like focusing too much on the words used in the argument, because it's very reductionist; "message" (and in general, communication between people) is much more complicated than that, and a message is created from many more moving parts than just "what you say"
    It is, absolutely. But there are times when the message is good enough. If someone offers a legitimately attractive deal, it doesn't matter how much stuttering was used to offer it, as long as the deal came across, the other person might be willing to accept based entirely on the deal. As I've said before, social rolls are the fallback in my games...if your message isn't compelling enough on its own, then you roll for all that non-message stuff and can maybe sway them anyway. But if the deal you present is actually something they'd go along with, it doesn't matter what you roll, they want to do it.

    Originally posted by WHW View Post
    This, in general, leads to a curious situation where you have a very incomplete message, so you intuitively "complete it" by injecting with the missing factors taken from the *player*, not *player's character*.
    When immersed in your character, those missing factors are not dramatically different from character to player. That's one of the points of immersing.

            Being immersed doesn't help you with these missing factors, because these missing factors are things like pitch of voice, clothing, touch, posture, distance between people, various communication noises, smell, and other physical (and some of them less physical) signals that blend together and are processed to create your impression of the person you are talking with. And because you lack these elements and there is a person standing right in front of you, good luck convincing your brain that it should consider the text of what that person is saying as totally unrelated to all other signals that person is sending to you. I know I often can't, so I just use dice result as a stand in for these.
            And yeah, just because someone isn't very good at talking doesn't mean they are not immersed. My friends are totally immersed when they play, and curiously, they tend to gravitate towards characters that aren't their strongest side; person who makes most social characters is terribly shy, person who plays detective isn't very good at solving mysteries, and so on. And it's definitely not my job (our my friend's who is alternate GM to me) to tell them that "nope, you suck at being socially graceful lady, you fail" or "you are too stupid to solve this shit, better give up on this character".

            As for message being good enough - try being offered immortality by a stinking, mad-eyed, bearded hobo, and try being offered immortality by a armani-wearing sharp suited devil whose voice makes you feel big in your pants. Their message, their offer might the same, but most people won't take the first dude seriously, even if they say the same thing. I mean, people agreed to electrocute other people just based on the fact that person they are talking to looked like a scientist...people acting in intelectually sound and reasonable way isn't a given. Hell, today we analysed a case where a dude randomly grabbed a gun from police officer's gun holster and shot him, ran away, and then given himself up to other police car that was nearby, because something made his brain take wrong turn while being escorted by the guy to ambulance. What sounds like a rational choice on a paper often isn't the choice picked. Some great business deals can be cancelled simply because the dude on the other side of the table is annoying you due to things you can't put your finger on - something wrong with his teeth, movement of his eyes, way he moves his fingers that subconsciously reminds you of your ex...how do you compensate for all of these factors - that are incredibly hard to include if you are not a professional actor - when deciding on the outcome of the action?

                                                            OKAY! Time for a new question unrelated to anything before. Kind of.


                                                            So, in 1e, Persuasion was pretty much an all-important skill for most vampires to cultivate. At least, that was my experience. That skill found its way into a lot of Discipline powers, served as the key skill seduction, and was my go-to skill for getting another person to do something. Not surprising, really, since that's the name of of the skill - Persuasion.

                                                            However, in 2e, we now use a general Social Maneuvering system for convincing others of doing something. And you can pretty much use any skill. In fact, we have three Maneuvering Styles - Fast Talk, Etiquette, and Enchanting Performance (CtL only). While you can use almost any skill with Fast Talk, it primarily uses Subterfuge. Likewise, Etiquette and Enchanting Performance use Socialize and Expression. Persuasion isn't as necessary to, well, persuade anyone anymore. Or seduce them - I've acomplished most seductions using Maneuver Styles and other social merits than relying on the Persuasion skill. In Vampire specifically, Persuasion is actually a pretty rare skill to use for powers. Only three devotions, two of which tend to rely on a person's agreement in the first place, and the last is Summoning. I feel like there's been a very deliberate step away from Persuasion as a skill in VtR. Which is actually thematic, really, with the focus on mind-control powers. You don't convince people, you tell them, and they follow.
