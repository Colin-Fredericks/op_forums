    There's all sorts of stuff you can do with it. My group has met local Kindred, investigated other supernatural creatures, begun discussing long-term plans to supplant the Proconsul with a high-ranking NPC they like better. They've made friends and allies, fought Strix, attended/participated in arena matches.

    There hasn't been much back-stabbing. There are a lot of secrets, but it's more "what you don't know you can't be Dominated to tell and can't be held against you if it's discovered" as "what you don't know I can use against you." It's really up to the players. Mine have chosen a sort of middle path - their goals aren't the same, but the initial stages overlap.

    We've had fun, and we haven't done much angsting or brooding. All the characters have been able to show off their awesome abilities. It's also the lowest-combat game my group has ever played - in half a year of weekly sessions, there were only three real combats plus a couple arena combats (one PC versus NPCs using quick and dirty rules) and an assassination.

    We haven't done as much with Touchstones as I'd like, but that's because there's more you can do with the game than we've had time to do it in.

                    The "what does mortal mean?" question came up in another thread recently... It's weird to me that this is an issue for people. I don't think the writers are thinking about the word "mortal" as a jargon term that specifically means "these four types of beings but not these other two." The books are written in natural language and expect the reader to use their own judgment in interpreting them. Sometimes mortal is used as a synonym for human to keep the word choice from getting stale, sometimes it's used for its literal meaning, to refer to anything that has a finite lifespan. Sometimes it's used to refer to humans and other things that are basically human like mages, but not things that are explicitly inhuman like werewolves. It's usually pretty clear from context how it's being used, and when in doubt, the group can discuss it and the Storyteller can make a call. For what it's worth, I definitely wouldn't allow Kiss of the Succubus to work on True Fae. Then again, I probably wouldn't be involving True Fae in my vampire games in the first place, or if I did, I would decide ahead of time how different powers interact with them and inform my players of those rulings in session zero.

                    Originally posted by rman88 View Post
                    another issue that was brought up is morality. for me morality should be more or less a grey area. where context is key to determine if you take a breaking point. for example, why is killing a mass murderer the same breaking point as killing a saint? why is killing someone who ruined your life the same breaking point as killing your best friend? why is killing someone who is guilty of many crimes the same as killing someone who is innocent? it just feels to black and white for me.
                    If you hadn't mentioned the Kiss of the Succubus Merit earlier, I would think you were talking about 1st Edition here. Humanity in 2nd Edition is not about morality. At all. It's about how in touch you are with the human experience. Killing someone isn't a Breaking Point because it's immoral, it's a Breaking Point because killing people isn't something most humans do, and when they do it tends to be a pretty traumatic experience. When your vampire kills someone and isn't shaken by it, they face cognitive dissonance and are forced to face their own inhumanity. It doesn't really matter who you kill, because any murder creates that same dissonance between the vampire's experience and the human experience. That's also why things that aren't the least bit immoral, like watching people eat food, or living for a century, are Breaking Points.

                                I's an RPG, you do what you want with it. The book says the game is about personal horror, sure but there's no wrong way of using it. I know I've used 1E for a game about going to rescue a PC's ex loved one from Arcadia, traveling to Rome to infiltrate the Vatican and retrieve an elder's torpid body, then going back home to fight off an alliance of an ex PC with two fae and a werewolf trying to take over the city (for various reasons, one of which was to get back the loved one) while a member of VII was trying to cause as many deaths as possible. Second game was about stopping a criminal organization led by Dorjan's Childe, then stopping an Icarian's coup and then an Strix invasion and the return of an ancient Strix on Earth, then finally, racing and fighting Zagreus and his pet werewolf in an attempt to hack part of the God Machine.

                                There was also personal drama involved in all of this, but I wanted games of epic proportions and I got them and it went just fine. There was backstabbing in the first game as I successfully led the group to start infighting for a few sessions, culminating in a PC executing another PC. The other game was much more peaceful in that regard. To me, vampires hit the sweet spot between the squishy human and the engine of destruction werewolf, while being ignorant enough of the rest of the occult world that they can be surprised by it. I like them because they can pretty much be used for any story and they're robust enough that they can take a lot of punishment without it making a character unplayable for a long time. That story can be about playing an unlucky everyday person who ends up in an abusive relationship with their sire that they, then, project on their own ghouls, but I tend to prefer the large scale adventure type. It works Simply adjust the detachment rolls and one or two other things to make it go more smoothly.

                                I'd say I like to take the more hopeful route than the advertised theme. The PCs are vampires. They're damned and they're going to be stuck in a violent and petty society forever and that existence is going to try and break them, but maybe even then, they can be heroes.

                                            The vanilla premise of the game is focused around personal horror. It deals with mature, adult themes (with a supernatural element) and is supposed to be addressed from a mature stand point in order to not come across as offensive. Sure, you've got super powers and an infinite life span; but now you have to eat people, avoid direct sunlight, integrate yourself into a cut throat society where nothing is ever done out of the kindness of one's own heart and have to instantly sever any and all personal attachments to your mortal life so as to avoid exposing yourself and the rest of the vampire community. It's supposed to be a pretty traumatic process in character.

                                            Making the game more lighthearted is usually the result of shard settings that take place in parallel / alternate environments.


                                            For me personally, however, this is exactly what caught my interest in the game to begin with. So many games out there are about being the big, flashy hero who kicks ass and doesn't care about the consequences. But WoD takes a more realistic approach set into the modern world. It also shows that the monsters aren't as mindlessly evil as society portrays them to be. They have their own struggles to cope with and you get to see the world from their side of things; instead of playing the hero that exterminates them without a second thought. It's new and intriguing compared to the scores of medieval / futuristic fantasy games out there.
