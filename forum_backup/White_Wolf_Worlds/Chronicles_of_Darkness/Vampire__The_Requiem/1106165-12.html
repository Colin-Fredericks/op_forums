                        Ok, I've read Thousand Years a few times (it was a long set of flights, blah) and will home in on one of the key points of contention in this thread: unimpressive elders, and the example threats.

                        It could be helpful to consider the situation from the ST's perspective of player dynamics; if you want to start an elder game, or a player has a reason to be running an elder, a tool now exists canonically to do that. It's somewhat weaksauce, but why? Storytellers have a harder time on average cranking player power level down, versus ratcheting it or NPC power level up.

                        Reading between the lines here, I view the XP for various age bands as starting points, for elders who have nominal activity cycles and don't get into extraordinary trouble or circumstances. They essentially live a life or two, erring on the side of caution and risking themselves only when necessary. I think that's fair, it meets the profile of most vampires.

                        PCs don't do this. Players don't gather around the table week after week to discuss a few bullet-points of how they pay the bills and arrange consistent feeding patterns and ensure the security is in place. For PCs, unlife is bigger, badder and usually much more terrifying. They may stack up experiences of a lifetime within a few months of in-game play, or at least a few years.

                        Similarly, the threats in Thousand Years are characters that are dangerous, active and antagonistic. They're made to reflect individuals who live far, far closer to the edge of destruction than any average vampire would feel comfortable with. Their experience totals reflect this. (Again, reading between the lines.) If you had a comprehensive history of Kindred, you'd see many, many characters who follow the same profiles as these threats, but are destroyed or burn out after short periods of time; the ones lucky or clever enough to keep themselves alive, while being crazy or driven enough to persist in extreme danger, end up legends whispered to terrify neonates.

                        The downside to very high activity/danger cycles is that a character will collect a large number of enemies, faster, or end up owing many boons. Enemies, if used, restrict action and help drive conflict. Boons act as hooks to pull characters into things they otherwise would ignore. If I had a player (or NPC) that wanted to reflect the same kind of high activity, I'd houserule categories of activity that act as a multiplier, ranging from x0 to perhaps x2.5, and grant automatic backgrounds in various side-effects such as these.

                        The fact the book does not acknowledge the activity dynamic is either a purposeful choice or an oversight, but in either case it's disappointing and a loss.

                        On another note: what I expected and was disappointed to see absent were more of the vampiric "stretch goals" for unlife. Things such as Live Bait's merit above, or Devotion examples that require BP 6+ to purchase (as appeared in a few 1e supplements). Elder's don't lose powers purchased at high BP after emerging from torpor, and that's what really sets them apart. The tools to reflect this fact well are missing.

                        I suppose the door stands open for homebrew.

                        --Khanwulf

                                    Exactly my point. Representing the figures in the book as a baseline from which more active and risk-taking kindred evolve to higher experiences accumulations is a fair take I think. I wish they'd just taken a sidebar to say so--or a few paragraphs even!

                                    Me? My games are all written, PbP affairs anymore, and that means you can timeline and wave a heck of a lot of stuff into the background. From there, going back over "downtime" between major sessions is pretty easy, you can break it apart into what looks like a session's worth of ground being covered and award nominal beats (a few) for the jump. This can work out to be a beat per year, or several depending on the amount of text that goes into it; more text == more "sessions".

                                    I would have loved to see some discussion of campaign journaling, timeline-ing and the like as tools for elder campaigns that span the ages. Covering flashbacks is good, and the background web is a great idea unfamiliar to me (harkens back to Chicago by Night, a bit), but there are other, more linear approaches. The appeal of vampire to me is the ability to capture bits of history and the aching weight of years these creatures supposedly drag around. But that's me.
