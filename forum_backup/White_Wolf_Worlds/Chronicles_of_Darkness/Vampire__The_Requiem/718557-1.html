        First of all, I agree completely with the first two points- you correctly extrapolated what my summation was going for.

        Originally posted by Leetsepeak View Post
        I'm not sure I follow. You're Unbound, so you're gonna get treated like an unbound person. This will undoubtedly lead to Conditions, beats and drama. All good stuff. If you're selecting to play one of the Unbound, you're obviously interested in exploring what it's like to not have affiliation in a society which is highly factionalized. It's interesting and fun.
        Originally posted by Leetsepeak View Post
        But really, it comes back to the drama of being Unbound. The game is not telling you it's a bad thing. It's a dramatic thing, arguably a very interesting thing, provided people are interested in exploring the reasons and consequences for not associating with the Covenants.
        I agree, but while not necessarily unbalanced, the kinds of stories that get told about an outsider successfully weaving through or ignoring or conquering the system tend to be... uphill struggles, lone unaided effort, increment by increment of progress. All legit, absolutely, but that's a harder vibe to play 1) in a group game, for both time and the "going it alone" vibe, and 2) when you're foregoing the advantages of Covenant. I'm not saying it should be easy, by any lengths, but that's already a more difficult story, that's more difficult to tell, for a character that's one step away from "country-club-crashing hobo who we're legally not allowed to exclude".

        If an Unbound is to succeed in those stories, it strikes me as peculiar that there's no means of demonstrating, mechanically, how they can take that independence and make it the strength by which they can navigate the system. Something that demonstrates that their ability to avoid assimilation isn't just a cross to bear to prove that they can beat the system by blood and sweat and near-compulsive monomania, but that it's the reason they were able to succeed at all.

        Just spitballing: being Unbound is about either purposefully not belonging, or surviving despite not belonging. I just think that they should have something that, while not completely (or even mostly) offsetting their difficulties, makes it clear that Covenants don't have it all their own way- an extra door to reflect that you've gotten wise to the most common recruitment/blackmail techniques, or a bonus out a sheer outraged individualism bonus against intimidation when being coerced to act in a Covenant's interests, or the ability to get things done more quickly because you don't have to worry about hierarchy and stamps of approval. Some quick, insouciant little wink to the fact that even when it's clear that resources and numbers will win the day, that flexibility and unpredictability were able to win you some key battles.

        As of now, the cost-benefit analysis of Unbound is basically "lack of responsibility vs. lack of support", isn't it? How would you respond to making lack of responsibility less passive and more active? Focus less on "Don't bother asking, he won't do it, we have no leverage over him" and more "infrequent Get Out of Jail Free card"?

            As of now, the cost-benefit analysis of Unbound is basically "lack of responsibility vs. lack of support", isn't it? How would you respond to making lack of responsibility less passive and more active? Less "Don't bother asking, he won't do it, we have no leverage over him" and more "get out of jail free card"?
            At the risk of sounding dismissive, you're discussing the struggle of the Unbound in a way that sounds more like the Anarchs against the Camarilla than what they are in Requiem. The lack of an identity is not really an identity, there's nothing special about them to draw from. It's like being an Apostate. There's different flavors of Apostate, but being an Apostate does not confer identity or benefit all by itself.

            Being Unbound is not an actual identity all by itself, so there's nothing to really hang any kind of advantage on it. There isn't an advantage to not doing something, and when you get into the territory of doing something (i.e. being more than "not in a Covenant") THEN there MIGHT be some kind of benefit. That's when you get into the territory of a local Covenant, though.

            But really, I think it should be said that you're too narrowly defining the Unbound as those who reject the system, rather than simply being those who are not a part of it. One implies a unified action, while the other is just a sort of categorization of a number of different stances. I think I made this mistake in my last post as well. The Anarchs from Masquerade are certainly the former, and fit what you're describing rather well. The Unbound are not that, though they can also encompass that.

            You can't systematize the Unbound identity without it ceasing to be that identity, which is why it can only by characterized by what it isn't, i.e. not belonging to a Covenant, not being beholden to a Covenant, not being seen as a participating member of the domain, etc.

            Really the point I'm trying to make is that once you start trying to give a Covenant Advantage to the Unbound, they cease being the Unbound and start being a local covenant. Maybe in your case that local covenant is basically the Anarchs.

                        The Carthians are weird. The LS and Invictus are very static, they embrace a very specific concept, even if it has variations. And even the Circle of the Crone is easily grasped as 'pagan' religion. The Carthians are meant to be looking for a new form of politics for the Kindred, the problem being that that can be anything from complete anarchy to communism, meritocracy to democracy. They don't follow the 'standard' neofeudal setup the Invictus does, but wherever they are, providing they're not espousing apolitics, they have some political belief. The Premier from Damnation City is a good example of a Carthian ruler with an organized Carthian city, complete with some people getting screwed. To make things weirder, they 'seem' to newcomers from Masquerade like the Anarchs and are occasionally depicted 'looking' like they espouse a certain ideal or another, like on the cover of their covenant book.



                        I asked Prince what setup his game is because when it comes to the long-running 'living' games like Wanton Wicked, I hear a lot of complaints about how there is a sort of ingrained clique and hierarchy with the older players, putting newcoming players in an unenviable position where they have to be subservient to them, providing they're in a covenant. This is just what I hear, not having played them, though I've certainly noticed similar structures of elitism in other places online.

                                                            Originally posted by Chattnos View Post
                                                            Is it possible that joining covenents may be just a thing older vamps do?
                                                            I'd actually expect it to be the other way around. Younger vampires need a family, a place to fit in, as well as the strength and protection of a possey at their back to look out for them and show them the ropes. Even if your covenant isn't a perfect fit for your mentality, it sure is nice to have "people" you mostly agree with to talk to and to guide you through the long nights.

                                                            As you get older you get less from the covenants. You are powerful enough, skilled enough, and smart enough to survive on your own - your networks extend through both the daytime and the night, and your allies have been with you for decades or centuries. And if you have stayed Unbound for this long, why would you want to throw away your hard-fought and won neutrality, even the respect that comes with that, to be the lowest rung in a social machine that has little to offer you?
