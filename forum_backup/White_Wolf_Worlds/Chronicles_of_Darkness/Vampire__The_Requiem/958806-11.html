    What is happening is that little by little (at a very slow pace, unfortunately) people are starting to question and demand evidence in order to believe things they are told.

    It is not enough to be told by a priest or for it to be written in a book. Even more so by a book that is a collection of (many times delirious) anecdotes by mostly unverified writers with a dog on the race. And then all this stuff got edited by people trying to institutionalize faith. Color me unimpressed if the facts are somewhat different than what is written.

    It is not sad that nowadays people question and scrutinize. It is one of the brightest happenings of the new times. We are at the first minutes of the dawn of reason.Many (most) people still need to open their eyes. Hopefully no calamity will reverse this.

    Everything will change, including history and religion. And that is a good thing.

            I get the impression that the catholic Church losing ground was more a result of people being fed up with it having so much political power than the people suddenly gaining incredible insight. Most big religious movements are just as much about politics and power as they are about spirituality. Religion, no matter how well nice looking, always creates power dynamics. Someone holds the truth and has the authority to distribute it to other people. Over time, that dynamic can become oppressive, and when someone starts speaking or acting against that oppression, they'll also want to subvert its spiritual component. You can fight against a corrupt church, but if people still recognize that the pope is infallible, you're the bad guy. This is a great tool for the powers, whatever they are. The French monarchy claimed it was chosen by god, so even it the king was being a giant douchebag, if that's god's will, you just have to shut up.

            Thing is nobody thinks they're the bad guy, so they'll have to find a way to keep opposing the oppressive power that makes sense. And I'm not saying that all revolutions are "fake" and that the spiritual, philosophical or political arguments are always just a pretext for a coup. These things are organic, they take time, and the people who do it are, I think, mostly honest about it. The protestants cut themselves from the Catholic church, but I'm pretty sure a lot of them really felt like it was corrupt and that not indulging in the cult of saints and decorating churches with gold would make them closer to god. The Russian Revolution probably targeted the Orthodox Church because it was backing up the Tsar, but socialism is a very serious current of thought, not just a way for Lenin to become the big boss.

            I'm missing a LOT of details here, but the point is that people don't just wise up suddenly, they don't revolt because they had an epiphany and now they know the truth. They stand up against an oppressive system that can take various shapes and usually get behind a certain way of thinking that contrasts that shape. In the case of the Catholic Church, there's little point in talking about it's spiritual elements without taking into account the power dynamics it generates, and vice-versa.

                This. This so hard. Divested of political power, a religion is just a spiritual and moral framework built around a metaphysical belief structure. Its adherents can be good or bad, but the religion itself very rarely is (please refrain from providing examples to the contrary as it is obviously a very subjective topic). The Catholic Church promoted literacy during the reign of Charlemagne, funded natural philosophers throughout the Middle Ages and Renaissance, and provided humanitarian aid to countless peoples across history regardless of the predominant sentiment towards those people. It also promoted witch hunts and crusades and graft among its clergy. Catholicism isn't to "blame" for those things, nor is it "responsible" for the former things. It's an idea, it can't take agency for anything. People are the ones who take the trappings of a religion and use them for selfish purposes, and when that religion is also the state it becomes easier to get away with. This leads, as Brachen said, to resistance against oppression, which naturally takes a contrasting view to what it perceives as the enemy in order to better define itself (we like doing this as humans, we're hardwired to do it, in fact).

                Every time in history that the Church and the State have allied, it's ended badly for both. It was true for Egypt (like, four times), China, Japan, and everywhere else the Divine Right of Kings or the Celestial Mandate of Heaven or whatever was present.

                                        I believe the desire to root Acolyte over Sanctified stems most from the default state of VtR being an Invictus city with strong ties to the Sanctified. This lends the underdog motif to the remaining three Covenants, which makes them far easier to sympathize with than those in power.

                                        If say....a Circle of the Crone controlled city was going around hunting down Lancea et Sanctum Coteries, like in that one story from the Strix Chronicle Anthology, along with any other threats to the faith then it would be much easier to sympathize with the Sanctified over the Circle. Same can be said about a Tyranical Carthian State or a Ordo Dracul Magocracy. To further reiterate this point, think back to Requiem for Rome and see this first hand. In the time of the Camarilla the nascent Lancea et Sanctum was the ultimate underdog in the face of the ever present Legion of the Dead and Tyranical Senex. The Wing of Oracles had a monopoly on magical power so long as the gods looked favorably upon the Senex, and the College of Strangers was the only group willing to even consider aiding the budding Cult of Longinus, and even then they would have dropped them like a hat should a more profitable ally made themselves available. (Tangent: still to this day believe the College of Strangers is the true progenitor to the modern day Invictus.)

                                        The inescapable trapping of Christianity will effect one's opinions of the Covenant and how well they can sympathize with their twisted parasitic beliefs, but people can have either good or bad experiences with religion. Every one of us instinctively want's to root for who we think is the underdog.
