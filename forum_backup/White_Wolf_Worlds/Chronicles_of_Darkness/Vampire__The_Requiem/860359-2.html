    Originally posted by The Storyteller View Post
    his eloquence is literally creating a reward from thin air, that the player now has to consider when weighing their decision, but that the character can't really know about or interact with in any meaningful way.
    The reward is not being created out of thin air. The reward comes from the fact that this persuasion is affecting the character on a significant level. That's where the Beat is coming from. Conditions give you Beats all the time, because they represent emotional, physical, mental or supernatural states that are having a significant influence on the character. Addicted doesn't generate a reward out of thin air when the character gives up an obligation to get another hit, it gives a reward because such an act had a significant impact on the character, enough to teach him a lesson or to slowly add to the inner experiences and power of the character, so that later on he can say "my experiences with drug use have hardened me against this [Takes another dot of Stamina]."

    Likewise, social eloquence has an impact on the character if the character goes along with it, precisely because the character is being influenced to do something they might not have wanted to do in the first place. This has a transformative effect on the character, albeit a tiny one in the grand scheme of things.

    Your particular problem with understanding these mechanics is twofold. First, you are dismissive of "meta" mechanics, and instead of allowing them to slot into play naturally, you resist them, creating these exact cases where people tell you repeatedly how this makes absolutely perfect sense, but for you it makes none whatsoever, since you have pre-emptively driven a wedge between meta mechanics and in-game situations. Secondly, you underuse mechanics in favour of freeforming, which lends itself to you not having the tools you need to further incorporate these meta mechanics.

    In short, you have created a situation through your years of roleplaying where these exact mechanics seem extremely foreign to you because you have pre-emptively rejected the tools you need to properly interface them with their corresponding in-game situations.

In D&D, if someone says &quot;Come at me, bro!&quot; you don't respond, &quot;Who is this bro you speak of, knave!&quot; you just translate that mentally into the jargon of the setting, the same way we don't think it's weird that two WWII Nazi officers speak English with German accents to each other when totally alone in movies. When Lord Snaketongue offers you deal, you view it in favorable light. But look, in the end, you can fast talk someone into ignoring or forgetting facts, but unless you lie, the facts of a deal are the facts, and they're not going to randomly change or whatever because the guy offering the deal is wily.

        If he wants you to help him, even though doing so is against your interest, there's no way that's going to fly, unless you just absolutely don't understand he circumstances or something. He would have to offer you something that is in your interest that sufficiently compensates for how put out you are by the request. The only thing he could really do to change that is lie.
        Half-truths and spin are the bread and butter of persuasion, and not being preternaturally persuasive myself, I will never know if there might have been a more appealing spin I could have put on the facts or not.

        Originally posted by The Storyteller View Post
        My problem comes when you throw the beat into the mix, because what's happening, is that his eloquence is literally creating a reward from thin air, that the player now has to consider when weighing their decision, but that the character can't really know about or interact with in any meaningful way. It's not part of the character's decision, just the player's.

        I see that side of things, yes, but what about the other side? Where the Beat becomes the a lure to someone to break character for more power? See, the assumption here is always that the character would/should take the deal, but what about when they really wouldn't/shouldn't? There are times when it just wouldn't make any sense to agree to something, but that tantalizing Beat is going to make them do it. A potential consolation prize for acting in character is not worth the risk of not acting in character for power. I'd rather just trust them to act in character and choose their actions that way.
        This is where we get into that "director stance" vs. "actor stance" thing that I mentioned earlier. The way I see it, there's no such thing as an out-of-character action. The player determines what the character would or would not do, so what ever action the player decides to have the character take, that is by definition the action the character would have taken. Players decide what actions their characters would take based on out of character factors all the time, whether they realize it or not. But some actions (usually those that benefit the character) have more out of character factors that encourage the player to portray characters that would take them, while others (usually those that are a detriment to the character) actively discourage the player from portraying characters that would take them. Beats give the player a reason to want to play a character that would take actions that might be detrimental to them. They don't force the player to do so. You offer options, and let the player make an informed decision. That decision will always be informed by meta-game factors anyway, so I don't see a problem with using meta-game factors to encourage a better story.

                This discussion has been really interesting and thought provoking.

                Since it's really had me thinking about why i do things the way I do, I've realised why I don't offer any mechanical carrots or sticks when an antagonist explains themselves and even when they attempt to get the PCs on-side is because, well, I'm genuinely interested in what they decide. I often plan in vague, or even concrete, terms, the fallout of siding with or against such a figure. And these are some of my favourite things when I'm RPGing, the moments where we all get to know who the characters are. Where they can surprise me and even their players. Are they merciful to a monstrous, soulless abomination that'll probably just keep hurting the world? Are they swayed by pragmatism or idealism? Justice or mercy? Who are the PCs?

                Interestingly. one situation that has come up when I should probably have been doing something mechanically (human PCs unknowingly meeting a Promethean), I figured mechanical trappings could give away too much. So I tried to describe even his mundane actions and mannerisms in a slightly suspicious way. The PCs, sure enough, were a little suspicious of this character. Long-term exposure would have required that mechanics get involved, but the Promethean character consciously avoided that.

                Note that I'm not opposed to, in future, using mechanical tricks, especially if supernatural power and actual blackmail come up. Off the top of my head, I've only used Nightmare to mess with PC heads previously.

                            It's worth keeping in mind that this Beat for going the other way isn't really a special benefit.

                            Ideally your players and their characters are going after their own Aspirations. Them going another way derails them from that, but they can still gain Beats that they won't be getting for pursuing their Aspiration.

                            It's also important to remember that your experience, no matter how vast you may consider it, is not the total experience of all gamers everywhere. What happens at your table, what the players bring to it, how they react to things, how you plan or don't, is going to be entirely different even from another table where they they follow the same rules. How you or your players would react to things is not how all players would react to things.

                                        Originally posted by The Storyteller View Post
                                        The real problem is because you're tantalizing them with an in game prize, you're tantalizing them with a meta game prize. It's not "Do this thing your character wouldn't do because of a thing they character can directly connect to what they want." It's "make your character do this thing they wouldn't otherwise do in order to magically learn 1/15 of a new super power." We kind of went through this before, though, so, we can just agree to disagree, that's ok.

                                        But, PCs do rely on their tactical knowledge to make smart moves in combats and deal damage. And they rely on their intelligence and ability to parse the system and spend their points/XP wisely to make the most effective characters. Real life skills leak into the game all over the place, so, objecting to this specific one (i.e. being persuasive) is always strange to me.
                                        I agree that PCs rely on meta-knowledge to make decisions. This is why you offer beats; it simulates that the person's offer is attractive due to superficial reasons (the person speaks very well, they're very good looking, they're in a position of power). It doesn't change what's really on offer, or what's actually being said, but it does change some of the context to better simulate real life.

                                        For example: People get used all the time. Go to any bar, notice that guys buy drinks for pretty women all the time. Notice how many of these men are used, and notice how many of them get absolutely nothing for their trouble. It doesn't make a lot of objective sense, and that's a perfectly fine excuse for not buying a pretty woman a drink, but there's certainly a temptation despite your better sense saying otherwise.

                                        It's not unheard of for a good-looking girl to ask you for a drink at a bar, and even if intellectually you know she's just trying to score a free drink from you and you'd be a total sap to buy her one, there's definitely a degree of temptation on offer. You can still say 'no', but you're still tempted to say 'yes'.

                                        This is what a beat on offer simulates. You're tempted by a charming voice, a pretty face. You're peer pressured.

                                        But it's the player's decision. Players should always have agency, it's up to them to make the ultimate decision. They can always say 'no', but the temptation can make it interesting out-of-character and in-character in my opinion.

                                                    For the persuasion discussion - I actually actively encourage social mechanics and rolling dice for it, and use "good arguments" or "good roleplaying" as a equipment bonus, because being persuasive isn't really about just picking good words. If that was the case, anyone could be persuasive with a good writer preparing their speeches for them and, as you probably can see in real life, even best speech can be delivered in a off putting way, while a persuasive person can buy you with just few words. Choice of words is important, but there are way more important things that are probably best emulated by characters dots and skills, and I don't like ignoring them in favor of "checking my player for persuasiveness". And I think this is working, because some of my very shy friends are finally starting to play socially "competent" characters, simply because *it's not their social skill that is checked* to succeed.

                                                            To provide some context for my mini rant: I don't like focusing too much on the words used in the argument, because it's very reductionist; "message" (and in general, communication between people) is much more complicated than that, and a message is created from many more moving parts than just "what you say"; and a lot of them isn't really included in role playing descriptions. This, in general, leads to a curious situation where you have a very incomplete message, so you intuitively "complete it" by injecting with the missing factors taken from the *player*, not *player's character*.
                                                            Like, I can say jokes that make people laugh, and I saw my friend try to say the very same jokes, but completely fail at it, because while he can copy the "words", he can't copy the other partsof it. Which leads him to be confused why people laugh at my jokes but not at his (and truth is, people laugh at his jokes too, when he says *his* jokes, not *somebody else's* jokes).
