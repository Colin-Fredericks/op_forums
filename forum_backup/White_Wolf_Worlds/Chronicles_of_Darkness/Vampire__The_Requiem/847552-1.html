    I think MCN asks a very good question: in the current chronicle, what mortals are your PCs actually interacting with the most and consider to be the most important to them? I know you said they're hardly interacting with their current Touchstones, the only outlier being the player with the dog (who I think chose the best Touchstone, considering theirs is getting screen-time and is being treated with importance by the character and player). The fact their Touchstone is doubling as a Retainer is perfectly fine in my opinion, because it makes them useful, thus more important, to the PC on a greater level than being some random NPC a character interacts with. It also gives them a great deal more agency as an NPC, because players aren't forced into interactions to avoid suffering a Detachment penalty - they're actively seeking these NPCs out because they need them.

    edit: mind you, if you and your players just aren't feeling them, there's really no problem with parting ways with them completely. They're supposed to be fun, and if they're not fun for your troupe, no shame in giving 'em the boot.

            Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post
            Side-note: Yeah, I wouldn't have allowed a dog as a Touchstone. Making them a Retainer could be interesting, but a dog is definitely not going to anchor you to the human experience.
            I respectfully disagree. If your Touchstone can be something as immobile as a house or a gravestone? Then a living being can serve just as well. I had a 1e character where her dog would have made a perfect Touchstone.

            If your characters were all recently embraced, they probably don't yet need to maintain a relationship with a human to remind them what it was like to be alive - after all it was only a week or so ago. The default rules kind of assume that the characters have been vampires long enough to at least gain 3 dots of Disciplines, 3 dots of Merits, and to drop to Humanity 7. If you're playing characters who were embraced that recently, I would also start them at Humanity 10, because they shouldn't yet be used to things like lying in defense of the Masquerade, going over a day without human contact, possessing superhuman abilities, and using those abilities to steal the lifeblood of the unwilling, or to deprive them of the agency to attest their unwillingness. That's valuable stuff to play through if you're starting that early. Don't bother with Touchstones yet, have the players acquire them in-game, at a time when it feels dramatically appropriate. When they start forgetting things like the feeling of a sunburn, the rhythm of a heartbeat, and why exactly they're supposed to care about consent. When they start to experience that cognitive dissonance of "wait... I should feel guilty about this. Why don't I feel guilty about this?" that's when it's time to find a human you can confide in and who can serve as an example of what humans are like and why that's important.
            With all due respect, I'm going to have to disagree here as well. I don't think there's any point here that I do agree with. While this is fine baseline for some games, I'm not comfortable with saying it is the default assumptions for everyone. "Time to learn 3 disciplines" especially stands out to me, because the book makes it clear that many Disciplines (and Devotions!) are spontaneously developed, no teaching or learning periods required - drinking the vitae of someone who knows the power is the only requirement in the case of non-affinity powers. Theban, Cruac, and Coils are the only Disciplines that seem to absolutely require time and tutorship/experiementation to learn, and no one forces you to start with those. Three dots of merits?                 Your problem seems to be that you aren't using touchstones as they are intended: This is, to be catalysts for drama and conflict. What happens when a Daeva takes Bob as a blood doll, because their clan bane makes the succubus obsessed with him? What happens when a Mekhet Hound keeps tabs on the character, finds out his touchstones and delivers pictures to the PC in a clear threat to him should he not do as he's told? How do you convince your coterie to give a fuck about Bob and help you one-up the Daeva or the Mekhet?

                What's better yet, how do you manouver the coterie into helping you, without tipping your hand as to why you are so opposed to the smug Mekhet Hound? Or does your character simply complies to the threats, and eventually forces you to go against your coterie's politic aims in a climax? Which do you choose? The mask, or the requiem?

                Not everything that goes on in the game should be displayed to the whole table. The above scenarios wouldn't. Email your players between sessions, text them or hold 1 on 1 sessions concerning these private issues over the phone, or skype. It works wonders.

                Ultimately, touchstones are weakspots. Savvy (power) players will take Two- or One-dimentional characters over having complications as above, because it detracts from some preconceived notion of awesomeness-in-untouchability. They want to play elders already, but becoming elders takes it's tools on the soul.

                The dog as a touchstone works, because anybody using animalism on it will predispose such complications. Do you take it to elysium? Did you ghoul it, so you won't risk detachment in say, 10 years? How does your character feels about violating the nature of such a loyal companion on such a manner?

                Regarding your STing style? Shit happens. STs are there to drop the shit on the moving fans. Otherwise it woudn't be much of a game.

                        Originally posted by MCN View Post
                        I made a Daeva PC that had a ghoul (also part of the herd) as a Touchstone.
                        So, my issue with this is, how does a ghoul remind you of being human? They're, well, not human anymore. You've taken that away from them. They don't live a normal human life anymore. If they're awake during the day, it's because they're doing a specific job for you. Otherwise, its their only chance to sleep. At night, they're serving you. That just doesn't seem like an effective touchstone--it's like trying to get closer to animals by eating a burger. I know there is a merit for ghouls that requires they be a touchstone, but that always felt off to me, and I feel like it had to be written by a different author than the main touchstone section.

                        The dog feels like cheating because it's utterly under the character's sway. It's something that accompanies him everywhere. It doesn't have a choice, it has to deal with him. It's just an animal. There's no risk at all--no risk the guy you're stalking will spot you, no risk your old buddy will notice something is off, no risk you girlfriend from life will reject you. It's basically a slave.

                        It's worked out ok, but it still feels wrong, for what appears to be the spirit of the rules.

                        Originally posted by MCN View Post
                        One reason I think its important to integrate Touchstones into one's vampire life is that one of the major functions of a Touchstone is to talk their vampire down from a frenzy.
                        I noticed that, but then, look at the example Touchstones in the book. I can't imagine any of them being around to talk someone down from frenzy. It seems like a mechanic more intended to keep you from accidentally frenzying and killing your touchstone than actually using you touchstone actively to avoid it.

                        Originally posted by MCN View Post
                        How do Touchstones develop? Again, this is something flexible. The bare minimum for being a Touchstone is just that it reminds a vampire that they're still human (at least in part). It could be something as simple as a song that reminds them of simpler times, happier times that they hear for the first time while hunting. I find it rather evocative that a vampire would hear some music, maybe a musical jewelry box, that reminded her of her mother, a century dead, do anything to get that little trinket, and obsessively listen to the song. And maybe, just maybe, one of her ghouls would record the song on their phone, and the next time, in Elysium, on the verge of frenzy and attacking someone, that song starts playing, and cuts through the red rage like a sharp knife, bringing the vampire to her senses. All this, from just wandering around, and snap, instant connection.
                        That's a great story, honestly. It makes for great italicized text. But in real play? That just feels like cheating even more than the dog. For one, there's zero risk. If your touchstone is something ephemeral, like a song, it is literally impossible to threaten or imperil your touchstone. Once again, you have total control over it and can even interact with it on your whim by just humming or singing the notes. That doesn't even match the mechanics where the touchstone is rolling and needs 3 x BP to stop the frenzy. What is this song's die pool? This kind of reinforces my "Touchstones are awesome in theory, but only in theory" thing.

                        Originally posted by MCN View Post
                        What about for neonates? Fresh fledgelings? Maybe it should be the car they're still driving around from their living days. Maybe its going to dinner every Sunday with their family, and eff any vampire that says they shouldn't. Its any number of things that mean they're maintaining their still-human lifestyle. Its something, anything, that they had a connection with as a mortal. The Touchstone should become a mechanical thing the moment they first rise hungry after Embraced.
                        None of them acted human at all after they woke up. Now, it was partially the game's circumstances--they were all killed and then post mortem embraced. They woke up alone in dug up pine boxes and had to, basically, figure things out. So, they actually all avoided their human lives--they thought the people they loved and cared about knew they were dead. They immediately abandoned human life and leapt head first into vampire stuff, because, well, they had little choice.

                        Now, I know those immediate circumstances were causes for detachment, but, as I had constructed the situation and forced it on them, I felt it unfair and just assumed they had all succeeded, instead having them wake up with a condition.

                        I also think attending Sunday dinner would be ridiculous and foolish for a vampire, as you'd be risking detachment every week for no reason.

                        Originally posted by MCN View Post
                        Now, the Ventrue clan bane. Its always been a bit fudgy, but to be honest? I think the Mehket and Daeva ones are insanely easy to ignore as well. Its insanely easy to pick a Bane that is rare enough that it doesn't impact your character at all, and a Daeva's weakness only comes into play if one's herd somehow becomes relevant to the plot. I've had games where neither one has had an impact in the story. You claim the Mehket is the most interesting, but it still comes down to the same thing as the 1e flaw - "same weaknesses as everyone else, but worse!" The new Ventrue flaw is actually meant to reflect the Julii flaw - they tend to become detached faster than the average vampire. The whole Touchstone thing is supposed to reflect them having a harder time holding onto their Humanity. They kind of dropped the old Ventrue flaw (madness) because too many people found that the whole immorality = insanity thing to be insensitive, and some of the derangements do honestly overlap with the general Banes that a Mehket might pick up.
                        So, to me, a successful clan bane is one that influences you to (un)live in a specific manner such that your flaw is minimized.

                        The Daeva curse is great. You must feed from a series of victims. You cannot have a herd. You cannot keep blood dolls. You must feed "promiscuously." Eating is never going to be easy. Ever. So, yes, the flaw "never comes up," but only because you have cut out an entire feeding strategy from your playbook and chosen to live in a specific way, which, well, is the real flaw.

                        The Mekhet flaw works the same way, except it's even more interesting because every Mekhet's flaw is different. Avoiding fire and sunlight is dumb. All vampires do that. It has no real effect. My group and I used to joke that the Mekhet flaw was dying in 6 seconds in the sun, rather than 9. It just didn't matter. But now? They all have a weird bane they have to protect. It seems simple. Avoid church bells or salt or whatever you pick, but you have to arrange your existence around both avoiding that thing and not letting anyone know that you're avoiding it, because you don't want your enemies to know. Again, the bane never comes up directly, but it's an ever present force in your character's existence. That's awesome.

                        Nosferatu is the next best. They take much larger penalties when relating to humans. So, uh, they stop doing that. I can say that in first edition, I saw the Nosferatu flaw actually affect a die roll once in like 10-15 years of play. Just like this version, you won't see it affecting rolls much at all--but you'll see it affecting decisions constantly as the Nosferatu chooses the way he interacts carefully, so he remains un-penalized. He cuts out entire options from his playbook to avoid it. Once again, flaw successful.

                        Gangrel is...well, also problematic. It's certainly better than the crippling mental flaw from before, which, I'm sorry, you just couldn't avoid like you could the Nosferatu social penalty. But, it's still not really character defining. You frenzy more as your humanity dips is kind of it. Given most people's Res+Comp pools, I don't even think it will come up very often until you're pretty far down the chart--at least to 5, and maybe not even 4. So, what does that suggest for a Gangrel? There's not much they can do to avoid it that every vampire doesn't do anyway. It's not a new wrinkle, it's just an additional consequence on top of something that's already bad. Daeva try to keep their humanity in addition to not feeding on the same person twice. Gangrel just, uh, extra try to keep humanity? That doesn't really feel thematically appropriate, does it? Are Gangrel supposed to be the saints? No, it's not even really that anymore--it's also avoiding stuff that reminds you of being human (like watching people eat). So, what, they're like the crazy guy shutting his eyes and ears as he walks around, so he doesn't accidentally experience a human-only thing? I don't know. I don't like this flaw, either, but it's at least better than the Ventrue.

                        Which, yeah, brings me to Ventrue. They don't even have an additional roleplaying consequence (getting angrier, for example), they just have to spend their 1 XP and extra roleplaying touchstone effort 1 humanity earlier than everyone else. That's...just not a concern at all. That changes absolutely nothing, no new wrinkles, not even piled consequences, just exactly the same thing as every other vampire on a slightly advanced schedule.

                        My version of the flaw isn't amazing, which is why I came asking for more suggestions. It still just penalizes a thing that all vampires deal with. But, it does a little bit of what I want. It sort of makes Ventrue into, maybe stuffed shirt types to a degree? They have to stick to this strict code more fiercely than other vampires because they are much more prone to falling than anyone else (around 20% more, as I mentioned before). I picture them having stronger codes of conduct and behavior, following social rituals to avoid human-stimuli and excessive violence/violation, etc. They have to be extra careful to avoid frenzy (the biggest cause for detachment rolls, it seems). You end up with Ventrue that follow this code of decorum, and Ventrue that have gone mad from failing to stick to the code. It's not great, but it's acceptable for now.

                        Originally posted by MCN View Post
                        There's a couple options here for a new Ventrue bane. One is to just copy the Julii clan flaw. -1 to all detachment rolls. Represents the same thing as the current flaw, imho. Another is to just take a derangement if you like the idea of them being a bit unbalanced, though again, we get into Unfortunate-Implications territory. I would absolutely not do the derangement on the drop - not only because of unfortunate implications, but it makes less sense with the current Humanity than it does with the old.
                        I don't want it to become a big thing, but I don't really think tying derangements to humanity loss has any implications. I get that it was pretty awful for some people unfamiliar with the gothic horror mindset when normal humans who did bad things went crazy, but vampires who slid from human to inhuman literally losing their human mind? Seems pretty damn fitting to me. Anyway, I'm not expecting it to come up, regardless. I just want a couple of crazy ventrue elders down the road.

                        Originally posted by MCN View Post
                        Have I ever downplayed Touchstones in a game before? Well, that one is a bit debatable. I consider Touchstones to be something that's Opt-In from a player's standpoint. As in, I leave it up to the players to decide what they're goign to do with them. I've had palyers that simply ignore their Touchstone and just go on with the game, and eventually had nothing to do with one. I've had others that made Touchstones a huge part of the character.
                        So, let's say no players buy in at all. You basically ignore, downplay it, then, right? So, what do you do for the Ventrue flaw to make sure it still matters?
