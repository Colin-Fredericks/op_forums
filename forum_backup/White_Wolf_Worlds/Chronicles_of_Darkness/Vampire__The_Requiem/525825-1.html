            Honestly, it seems a bit odd to me to call out the scales of the dragon as particularly "evil," when three out of the five clan-specific disciplines basically involve inflicting varying levels of psychological abuse. Majesty robs victims of their agency, turning them into dependent sycophants unable to so much as think ill of the vampire. Nightmare inflicts terrifying hallucinations that can shatter a victim's courage and self-confidence. Example commands that can be enforced with the first dot of Dominate include "shoot your husband" and "you killed that man."

            Sure, the scales of the dragon often involve a more obvious, gross sort of torture or violence, but I suspect that most would be far less psychologically damaging in the long term than any of these disciplines. Opportunities to be monstrous are abundantly available to all vampires, regardless of covenant; the onus is on the individual character to decide how much evil they're willing to live with, and what powers they'll choose to reject for moral reasons.

                        The Defiant aren't meant to be nice. None of the covenants are. They exist as dogmas - representations of philosophical points on the nature of vampire existence - that ultimately aren't particularly friendly or humane or altruistic. It *should* be challenging for a regular, young vampire (read: player characters, likely) to be part of a covenant and to keep a human mindset for long. That's the point - the covenant offer the devil's deal of temporal power/mystical power/understanding/answers/faith in return for your Humanity.

                        One of the many things I liked about the Mekhet clanbook is that Wood demonstrates how it works - how someone who's actually a nice, gentle person ends up joining a vampire cult and becoming a believer.

                            I really hope you're proven right, OP. I very much enjoy a dark overlay for nWoD, and anything which shifts the default stance of Covenants members away from being 'real world moral' is a positive for me. You can take it in the evil direction, though I'd say that's mostly the Sanctified. And even then, the purpose of being evil is very specific - you were damned for a purpose, and that purpose is in service to God for the benefit of his flock. And just because the bar has moved, doesn't mean everyone has to be evil for evil's sake all the time.

                            But I can very much see religious concepts of good and evil being one of the first things tossed out the window in a Covenant devoted to vampire blood science.

                            And that is not to say individual characters are shifted one way or another automatically. A high humanity Dragon PC just screams dramatic possibilities. And there are kindred who will absolutely try to do all the right things to maintain humanity - invest in humane medical trade for fat/blood, avoid the torture scales, etc. But I would immediately put them in competition with other Defiant who completely ignore that handicap to produce better / quicker / stronger results. And I would put results as the path to status / power / opportunity in the Covenant.

                            To put another way, I don't tend to enjoy themes of "Covenant X are the good guys." - But I can enjoy, "I'm going to try and be a good guy in Covenant X despite the odds and disadvantages it will bring."

                                Originally posted by Smarmy Vamp View Post
                                My impression of the Ordo Dracul is mostly informed by the 1e information on them, so let's be clear about that potential bias up front.

                                One of the complaints I'd seen regarding the OD is that they weren't really "mad scientist" enough. There was discussion of their varying methodologies in the covenant book, but there was nothing to back up what that becomes in terms of playing the game, and putting that research to use. Except for the Coils, sure, but those are long term, and low-impact in the sense of having a character trait that you then put to use. The 1e Coils were almost uniformly passive. That doesn't scream vampire scientist tinkering with the workings of the very Curse. There were a couple merits that were mechanical misfits (it eventually came out that the merits relating to the Impaled were from an older draft of the rules, or some such), and the rules for Crucible Nests were neat, but it was missing something, mechanically, to capture that vampiric, transcendent scientist feel. The Mind of the Devouring Wyrm merit chain was excellent, though. I loved that one for the introspective, scientific, almost mentat-like quality the Ordo Dracul could invoke.

                                As far as flavor, there were definitely some inhuman elements intrinsic to the OD even then, see Chasing the Dragon's Tail, and any Fontal nest ritual. But overall it, like all the covenants, has room for whatever type of character you want to play. Diverse motivations fit under the same general "goal" umbrella.

                                Take that as context for what I saw when I first skimmed the drafts at Gen Con, and then read in depth when Blood and Smoke/2e released. Suddenly the majority of the fluff, description, and night-to-night workings of the OD were the same (mostly), but the mechanics now have this incredible, dark science, active component in the Scales of the Dragon. You can absolutely go full Mengele with it, and the description, as you've quoted, warns that the Scales are grisly business. The 2e write up of the Ordo Dracul is certainly dark. But, I read that as keeping in line with the tone of the game itself. The Ordo Dracul are about results in the general sense, but how far a given Dragon is willing to go to get those results will vary. Just like what any given Invictus will do to rise in the ranks.

                                I suppose I read the Scales as another facet of what the Dragons do, but not necessarily the sum total. You could have a squared-away, scientific Dragon who insists on informed consent for his subjects, and gathers materials from willing donors or via medical waste or cadavers as Leetespeak suggested. Or... ya know... Mengele.

                                My early experiences with Ordo Dracul players were of mitigating the horrific demands the Curse placed upon them, mostly for reasons of morality, even in the face of a more utilitarian view of the covenant at large. I enjoy the juxtaposition and challenge of trying to maintain Humanity, and being expected to murder a person as an object lesson in observation and unintended consequences.

                                Does that make sense at all? Having a hard time pinning down exactly where my impressions are coming from. This is an interesting exercise.

                                Edit: Oh, one other thing. I think my personal impression is that all the covenants are basically "evil," if you want to boil it down. I think it's an oversimplification, but they're large groups of vampires. In my estimation they start in moral debt and have to work their way out (again, oversimplification, but I hope my point is coming across). Many don't even try. Many give up after a while. Some manage to stay the course, in their various ways.
                                This is actually really good. You raise some excellent points here.

                                I absolutely support the idea that individuals in a Covenant, and even a Covenant as a whole, can be placed on the Sliding Scale of Morality wherever the ST and players think they fit best, or need them to be for the story. My objection with the Ordo in 2E (and maybe the other Covenants. . . haven't read as deeply into them because I'm mostly stuck with what I did with them in 1E in my ongoing game) is that they seem to be, as Smarmy suggests, planted farther on the "dark" side by default then they had been previously. It's not that it's impossible to play them as "good guys," but it seems more difficult and inherently against what the book makes them out to be.

                                But I think we've touched on something that does spark my interest now. . . exploring the ideas of when ends justify means. What rules and codes are the Dragons willing to break in the name of progress, and when do they decide that a path of research is too unethical to pursue? If something seems promising enough, will they allow wholesale slaughter, or will they stop shy of a potentially paradigm-altering discovery because the means to get there are distasteful?

                                I'm reminded of a first season episode of Babylon 5, in which a Dilgar (evil alien race) shows up on the station. She's a famed war criminal among race noted for war crimes, but is soon being protected by every major faction on the station, since she has an immortality formula. Turns out, her formula requires a key ingredient that cannot be synthesized, it must be harvested from living, sentient beings. She goes on to explain that those who have it will destroy those who don't to keep living forever, and her work will reshape the galaxy in her image. Instead of destroying it and her, Commander Sinclair follows his orders and transports her to Earth. . . and a Vorlon ship intercepts her just as she leaves the station and blows her up, with Kosh stating that "You are not ready for immortality."

                                That kind of questioning seems very interesting to me. What ethical guidelines do the Ordo place on the research, and are they times when they break them because the potential rewards are too valuable? Hopefully, that kind of thing can be expanded on in 2E Covenant books.

                                        I think the Ordo coming off as darker overall in 2e than they did in 1e is part of a Requeiem-wide tonal shift. Or... Not really a shift, so much as a refinement. 1e was all over the place in the beginning, and as the game went on, it steadily narrowed its focus as of developed its own identity. But by the time it had figured out where it wanted to go, all the old material was still out there, coloring people's impressions of the game. When 2e released, it had the benefit of starting off where 1e took almost 10 years to end up. From day 1, 2e has been about the slow descent into monstrousness. For some people, that's reading as "all vampires are complete monsters in 2e", and I don't think that's the intent. I think what the writers are going for is "all vampires constantly struggle with the potential to become complete monsters." The Scales are there as an example of what a Defiant doctor can become, if they give up on their humanity and abandon their ethics. Remember, these scientists don't start out doing these horrific experiments from day 1. They started out as humans, with human values and human morals. Over time, they began to lose touch with their human nature. As a player, you get to decide if your character goes down that same path, or if she keeps up the fight to hold on to her fleeting Humanity.
