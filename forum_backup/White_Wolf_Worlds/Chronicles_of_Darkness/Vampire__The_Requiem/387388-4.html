                                        Going by wikipedia (http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of...cal_population) the top ten towns in England (in 1523) by population were London, followed by Norwich, Bristol, Newcastle, Coventry, Exeter, Salisbury, Ipswich, King's Lynn, and Canterbury. Canterbury, Ipswich, and Norwich are no more than a few days' ride, but the others are more distant. By 1662 York has risen in the ranks along with Great Yarmouth, Oxford, and Cambridge, and Canterbury and Salisbury have receded (Lynn has totally dropped off the list). Birmingham, Liverpool, and Manchester are all products of the Industrial Revolution which has yet to occur. Nottingham is a smaller town but has some interesting caves. While population is not necessarily connected to Kindred domains I'd venture that since most of these towns were medieval trading centers they would have had more vampires and more powerful ones. You may want to consider Anglo-Saxon and Roman sites that have fallen into obscurity for elders, however, as well as fortresses, so perhaps Rochester, Pevensey, Dover, the Chiltern hills and the Essex forests, Winchester, Silchester, and Doncaster as interesting places with considerable history. There are also the Catuvellauni settlements, such as Verulamium (St Albans) and the Iceni of Norfolk - the folk of Boudicca - for old and dangerous powers.

                                                Originally posted by An Fhuiseog View Post
                                                Ah, my knowledge of early modern England is hilarious off then! What is/was in the Essex forests actually, sounds interesting.
                                                Well, you've got Epping (then known as Waltham) forest, a woodland to the northeast of London in Essex. It contains a royal hunting lodge that still stands. Further away you have Hatfield forest, Writtle, and Kingswood. All are remnants of a much large forest that once covered Essex. These are real forests, not just hunting preserves. See this site: http://info.sjc.ox.ac.uk/forests/index.html ("Forests and Chases of England and Wales"). It has maps, info on forest law, and images.

                                                        Cool bit of material I stumbled on for adding other interesting parties to Elizabethan England as a setting

                                                        Originally posted by Crime in Early Modern England 1550-1750 By James A Sharpe
                                                        Thus, in 1585, following what appears to be an unusually active drive against crime by the London authorities, a remarkable report was sent to Lord Burghley. This not only listed a number of notorious thieves and fourty five "harbouringe howses" for criminals, but also described a school of pickpockets suggestive of the presence of an Elizabethan Fagin.

                                                        'One Wotton, a gentilman borne,' the report ran, had "procured all the cutpurses about this cittie to repair to his said howse. There was a schole howse sett upp to learne younge boyes to cutt purses.' Among the teaching aids at this unusual academy was a purse containing counters and money which was hung with bells. The youngsters were taught to take money out of the purses without sounding the bells, 'and he that could take a peece of sylver out of the purse without the noyse of any of the bells, he was adjudged a judiciall Nypper.' Such accounts do lend substance to literary evidence of the existence of an 'Elizabethan underworld.'
                                                        While the story itself may not be true (It's apparently from real Tudor court documents that were compiled in 1924, but who knows if the original documents are actually true.), the idea is absolute gold. Especially in light of other famous stories of pickpocket schools, like the School of the Seven Bells.

                                                        Nevermind that the gentleman Wotton sounds like a cool character addition to any game set in Elizabethan England, combining the idea of his school with a more mythical version of the School of the Seven Bells sounds almost worthy of its own Mystery Cult Initiation writeup.
