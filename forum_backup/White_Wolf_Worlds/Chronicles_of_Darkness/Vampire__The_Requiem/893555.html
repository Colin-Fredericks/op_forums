      I started Requiem with 2E, so I'm used to that take on Covenants and I don't know about 1E's presentation.

      I think the primary selling point of the Covenant structure is that they represent different ways Vampires make their night to night existence worthwhile beyond the mad scramble for blood.
      Some go for religion; some go for social activism or political power. A few try to try to see how far they can push themselves as Vampires.

      Each covenant gives context for your place in the world. The Lancea et Sanctum teaches that you're damned, but still have a place in God's plan. The Circle of the Crone tells you that the Beast is to be accepted as just another part of nature. The Carthians and the Invictus focus on what kind of society you're going to live in.

      One thing that I like about this is that every member of the coterie could be from a different Covenant. Another is that by specifying which groups are allied and in power, you can have more kinds of city structures beyond just Anarch/Cam/Sabbat cities.

                  The Sects in Masquerade were all about choosing sides in an ancient war between, and about whether or not you wanted to cling to your Humanity. Requiem has moved away from the former and emphasized the latter, with the Covenants existing primarily as support structures for campires to hold onto through the long eternity of unlife. Covenants shape the beliefs that help keep someone relatively sane as they spend an immortal lifespan as a cruel predator, without which they would quickly fall to the Beast and become a ravening monster. Thr Covenants are there to answer the question of "what do I do now?"

                  For the Invictus and Carthians, the answer they provide is political. The Invictus sees opportunity in their unnaturally long lives, pursuing worldly influence and wealth through the long night of the Kindred experience; they also focus on maintaining their Humanity with oaths of obligation and with petty politics, zooming in on ultimately meaningless titles as a distraction from being a mindless hungry beast. The Carthians hone in on mortal feelings of oppression and injustice, allowing passionate and politically active Kindred an outlet for their fury and frustration that isn't just murder and anarchy in the streets; it gives them hope for a better future with less social heirarchy and perhaps even a chance to do away with the Masquerade and return to something resembling normalcy.

                  For the Lancea Sanctum and Circle of the Crone, the answer they provide is spiritual. The Embrace is a jarring, horrifying experience, and many feel lost as they must now turn to actions they would've found morally reprehensible in life in order to survive. Both of these Sects explain that it's okay go be a monster, assuaging feelings of guilt with either a divine purpose from the God Himself or a more general pagan belief structure that varies wildly. It's about acceptance and religious succor, distracting the hungry Beast with a new goal in (un)life and a new dogma to base one's identity and actions around.

                  The Ordo Dracul are the odd ones out in this equation, and are the smallest Covenant because of this. Rather than pretend at being human like the political factions or embrace the Kindred faiths, they instead fixate on the transformative act of the Embrace and seek to change themselves even further, but this time under their own direct guidance. The Ordo offers control, freeing a vampire to try and shape their own existence with hard work and giving them a goal to strive towards.

                  If you're looking for a more VtM-centric answer, I'd have to say that it had one interesting Sect - the Sabbat - while the Camarilla and Anarchs were played as both the boring default option (compare the number of clans and bloodlines in the Sabbat to those of the Cammies) and as the idiotic dupes of the setting (who bury their heads in the sand and pretend Gehenna isn't real) - while VtR presents all five Covenants as being equally compelling, diverse, and storied. Another major benefit is that you can bring together a coterie across Covenant lines, because they're all competing belief systems rather than outright warring factions; that tension offers far more story potential than "if you see the other team, you kill them on sight."

                  Remi. she/her. game designer.

                      I would add that the new Covenant system much better represent the use of organizations as coping mechanisms to a traumatic transformation to something alien from previous life experience, and trying to deal with how that separates them from known social coping mechanisms for stress and loneliness. Two Covenants busy themselves with distracting politics, one seeks solace in fulfilling their divine role via the Judo-Christian paradigm of God's sovereignty, another seeks meaning in a unified collection of alternative spiritual practices, and one Covenant driven by a self-improvement paradigm (Very similarly to many Path found in the Sabbat).

                      You'll see echoes of the coping mechanisms used in the three Sects of VtM in the Covenants of VtR, but because there are more Covenants than Sects, you don't have weird pairing of groups that don't share the same motivation and the schisms which would occur if it wasn't artificially held together via the flimsy baling-wire of the pen-strokes of white wolf writers.
                      The welding of spirituality and political war verses pragmatic materialism is a very valid storytelling metaphor (and is currently relevant for social commentary due to the Violent Islamic Extremist struggling against the passive-aggressive onslaught of Corporate Westernized marketing) and is the heart of the two primary Sects conflict of VtM.
                      VtR, on the other hand, doesn't galvanize the conflict between materialism verses spirituality by reducing every significant dispute as a loose dichotomy in nature and instead makes a much more nuanced interplay between several interests, reflecting greater complexity than an mere "our side or your side" conflict.

                              I look at the structure of Vampiric society in Requiem in general as a de facto Camarilla when the covenants are at least agreeing to disagree with a minimum of violence. I like the fact that your clan, the part of your character's origin they don't get a say in, does not wholly dictate the course of their life and philosophy as it does in Masquerade. The covenants, which are entirely optional, provide an extra dimension to the character to explore and 4+ different approaches to being undead and meeting night to night survival needs. If you want to play a Lasombra in Masquerade who despises the Sabbat and the theology of Caine, then your character automatically has to play through at least some degree of suspicion and intolerance from Anarchs or Camarilla. When 99% of your clan, which you had no choice in joining, is antagonistic towards the rest of Vampire civilization, its highly restrictive of your choices of character arcs as a player. Play the struggle of being branded a traitor and likely spy or choose a different concept because a setting in which a Lasombra can be Cam or Anarch without a lot of fear and suspicion that may be undesirable for the player is very, very niche and very difficult for the ST to contrive.

                              In this way Requiem is more flexible game in terms of what characters you can play and how you choose to play them. However the clan archetypes in Masquerade and their rich, detailed histories work for Masquerade so that's a plus if you want a very good idea of what you're dealing with just by knowing what someone's clan is.
