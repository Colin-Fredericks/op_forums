    I've seen really valid arguments for both sides of the issue.

    If you have it down to dice, you ignore the importance of the PCs that some games foster where the PCs tend to be a little better than everyone else, but also you risk an NPC or another PC just taking over the game with their dominate. But then everything is done in-game. You have your entire character sheet to work with, as does the ST, and you can pull out your bonuses and counter abilities.

    Just relying on role-playing, you potentially allow for metagaming, as players might not want to do what the convincing person suggests their character do, even if their character has low Resolve and mental fortitude. There's also the problem of the Storyteller, if they cannot role-play a convincing or charismatic person it can all fall flat, especially in games where you have beings that can reach inhuman levels of persuasion. It's easy to role-play a character who can punch through cars, but someone who could convince anyone to do anything with a single sentence is beyond most people's capability. However then you do have more 'role-playing' as opposed to 'roll-playing' and the players have more of a sense of agency.

        Beats make unattractive options seem more attractive. That's basically what being persuasive is all about.

        If you want to do something quick n' dirty, make a contested roll whenever an NPC wants to convince a PC into doing something unattractive, or one PC wants to convince another PC into doing something unattractive: Attribute + Skill vs Attribute + Skill.

        If the player resisting wins, give them a beat for resisting the NPC's / other PC's persuasive charm. If the player resisting wins with an exceptional success, give them the Steadfast condition.

        If the player resisting loses, give them the Leveraged condition. If they lose and the NPC / other player scores an exceptional success, give them two conditions: Leveraged and Inspired. I would give the player trying to persuade the other the Steadfast condition on an exceptional success, too. If this is done in a public space, I would give them a variance of the Connected condition with an exceptional success on top of that.

        Basically, Leveraged is a carrot on a stick for any player. If you go along with what is requested, you resolve the condition and score a beat, and Inspired can add to that by rewarding the player with a Will Power point, another beat, and allow an exceptional success on a roll pertaining to whatever they've been persuaded to do with three successes instead of five. Steadfast is a great way at rewarding those with extra beats upon resolving the condition and by giving them an option to turn future failures into successes, and to make impossible situations seem more possible. The Connected condition in the context of trying to persuade and charm somebody publicly basically makes onlookers marvel at your character's charismatic prowess, and therefore gets a +2 bonus on all rolls relating to this group (simulating he's become more chummy), or resolve it to score a beat and get a one-off +3 instead.

        HOWEVER! Your players still have agency, that's important. They can still say 'no'. They aren't under any obligation to say 'yes'. But if they do, they just don't get any reward. And there's outside factors to take into account, too - that's what the Connected condition represents - if these negotiations are happening publicly, and a person still says 'no' after being trounced, they're going to potentially look foolish. And the persuasive aggressor certainly has the chance to turn the public against them with a second roll (now incurring a bonus) to make that so. However, that person might want to use that Connected roll to allow the other person to save face, trying to get in their good books instead.

        edit: also, don't forget modifiers. If you're wearing a crown, or the other person is in a position where they aren't going to be very inclined to say 'no', then feel free to give modifiers to the roll (-5 to +5). The way I figure it is requesting somebody to do things that personally costs them incurs negative modifiers, and requesting somebody to do things that they're being incentivized to do in-character incurs positive modifiers. The size of the modifier depends on the extremity of what's on offer or what the cost is. Modifiers could be "it goes against your morals", "you could be punished severely if you do it", "it would be personally embarrassing", "bribery", or "a loved one has been taken hostage".

        On top of that, it doesn't need to just be the Inspired condition that you give players. You might want to replace the Inspired condition on an exceptional success with something more contextually appropriate: perhaps the Swooning condition (like Marcus suggests below, seduction as a means of persuasion), Guilty condition (making the person feel bad until they do what you want), the Notoriety condition (you make them look awful in front of everyone), the Distracted condition (you've managed to fast-talk your way into distracting and confusing them, in fact it turns out your initial persuasiveness was merely a red herring for something more sinister).

                        There is no one true way to doing it, so if that's your philosophy at the table, that's that.

                        That said, I personally try to roleplay even when making those combat decisions. I try not to just think about what is the most mechanically efficient, but also what's in-character. For example, despite it being unwise to do so, I tend to make a lot of all-out attacks on characters who aren't that combat savvy. What that represents to me is that the vast majority of people don't really know how to defend themselves when they actually try to hurt other people. It's little stuff like that which you can do to further evince your character in combat and not just be focused on "winning" the encounter.

                        But I think there's a more fundamental disconnect here. I sincerely doubt anyone is objecting to people "allowing real life skills to leak into the game." But rather protesting mismatched expectations. If your ST shows up expecting people to do what's interesting and people only do the thing that gets them the most dice while ignoring things that complicate them, that's just a case of mismatched expectations. It's not wrong for the players to not be interested, but it's definitely something the group needs to feel out beforehand.

                        For those who don't mind surrendering their character to the dice, or going with where the roleplay leads versus choosing not to do something because you think it would end badly as a player, then for those people maybe the dice will inject a bit of randomness to enjoy themselves. Maybe it doesn't work for everybody, but I do suggest people give this style of playing a shot before categorically refusing stuff like social checks and others.

                        Plus, it stands to reason that CofD would try to push this way of playing. This mindset is pretty vital to players utilizing and playing with Conditions in the way were you get the most mileage out of them. Why would taking the Leveraged Condition be any different? It encourages you with Beats.
