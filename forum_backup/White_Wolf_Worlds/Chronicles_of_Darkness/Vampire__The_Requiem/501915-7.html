                    I think the only moral thing a vampire can do is walk out into the sun and burn. There are Buddhists that believe it is not moral to kill animals, including bugs. There are people that when forced to eat other people or starve chose long pig. Morality is personal and therefore subjective when applied to a group of people. It could be that a vampire sees their parasitic existence as necessary to further a greater good. A vampire has all of eternity to do good, or evil. Vampires have power by virtue of their existence. They have time beyond comprehension. It could be that choosing to end that existence and foregoing all the good a vampire could do is not moral. Using people as food is not human, but using other animals is human. Vampires are not human, although they sometimes try to be. How successful they are and how that success is even defined is the game. There is a great deal of room when answering those questions. You can play superhero vampires in Requiem. You can play monsters playing at humanity too. Those are not mutually exclusive either. It's complex.

                        Originally posted by TunnelsofDarkness View Post

                        I don't exactly believe that but however I just pointing out that Reqiuem's extreme version of the personal horror theme leads to that ultimate conclusion where one is better off playing as a Hunter and Vampires as antagonist where genocide is the only solution apparently.

                        Unless of course if the Personal Horror theme is removed and make Vampires somewhat have redeemable aspects to them.
                        I really don't agree with Cat Doom's extreme view on the personal horror of Requiem. I mean, if that's what Cat Doom enjoys in their game, more power to them, but I really don't think that's the intended message of the setting. It's certainly not how I run my games, and it doesn't have to be the way you run yours.

                        The personal horror of vampire, at least for me, is in losing touch with the human experience. A newly embraced vampire probably isn't going to immediately get all woe-is-me about being immortal and having super powers. Hell, I'd be pretty stoked about the whole deal. But they slowly begin to realize that the reality of being a vampire is worse than they may have initially thought. Maybe they were a bit squicked out by the idea of feeding at first, so they tried doing the blood bag thing, or sneaking out to a farm and draining a cow a few times. But eventually out of necessity, or curiosity, or peer pressure, they tried feeding on a human. Surprise! Not only is it not as bad as they thought, it feels incredible. But that's the problem - having been human only recently, they still have human ideas about morality and ethics. On an intellectual level, they know it's wrong. They know they should be horrified at what they're doing. But god damn if it isn't the best thing they've ever felt..

                        Being confronted with this cognitive dissonance of knowing they should feel revolted at what they're doing, but instead feeling elated, they could deal with this identity crisis in a few ways. They could come to Cat Doom's conclusion that they are doomed to bring nothing but harm to the world and the only solution is to meet the sun. I would call that the high humanity route. Or, they could harden themselves, justifying what they're doing as no worse than a human eating a steak, adjusting their worldview to account for the fact that they're not human any more, and the same rules don't apply to them. I would call that the low humanity route.

                        The realities of vampiric existance lead to one of these crisies of humanity after another, and the covenants exist, in part, to help those young vampires struggling to come to terms with what they are cope. They offer context for the confused neonate, give them structure and purpose, a way to put their newfound monstrousness to productive ends.

                        Now, this generally means Humanity loss - their worldviews may make a certain kind of sense, but they are certainly not human worldviews. But inhuman doesn't have to mean evil. For every Acolyte making human sacrifices to heathen blood gods, there is another who merely wants to do as she will and harm none. For every Sanctified who uses religion as a tool for fear and control, there is another who just wants to help other Damned souls realize that God still has a plan for them. For every Defiant who performs cruel and unusual experiments on human subjects in hopes of learning new ways to become a more powerful monster, there is another looking within for personal enlightenment, seeking to become something more than just an undead parasite. For every firebrand who just wants to burn this motherfucker down, there is another who truly cares about making vampire society better, make it so that a politically-minded vampire doesn't have to lie, cheat, and backstab their way to the top. For every Invictus who uses the masquerade to infiltrate human power structures for their own personal gain, there is another who defends the masquerade to protect humans, because they know for most people it's safer not to know.

                        EDIT: to clarify, I don't mean it's a literal 1:1 ratio, I just liked the way "for every ____, there is a _____" sounded.

                                                So first of all, Humanity loss is not a punishment, it's a dramatic device. World of Darkness is very big on the idea that what's bad for the character is not always bad for the player, and in fact it's often good for the player because it creates conflict and makes for good stories. Especially in 2nd edition, which actually mechanically incentivizes players to allow bad things to happen to their characters. Second, you should take a closer look at Humanity, because it is absolutely not an Abrahamic system of morality, at least not in 2nd edition. It's not even a system of morality at all. Humanity Breaking Points happen when a character is forced to face the fact that they're not human any more. Vampires spend a lot of time pretending to be human - not just to disguise themselves from others, but to convince themselves that they're not monsters. Humanity Breaking Points happen for things like surviving something that would kill a human, spending too long without human interaction, depriving someone of consent with your supernatural powers, seeing people you knew in life die of old age, or on a larger scale, watching civilizations fall. Yes, they also happen for things like murder, because murder is really not part of the usual human experience (and in my personal head-cannon, because they don't feel guilty about it, and that freaks them out because they know that they logically should), not because it's immoral. Finally, no, humans and other supernaturals cannot just kill anyone they want without fear of becoming a complete monster. In 1e at least, almost every template had Murder as a sin against what ever stat was their equivalent to Morality, and they became unplayable when their Morality-stat reached 0. Sometimes they became monsters, sometimes they were driven mad, but in general, Morality-stat 0 meant game over. 2nd edition has challenged that status quo a bit, but having a low Integrity-stat (or either extreme high or low for werewolves) still causes complications. Not to mention the fact that someone who commits wanton murder with no compunctions is pretty much a monster regardless of whether or not the rules call them one.

                                                            Well, that's unfortunate. I think some really interesting ideas were starting to crop up, but the constant nay-saying was getting annoying.

                                                            To respond, even though Tunnels won't see it. . . yeah, sure, behaving like a blood-soaked B-movie villain will eventually lead you to Humanity 0. That's why I specifically called it out as the low-Humanity path. "Low," to my mind, being, say, 0-3.

                                                            As for loss of Humanity being a punishment, it's no more a punishment than taking wounds in a combat situation. If a player has their character make choices that leads them into a violent conflict, then they should be prepared to accept the consequences of that scene, which could include lasting injuries and even character death. In the exact same way, a player who chooses to have their character act in a manner that consistently provokes Detachment rules should be prepared to accept the consequences of those decisions, which could include losing Humanity dots and their character becoming an unplayable Draugr. If you don't want your character become a Draugr, don't provoke Detachment rolls below a certain level. Simple.

                                                            The one valid point is with Frenzy, which can take control away from the character long enough to provoke that last Breaking Point. However, let's be honest here. . . if you're at one Humanity, and the ST forces you into Frenzy and makes you do something to lose that last dot, and thus your character, your ST is a dick. Frenzy is a fairly rare thing, in my experience, and there are plenty of ways to stave it off or minimize the damage, so you can keep things from truly spinning out of control. In my opinion, Frenzy is best used as a kind of Sword of Damocles. . . always there, always something the characters (not necessarily the players) should be aware of on some level, but it should only rarely descend and make a particular vampire's unlife miserable.
