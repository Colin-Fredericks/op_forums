                    Originally posted by Varsovian View Post
                    As for the issue of Embrace - so, how many people end up Embraced against their will? Somehow, I ended up coming to the conclusion that they are the minority. Am I wrong?
                    Salt to taste; this is one that can vary a lot between game tables as needed and there's not really a wrong answer. My assumption has always been that the majority of Embraces were not able to make an informed decision, for any number of reasons:
                    • They arose as a vampire without a direct sire, spontaneously. This is rare and unusual, but possible.
                    • They were dead or dying, and were given the Embrace either because a manipulative sire found them a convenient choice who would be indebted to her, or a caring sire wanted to save and preserve them and the Embrace seemed the only way to do so.
                    • They were outright Embraced against their will, such as a ghoul being Embraced by an enemy of his sire to be used as a weapon against her, or a mad vampire in the mountains Embracing prey who impress him with their ability to survive his predations for a long chase, as a perverse reward or to nurture a challenge to relish.
                    • A sire found them interesting and desired them at her side as a friend, an aide, a protegé, a patsy, or whatever, but didn't fill them in on the whole deal. They probably knew their sire had some strange power or inhuman aura, but not necessarily that she was a vampire. She might give them a vaguely worded, mysterious offer to mull over, or even secretly arrange a circumstance that would leave the fledgling no choice but to accept.

                    I consider that last entry, the incomplete-information offer, the likely most frequent scenario for how most surviving vampires get Embraced. (Some Embraces, like those that are inadvertant or coercive, might also be plentiful, but vampires Embraced under those circumstances are less likely to unlive as long as those eased into it and not born straight into a turf war.) The likely second most frequent, in my mind, is a ghoul of several years' service being chosen to receive the Embrace, already having been inculcated with an unnatural loyalty to his regnant and familiarity with vampiric society. A ghoul is likely to just view the deal as a step up.

                    In general, being inducted into the ranks of the Kindred is a shady deal. Their society is a respectable veneer of etiquette and grandeur concealing a room of thugs with knives. Like the mob, often by the time you realize how deep you're in, you're already too deep to get back out.

                    Originally posted by Varsovian View Post
                    BTW. Which sourcebooks would you consider the most useful for a vampire GM? Are the covenant books useful? The clan books? Something else?
                    #1 most useful: Damnation City. Some GMs may find it less useful than others (it's not that useful to me personally), but if you're the kind of person wondering what happens in a Vampire game, how do you set up stakes and what kind of interesting events could ensue, this book is for you. It's a big old toolkit running down how to build a domain from the macro to the micro level, with lots of looks at different aspects of life in different sorts of cities for the Kindred. Take a look at the section on sample Princes; they're not individual NPC writeups so much as each writeup is an entire campaign setting and genre of game to run.

                    #2 most useful: The clanbooks. The clanbooks are majority in-world artifact letters and writings – they're literal books about each clan that a character could ostensibly find in play and get caught up in. Each book tends to follow the story of a figure iconic of that clan, but is richly interspersed with episodic writings, vague historical references, and apocrypha associated with the themes and history of the clan. Each one also has a short appendix of mechanical widgets to play with. Let me put it this way: I didn't like Requiem until I first read one of the clanbooks (Mekhet: Shadows in the Dark). I'm not even normally a fan of this kind of in-character fiction in game writing. But these books do an excellent job of establishing through implication a defined world of uncertainty, forgotten omens, lonely monsters and secret betrayals. They contain vignettes that will show you what awful things a vampire does and why they are interesting. They're not humorless, either. Two of the Daeva clanbook's most biting spreads are given in the form of underground comic tracts.

                    Past that, your mileage may vary. Depending on what you find interesting, you may look into Mythologies (alternate twists and strangeness associated with the nature of the Curse), Ancient Mysteries (on the psychology and behavior of elder vampires, and a collection of strange flashpoints in history as they relate to the Kindred), one of the covenant books, one of the Night Horrors books (Immortal Sinners for a rogue's gallery of trouble-stirring vampiric monsters, Wicked Dead for alien threats: vampires that are not Kindred, and Kindred that are not vampires), or something else entirely. These depend on your tastes and what kind of game you are interested in running, but Damnation City and the clanbooks are pretty universally recommendable.

                                I'm not personally that big on the covenant books either, I think they tend to be dry. They did lay groundwork that later supplements ran with rather successfully, though.

                                Originally posted by Varsovian View Post
                                (for once, it's not actually explained what Lancea Sanctum's religion really is...).
                                The Lancea et Sanctum is a religion. They're not Catholic, or Orthodox, or Protestant, or Jewish or Islamic or what have you. They resemble denominations of Christianity, especially Catholic due to their emergence during the decline of Rome, but their beliefs are distinct enough that most Christians would probably consider them offensively blasphemous. They believe that vampires are God's chosen scourges, at once cursed with damnation and blessed with holy duties to predate upon humanity, to test the pious and punish the wretched. They have their own Dark Father whom they arguably hold in higher regard than Jesus, shepherd of the dead as Christ was shepherd to the living.

                                They're not vampires following a human faith. Their spiritual answers address the problems of the Requiem. And they start to sound really scary and unpleasant when you look at them from the perspective of those God commands them to eat and harry.

                                                    Originally posted by Varsovian View Post
                                                    I admit that I have the Ventrue clanbook at home and... I didn't make it through it. Maybe I was too quick to jump to conclusions, but there was too much in-universe vague tidbits and not enough straight facts about what the Clan is like...
                                                    I tend to think the Ventrue book is the driest of the bunch, but if anything it has much more in the way of straight facts than the clanbooks that follow, what with all of the sections on things like proper etiquette or the tale of Troy. The clanbooks are all about presentation through examples, show-don't-tell – here's what a Ventrue looks like when he comes out of lowly roots (the Lord of Paradise Lake), here's what a successful young Ventrue looks like (the Mogul), here's an outre spiritualist Ventrue (the Witch of the Woods), and here are some of the sides the Ventrue try not to show and how they end up coming out anyway (the dead girl's ritual, Malkavia). You notice the ways in which they differ and the ways in which they still resemble one another, and that's your picture of the clan. Requiem clans are neither monolithic nor do they tend to hang together in coherent political blocs. They're not covenants; they're tendencies and natures. That can be expressed a lot of different ways.

                                                    Maybe give one of the later clanbooks a glance and see if you still feel like you don't get much out of it. Gangrel and Mekhet have central narratives about particular characters to wind the digressions around, and Nosferatu ties around exploring the mysteries and unfolding intrigue of a particular domain in a violent power struggle, so those might possibly give you something to hang onto. If they're still just not really your speed, then maybe it's just a fair cop. I can still recommend Damnation City even if you don't like the clanbooks, it's a much more traditional format for a supplement book.
