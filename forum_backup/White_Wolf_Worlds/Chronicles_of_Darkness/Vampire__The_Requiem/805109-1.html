                Agree wholeheartedly. Also, it's one thing to keep the truth vague, and another to assume that somehow the denizens of this fictional world are fully aware of our inability to know for sure. People, let alone vampires, love being opinionated. The fourth wall comes crumbling down when this kind of conviction in one's personal version of how things happened in the past, is solely reserved for the five clans and the five covenants. Everything else feels like it has been subjected to a clean-up crew's treatment, with nothing but loose ties in the form of bullet points at the point of an otherwise promising write-up.

                Sometimes I am misunderstood as a fan of how Masquerade did things in the mythos department. Personally, I can't imagine playing or ST'ing in VtM without assuming the entire Caine mythos is fabricated! But it's nice they have historian NPCs investing into the fiction, panicking over how their mythology could be true. That's the whole point of religion and politics after all, no? Where were the lovely Edimmu later on in the line? Out of all places, how no one in the world of chronicles of darkness simultaneously researched both them and the Strix? Oh, and where are the Strix experts in the fiction? Unless I'm missing something, there's a sort of pervasive inconsistency that although declining, hasn't completely died out. No, not the kind of inconsistency that makes things optional and easy for the ST to come up with original ideas. I'm talking about the kind of inconsistency that makes anything beyond the core awkward to co-exist with elements across the books.

                I think the clanbooks were impeccable, and clearly representative of the kind of writing that should be allowed to shine in 2e.

                Bloodlines are another perfect example. I'm all for choice, but it's kind of sad that players can't really relate and grow attached to bloodlines because of their absence from any other book published later on. The Khaibit and a few others are the obvious exception, but it would still be nice to see iconic characters and moments in history where certain bloodlines made a difference. Certain broods, or elder conclaves, or coteries, actually made a name for themselves and pushed the covenants into a tight, uncomfortable spot.

                    Originally posted by Leetsepeak View Post
                    I'm not dismissive of the fluff in the sense that I think it's bad. There's just stuff in the 2nd edition writing that contradicts it, which calls it all into question.
                    While a lot of the earlier books have this issue, the later books, especially the clan books, were what actually inspired there to be the 2nd edition in the first place! The lore had changed too much from the early days to the latter that we needed to update the core book. The Ancient books tend to reflect more of 2e's mindset and history than 1e's.

                    So, while the mechanics aren't great, the story is actually fairly up to date in terms of 2e.

                    Further, while a lot of it is really cool (I loved those books!) I think a lot of it is still constructed with the mentality that all the groups need to reflect the Five Covenants in modern nights, which is IMO a weakness of the material.
                    The core five covenants are archetypes. In fact, they're kind of reflections of clan-mindsets. The Invictus (and all similar covenants) reflect the Ventrue mindset taken to an extreme, the vampire noble houses. The Movement reflects the Daeva (passionate rebels - Bruja anyone?), the Ordo reflects Mehket (occultists), the Circle and Lance the Gangrel (wild-nature) and Nosferatu (empathy with the dead/damned, necropolis) respectively. So, naturally, you're going to see any other covenant as a reflection of the core seven once you include the demonic Brood (strix) and the killer VII. Because all the covenants are derived from the same universal vampire archetypes.

                    There's a reason that the "lost clans" and "broken covenants" still look like the modern clans/covenants. Even the Child's Crusade became a reflection of a kid-infested Brood line, and the Legion of the Dead became mercinary assassins like certain other covenant based on the Aasimites.

                        Originally posted by MCN View Post
                        There's a reason that the "lost clans" and "broken covenants" still look like the modern clans/covenants. Even the Child's Crusade became a reflection of a kid-infested Brood line, and the Legion of the Dead became mercinary assassins like certain other covenant based on the Aasimites.
                        The Broken Covenants aren't like the big Five though, which is what makes them interesting. The Legion of the Dead's closest analogue (and its ancestor) is the Legio Mortuum, and they're unique from the Five Covenants. The Gallow's Post is not like any of the Covenants in Modern Nights. The Weihan Cynn merely share an aesthetic with the Crones, but they're refreshingly distinct. The Children's Crusade as a 'covenant' does not align with any of the others, even if they become a chaotic enemy that resembles the Brood if you squint.

                        The Ministry of Silence in Beijing does not feel the need to look like any of the Big Five. Neither do the Watchful Eyes in Berlin. The zaibatsu of Tokyo also aren't so easily pigeonholed, it's one of the best parts about the 2nd edition.

                        I say all this to make the point that the Lost Covenants don't resemble the Big Five at all really, and it's one of the strengths of the new edition.

                        As I recall, in the 1st edition, there was a certain obligation to represent the five as archetypes. I think they even had to. That's a weakness of some of the later products that couldn't really be helped. What I'm talking about doing is being strategic in what inspires you in the books. Requiem for Regina makes it clear that the Icarian Bloodline plays a big part in the politics of the time period among European Kindred, for example. I'd probably use them since they made it into the 2e material. There's mention of the different Creeds in Regina as well, though because it's from the Lancea's sorta iffy Covenant book, I'd look carefully at which ones to use (I probably wouldn't use the Dammitic Creed or the Iblic Creed for instance, because they actually are the perfect examples of victims of 1e's everything must be Big Five vibe.) but I'd certainly look at them to inspire. (I've used the rituals and shit in the Lancea et Sanctum book as inspiration for what Sanctified characters actually do.)

                        To bring it back to the question of vampiric buddhism, the previously mentioned Beijing Covenants are not going to give you extensive writeups that describe what they do, but what IS said about them might serve as inspiration for how some vampires might internalize buddhism.
