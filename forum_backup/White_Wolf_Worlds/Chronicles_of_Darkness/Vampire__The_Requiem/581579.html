    Circle of the Crone in New York

    07-06-2015, 06:38 PM
    So I'm in a bit of a bind with the setting my group has been playing in. We've only had a few sessions so far and haven't gone into great detail about the Circle of the Crone in Brooklyn, where our game is taking place, but the problem is that I'm feeling tremendously uninspired in writing what the Circle are like in the borough and in the city at large. It's New York, so it's a very cosmopolitan setting that can pull on a lot of different traditions from around the world, so that's cool, but it can make it a little overwhelming to deal with.

    We have a very basic idea of how the Circle got its start in New York City: After they sort of had their breakout time in the 19th century. The Acolytes that came were part of the the wave that spread out through Western Europe and into the Americas after they rose up against the Sanctified in Scotland & Ireland. They prompted a similar uprising among Kindred that were practicing disparate heretical traditions in New York. Previously, these individual cults or covens would get snapped up by the Sanctified who acted as watchdogs of a sort against nomads and newcomers to the Night City spreading their heresies, until they slaughtered a number of Sanctified and earned their place at the table through violence. They killed a lot of the Sanctified in New York until they were brought to heel, and for awhile, there was a truce negotiated between the Covenants.

    While it's still in effect today, the Sanctified have been weakened even further on a city-wide scale in the last 50 years, allowing the Circle to gain more prominence than its ever had in New York.

    So while we've got that cool backstory, we're sort of lacking in a compelling idea of what might make New York's Acolytes striking or interesting. This could be ideas for specific cults or it could be ideas for the overall organization of the Covenant in the city, I'm honestly shot for ideas.

    What would you do to make the Acolytes interesting?

          Well, personally, I would throw out any relationship to a human religion. Instead, I focus around the concept of the Dark Mother, ala Beast, and the Strix. The default Circle of the Crone are basically the vampiric version of Wicca, with a touch of other neo-pagans. Go full out primordial monsters and dark gods with the Crone. It doesn't matter if we're talking Celtic, Norse, Voodoo, or whatever roots - each vampire in the Circle takes their own belief, and puts themselves as the god figure of a cult.

              I used a dash of the Mother's Army from the Danse Macabre. Essentially in that, the Crone is a thing, theoretically a progenitor to the Kindred. And through the Cruac, witches can create her Vitae, which they then feed to the initiates during their entrance ceremony. From there, the Crone is sort of like the Beast, a voice in their soul, assuring them of certain deeds and balking at others. There's a certain amount of zealous aggression to this, too. They can hear Her voice, She is real. But the Lancea et Sanctum is the 'second state,' practically just due to being organized and structured longer. Other covenants get respect for outright denying the gift of the Crone or sidestepping it with politics and pseudoscience. It should seethe in some.

              The problem with the Mother's Army was that it was an actual army, so they weren't really in the game of 'playing' politics, their standard operating procedure is outright invasion. So, like I said, just a dash. Some of the members of the Circle are aggressive, they want to spread the word, and maybe in some settings they can. But others are more wise, they know that if other Kindred don't appreciate it first they won't understand, they'll take the gifts of Cruac without a deeper understanding of what they represent, and twist them to impure ends.

              In my own game the Circle was one of the weaker covenants in the beginning that became one of the most powerful, second to the Invictus, at the end. They started off with a schism, with the Mother of Scales ruling over it all as Hierarch, but not really leading the way she should. Essentially she was selfish, prideful, enjoyed her power and the freedom it gave her and didn't want to share it. So she kept telling the 'leader' of the younger ones, Krystal, that she wasn't ready for the next stage and so forth, as well as completely humiliating her on a political and social front in regards to the rest of the Court. Some other older members of the Circle thought she was being irresponsible about it, but as they were mostly independents and busy with their own things, they didn't have much clout in the politics of the covenant, and the Mother of Scales was probably one of the toughest vampires in the state so forcing the matter wouldn't get anywhere.

              Krystal persisted, however, the PCs helped a lot with her side of things. And eventually their actions got the Mother of Scales killed, allowing Krystal to rise up and put things in order so the knowledge of Cruac could be dispensed among the coven as it was meant to be.

              Things like that help. Not always thinking about covenants as one big monolith but just a bunch of people loosely tied to one another. Best way to make the Circle interesting is to make the members interesting. Then figure out some interesting personalities, squabbles, 'civil' wars, see how those things would turn the Acolytes into the ones in your game.

I used a similar idea to replace Octavio, the prophet of Transylvania Chronicles, in my game by three female Kolduns inspired by a slavic Trinity of mythical figures: The Zorya. I blogged about it: here. Probably too tailored for Transylvania to be of use for you, but maybe you can cherrypick some elements that you like.

                              2. Assimilation Strategies

                              Originally posted by Leetsepeak View Post
                              I don't think it really matters if the Circle is right, so much as what their myriad beliefs mean and how they construct an organization that bridges so many gaps with an ideology, even if practices and some beliefs differ.
                              ​When I first read this, it reminded me of something I couldn't quite pin down. So it's been on my mind for half a day, and now I remembered.

                              Years ago, I wrote an essay about empires and mythology. I'll spare you the details, but the basic idea was that in the Bronze Age, military technology made a leap forward. Not only in regard to metallurgy but in regard to logistics. Some cultures figured out how to organize a supply train for large armies. Up to that point, warfare is only possible at the fringes of your territory. The people there have had cultural exchange with your people for a long time and probably have a culture and mythology similar to yours.

                              With that supply train technology (technology in the original sense of "know how"), you can make war on people who live farther away and who's culture differs significantly from yours. You can conquer large territories and thus forge the first empires (think Babylonians, Assyrians, Egyptians etc.).

                              These first empires were tasked with a new challenge: They had to assimilate a culture that differed significantly from theirs. Most importantly, they had to make the "new people" accept the legitimacy of their leadership. Since leadership was almost exclusively charismatic ("by the Grace of the Gods", so to speak), this meant you had to assimilate their mythology.

                              So, long story short, I came up with four possible strategies to deal with this task:
                              • Identification
                                You make a claim that elements of the the mythologies are identical. It essentially means to say: "Yes, your sun god and our sun god is the same guy, we just call him differently."
                                The Romans did that a lot. They had veritable translation tables. The Phrygian goddess Cybele and the Egyptian Hathor are also good examples for this.
                              • Diversification
                                You try to incorporate the foreign mythology in your own system. It essentially means to say: "Yes, your sun god is real, too. We acknowledge that. He is the friend/assistant/son of our sun god." You find that a lot in the Greek pantheon. The old sun god Hyperion got integrated as a servant of the new Helios.
                                Sometimes the narrative was more martial: "We conquered you and our gods conquered your gods." That variant can be found in Norse mythology, too. The Vanir are leftovers of a pantheon that got assimilated into the Æsir.
                              • Unification/Reinvention
                                You concentrate on the common mythemes that the two mythologies you want to assimilate have and merge them or create a new one that embodies that common core. Egyptian Isis Hathor and Roman Sol Invictus are good examples for that.
                              • Abstraction
                                This came much later and is the main idea behind Gnosticism. It essentially means to say: "We mortals can not have true knowledge (gnosis) about the gods. They can only show us masks and representations of themselves. The real divine sun has revealed itself to us in the guise of our sun god and to you in the mask of your sun god."


                              Now, finally to the Circle in New York :-)

                              With the constant influx of immigrants from all over the world, the Circle faced a similar task as the ancient empires: assimilate the mythologies they brought with them. Over time, probably all the strategies above have been tried.

                              So, if you want to come up with something that no longer corresponds to a mortal religion, you might use these strategies to build some syncretic amalgam.

                              If I had to come up with something, I'd assume two main lines of assimilations within the Circle, which now begin to merge:
                              • The first and older line corresponds to European immigrants. Its cultic practice is an amalgam of Celtic, Norse and Greco-Roman mythologies. All centered around Gaia, Mother Earth.
                              • The second line corresponds to the Asian immigrants. Its practice is some mixture of Asian traditions, dominated by Chinese folk religion. Lots of Dragons, here.

                              Building on the Chinese Earth Dragon, the Greek Python, the Welsh (and Arthurian) Red Dragon a merging process has started some decades ago. Especially Python is interesting here, because in Greek Mythology she was bound to Gaia and was killed by Apollo who is a god of light (and in later Greek Mythology a sun god).

                              The result is a syncretic cult of the "Mother Dragon" who was an enemy of the sun god.

                              Something like that?

                              3. Acolyte Boheme
                              My favourite idea, however, is that the "Semioticians" found in the Covenant book are particularly numerous in New York's Circle of the Crone. They are drawn to the "abstraction" strategy mentioned above. For a New York flavour of them, I like the idea of intellectual hipster vampires hanging out in Greenwich Village coffee shops, discussing mythology, quoting Joseph Campbell and Georges Dumézil and looking everything but sinister with their iPads. But when push comes to shove, they pull a vast diversity of Babylonian, Celtic, Chinese, Indian and Cabbalistic rituals out of their pockets and start wreaking havoc with their blood sorcery ;-)

                                          The Python might be a keystone for this. So, a bit of background info: In Greek mythology that dragon was said to have the power of prophecy. And fittingly enough, the prophetic power was in its blood. In fact, when Apollo slew this "earth dragon of Delphi" near its lair at Mount Parnassus, the soil there was soaked in its blood. From then on, the earth gave off noxious fumes that brought prophetic visions. That's why the Oracle of Delphi was located there. The chief priestess of the oracle was called the "Pythia" because she wielded the prophetic power that came from the Python.
