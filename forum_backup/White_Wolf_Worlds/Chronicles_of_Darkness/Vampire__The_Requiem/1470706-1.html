    Originally posted by Tessie View Post

    Page 176, "Surprise":
    "Any character who fails the roll cannot take an action in the first turn of combat, and can’t apply Defense for that turn."

    I also think that's a pretty cool idea. That's why we've homebrewed Quicker Than Sight:
    Quicker than Sight
    (Celerity ••, Vigor ••)
    This relatively rare Devotion is based on the idea that an enemy cannot defend against what they cannot see coming. While most Kindred would let such subtleties go through the Obfuscate Discipline, some few have chosen speed rather than deception. Quicker than sight is a trick that allows the Vampire to strike before anyone watching them could possibly have reacted, to most the vampire is simply a momentary blur, and their enemy falls down dead. This Devotion is rarely taught from one Kindred to another, but rather individually developed when Kindred have contemplated the value of speed. Versions of this power have been developed by Kindred as far apart as Japan and the American Southwest.
    Cost: 1 Vitae
    Dice Pool: None
    Action: Reflexive
    When activated, Quicker than Sight lasts for a single attack, but that attack is so fast that unless the Vampire’s victim has any defense that applies against firearms, they simply cannot react quick enough to apply defense.
    This Devotion costs 3 Experiences to learn.

    It requires a little more investment, and is naturally countered by anything which grants Defense vs firearms (such as Celerity, partially, and the Quicken Sight Devotion from the core book). It's actually even more powerful than your interpretation of base Celerity since it bypasses the surprise roll completely, so it might not be to your liking.

    I like this devotion, I'm going to ask my ST if he can add it, still its a shame that the writers didn't thought about such a possibility for celerity, I'm alway uncomfortable whenever I have to use an homebrew rule.

                        Where in the description for Obfuscate 3 does it say that you can re-establish surprise?
                        All I can see is that if you use Obfuscate 3 when attacking someone who can't see you, you get to add your Obfuscate dots to the surprise roll. I don't see where it says that you can do the surprise roll even though you're no longer qualified to use the surprise rules.

                        Just because Obfuscate grants a bonus to a certain roll doesn't mean you get to roll that if other rules doesn't allow for it to be rolled. And even if you did get to roll surprise, you wouldn't gain any benefits whatsoever because surprise only affects characters during the first turn of combat. That's a core part of the surprise rules.

                        It's the same reason Celerity can't be used to gain surprise during combat: The fact that System A gives a benefit to System B doesn't mean you can use System A to invoke System B outside the stated limits for System B. Not without System A explicitly changing that limit, which it doesn't do.

                            One of the activated effects of Vigor allows you to lift and throw large objects. Would you say that you could use Vigor to lift and throw stuff around even if your arms are tied? If they're broken or outright missing? If there's nothing around to lift or throw?
                            Using Vigor to boost your lifting and throwing capabilities doesn't mean in any way, shape, or form that you're automatically able to lift and throw stuff if other circumstances makes it impossible.

                            That's the problem with using Celerity or Obfuscate to make surprise attacks during combat: The rules for surprise attacks doesn't work during combat so you can't make surprise attacks during combat regardless of how much Celerity or Obfuscate would boost surprise attacks.

                            It's a shame there's a glaring hole in the combat rules for unexpected attacks outside the very first turn of combat, and in my games there would be no questions about unexpected attacks ignoring Defense (the victim would still retain their attack since they're already aware of them being in combat, and unless the attack is nondetectable (which is generally terrible game design so almost certainly not) a roll would still be made to see if the attack actually is unexpected) but that's a clear example of a houserule.

                                    The rules are pretty clear about no surprise attack being possible after combat starts, and since surprise affects action economy, it makes perfect sense.

                                    Satchel also already said that unexpected attacks already ignore Defense, there's just no direct rule for that, which isn't so much of a problem.

                                    Making your attack be unexpected is far more complex than just being fast. The way Celerity handles things I'm already disinclined to think it naturally allows that amount of speed. You may be fast, but your opponent is already aware of a combat going on, reacting to you as best as possible, and a turn is just too short an amount of time anyway to change that expectation. You're fast, but not a bullet.

                                    Now, Merits and Devotions to solve that are another thing, while personally I wouldn't handle that just with speed. Blinking behind of your opponent doesn't change that your opponent is in the high-adrenaline state of a fight and expecting things from any direction, especially if they know how fast you are. Feinting and otherwise fooling your opponent makes a lot more sense to handle this, and should take into account their ability to see through your trick.

                                    But personally I'm against even firearms having this silly benefit of ignoring Defense. A person may be unable to dodge a bullet, but it is still far harder to correctly aim at a target actively avoiding to be aimed at.

                                            Originally posted by Newb95 View Post

                                            No, I just disagree that this speed equates to a penalty on Defense.

                                            Celerity has still a key limitation: you're still having only one action a turn. It gives you a burst of speed, but not an equal burst of perception. Your reaction time is, indeed, improved, but not by that much.

                                            It is the exact same situation of the bullet. Your target can't react properly, which is already covered by Interrupt with messing with their decision to act, but you can't fine-tune your action either, just commit to the action you would do anyway.

                                            Or increase speed, the option you quoted and that is more powerful a speed burst, giving you movement equivalent to multiple actions, but is limited to your movement alone, hinting that you can do only so much so fast.

                                            My take also considers how broad and abstract I see the Defense stat, because it is the only property of the target that affects your own attack. Denying Defense makes the target effectively as vulnerable as a dummy or a box. So the question is: should this equate the target to a non-moving target? No reaction, no positioning, no difficulty whatsoever? Unless the answer is an unmistakable "yes", I'm against denying Defense and even wary of penalizing it.

                                            But I do think other avenues may exist to explore the question. The Dodge roll certainly shouldn't be possible against such an attack, for example. And if you have a roll to feint or otherwise make an actually unexpected attack, then this roll could be benefited by the speed. A character that specifically hones their ability of exploring this blinding speed to penalize or ignore Defense through a Merit or Devotion would be a valid approach.

                                                Originally posted by monteparnas View Post

                                                I think this is overstating things, because attacking a completely vulnerable target just uses the Killing Blow mechanics.

                                                Quoting from the newer iteration of the core mechanics used in 2e corebooks published after Promethean, "Defense measures your character's ability to react to danger and mitigate harm to herself;" the latter is the most probable explanation for why the Stalwart Numen changes what Attribute an ephemeral being uses to calculate Defense rather than giving it an Armor rating, or why the unnatural forms of a werewolf can still apply Defense against Firearms attacks (or, for that matter, why Celerity effectively gives a bonus to Defense that still applies against ranged attacks).

                                                If you can apply Defense to an attack, you choose whether or not to (such as if you want to avoid the cumulative penalty from multiple attackers and prioritize warding off a specific source of damage); the inability to apply Defense against ranged attacks is as much "you can't really meaningfully roll with the punches of getting winged by a bullet" as it is "bullets move faster than most human reaction times."


                                                Quoting further from that same iteration of the core mechanics, Surprise is sorted into the same part of the section on Action Scenes as Initiative, where it leads with talking about how surprise rolls might be called for "if the start of an action might take a character unawares," with the examples provided being an ambush or being lulled into a false sense of security rather than simply vanishing for less than thirty seconds.

                                                The specific phrasing of not being able to act or apply Defense "until their second turn" is consistently used between both wordings of the mechanic, so I feel pretty confident that even in a fight where you can disappear at will you're not going to be automatically stopping your targets from ever taking a turn through the Surprise mechanics alone.
