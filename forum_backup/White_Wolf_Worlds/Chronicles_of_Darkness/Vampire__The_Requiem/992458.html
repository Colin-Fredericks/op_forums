    Understanding Combat/Initiative (in general) + Roman weapons

    10-24-2016, 01:35 PM
    It looks like I'm finally going to be running my Roman-era campaign beginning in January, so I'm taking another look at some of the rules.

    I don't understand the initiative penalties for weapons (in general, in some cases it makes intuitive sense). What does this represent? I would imagine that drawing a sword would reduce initiative (I don't think I've seen rules for that), but once a vampire has it in his hand, should it really slow him down that much, especially since it would increase his reach a bit?

    I don't necessarily want to change this, just to understand it better.

    My second question is whether anyone has done stats for 2e Roman weapons?

    I have a preview doc of weapons saved (I think for Hurt Locker, not sure, but I don't think there's a release date on that one yet and the preview didn't have a lot of pre-modern weapons) that has a long sword doing +3 damage, -2 initiative. If I let that represent the sort of swords equestrians used, the infantry gladius might be +2 damage, -1 initiative.

    It lists a two-handed spear but maybe I will just use it to represent the pilum anyway (+2, -2, reach).

    I don't know what to do about artillery. It's less likely to come up but if it does I think a ballista should seriously mess up a vampire.

    Of course, if I prep thoroughly to run combats my players will invest entirely in mental Disciplines and subvert, charm, scare or evade anyone who might threaten them... but if I don't, they'll find a reason to take on a whole Roman century.

                  Yeah, that's what it nominally represents. But if you look at the way weapons are designed, the Initiative penalty has a closer correlation to the weapon's damage and number of additional effects than it has to do with the weapon's actual weight (which is already represented by its strength requirement) or its size or shape (which is already represented by its Size).

                  Note how there are a couple of different weapon templates. An average, well-rounded melee weapon like a crowbar or a machete is +2 damage, -2 Initiative, 2 Strength, Size 2. Then you've got your more tactical weapons like chains and tire irons, which are +1 damage, -3 Initiative, 2 Strength, Size 2, and have one tag like Grapple or Defensive. A tire iron isn't significantly heavier or more difficult to weild as a weapon than a crowbar, it just that the system treats the Defensive property as worth slightly more than 1 damage, so they increased its Initiative penalty to balance it out. You could probably work out a point-buy system for designing weapons, and a few of the sample weapons would be a little ahead of the curve (like Rapiers) and a few would be a little behind (like Knives), but for the most part you could reproduce any of the sample weapons by treating +2/-2/2/2 as the baseline and increasing Initiative, Strength, and Size to balance out increases in Damage and special properties.

                                  Originally posted by Jakondite View Post


                                  Think that's covered under Heavy Weapons "Threat Range" Manuever anyways

                                  But not very well, and rather redundantly. Let's set aside system balance considerations, because as we all know weapons were not created to be "balanced" to one another, but to do the job as quickly and effectively as possible.

                                  A quick-and dirty modeling of initiative and weaponry would look like this:
                                  0. Let's assume initiative penalty represents recovery time and tends to scale with the size, weight and relative unbalancing factors of a given weapon (i.e. a chain is awkward compared to a machete).

                                  1. If neither you nor your opponent are within weapon range, you are both ready and you move to attack each other, your weapon initiative penalty is set to zero and replaced by a bonus equal to weapon size (representing reach factors). So if you are both using size 2 machetes, it cancels out, but a machete vs. fists is net +2 initiative. If this complicates initiative with other interested actors (say, those with firearms), you could zero weapon penalties and then apply a penalty of the difference in sizes to whomever has the smaller weapon--but to me that's more complicated.

                                  2. If you and your opponent are both within weapon range, apply normal initiative penalties. You have struck and are recovering to strike again or otherwise act. The smaller, faster, less awkward weapon will have a natural lower penalty, representing movement of the combatant to within their opponent's reach. In the example both machetes would enjoy a -2 initiative penalty, while fists have a 0 penalty. Maneuvers (for knife fighting, for example) could increase initiative penalties for opponents using larger weapons, or even penalize attack dice.

                                  Done. You now have a good, mechanical reason for a sword fight to look like a series of passes as one or both parties try and put enough distance to use their reach. This is essentially how it was done outside of grappling. For amusement: 3 fencing masters vs 50.

                                  And, to circle back, "Threat Range" is--what? Representing an extra attack? If so that attack should come at full power, as reach is the reason the knife loses to the katana in a prepared exchange--Mr. Knife is going to end up slit from throat to hip before he gets his stab in, not take "1 lethal". If Threat Range is supposed to represent positional experience then it could give a defense penalty and that would be enough, but mechanically a maneuver that opens space to take advantage of reach per above is more appropriate.

                                  --Khanwulf

                                      I like that idea of treating combat as a series of passes and some maneuvers being able to create space. That said, it would need a complete overhaul of the fighting system. I have seen few both solid and elegant fighting systems, and pretty much none that was manageable without miniatures and a board. It could be interesting to have a range system in close combat, maybe represented by a series (two or three, let's not get crazy) circles around each participant. At this point, I just have to let things go and admit that realistic fighting (pretty much fencing at this point) mechanics are not so central to world of darkness that they need such an elaborate system. I'd like to see one, if it is ever created, as long as it's optional. Otherwise, your mod seems to have potential.

                                      Mysteries of the 7th sea (the old edition, there's a new one apparently) had a pretty elegant and still manageable fighting system that felt rich and strategic.

                                      To go back to initiative. There are other factors. Initiative is a very abstract notion. First, we're accepting the idea of turn-based combat which is still hugely abstract. A good fighter will not only look to attack and defend, but to deny his opponent opportunities for attack. Constant movement to stay out of range, feints to force the opponent to guess, pressure to put them on the defensive and prevent them from attacking at all (the downside is that it's tiring to keep the pressure on like that, an aspect that is also rarely represented). The idea of being the first to hit, though, is sound and it's frequent to have opponents disengage in a fight and come back to a position when they're getting closer and one will attack first (although there's also the principle of not attacking first and drawing the opponent's attack in order to counter it from a more advantageous position). I guess that's what most combat turns in RPGs are supposed to represent, an exchange of blows where one is one step faster than the other.

                                      But now as to what is used to calculate initiative, it's also very vague. Initiative isn't just how fast you can move, or how long your weapon is. Those things matter, but other factors matter just as much. The idea that someone with zero dots in weaponry going up with someone with five dots can have a better initiative modifier is laughable. Training, experience, technique is key to knowing how to handle a weapon and that translate into being able to strike first. Another related but broader aspect is general fighting experience. Your prowess with weapons might not be that great, but with experience, you start picking up on signs that someone is about to attack, you start to understand how to move so that you can hit relatively safely while the opponent isn't set yet, and most importantly, you learn not to freeze when you know you're in danger. You can take a very competent amateur boxer but that one day where he's cornered in a street by a dude with a knife, he might find himself mentally unable to use any of his boxing skills. He'll be scared, he'll be afraid to move first...

                                      So all in all, I guess the current combat system is fine. It's not a proper reflection of what a fight is but it's dramatic enough that it does its job and offers some tactical possibilities (more, I feel, than first edition).

                                              As said above, there's a lot of abstractions involved in CoD's combat system. In highly abstract models, trying to define a given element, like Initiative, by determining an exact representation is counter-productive. It is doomed to lower the utility of the model itself by inducing error. Instead, it is better to focus on the relevance of the element to the model itself, and use its abstract nature to let the representations fluctuate and adapt as needed.

                                              What that means? In short, Initiative is a lot of things, there are too much little details about combat involved, and it is, ultimately, there just for balance. Because it is a dramatic game, not a model of reality, so realism isn't a thing here, but drama and game experience are. If you try to define Initiative Modifier more, you'll create a case where the system will necessarily falls back to error.

                                              But instead, lets use its abstract nature in our favor, and make this more realistic and interesting in the process.

                                              You see, literary concepts about weapons are usually flawed. A lot of weapons people think unbalanced or heavy are actually quite light and easy to handle. Readying weapons isn't a thing in the sense people think because any breach is lethal in a real combat. Well, since any real weapon (not ceremonial or ornamental ones, those always existed and were never meant for combat) is actually balanced to make the best in a fight, and this includes seemingly crude weapons, because bad weapons are discarded as such, I think we can actually use a "point-buy" system effectively. So, every true weapon fits in the system, not because the world magically balanced things, but because worst options were naturally discarded when they came up. If something remained in the "true weapon" classification, it is because something there works well enough to keep with the competition.

                                              I don't think it should be only the 2/2/2/2 distribution in place. Actualy, it is probably our bottom line: crowbars and tire irons are quite lethal, but they are [u]not[/] actual weapons in the sense of being "combat approved". So they're probably a little less functional. Most weapons would be a little more, maybe with a +1 or two thrown in the distribution, even things that don't seem good at first, like daggers or maces, but are classified as weapons (they're better than most think). On an upper level Exceptional Weapons can exist, but they're not easy to get, or they would be the new standard and that simply isn't the case (for close combat). In addition, Resources or any other Merit, alone, shouldn't be the factor to acquire Exceptional Weapons, because the weapon's race through human history always considered the relevance of equipping well enough with the resources available. It should certainly be a thing to access more options, have quicker access to them, easier maintenance, and make them better looking (true weapons can impress socially when they're finely crafted). But Exceptional Weapons are a level further in the effort to get them.

                                              Further attention is needed for Ranged Weapons. They never were, in all of human history, balanced with "melee" weapons. They always were more valued, since the first bows, but also riddled with difficulties and conundrums, and harder to use at close range. They're the weapons that you more clearly see distinctions of effectiveness, but never replaced truly close quarter weapons. Not even today.

                                                      I don't necessarily agree with the idea of a point buy system on the grounds that weapons necessarily end up relatively balanced because bad weapons are discarded. That is unless you introduce other variables. Armor-piercing is one (one which should appear more on blunt weapons)

                                                      There's a pretty fun channel on youtube called Sholagladiatoria with a guy that often takes a look at ancient weapons and talks about how they might have been used, where dhtye come from, etc. One of the leitmotifs of the channel is that weapons are tools that are made for a specific use in a specific time and in a specific culture. He argues the rapier is probaby the best sword in a 1 on 1 unarmore duel because it tends to have the longest reach while still being relatively light. Then, using a dagger or a buckler is also better because it gives you more defensive options. He also agrees that a polearm is still better anyway most of the time, again, because of the reach.

                                                      The thing about weapons being tied to a specific context is important because while in each context, poor weapons are discarded, you never really had all weapons in the world compete with each other until the modern wars, so there was no long process of trial and error between cultures. For example, Katanas are amazing craftsmanship pieces, but one of the reasons why they had to go to such length to create a sword made of different types of steels layered like that was because the quality of the steel found in Japan at the time was quite poor. There are many weapons that really only made sense on horseback and so weren't as suited to combat on foot. Maces and hammers are good weapons, but they were used to bash armors against which a sharp but light sword couldn't easily be used. Some swords lack a wide hilt which makes them very risky and prone to have your fingers cut, but that's because they were supposed to be used with shields, so on their own, they're dangerous to use. When it comes to daggers, they're not worth anything against a sword/polearm which will have a crippling reach advantage over it, but its advantage is that it can be hidden and carried much more easily (polearm were great weapons, but nobody casually carried one with them, while the sword, in many cultured was accepted in some circumstances if you had a certain social standing). They were used in war, but only as side weapons for when you lost your sword (swords break, especially in battles after repeated use) or for ranged units who didn't carry large weapons except from their bows.

                                                      Finally, yes, bad weapons were not often used, but price was also an issue. Having an army meant having as many weapons as there were soldiers. In some cultures like the middle ages after Charlemagne, war was a private affair. Local lords who later became knights were (moderately) powerful because they owned horses and a few weapons. They could afford to wage war on one another. For footsoldiers, it's more complicated. A decent weapon is still expensive, and not everyone had the military training to be able to judge whether a weapon was really well balanced or not. Of course, nobody had absurd weapons, but in many cases for those who couldn't afford them people had to make do with what they had so I'm pretty sure there were poor weapons used around.
