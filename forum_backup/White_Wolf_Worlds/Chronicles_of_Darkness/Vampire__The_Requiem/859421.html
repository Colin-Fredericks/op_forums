    What's the "point" of politics in Requiem?

    04-04-2016, 11:16 PM
    Let me preface this whole topic by saying that this is not meant as a criticism, but rather something of a thought experiment. I adore Requiem and quite like all of the myriad factions and mythology that's been built up for it so far, and have no beef with it continuing.

    What's the "point" of the large focus on elaborate factions, games of reputation and rank, and all the other politics so central to the society of the undead that Requiem revolves around?

    Vampires are, at their core, hungry monsters who were once human, and hold to that former identity with varying degrees of dedication. Most of the vampires we see in folklore, legend, and fiction are largely solitary creatures, with the most famous (Dracula) being sire to four others, none of which he seems to care terribly much for. The immediate and obvious metaphors represented by vampires - either as abusers or as addicts - don't have much related to elaborate social plays either, which has me asking the question at hand.

    The average chronicle of Vampire: the Requiem is one that deals with five globe-spanning conspiracies, each with their own beliefs and detailed hierarchies, all intermingling in a Danse Macabre that resembles nothing so much as feudal court. In order for these factions to have any meaning whatsoever (and faces to attach to their varied positions), every domain has to be stuffed with a hilarious number of undead, many of which seem to revolve around their new unholy jobs.

    I have to ask: why? Is this merely inherited from Masquerade, which (as a consequence of its ever-expanding setting via continuous supplements) had to constantly add new details to the point of absurdity? Is there some vital connection to either vampires in general or the themes of the game that this relates to that I'm simply not seeing? For other games, it makes some sense - wolves already run in packs for Werewolf, there are countless stories of wizard schools and secret societies for Mage, and even at the stranger end for Demon you still have spies being tied to wider organizations - yet I see no such connection for vampires.

    I'd love to hear everyone's answers, since this has been stuck in my brain for the last few days.

    Remi. she/her. game designer.

      Again, it's worth considering how many elaborate rules are actually in play in a given domain, and whether rules like the Traditions are really that elaborate. Further, I think if you look at the sections describing the All-Night Society in the 2e core, part of the suggestion of the rules is that it helps everyone maintain civility when they're all predators competing for the same resources. On top of that, rules are a big deal for helping you keep up with the Man. The Beast has no rules, no society. Society by Kindred is a constant, difficult test, a little battle of will between Man and Beast.

      Another thing to look at is how the 2e core describes the importance of Vampires keeping company. Without society, without the things it provides for you to do, there is just feeding, and when there's just feeding, there is only the Beast. Engaging in society is a practice for not falling into the inhuman behaviors of low-humanity creatures, which, it just so happens, often resemble many classic vampiric figures. (Banes and the job they do maintaining Humanity is also an important component of how the mechanics of Humanity reflect the narrative of such creatures.)

      I think this is the first misapprehension that fuels this issue. The Covenants are ubiquitous but not globe-spanning conspiracies. They're locally distinguished and localled focused, with few Domains having the luxury or the ability to affect things further than neighboring Domains, and while the 1e Covenant books provide the bevy of positions you're describing, there isn't a great deal of reason to think that these positions are 1. still canon or 2. universally applicable and found.

      As was stated above, part of what the Covenants do is provide things for vampires to do beyond feeding. I don't mean this on an OOC level (Though that is the case as well!) but on an IC one. Vampires who have no code that governs feeding or nothing to do beyond feeding and waiting for the next night are the ones who quickly succumb to the Beast.


      Originally posted by atamajakki View Post

      That's actually the root of many of the ridiculous titles, but rather noticeably, such titles are rarely mentioned in the 2e core, and in the case of the sample settings, is almost always replaced by a local named for the corresponding things.

      Example: Sheriff began as the title of a character in the Chicago book for VtM. It was then retroactively made the title of the chief enforcer of the Camarilla in Vampire domains in Masquerade, and then it made the leap to Requiem's 1e.

      BUT that's why these things aren't really being retained in the second edition so far.

      Originally posted by atamajakki View Post

      Given that part of your understanding of this is based in Masquerade artifacts from the first edition, you're half right. Cutting away that baggage, the All-Night Society and its rules are an attempt to make vampire society not implode in territorial infighting, and a means of maintaining Humanity with others that understand your condition.

      Originally posted by atamajakki View Post

      I'd love to hear everyone's answers, since this has been stuck in my brain for the last few days.
      A lone vampire is a crazy lunatic who either drinks people's blood in a cave or acts like a freakish count in the countryside, and both run the risk of attracting hunters, either through the lack of infrastructure to assist with their indiscretions, or through the consequences of dwindling humanity.

      Vampire society is a regulatory body, an exercise in maintaining humanity in the face of isolation and ennui, and an attempt by vampires to find meaning in their existence. All these things are pretty vitally connected to themes of Requiem + Masquerade, Old + New and Piety + Blasphemy. In fact, vampire society is kind of essential for exploring a lot of these ideas, and like the pack of werewolves or the society of wizards, are emergent from the conception of the lone wolfman or the mystical hermit, or in this case, the isolated bloodsucker.

          Well, for one thing, you're under some misconceptions. While the neofeudal court setup is popular and arguably the 'default' setting, it's not nearly the only one. Just a look at Damnation City can show you how other Princes rule and set up their courts, such as the Premier having a senate and limited democracy, the Tyrant having a cult of personality around her random whims, and the Chieftain not really allowing for any organization unless it can challenge him and his childer.

          And of course not everyone does the politicking. The clan books really get this across through many examples. The vampires who get involved in politics usually stick to it strongly, but the average vampire seems to see it as a drag and not something they want to deal with.

          As you pointed out some of the older stories involving vampires don't have them with massive vampiric societies. But the vampires do tend to have a mood of loneliness about them. So if you are not the vengeance-obsessed Dracula, and instead 'just' a normal person, how would you go about alleviating that loneliness? You can try to hang out with humans, but there's always going to be a disconnect. You can reinforce it with magic, make some ghouls, but you'll still have that difference. Just like humans do, vampires seek out other vampires for companionship. Theirs isn't a happy, saccharine or ven ideal social life, but it's better than trying to be friends with the kine.

          And whenever there's a group people, even dead ones, you're going to need some sort of governance. The Carthians might disagree, but still. It starts off small and light, just three traditions, outlining the basic 'rules' of being a vampire that neonates should know just like the living know their pleases and thank yous. But then there's a fight over a ghoul. Someone breaks the Masquerade and the two vampires there are pointing accusingly at each other. Someone has commit diablerie and he's powerful, no one wants to risk their unlife doing anything about it. Or that most egregious of transgressions, you're not like them so they don't like you. So someone decides they'll make that sacrifice. They'll make the decisions, providing others agree to follow them.

          The other titles, they expand from there, sometimes, but the smaller your domain the less you're likely to find. A small town might have a sheriff and no Prince, while New York might have a dozen sheriffs or none, instead having some sort of Lancea abbot-templar. 'Sheriffs' are the cops, the law and order part of the 'standard' court and they come in many different titles and aspects. Most of the other titles are really unofficial. A primogen is often the most powerful member of a clan, they can help you understand what your clan is all about, take responsibility for you if they feel like it, if you've got no sire. A harpy is just someone tuned into the scene, they can know what's up, from new laws and newcomers to the real juicy gossip about who's screwing who. If there's a niche, some people will want to fill it, vampire or not. Most, again, most will likely just stay out of it if they can, the way many real people do, not paying much attention to politics, occasionally breaking the law and trying to hide it, taking advantage of it when they can but otherwise just appreciating the fact that it lets them walk around without worrying about some gang of diablerists kicking in their door.

                      The way I'm playing Requiem with my players, and I'm nowhere near confident it is as intended to be played, is to experience the personal horror of the "You are the Monster" aspect of the game. There is a lot of overlap with some concepts found also in Masquerade, but all these are examined from the perspectives of the protagonists and the local ecosystem, and how both are scarred by what Vampires can become.

                      The political establishment is there as a defense mechanism of the collective undead unconscious, to regulate what sort of trauma surfaces and how it is processed in the preconscious of the All Night Society.

                      I am tempted to share my most recent work in a chronicle set in Athens, Greece, to illustrate how I probably get the game wrong. :P The basic premise is that information surfaces on a lost clan or bloodline, established by a nomad from either the Middle East or Africa, came to Greece to lay the groundwork for classical civilization, in an attempt to defend against the Strix (who had supposedly decimated the founder's original society). A Mystery Cult was formed in Athens by some who believed to have found the resting place of a Methuselah from that clan, who claim that line's legacy for Ypochreosi, after they discovered Invictus records of the ancient's rumored resting place. Rituals conducted near the tomb have been stirring the ancient, resulting in an aura which makes politics an explosive matter. Mortal politics are thus intensified inexplicably, drawing crowds to Syntagma square while troika members often leave Greece wondering why the negotiations were so tense and resulted in an uncomfortable deal for all sides with little progress in putting the country's economy back on track.

                      This kind of story is, I feel, the major selling point of Requiem over Masquerade. It shows how an entire city has been scarred by events and supernatural energies beyond the control even of the protagonists of those events. It gives you the tools, and allows for new tools to be developed by STs, to create unique monsters of intense drama and horror.

                                  Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
                                  What's the "point" of the large focus on elaborate factions, games of reputation and rank, and all the other politics so central to the society of the undead that Requiem revolves around?
                                  To give you something to do. And I'm not just talking about as players. I mean, from an in game perspective, individual vampires have all these factions and games and ranks because, frankly speaking, they're bored.

                                  Vampire is the closest thing to a game line about immortals we have at the moment. Mummies "live" just as long, but they're asleep for most of it, actually being alive only for a small portion of the time. Prometheans and Unchained demons theoretically can live forever, but their goals (New Dawn, Hell) tend to come with a large turnover / mortality rate. Other splats can extend their lives, but vampire-length lives seems to be the exception rather than rule. That means that, unlike any other game out there for the CofD, Vampire revolves around a very simple question - "What will you do from night to night?" Ennui is a very real threat to Kindred. When you're a neonate, scrambling for blood and survival, the night is pretty darn busy. Once you establish yourself? Nice haven, stable herd and feeding ground, and you catch up on all your reading and Netflix? What's next? Humans, running about their short lives with their families and morgages, simply have different concerns than a vampire. The vampires can't simply take off for a road trip to see Paris or Old Faithful - daylight restrictions. So, they make friends, they try play nice with people that understand them. They find vampire hobbies to fill their time. They take up causes. And those causes clash. The Invictus and Carthians are diametrically opposed for a reason - to create drama. Same with Lance and Crone. The Ordo Dracul is the Wild Card, who side with one or another depending on the current needs of their cult.

                                  And that's just assuming vampires are acting like humans with just a very long life span. There's also the part where vampires undergo a spiritual transformation. Their base natures have been amplified - vampires are routinely called things like "embodiment of sin" in the core book for a reason. Sexual deviance, the thrill of the hunt, dominance and power, seflishness, an urge to violence, all the ugly parts of humanity become more prominant in vampires. And vampires are always, always hungry for more. Combine that with the daylight traveling restrictions and the need for feeding grounds - no traveling the world for the average vampire. They're effectively trapped in their cities. So, you're someone with the potential to turn into a complete psychopath, bored out of your mind, and the only ones who really -get- you are other territorial and selfish vampires.

                                  In theory, its possible that vampires could reach an equalibrium, make a society that's both entertaining and balanced. But then, we get the VII and Strix, who very much are designed to upset the status quo and keep pushing vampires to extremes and keep them from settling.

                                  The average chronicle of Vampire: the Requiem is one that deals with five globe-spanning conspiracies, each with their own beliefs and detailed hierarchies, all intermingling in a Danse Macabre that resembles nothing so much as feudal court.
                                  Western vampires. After Rome fell, the Invictus and Lance were pretty much the only game in town. They're the ones who made the vampire society like a feudal court. It needs to be noted that in cities where the Invictus does not rule, vampire society does NOT resemble a fuedal court. The Invictus in power tends to be the default, yes, just as vampires in America or Europe tend to be the default setting. But there are variations at play here that differentiate it. Other cities, especially in non-Western cultures, don't follow the same model.

                                  This is rather important to note, because for those vampires who DON'T want to live in a feudal society? Namely everyone but the Invictus and their oftentimes-allies in the Lance? Are doing their best to bring the system down and replace it.

                                  Originally posted by Nyx View Post
                                  There's an old analogy about being Kindred and working in a corporate system.

                                  You work long hours for little relief, and your coworkers suck. You get promoted by stepping on, betraying, killing and manipulating those around and above you. Your evil, demanding boss is your boss forever, unless something happens to them. And then a new generation of hires come in, and you're in the way of their promotion.

                                  Play politics or get ashed.
                                  This is a great description of the Invictus, I'd say. I can't say that I think the same description applies to the other four core book covenants, though. Neither the Carthian Movement nor the Circle have a hierarchy that would work like this, the Ordo system is based on Coils known, oaths sworn, and access to Wyrm Nests and the Lancea et Sanctum, while definitely hierarchal, aren't necessarily power hungry to the point that they'll look to constantly unseat you. The Lance cares about Theban facility, service as a priest or paladin, or some other spiritual function. Sure, there's some friction there, but its generally going to spawn from theological differences rather than coveting power and position for its own sake.

                                  The Invictus all care about power. Its literally one of the two defining features (the other being the self-entitled keepers of the Masquerade) of the covenant. So, yeah, you're a pool of hungry sharks there. But the other four? Not as much. In fact, its common knowledge that trying to reach positions of power in the Movement or Circle might get you attacked from your own allies.
