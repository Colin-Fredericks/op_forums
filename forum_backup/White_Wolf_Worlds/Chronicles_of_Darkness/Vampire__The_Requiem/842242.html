                  A lot of the zoomed-in, local level personal horror and individual drama isn't stuff that doesn't happen in Masquerade, simply stuff that is emphasized a little more and, with 2e Requiem, highlighted with mechanics here and there like Touchstones. (The 2e corebook in general is rather more confident about Requiem's distinct identity, having benefitted from years of supplements and different writers exploring and fleshing out that setting.)

                  The clanbooks are good. They're packed with little snapshots of interesting stuff you could focus a chronicle around. You'll have to do the heavy lifting, though.

                  I'd say one of the tonal differences between Masquerade and Requiem that you could capitalize on is the fact that Requiem more firmly exists in a world where supernatural strangeness is prevalent, unpredictable and poorly understood. Masquerade, especially when played by longtime veterans, tends to have a feel that while vampires' unlives may be complicated by alien monsters with whom they have to share the Earth, those monsters are somewhat defined and even navigable. Ventrue capitalists who deal in a built up Pentex company town are eventually going to learn at least something about banes, Black Spiral Dancers or Pentex First Teams, and if they cross state lines to a neighboring domain, that knowledge will relatively hold. Requiem has more in the way of outside-context problems and miscellaneous strangeness. There's shit in the Chronicles of Darkness that none of the big name gamelines have a firm grip on. Tumorous growths feeding at the bottom of hydroelectric dams. Magical phrases that recur and foresage doom. Lakes that hate. Requiem politics often are thrown bizarre curveballs and then prove themselves by how they adapt to and capitalize on them (or how they fail to do so). If you're short on ideas for the uncanny and bizarre, browse urban legends on Snopes or conspiracy theories and UFO experiences on forum sites like AboveTopSecret. The Chronicles of Darkness take place in a world where some of these are roughly accurate.

                      Just emphasizing how the covenants are should help. Don't go with a standard Invictus Prince and choose something more out there, like the Carthian Premier or the Crone Oracle. You could play off the clans, how they are not nearly as tight knight and insular as they were in Masquerade. You could show how a particularly skilled and disciplined neonate or ancilla could easily 'take out' a vampire hundreds and even thousands of years older than them. The Ancient Mysteries book has a number of really old vampires that are comparable to neonates in 'power,' just having a vampire who was around in the days of ancient Egypt but still has the same amount of disciplines and abilities as the players could really show off the difference.

                      You also have the strix. And werewolves don't harbor any in-born animosity towards vampires, while some still hunt vampires it's not a given for all werewolves, and some even have vampires in their packs. Offering options for bloodlines might go a long way, too, as they were very different in Masquerade.

                              Originally posted by dman1123 View Post
                              I'd also prefer that this campaign go less like the last one I was part of, where it was kick in the door and stake the bloodsuckers that you didn't like.
                              Combine carrots with sticks.

                              The stick is easy: emphasize consequences for the blunt approach, not just in terms of making vampiric enemies but in leaving a trail behind. The police are going to stake you out for attempted murder even if they don't realize you're vulnerable to a literal stake. Put disappearances on the front page of the local paper and watch the kine get skittish and make you work for every meal.

                              The carrot is harder but probably more important than the stick. Give them a reason to actively want to spend time peacefully, not just a reason to avoid firefights. Really use those Touchstones. Find out what your players want their characters to value and what they find entertaining and likeable, and give them that, no strings attached. Likeable, funny, helpful NPCs, who aren't there just to be endangered and snatched away... except by the PCs themselves. Never put them in your crosshairs unprovoked, but leave them vulnerable to fallout. "Vulnerable" includes emotionally vulnerable, too: if you manage to endear them to their allies well enough, just the idea that their allies might be shocked or disgusted by certain actions might carry water.

                              "Make likeable characters" is easier said than done, more an art than a science. But if you just use the stick, players who like violent solutions aren't going to get more peaceful. They're just going to make sure they're ready to cut a bloody swath out of a crowd for an escape route at a moment's notice.

                              More important than all of that, though, is the direct approach: Talk to your players and make explicitly sure they understand that you're hoping for a game where murder isn't the first recourse. Communicate. Make sure they're on board with it before they even make characters. If your players don't want to play along then you can't make them and you'll just make everybody miserable trying. The carrot and the stick are just there to make it easier for them to play along so it goes smoothly.
