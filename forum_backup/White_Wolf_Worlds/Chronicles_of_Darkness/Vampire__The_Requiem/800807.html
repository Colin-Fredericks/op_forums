                  I love using crossover elements in games, but only sparingly. Games where the players are all playing characters from different game lines almost never work out in my experience. It dilutes the themes of each game line and tends to require huge contrivances to get the player characters to work together. On the other hand, whenever I run (for example) a Vampire game, I like to have other supernaturals in the city, with their own problems and agendas, that can end up interacting with the player characters in various ways.

                      I ran a game that was primarily Changeling for more or less two years. We also had a Mage (and a second, briefly), a custom Changing Breed, and a Sin-Eater.

                      I focused mostly on Blue Book threats, but I tried to spotlight each splat in whatever way we could. A trip to the Underworld. A trip to the Hedge. Tracking down rogue mages. That sort of thing.

                      It went pretty great, but you do need to put restrictions. Putting a Vampire in a party with a Geist would have been unwise, for example. The biggest problem was making sure everyone had ways to refill their various energy stats.

                          I'm currently running a mortals --> crossover game, and I think it's going quite well. In a crossover game, it's less important to stick to the general themes of the specific gamelines for each character (eg retaining your humanity for vampires, obsession over mysteries for mage, etc) and more important to find a central theme to tie all the characters together. Maybe all the characters are discovering who they truly are, or are all looking for something in common.

                          Another thing that can help is keeping the game going between sessions. I often run solo scenes with my players via texts over the week, saving the group sessions for group scenes if I can. This can keep solo themes running for each character alongside the chronicle spanning themes.

                          Finally, make sure you have a hand in character building, or have everyone discuss their ideas openly. While your party probably won't be balanced, you can at least try to ensure that no one is overshadowed, which can easily happen in a crossover game. If you have (for example) a werewolf built for social skills and a gangrel built for combat, there's going to be jealousy, because the wolf will still likely be better at combat. Every line has things that they are just better at, so try to get players to embrace that rather than trying to play across type.

                                  I don´t like to run full crossovers because each CofD game is deep and good enough for it´s own merits. If you mix PCs from different gamelines, you will not have a better game, you will get a more diluted one. In example, if you narrate a hunting scene, or a mage´s astral travel you will alienate other players. So you must put aside those scenes to keep the action going for everybody. It´s like having 3 small childs looking for your atention at the same time.
                                  If you use other supernaturals as NPCs, the things can work better because you have 100% control over them. In this case I just recommend to adapt the system to the game you´re runing. In example, If you´re running vampire game and a wild mage appears, don't use the full Mage the Awakening rules for Magic, just decide what the mage can do and throw some dice pools for it. It will save you A LOT of time.

                                  I know crossovers are popular, but for me are better in theory than in actual gameplay.

                                              Yes I agree with everyone that it's an extremely difficult thing to do and will in all likelihood fail. However, just remember that a failed gaming session isn't really all that bad a thing. Sometime's it's worth taking that risk to try something fun and innovate a bit with your storytelling. Just adding that in for perspective.

                                              That said, it's a very difficult challenge. Leaving aside the difficulties of the different themes and everything, just from a rules mastery perspective it can be challenging. It's hard enough to answer a question for a Mage player about the difference between a Vulgar and a Covert spell, but then if you're immediately asked a question by a Changeling player about how to access the Hedge through a Hollow it's even more difficult. It's hard to reference that many books so quickly so you'll probably have to memorize everything. Then you're also bound to get cross-over questions like whether Supernal magic is Vulgar inside the Hedge, and that's not easy to figure out or look up in the middle of a session.

                                              Right now is a really difficult time to do cross-over games as well. Half of the games are still only available in 1st Edition rules. Which means either you use 1st Edition rules for everything, tough if like me you've fallen in love with the 2nd Edition rules, or you try to update the rules yourself, or you run a hybrid game where not everyone is even following the same base system.

Still, I'm confident now that if a player decides to start looking into vampires I'll have that information close to hand.

                                                  Crossover games work exceedingly well when your players aren't actually interested in a gameline's theme and/or mood, but really enjoy the mechanics or the aesthetics or some combination of both. For example, if you have a player that wants to be a Daeva, be really good at social and have a lot of social merits, but isn't interested in Kindred politics or the struggle to retain humanity, he's going to fit right in a crossover, because what the player wants is to have her handsome vampire do social stuff. So long as you make sure the player gets what she wants out of her character, they'll be happy.

                                                  Then, all you need to do is come up with an overarching plot that ties those characters together, whether because their territories/power bases/loved ones are in danger, or because they know each other from back when they were humans, or because some external force recruits them for a cause.

                                                  For characters that are really into the gameline's theme and mood, you can simply give them regular times to explore them. It's in general a good ST practice to make sure each player gets their turn in the spotlight, so what better place to explore the gameline's theme and mood?
