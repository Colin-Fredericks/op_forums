    A Muslim Covenant

    08-23-2015, 02:13 AM
    However one interprets the Lancea et Sanctum, the fact remains that it’s grounded in a distinctively Christian theology. Given that Islam is the second largest religion in the world, it seems appropriate for there to be a covenant that’s equally grounded in (and equally distinct from) Islam. Here’s my attempt at putting together something like that; it's pretty rough, but let me know what you think!

    (Note: I haven’t come up with an appropriate name for the covenant; where it would appear in the text, I’ve currently got a bunch of XXXs)

    A Little Background

    The Kindred of what would become the Muslim world were, on the whole, slower to adopt Islam than were their mortal counterparts. Before the establishment of the Caliphate, the most significant faith among the vampires of the region was Longinian Christianity, which was prevalent throughout the Eastern Roman Empire, particularly in Egypt and the Greek cultural sphere. In addition, many vampires in Mesopotamia continued to practice truly ancient rites in honor of deities all but forgotten by the living, and throughout the region scattered sects of blood-drenched celebrants embraced deviant creeds that rejected all human morality.

    As Islam gained ground among the cities of north Africa and southwest Asia, however, more and more members of the new religion were embraced. Those neonates who held firm to their faith were driven to the edge of despair, however, for the mockery of life into which they had been thrust seemed incompatible with the strictures of their religion. Relief came during the 9th century, in the form of the Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn al-Dūda.

    The Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn al-Dūda is a short collection of ʾaḥādīth attributed to an eponymous kindred known only as Ibn al-Dūda, the “Son of the Worm.” It is, to say the least, an unconventional example of its genre, particularly in that the author of the text claims to have been embraced some time before Muhammad experienced his first revelation, and that what he has recorded consists solely of his own first-hand experiences in the company of the Prophet. Mortal Muslims of any major sect would almost certainly reject the ḥādīth of the Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn al-Dūda as mawḍūʻ, fabrications, but among the Muslim kindred of the XXX it is held in the highest regard.

    The Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn al-Dūda reports that Muhammad preached not only to the living, but to the kindred as well. As it is the will of Allah that vampires remain hidden from mortals, Muhammad never revealed his dealings to the main body of the kindred. The vampires of the Prophet’s time were proud and stubborn, and of them only Ibn al-Dūda paid his words any heed, dutifully recording them in preparation for the day when his kindred would be ready to accept their truth.

    Arkān al-Dīn: The Pillars of the Faith

    The Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn al-Dūda affirms that Allah has prescribed a sharīʿah for the kindred, just as he has for all of the other creatures in His creation. Just as the kindred resemble mortal human beings in many ways, the sharīʿah of the kindred bears many similarities to the sharīʿah of living Muslims. That said, the truth of the matter is that the kindred are not human, and the duties that Allah has set before them are different from those which He has given to humanity.

    Shahāda: Testimony

    Lā ʾilāha ʾillā-llāhu muḥammadun rasūlu-llāh - “there is no god but God, and Muhammad is the messenger of God.” This statement summarizes the core of the Muslim faith, and It is essential for both mortals and kindred to utter it in order to become Muslims and to practice Islam.

    Ṣalāh: Prayer

    Because Allah has decreed that the kindred shall rise only at night, and because a public call to prayer would run counter to the secrecy of the kindred, Allah has prescribed a somewhat reduced schedule of prayers for the kindred. Kindred are expected to perform the ṣalāt al-maġrib, the evening prayer, upon arising for the night, and the ṣalāt al-fajr, the dawn prayer, before return to daysleep. The ṣalāt al-ʿišāʾ, the nighttime prayer, is ideally performed after the vampire first feeds during the night, but may otherwise take place any time between the other two prayers.

    The kindred are expected to pray in the same manner as their mortal counterparts, and must likewise perform al-wuḍūʼ, ablution, before each prayer. However, Allah has decreed that the living and the dead shall not worship together, and the kindred are forbidden from praying at mortal mosques. The vampires of some domains have constructed their own, hidden mosques for their nightly prayers, but most of the XXX simply pray wherever it is convenient.

    Zakāh: Charity

    Among mortal Muslims, zakāh, literally “that which purifies,” refers to the practice of alms-giving, which is considered a way to purify one's income and wealth from sometimes worldly, impure way of acquisition. While vampires who continue to accumulate wealth after the embrace are required to continue paying zakāh as they did when mortal, vampires who control territory and herds are expected to provide for their fellow kindred as well.

    In all domains where the XXX have influence, an area is set aside for the use of the “poor and needy” - those who have no other means of feeding themselves. In some domains, vampires with extensive herds may even make some of their vessels available to support those in need. These resources are generally only available to muslim vampires, and sometimes only to those who have sworn allegiance to the covenant. In addition to being a divinely mandated moral duty, zakāh has the added benefit of reducing the number of hungry vampires in the domain, which reduces the incidence of frenzy and thereby helps preserve the secrecy of the kindred.

    Ṣawm: Abstaining

    Among mortal Muslims, Ṣawm, meaning “to abstain,” refers to fasting, particularly during the holy month of Ramadan. Among the vampires of the XXX, it refers specifically to an injunction against feeding on one’s kindred. In particular, the Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn al-Dūda condemns the practice of diablerie, but many Muslim kindred believe that this prohibition extends to forced blood bonds and “perversion” as well.

    Ḥajj: Pilgrimage

    It is not the will of Allah that kindred should travel far from the places of their embrace, save under the most dire of circumstances. This is one reason why he has made them vulnerable to sunlight, so that it is difficult for them to travel anywhere that cannot be reached in a single night. Thus, the kindred are not required to engage in a physical pilgrimage to Mecca, as mortal Muslims are.

    The Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn al-Dūda uses the term Hajj in an allegorical sense, to refer to the often lonely journey of the kindred through an eternity of night. Much as the Druze interpret Hajj to refer to "fleeing from devils and oppressors," the kindred of the XXX use it to refer to the necessity of turning away from one’s mortal life and passing into the Requiem. It is right that mortals should not know of the vampires in their midst, and that the two societies should remain ever separate. Open interaction between the two could only result in conflict, and great suffering on both sides.

    In addition to this usage, the Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn al-Dūda makes it clear that kindred are to respect those mortals engaged in the pilgrimage to Mecca, and to refrain from feeding from them or impeding their progress in any way. Most leaders of the XXX go further than this, taking it upon themselves to protect pilgrims from other predatory denizens of the World of Darkness where they are able. The kindred who dwell in the holy city itself are particularly hostile to non-Muslim vampires for fear that they may prey on those coming to city, and maintain a constant vigil for such “poachers” during Hajj season.

                      Well, this is generally supposed to be just the bare bones. I imagine that the covenant has a fair number of similarities with the L+S, though that doesn't come across all that well here. Predestination (al-qadar) is a fundamental part of the Muslim faith, and this belief lends itself to the idea that vampires have some kind of divine mandate that justifies what they do. They would have some kind of proprietary advantage similar to the other covenants... possibly Theban Sorcery, particularly considering that the Muslim world encompasses the region where it originated. They'd probably describe their blood sorcery it in terms of Barakah, blessings or divine power, which flows from God and allows those to which it has been given to perform miracles.

                      As for jinn, in Islam they're believed to have free will just like humans. They can be evil, but they can also be good Muslims. Some are believed to have the ability to possess people (at least in parts of the Muslim world), and Iblis was a jinnī in the early history of the world who disobeyed God and subsequently became the rough equivalent of the Christian devil. He and his minions whisper to people and tempt them to evil, but that's a choice they've made rather than something that they're compelled to do. Most jinn keep to their own, invisible communities in remote areas and don't interact much with people.

                      Incidentally, as you may have noticed, predestination and free-will are both part of Islamic thought, and are considered to be compatible with one another. It's not all that different, I don't think, from the idea in many types of Christianity that people are responsible for their own choices, but that God already knows what those choices are going to be. Naturally, there has been a fair amount of discussion of the problem of evil in Islamic philosophy.

                              I dunno; I feel like the distinction is somewhat artificial. The Lancea et Sanctum is basically built on a core of Christian beliefs (the Crucifixion and Resurrection, Sin and Hell, the Eucharist, the second coming and the final Judgement, etc.) with a number of noteworthy deviations. These deviations are justified by the Sanctified's claim that they have a unique holy book (the Testament of Longinus), which, among other things, provides instructions on how to be a Christian vampire. This book is nevertheless broadly compatible with, and (as far as I can tell) used alongside, the Bible; it even contains direct quotations of Bible passages.

                              My Muslim vampire group has a unifying philosophy built on a core of Muslim beliefs (thus far described in terms of the "Pillars of Islam," though obviously there's more to it than that), with a number of noteworthy deviations. These deviations are justified by their claim that they have a unique holy book (the Ṣaḥīḥ Ibn al-Dūda), which, among other things, provides instructions on how to be a Muslim vampire. This book is nevertheless broadly compatible with, and used alongside, the Koran, and it probably contains direct quotations or references to Koranic passages.

                              As for the Sanctified being the priests of their religion, that's not unambiguous. Their write-up in Blood and Smoke makes it seem that way, but the "Anointed" merit explicitly states "Not all Sanctified are members of the clergy. Most are lay members." These two sections aren't necessarily contradictory; in many forms of Christianity, laypeople can hold positions in the church hierarchy or otherwise take on specific religious roles. For instance, most monks and all nuns are laypeople, in the sense that they haven't been ordained as priests.

                              In any event, while Islam doesn't have priests in the sense of religious specialists who interact with God on behalf of the faithful, it certainly does have clergy. Most of the religious titles in Islam are scholastic, rather than authoritarian, recognizing one of the faithful for their knowledge of the theory and practice of ad-dín (religion). However, there are, in most branches of Islam, specialists who have made a career of studying and administration of Islamic law, and there are additionally congregational leaders who regularly preach sermons and instruct the faithful on the principals of the religion. In essence, you could have a Muslim religious covenant that wasn't too fundamentally different from the Sanctified, who are, after all, described as often being scholars and historians, in addition to being priests. There would be considerable differences in the details of the organization, of course, but that's why you'd want a separate covenant, as opposed to just having a Muslim congregation of Sanctified.

                              As far as hierarchy goes, it's true that the structure of a Muslim covenant would presumably be less hierarchical than most incarnations of the Lance. But we have examples of covenants that are less hierarchical, so I don't see that as a huge problem.

                                                      Honestly, I kind of feel like it wouldn't do justice to the diversity of cultures in the world to simply take the existing covenant archetypes and add a little local flavor. I think that parallels are great, but there's no reason for there to always be five specific kinds of power bloc wherever there are vampires. In the case of the Muslim world, I don't really think there's a compelling reason to have an "Islamic Invictus" as an entirely separate group from an "Islamic Lancea et Sanctum." If the al-Amin are scholars of Islamic law, what do the Banu Shaitan even do, really? As has been mentioned, Islam doesn't really have priests, per se, and the religious leaders they do have tend to be scholars of Islamic law, in addition to philosophy and ethics (since the three subjects are closely related, particularly in Islamic thought).

                                                      There would certainly be more than one Covenant in the Muslim world. In addition to the Circle of the Crone, the Carthian Movement (who have probably tried to take advantage of the the Arab Spring to make a power play), and the Lancea et Sanctum (who are probably still holding on even in areas that have very few Christians these days), I could see there being a home-grown spin on the Ordo Dracul, particularly since there are rumors the the Mae-Ji, who founded a similar (though explicitly Buddhist) group in Thailand, disappeared after traveling west. I think that was intended to leave open the possibility that Dracula may have actually met and learned from her, but if so she would have passed through a whole lot of Muslim territory.

                                                      You might also have some sort of Sufi-inspired, mystically-oriented group like the Sufis, who might practice some kind of blood magic of their own.

                                                      If I may digress for a moment, I am of the opinion that the Carthian Movement and the Circle of the Crone should be among the most omnipresent of the covenants, even outside of places directly colonized by Europeans. The Circle of the Crone have always been presented, as far as I can tell, as more or less an alliance of convenience, made up of radically different religious groups united mostly by the fact that they're relatively small and opposed by larger, more monolithic religious movements. The Carthians, on the other hand, probably recruit anywhere that political ideologies like democracy and communism have taken root, to whatever degree. That includes more or less the entire world at this point, since these ideologies have been among Europe's most successful exports.

                                                      Anyway, I do agree in principal that "Muslim vampires" is too broad a concept for a single covenant. You can be Muslim and belong to lots of different kinds of organizations. I've tried to narrow the focus of the covenant to a certain extent by placing an emphasis on interpreting Islamic law for vampires, and by giving them a specific historical origin, their own hadith, and so on, in much the same way that the history and unique dogma of the Lancea et Sanctum prevents them from simply being "Christian vampires."

                                                      Sorry if that came off as kind of rambling, or as defensive. I do appreciate your feedback, and I'm really tired, so it might not be the best time to be writing a response.
