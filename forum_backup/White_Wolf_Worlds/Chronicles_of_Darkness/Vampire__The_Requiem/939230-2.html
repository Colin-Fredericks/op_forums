                As the player of the character and event that sparked this whole discussion I feel it's pertinent to quote this piece right here:

                VTR 2E 91: "Every step she takes, every corner she turns, a Kindred's Beast stands ready to face threats, to defend territory, and most importantly, to feed. To the Beast, everything is a challenge, a potential conquest, a meal, or a combination of the three. To other predators, the Beast is a palpable thing, something that identifies another monster. This presence, the predatory aura, builds a paradox in Kindred society. On one hand, it guarantees vampires will never be civil, as all remain one misstep away from each other's throats. On the other hand, it forces Kindred to hold tight to traditions and propriety, in order to keep the Beast at bay and force a modicum of nicety."

                Let's not forget what we're playing. monsters, always lurking under the surface, always ready to compete and fight, fuck, feed, or flee. While I agree that calling for a frenzy roll for every particular provocation is hardly good or effective role-play, there is absolutely nothing in the first 2 paragraphs (of Ride the Wave) that requires the player to have a provocation. The rule states a WP must be spent, and dice must be rolled. The question that should be asked is "what modifiers do we place on the roll?" Minor wound? +1. Party members dying?? -1 depending.

                Provocation's mention in paragraph three is a comparative reference to the fact that under normal Frenzy circumstances a character does not choose the target, nor the desire, it is not a statement of requirement to initiate the task.

                The resistance mentioned in paragraph two is both comparative to the fact that it is the same type of roll as a Resistance roll but the second portion clarifies how they function differently. It is also indicative of the fact that from the moment you choose to spend a Willpower point to initiate a Ride the Wave, you WILL frenzy. It becomes a question of what kind of frenzy, not IF you frenzy. Your resistance roll at that point is to see if you can keep ahold of the leash and your footing, after issuing a Kill command.

                Furthermore, I don't need a lesson in the fact that 2E is different than 1E, but 1E DID have NO requirement of provocation to initiate a Ride the Wave, and neither did Dark Ages: Vampire. I can't seem to find it in an edition of VtM that I own, even mentioned as an option, I'm sure it's under Path of the Beast in a splat book I don't feel like digging for.

                Some things do change, and some rolls get modified. But the Beast is still the Beast, and not the Cuddle-Bear.

                And for anyone curious, in the third round, I did lose my shit and resume Normal Frenzy.

                            I've mostly been trying to stay out of this due to not wanting to rehash an old argument, but this has been bugging me for a while;

                            I mentioned something related to the lack of target for you to change when you Ride the Wave without provocation.

                            The response obtained was "you pick the provocation when you start trying to ride the wave," paraphrased.

                            And then you get to change it if you succeed at riding the wave? How does that make sense?

                            More importantly, that also means that you get to pick your frenzy target whether or not you succeed at riding the wave.

                            Does that really seem like the intent? Anyone can frenzy at any time and set their goal and target freely, without needing to succeed at a roll, without even needing Coil of the Wyrm?

                            If you attempt to Ride the Wave and pick your target/goal and then just... stop spending the Willpower required to keep rolling so that you deliberately fail, how is that any different from the Coil of the Wyrm that people were claiming is so much better than normal Ride the Wave? Because that's exactly what it lets you do. Start a frenzy with a target/goal of your choosing.

                            I don't think "well you don't get to pick a target/goal until you succeed" is a valid option, either. Because then what if you fail? Does the ST just pick a random goal/target?

                            I kind of think "does this interpretation follow logically and consistently" should be reason enough to accept or dismiss an interpretation of the rules. This "you pick" counter-argument actually makes me think a strong "no" on that front, in addition to the support the text gave in the quotes I provided earlier. In my opinion, at least.

                            And again:

                            This desire does not have to align with the
                            provocation. For example, if Victoria risks frenzy because she’s
                            starved, she might choose to kill Dominic instead of feeding.
                            "The provocation." There is a provocation. It doesn't give any indication that a provocation is optional. "The provocation" gives a contrary indication; that there has to be one. It doesn't say "does not have to align with any potential provocations" or "this desire can be anything the Kindred chooses" thereby removing the implication that there has to be a provocation.

                            Can someone provide an explanation for the provocation issue, supported by the text rather than conjecture or a previous, outdated edition of the game?

                            I do grant CrimsonOwl's quote does provide some support for the point. I've always had a problem with that paragraph of the book, though. It directly contradicts everything before and after it, and is a pretty direct reading of 1e Requiem that doesn't take into account the fact that Vampires aren't always one step away from each others' throats anymore, with the changes to predatory aura. Feels really out of place in context.

                            On that note, VtM allows Instinct rolls to Ride the Wave if you are on a Path that replaces Self-Control with Instinct. Characters with Instinct cannot even resist frenzy at all, unless the difficulty is below their Instinct, in which case they can resist without rolling but can't Ride the Wave. Characters with Self-Control can spend a willpower, I believe, to redirect their action for a turn. The game only calls it "ride the wave" explicitly for rolling Instinct during an existing Frenzy. I'm not sure what you're talking about with Dark Ages. Admittedly, I may be missing something. VtM isn't my area of expertise. But even with these rules supporting my point, I think it's really silly to base this argument on previous editions of VtR, and I think it's even sillier to base this argument on an entirely different game line that happens to be about vampires but otherwise has different themes, design philosophies, and mechanics.

                                                    Yeah, I don't think a poll would help anyone. If you're the sort who thinks at-will Riding the Wave makes no sense, seeing that 75% of the people on these forums disagree with you isn't going to make it make sense for you. If you're the sort who thinks at-will Riding the Wave is the more logical interpretation, seeing that 75% of the people on these forums think it doesn't make sense probably won't change your mind. If you're the sort who can see it working either way and just wants clarification of developer intent, you probably aren't going to care what the popular interpretation is. Discussion may not get the staunch supporters of either side to change their mind, but it can help sway someone who is on the fence one way or the other. I'd prefer to talk out the reasoning behind our interpretations and let people make an informed decision. If Rose wants to weigh in on the discussion, great! That's certainly very informative and should go a long way towards helping people decide if the intended functionality works for their group's needs or not. A poll only tells you what opinion is most popular among the people who decide to participate in the poll.

                                                    All that said, I would be remiss not to point out that we do have a poll function. When you start a new thread, just hit the little graph icon at the top of the text box.                                                         Originally posted by Tabanese View Post
                                                        My suggestion was that the forum users not stop at Author Intent.
                                                        For what it's worth--and I'm just one writer and one who wrote for VtM, not VtR, so maybe it shouldn't be worth all that much--I seriously agree. I've written this before, but I really, really believe that author's intent does not matter once the manuscript is out there. Y'all are very fortunate--and so are we, as writers--that we have a forum like this where we can answer and clarify things. When you're actually reading the book, I can't be over your shoulder saying, "actually, what I MEANT is..."

                                                        Our work has to speak for itself. If it can't speak for itself, and if there's any grey area for something we did not intend to leave unclear, that's our fault, and we haven't done our job. Communication goes two ways. If you are putting forward a good-faith reading of my work and there's still grey for something that should be hard and clear, that's my fault as a writer.

                                                        Just my opinion.

                                                        I hope Rose does respond, if she wants to. I've become a lot more active on these forums myself since my work's been published, but that's my choice, and, again, it's a privilege we both have--readers get to ask us for clarification, and we get to clarify. But that's really a shift in practice. When it comes to texts of any sort (books, film, academic lectures, drawings), author's intent does not matter. What matters is what's on the page.
