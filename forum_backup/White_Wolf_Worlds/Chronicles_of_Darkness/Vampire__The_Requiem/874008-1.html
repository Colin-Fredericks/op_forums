                            Originally posted by VtR 2e FAQ
                            What happens if a vampire feeds on an intoxicated mortal?

                            The vampire suffers the effects of the drug. The vampire may remove these effects by spending one Vitae, but could conceivably be too intoxicated to concentrate enough to do so.
                            Poisons not specifically harmful to Kindred follow the same rules as drugs. A Storyteller may choose to make an exception for poisons which break down cellular structure, but that’s at her own discretion.

                            regarding poison.

                            In short, Vampires are only affected by poisons if they feed from a poisoned human. The only exception is Storyteller's decision, not a standard rule.

                                                            04-27-2016, 10:01 AM
                                                            Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
                                                            2e changed the default assumptions about vampire physiology a bit, so it taking something that was clearly left out of 2e from 1e does seem to be pushing the "far fetched" line a bit.
                                                            Actually Occam's Razor would be a contra-indication that this is the case. A bird in hand is worth two in the bush afterall.

                                                            Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
                                                            You can't actually categorically make these distinctions, plenty of substances are dangerous or living and non-living things; especially in a narrowing comparison of living vs non-living organic matter. There are plenty of "poisons" that would damage the physical structure of a corpse.

                                                            The difference between a poison and a toxin is not what it does (or what it affects are) but how it is made. Poisons are manufactured artificially, while toxins (which includes venom) are found naturally occurring in organisms.
                                                            Actually you're just straight up wrong. Toxins include any chemical substance that in a sufficient quantity can cause damage to a biological creature's biochemical processes. These vary by creature and concentration. For example, chocolate is a deadly toxin for dogs and cats (known to cause acute kidney failure). In sufficient quantities caffeine is a deadly toxin to humans (consuming 2 entire carafes of espresso in the space of a single hour will give you a large enough dose to cause fatal cardiac arrhythmia--its essentially 2000+ grams of caffeine).

                                                            The difference between poisons and toxins is not, as you suggest, how they are made. Toxin is a descriptor for chemicals that can cause poisoning (both acute and over time) and more subtle damage to metabolic, nervous, and/or genetic processes. There are many poisons that occur naturally. Some examples that you haven't thought about -- Lead, Copper, Iron. The skin oils of the Poison Dart Frog. Holly Berries. Nightshade. Toadstools. Etc.

                                                            Venom is different from poison in that the mode of entry into the body is not either a) ingestion through the digestive tract or b) absorption through the skin. Venom must be injected to be effective. Some venoms are also poisons (spitting cobra venom in particular).

                                                            To sum up, all poisons and venoms are toxins but not all toxins are poisons and venoms. Word to the wise, anything in sufficient quantity can be toxic.

                                                            Regarding the whole acidity debate. Many poisons and venoms (especially) are acidic because the biological processes of the creatures that produce them are also acid-based. For instance your blood is quite acidic and a good thing too otherwise your heart wouldn't beat. Your entire nervous system is essentially one big salt-acid battery.

                                                            That being said, the most toxic naturally occurring poisons that are often deadly to humans are alkaloid poisons. These usually occur in fungi and plants.

                                                            Regarding how this all affects vampires, I'd say that the undead are pretty much immune to most poisons because they don't really have metabolic or genetic processes unless they want to. At best, the presence of poison will make it more difficult (or perhaps impossible) to activate the Blush of Life effect. I would say though, as in all things, storytellers have more than enough space to play it as complex or as simple as the needs of their story and troupe dictate.

                                                            Finally, regarding the question in the OP. Like toxins, the harm caused by radiation depends on its concentration. IMO, vampires are probably partially immune to alpha and beta particles (downgrading any damage just like being exposed to acid or what not). Gamma radiation in sufficient concentration (like at the heart of a nuclear blast or inside an operating reactor) is probably going to be as effective as bright sunlight on them. Afterall, it must be some quality that sunlight has that iridescent and florescent lights don't have that serves as the bane.
