            Retainers, Herd, staff. Domain. It's all about domain and what's "yours" within it. Which circles back to the core concept of the vampire as a hidden predator and why the books focus on city politics as being centered around domain squabbles or influence plays (to gain control over domain).

            It's a surprisingly tight thematic bundle, really.

            If you have decent attributes and skills, and the right disciplines, then that really makes hunting easier. The questions that follow are only "what else do you need to do to maintain your domain"--and if it's as small as a single house or subway platform then the answer may be "not much"--even the prince may not care to have you check in often, though a wise sheriff would confirm you're still active periodically.

            And this can apply to travel time as well: being able to turn into a bird or bat does wonders for covering ground, and low-level presence doesn't even cost blood to activate, so it won't increase your feeding requirements as dominate would. There has to be as many solutions to the core vampiric problem of sustenance as there are vampires, but it will revolve around ensuring a safe, productive domain.

            Let's talk hobbies, however. If your Kindred isn't up to their earrings in local socializing and relationships (and assuming those aren't her main hobby), she'll still have SOME free time left--an hour, maybe two for something. Yoga. Stamps. Casual torture. Usual stuff. So the thought that should follow is "how much does maintaining this hobby cost in resources?" Resources in money (which has to be earned from some income stream). Resources in security concerns (have size is larger for your collection of welded used prosthetic art, after all) and maintenance.

            Further, this being an RPG with advancement as a core concept, hobbies for players and key NPCs are likely to be ones that are active. They take the vamp out of the attic, give the opportunity for skill practice, have an inherent risk (personal harm, security loss, resource cost) associated and therefore provide a chance of improvement in skill-levels. Which means they take even more time to indulge in ("Sorry Gridley--tonight's bowling. You'll have to manage the ticker yourself and let me know how that argument with the missus' works out. Ta-ta!").

            Digression: Is this a good time to point out how fantastic Gilded Cage "Red Light" would be for totally exasperating an opponent in just their normal operating affairs? Give it a month: the frustration would be tangible next Elysium!

            So here's a question: assuming a character is operating in a fashion where they can get an hour or two of 'free' time a night to pursue personal development, how long before they accumulate an XP point?

            --Khanwulf

                                Oh, for downtime purposes? I would say it depends on how long the downtime is. I swear there is a section in one of the books that details guidelines for experience gain during downtime, but I can't find it. As a general rule, I usually go with one beat per year, or two experience per decade. It may sound stingy to some, but my thinking is that if it's not important enough to be covered in an actual session with roleplaying, it's not important enough to get a good reward for it. For shorter timespans, you may want to up it to a beat per six months or in extreme cases, a beat per month. I would only be that generous if the downtime was limited to one or two years, though.

                                ​That of course, is my rule for experience and downtime in general. As pertaining to a specific hobby, you might have the same distribution of beats, but I would probably only allow that experience to apply to a narrow window of skills and merits related to that specific hobby.

                                        Thanks folks, much appreciate the comments--very useful.

                                        I'll add a few thoughts: there are, broadly, two types of downtime. The first takes place between play sessions when the characters have reached a state of "normal" and their main distractions are the usual from life. Generally speaking during this period of time characters will be unfocused and do whatever is necessary to maintain existence at the desired level of comfort, with their free time sliced off toward a variety of interests. Beat (XP) generation is going to be slow, primarily because the characters are not pushing themselves in this environment--if they were, then you'd semi-play it out as per the next type.

                                        The second downtime type is what you might otherwise call the "montage scene", in which there is a break of some length in the main story but the characters are strongly motivated to prepare in a specific direction for their goals. The Storyteller keeps the focus on the characters and their action following the basic ideas of their direction, and then resumes episodic play. Beat generation will be faster but likely limited toward advancing the key interest being practiced. You can have a montage scene that lasts years or decades (Conner in Highlander is an example of this), but the end result will tend to be "the Storyteller wants me to have X at Y level" in that case.

                                        --Khanwulf
