    Join my Research Team: Egyptian life under Roman rule.

    06-08-2016, 03:12 PM
    I rolled two dice via a python function. The first result was 53. The second was 168.

    As I got 53, I scanned down through the UN list of countries and got Egypt.
    As I got 168, my game would start in 168 C.E.

    Ah, that's kinda handy. There is already a Requiem for Rome. It's baseline is slightly later, but that might make it even better, as 168 seem like the end of Rome's high point. So more to explore.

    Anyway, I like to get a solid idea of life in Egypt at the time and Rome in general. As such, I am currently exploring the topic and humbly invite others interested in helping.

    Do some reading and do a sketch of what life was like.

    Find some weird things that might go against expectation. (For example, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milita..._Rome#Policing. It would seem the civic guard was unarmed. Was the populous then? Will there be less swords than I expected?)

    Go into detail on a topic that might come up. (Example: What swords was I expecting?)

    Tell me what CotD has already stipulated about the place and time.

    Find a great story seed that would be worth playing a story around.

    Only ground rule right now is that we start in Alexandria. Having done some research, it has some nice caste tensions at the time (Romans, Greeks, and Egyptians were not equal) and is a capital that might allow flight to Rome latter in the game.

    Lastly, if you want to do your own rolls and tell us where you ended up, go for it!

          I love doing research! I'm starting with Wikipedia, but I'll see if I can go a bit deeper in the near future. Please forgive the wall of text; I thought a bit of distillation might be more useful than just linking to Wikipedia articles.

          So, as in most of the Eastern Roman Empire (even before the Empire was legally split into East and West), the elite of the province of Aegyptus were culturally more Greek than Roman, and the lifestyle of her people was not much like that of a citizen of the capital. The Romans modified many aspects of the Ptolemaic government of Cleopatra and her forebears, but they never replaced it entirely, and in many parts of the province a commoner could probably be forgiven for not really noticing that the country was under new leadership. The land was divided into a number of nomes, each of which was overseen by a strategos, a sort of military governor. Sufficiently wealthy citizens (almost always from Greek aristocratic families) were required to perform public service in the administration of the province, while poorer inhabitants of Aegyptus had no legal representation in government.


          Aegyptus was a diverse province, home to Arabs, Berbers, Libyans, Nubians, Phoenicians, and traders from yet more distant lands, but the most politically important groups were the Greeks, who formed the upper classes of the province, the native Egyptians, who made up the bulk of the population, and the Jews, who settled in large numbers in Alexandria and other Egyptian cities after Jerusalem was razed in 70 AD. By the time the Romans took over, the Greeks had long since displaced the local aristocracy and had taken pains to promote their culture over that of the natives, although Egyptians could sometimes still rise to prominence, often as priests or other religious figures.

          One such holy man, known to the Romans as Isidorus ("gift of Isis"), led a large rebellion against Roman taxation in the Nile Delta in 172. This conflict, which would become known as the "Bucolic War," would do significant damage to the economy of the province and bring an end to the first golden age of Roman Egypt. In the 160's, however, Aegyptus remains one of the richest provinces of the empire, being both the breadbasket of the Mediterranean and the base from which Rome could take part in the lucrative Indian Ocean trade.

          Ethnic tensions always simmer close to the surface, however; there are many still alive who saw the Kitos war, when the Jews of the eastern Empire, including those in Alexandria, rose up against the Greek elite and had to be put down to Trajan's legions. The policy of the Roman Empire has thus far been to promote the Hellenization of the province, levying heavier taxes of Egyptians and Jews than on Greeks and sponsoring works of Greco-Roman art and architecture throughout the Egyptian cities. Likewise, the officers of Rome have been working to phase out the native Egyptians who made up the bulk of the Ptolemaic army in favor of loyal Roman soldiers.

          Citizens of Alexandria formed the highest echelon of society, enjoying privileges beyond those of their peers in other Egyptian cities. No Egyptian could become a Roman citizen without first becoming a citizen of Alexandria, and this was virtually impossible for commoners whose parents were not themselves citizens. The use of corporal punishment in Aegyptus provides a lovely illustration of these class distinctions: whereas most Egyptians could be whipped, Alexandrians could only legally be beaten with a rod, while actual Roman citizens were exempt from all forms of corporal punishment.


          The religious situation in Aegyptus is no less complex than her ethnic divisions. The temples promoted by the Greek and Roman elite are largely Hellenic in origin, or else promote syncretic deities which combine aspects of Greek and Egyptian mythology, like Serapis and Harpocrates, although the cults of Isis and Anubis have gained some popularity as well and even spread to other parts of the Empire. The native Egyptians, on the other hand, are increasingly abandoning their ancient religion in favor of Christianity, and Alexandria is quickly becoming one of the main centers of the upstart faith. The most influential Christian leader in recent times was Valentinus, who died around 160 after forming a highly influential sect of Gnostic Christians. Indeed, Gnosticism will continue to be the dominant form of Christianity in Aegyptus until the reforms of Pope Demetrius I of Alexandria in the 3rd century.

          Speaking of Christianity, Roman Egypt plays a very significant role in the early history of the Lancea et Sanctum. According to the Sanguinaria, after being driven out of Jerusalem, the Monachus (the first and only disciple of Longinus) and his "Dark Apostles" fled to Egypt, eventually establishing a base in Thebes, where they discovered the secrets of Theban Sorcery. Now, the traditional dates ascribed to those events are in the first half of the 3rd century, but more recent archaeological discoveries open up the possibility that the earliest books of the Testament of Longinus were written in Egypt in the second half of the 2nd century. Either way, if the PCs manage to stay active long enough they could very well witness (or interfere in) the earliest foundations of the Sanctified faith.

                  Just for reference, I got that bit about the Sanctified from the Testament of Longinus, a first edition book written in an in-universe style as a "new translation" with scholarly footnotes and appendixes. Second edition canon could theoretically contradict what's in that book, but there's at least one instance where a passage from that version of the Testament is quoted verbatum in a 2e source (the core book, actually), so I figure it's still a good source.

                  According to 2Sanguinaria, one of the books of the Testament, the Monachus (a scholarly monk who was supposedly embraced by Longinus and basically founded the Sanctified) entered Egypt with three childer/disciples, Pazit (formerly a spurned wife who strangled her own infant daughter when she didn't have enough food for both to eat), Maron of Icaria (formerly a Roman soldier who had used his position to get away with murder and robbery), and Daniel (supposedly the sole survivor of the Jews who fought the Empire in the Siege of Masada). They settled in Thebes where the secrets of Theban Sorcery were supposedly revealed to them by an Angel called "Amoniel", and they attracted a following among the local vampires.

                  "Foremost" of these recruits were vampires who the Testament calls Michael, Penelope, Valentinus, and Hostilinus the Numidian. The text says almost nothing about the first three, although it is possible that one of them is the one who would eventually write down two books of Sanguinaria, which are the only parts of the Testament not attributed to Longinus himself. Hostilinus is actually a character who shows up in Requiem for Rome, and would eventually the de-facto leader of the Lancea et Sanctum in the city of Rome itself. He's also probably the founder of the Morbus bloodline, but he didn't develop his affinity for disease until after arriving in Rome, where he resorted to feeding on tainted blood after the Kindred of the Camarilla pushed him out of more desirable hunting grounds.

                  Somewhat less than 50 years after the Dark Apostles settled in Thebes, Daniel supposedly went off with the legendary Theban Legion and was martyred somewhere in Gaul. Some time thereafter the Cult of Set, which was apparently the dominant Kindred organization in Thebes at the time, drove the Sanctified out of the city, and Pazit died essentially covering her master's escape.

                  That being said, 2 Sanguinaria seems to have been written long after all of these events took place, and nobody knows who exactly wrote the events down. They include a some archetypes from Christian folklore and Greek myth (the Theban Legion probably never actually existed and Pazit's story resembles the myth of Medea), which has lead some to believe that the events described must have been at least partly fictional. Still, there's just enough evidence to suggest that the earliest Sanctified did settle somewhere in Egypt, and that they somehow discovered Theban Sorcery there and recruited some of the vampires who would become the great leaders of the Santified in centuries to come. Any of the figures mentioned in the book could make interesting NPCs, and there's enough uncertainty surrounding the events that you could pretty much interpret them however you wanted.


                  And sure, it could be interesting to look into what's known about some random point in time and space. :P
