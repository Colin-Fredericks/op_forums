                Originally posted by MachineIV View Post
                Yeah. In my experience, "don't split the party" spells death for a Vampire chronicle.
                Don't split the party is among the worst storytelling advice I can imagine, and betrays a deep distrust of the storyteller (who, it is assumed, will seize the opportunity to afflict all forms torments upon the disparate groups).

                Splitting the party gives the storyteller the opportunity to 'cut' from scene to scene as action rises and falls, breeds openings for distrust, conniving and misinformation. More importantly it allows characters of considerable power disparity to shine independent from one another, provides opportunities for challenges that a full party would easily thwart and gives characters a chance for more intimate development. Perhaps most importantly, it's realistic and actually how lives (both real and storied) play out.

                Basically, split the party. Split it often and with impunity until the very concept of a 'party' (a tiresome D&D holdover) is disillusioned.

                        Guess I really do have to try it, then, if that's the main concern.
                        There are, of course, limits. A four way split is likely to be too much, unless your players are really invested in the action (which is entirely possible to achieve). But in general I find players more than willing to spend some time on the sidelines if they know it will mean they get the spotlight in a few minutes. Interestingly I find it cuts down on power gaming as well, if there is no one to show up there is less reason to twink out.

                        If you've got the right players you can go for some non-standard structures: I've had player antagonists, split opposing parties, lone wolf characters, characters who spent the majority of the session on the sidelines. In other words, there are no rules, it doesn't matter if two characters are starting, one is an antagonist, a forth has a personal agenda, and the fifth is basically a spectator. If your players are enjoying themselves then the game is good.

                            I wouldn't jump to conclusions on either side. I do split the party, and know it can be fun. By the way, I do it in D&D, also, so the idea that such is a sin in D&D or in more combat oriented games isn't true. By the way, there's a lot of things you can do in this kind of game where you do need to split the party, and it doesn't have to do with "killing our maiming your PCs".

                            This kind of advice is born precisely from what Maina said. Splitting the party demands its own kind of logistics. As everything else, is pretty much intuitive for some STs, but not so for everyone. Know your players, know your limits and know your timing. Try it a few times prepared to cut scenes in the middle if needed to jump to others, so no one gets bored. Get your hang of this, and them go ahead to do it more oftem.

                            And I wouldn't go as far as to say there are no such a thing as a party. There certainly are: YOUR party, with ST and players. The characters can be pretty much at each others throats, or even unaware of their existence, but you shouldn't forget the party around the table.

                                                            Originally posted by MachineIV View Post
                                                            My current favorite trick is calling &quot;CUT&quot; at a high point in a scene, and moving on from there. So we don't have the piddly shit that happens in wind down.

                                                            But when I have a split thing going on, I usually pass rapidly around the table, one beat of action at a time as we resolve things. It creates tension, and gives everyone a moment or two to think about their next move, while enjoying what's going on in the other scenes. It feels a lot like a Guy Ritchie movie.
                                                            One cannot stress enough how important cutting scenes is to roleplaying. Just imagine your favorite movie, now imagine if all the scenes dragged on until every character had their say and left the scene.

                                                            Failing to end scenes is the number two death of games, right after everyone's schedules being too full. A scene that drags on loses all tension, all sense of urgency and purpose, and more often than not leaves the players confused with too much information.

                                                            On that last note, I also recommend 'poking stick' characters. NPCs you can use to organically push scenes forward and cut the circle think that can occur when players start brainstorming. They also serve the purpose of letting the storyteller convey what he's hearing, thus curtailing miscommunication and disappointment in later scenes.
