          In first edition there was an adjustable ratio. Second accepts that 'whatever your story calls for' is appropriate.

          Originally posted by First Edition
          'In most small cities, the proportion of undead to mortals tends to be relatively low: One vampire per 100,000 or more mortals is not uncommon. In large cities, the ratio is usually nearer one vampire per 50,000 mortals. In some cities that seem to draw the Kindred for whatever reason - cities such as New York, London, New Orleans and Chicago - the ratio canbe substantially higher. Most Princes don't particularly keep track. As long as the population doesn't grow so high that people take notice of the predators among them, numbers don't matter.'
          Regarding age, Blood Potency can help determine starting ages. But there's room for adjustment. You start at Blood Potency 1, but could have been a revenant or in torpor for eons. You get a point of Blood Potency every 50 years of being 'up.' Diablerie or experimentation can make it rise prematurely. I imagine there's going to be ways to reduce Blood Potency permanently.

                          The 1st edition core book said 1:50,000 was a very rough average, but more densely populated areas tend to have more Kindred per human and more rural areas tend to have fewer. That figure was never mentioned again, and in 2e not only does it not say anything of the sort, there is a lot of variety in the sample domains' population sizes.

                          As for age, I like to say that starting characters with no Experiences are less than 150, since 3 is the highest Blood Potency you can start with without spending Experiences and it increases roughly at a rate of 1 every 50 years. But, there are guidelines for starting characters off with bonus Experiences, so you can start with Ancillae or Elders if you want, and it's easy enough to excuse starting characters being outside the rough 0-150 range if you want to.

                                  It depends on the domain and the 'story' you want to tell. In saying that, I don't mean a literal story - but the vibe you want to give when describing the setting to your players. A setting's population can create story seeds in the sense that an overpopulated domain could mean there's a vampire on every corner, bitterly fighting each other for territory, and there's multiple elders fighting for rule over a whole city. It would mean there's a lot more rank and file, a sense of hierarchy (e.g. prince, primogen council, sheriff, keeper of elysium, harpies, hounds). An undead society that interacts frequently. In an underpopulated city / town, I imagine that it'd be rare for vampires to see one another, everybody is pretty happy with their own, and hierarchies would be very, very basic (perhaps there's a rivalry between two lone elders who frequently pull the other four vampires in town and a bunch of ghouls into their plots against one another, perhaps one of them has a second-in-command that's their childe and the other has a younger vampire they've taken as a concubine - but that's as complex as the hierarchy gets on a cursory glance).

                                  I wouldn't worry too much about the exact populations. I'd just think about it in vague terms like the above, and using that as a springboard, start looking at the actual territory or play-space (the city your game is set in), then consider who claims what as their domain. Create some NPCs. When I create NPCs in well-populated cities (hierarchies included, a lot of my experience stemming more from Masquerade), I like to take heavy notice of their power levels compared to my players (not in the sense of how well they crush things with their bare hands, but to get a sense of the social dynamic that you're going to give your players when they interact with your NPCs - too many powerful NPCs means your PCs are going to be bullied, ignored, or treated in a really condescending fashion - and that's just not fun). I like to give PCs options and agency, and to do that, I like to make a variety of NPCs of differing power levels in comparison to the PCs - you need a few movers and shakers to create a seed for overarching plots (the guys who can bully your PCs without a thought), you need others at their level (rivals, those they can beat and outsmart or be beaten and outsmarted by in turn), and you need those that are simply below them (to give them a chance to help those weaker than them or potentially bully, a moral dilemma). All this depends on the PCs' ages, because a fresh embrace is going to be just as bullied by a 100 year old as much as a 300 year old vampire. Their rivals are going to be their peers, other freshly embraced vampires. Those below them are ghouls, mortals, failed / laughingstocks of vampires.

                                  When I create a vampire NPC, I think about their social circle, branching out - think about any vampires that may serve them, think about any ghouls that may serve them, think about any unknowing mortal servants, think about the social clique they flit around (high society parties, biker bars, music festivals, goth clubs), and think about where their public domain / haven is located (why, how?) - all in vague terms. I can't be stuffed being too exact with populations and preparing too many fully-written up NPCs, simply too much work. I will have my basics prepared in detail, and if I need to introduce a new NPC, I can introduce them on the fly - and if they happen to become important, and that's a frequency - then I can give them a more detailed look-over as the game demands.
