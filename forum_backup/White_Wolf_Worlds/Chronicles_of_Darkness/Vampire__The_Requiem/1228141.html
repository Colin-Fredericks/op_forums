    A Louis Covenant?

    06-17-2018, 07:18 PM
    For those unfamiliar, Louis is a character of the novel/film "Interview with a Vampire" who became a vampire, but was generally a good person. Naturally, the poor dude was tortured trying to reconcile his morals with his vampiric nature.

    So what about a Covenant of these kind of vampires? It'd be an uphill battle to be sure, considering the other Covenants. Still, I think there'd be enough Louis-types out there determined enough to establish one.

    Then again, I'm not the most familiar with Requiem, so I'm probably missing something here. Any resources out there for these types of vampires?

      Generally if I've learned anything, it couldn't be a Covenant because merely joining a Covenant is a breaking point on the humanity scale. Covenants are in general used to help ease the minds of vampires as they descend into being a monster.

      Now, I could see a group of Kindred who loosely associate themselves together in trying to maintain their humanity, but I would believe they would remain apolitical and not truly gather themselves into a Covenant to prevent themselves from slipping down that slippery slope.

          The Vampire Chronicles characters are kind of hard to translate because there are some significant differences between the settings (namely Anne Rice's stories assume a much smaller population of vampires). It's also difficult because the Vampire Chronicles are largely written from the point of view of characters, with different books contradicting each other based on differences of opinion, experience, and certain character bending the truth and even lying for personal reasons.

          I think "can't be a Covenant" because joining a Covenant is a breaking point isn't a good way of thinking about it. Any longer association of vampires is a breaking point on some level, because you're reminding yourself you're a vampire by hanging out with other vampires. It's a challenge that needs to be faced regardless of the level of organization.

          To an extent most of the existing Covenants have some appeal to the Louis types depending on their cultural background. The Carthians are probably the ones that best reflect Louis' particular arc, in the form of the more intellectual side of the Covenant. Louis isn't a bomb-throwing revolutionary, but he's constantly in search of a better way of doing things than the traditional vampires, and looks towards humans as inspiration for how.

Covenants are basically a group-wide touchstone in the long-term. They may not keep you from facing Breaking Points, but they'll give you reasons not to become a draugr, and they all have workarounds to dealing with common vampire problems.

                  There's some extra covenants in Danse Macabre, like the Sun-Walking Knights that might appeal to Louis, though he didn't seem to care much about people. But being good person who feels bad about what other vampires do (and you do too sometimes) is something that can play in every covenant. That described three of the games original signature characters, Persephone, Loki and Bruise.

                      A Covenant isn't necessary; you could have a smaller association, or even a secret society. Here is something that might interest you (it uses 1st ed system, so update as desired):
                      Shadow Cult: The Path of the Man Ascendant

                      The war between Man and Beast rages within each vampire. More often than not, the Beast wins, or at least gains enough ground that the Man is barely recognizable from his breathing days. Yet there are many who stubbornly cling to what made them human – whether beliefs, interests, or friends. A few have chosen the narrow path back towards salvation, to become more human than most humans – to quiet the Beast as much as the animate dead can. They have chosen the Path of the Man Ascendant.

                      History: The gentleman-philosopher Nathaniel Etheridge was Embraced as a Mekhet in 1718. In his first few decades he experienced the predictable loss of his morality to the Beast within. In 1761 Etheridge wrote that he was at the last turning point; he could accept the evil he had become and lose himself to the Beast, or he could claw his way back to the higher path, becoming the Man which even mortals aspire to. He saw the curse as lethal to what made one human, and decided to take the hard route. Over the next century, Etheridge intensively studied philosophy from around the world, watched the interactions of Kindred, and developed mental exercises to focus the will and clear the distractions and temptations of the Beast from the true path. In time, he found others who wished to rein in their inner monster, and taught them as best he could. The teacher dropped out of sight shortly after producing a few copies of his seminal work, Testament of a Humanist. As copies of the book circulated, a cult grew up around the philosopher’s work, and continues to quietly expand.

                      All this can be confirmed if one looks hard enough. The fate of Nathaniel Etheridge is unclear and controversial. The anonymous author of The Lives and Deaths of a Humanist claims that Etheridge reached Golconda, and chose to become mortal again. Humanists say the Testament is the more important work, and the claims of Lives and Deaths, even if true, are beside the point. Yet often these naysayers secretly hold the promise of Golconda deep in their hearts.

                      Texts:
                      Testament of a Humanist. First published by Nathaniel Etheridge in 1924. The first section of the volume is largely autobiographical. The second section examines the nature of the beast and the effects of moral degeneration, drawing examples from various real-world Kindred to illustrate the points. The third section discusses beliefs, behaviors and exercises to refine the mind and retain and strengthen moral character. A final few pages discuss Golconda as myth and legend, and as a worthy goal (It does not claim factuality in this matter).

                      The Lives and Deaths of a Humanist Copies first appeared in the 1990s. Written anonymously, it reiterates and elaborates on the biographical information in the Testament, and goes on to fill in the story of Etheridges’ existence after 1923. It claims that Etheridge left his handful of disciples in 1943, questing for Golconda. According to the book, he reappeared to the author in the 1960s as a mortal, proclaiming that Golconda was a real transcendence. They continued to meet every few years until Etheridge died in 1990.

                      Praxis: Cultist believe that they are cursed, pure and simple. Like an illness, the curse must be diligently treated, less it get worse and ultimately destroy the host. Humanists seek to strengthen the Man within, to cling to the vestiges of the human soul in their animate corpses. Members study and meditate. They stay close to mortals, developing connections that keep them engaged with the living world beyond the predator/prey mode. It is a dangerous practice – a moment’s slip or bad luck could undo years of self-control, not to mention expose the vampire to unfriendly eyes. Also, should the cultist’s beliefs and practices become known to other undead, the established Kindred hierarchy is liable to see a potential threat to the Masquerade – or use the Humanist’s mortal ties against them.

                      Organization: Humanists often collect in small congregations, usually centered around a charismatic leader. Whether in a group or walking the path alone, most initiates try to distance themselves from Kindred society – both to avoid persecution and to reduce exposure to the immoral behaviors and temptations inherent in the vampire lifestyle. Yet, like most Kindred, they seldom stray from where mortals congregate, for without humanity to guide them, vampires would lose their way as easily as if they were in a depraved Prince’s court.
                      Initiations
                      • The Humanists rely on mental discipline to focus the mind on what is right. The character gains the Merit: Meditative Mind.
                      ••• The Humanist gains an extra die to resist degeneration. This is for Humanity loss only, not for acquiring a derangement.
                      ••••• The Humanist’s moral compass guides him to salvation. Once per chapter, the Storyteller may make an Intelligence + Composure roll. Success on this roll may be used to alert a player to the Humanity risks in a given course of action, and to suggest an alternative which poses less peril of Degeneration. Alternatively, the character may have a mental breakthrough on the path to regaining the soul – in other words, success shows the way to gain a dot of Humanity. Note that only one of these options is available for any one roll. The player may ask the Storyteller for a roll, but there is no obligation to comply.
                      Covenants: The typical Humanist belongs to no covenant. Of those who do, the Carthian Movement is both more accepting and (sometimes) more palatable to those trying to keep their Humanity. Some members reside within the structure of the Invictus, although they do well to keep their initiation secret. The Ordo Dracul, while not actively persecuting the cult, feel that seeking to return to humanity rather than working to transcend vampirism is a backwards step at best. Members of the Lancea Sanctum feel that rejecting their divine role by seeking to become more human is heretical. The Circle of the Crone is even less tolerant, finding the Humanist view antithetical to their own beliefs.

                      Key words: When talking with some suspected of also walking the Path, somewhere in an initial conversation a Humanist inserts, “I lived many years ago.” To which another Humanist would reply, “And may you live again one day.”
