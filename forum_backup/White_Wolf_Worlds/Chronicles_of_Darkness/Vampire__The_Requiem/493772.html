    How do you fit so many Vampires/groups in smaller cities, what ratio do you follow?

    04-27-2015, 02:48 PM
    Hi !

    I'm designing my first fictional WoD city, and its neighboring towns, and the more I think about how it would work, the more I am curious as to how people usually do it.

    First off, the city I'm trying to develop would be located in the New England region, preferably on the very narrow seacoast along New Hampshire (since it connects to Maine and Massachusetts).

    My problem is that unless I pick one already existing Big city, I have a hard time respecting the 1 vampire for 100,000 people ratio that's often tossed around. I've read quite a few threads discussion the "number of vampires per capita" question, but unless the city is truly humongous, unless you play a lot with the ratio, it's hard to justify having so many factions, covenants and sects with so few people in each.

    Even New York would only have roughly 200-300 vampires at the above ratio, and Boston proper would have ~13, or up to 40 if you include the huge metropolitan area around.

    So how do you do it? What ratio do you use? What point is "too many vampires in one area" in your game? How do you manage so many covenants, sects, factions, etc. with so few vampires to play with?

    Also, where do you put werewolves and other supernaturals?

          Personally, I would never respect a ratio given in any book. I pick a number that sounds comfortable for my game (I put 400 vampires and 200 Unchained in Berlin c. 1998, for instance).

          I understand that some people find that unsatisfying though.

          The best way to handle small populations is to start culling the organizations you won't be using. If you've already got a plot in mind, that makes it easy enough: just drop the Lancea et Sanctum, Ordo Dracul, or Ventrue entirely. Look to the domains in the 2E core book for examples of them playing with the Covenants and political factions in exactly that way.

          Next, there's not going to be much of a hierarchy. If there are only a couple of elders they'll probably dominate the power structures. There will be less political intrigue than there will be territorial disputes: coteries operate like gang cells, and are expected to get along by the leader, but obviously you need to keep up the political drama. Perhaps everybody vies for favor of the leader: if your cotery does something that's seen as weak then your neighbors will take the opportunity to try and push you out of the spotlight.

          Alternatively, have the city be up for grabs by two or three organizations: there's an ongoing turf-war and everybody's expected to pick a side. Neutrality means you're an acceptable target for everybody, but there could be fun had by playing the sides against each other.

              I don't remember where, but where I first saw a ratio, it said that 1:50,000 is also good, if the city is of particular interest. I typically find a comfortable number somewhere between 1:50,000 and 1:100,000.

              300 vampires is a ton. Remember that each vampire is probably going to end up with at least some amount of territory that is theirs. Each vampire is going to be feeding from multiple humans to sustain themselves. In that respect, I'm perfectly fine with small amounts of vampires.

              Really, I don't see much problem with as few as 50 vampires representing all of the factions. They're more akin to college clubs in scale than corporations, I feel. Or something like a Masonic lodge. They don't really need huge populations to function.

              For smaller, like Boston, there's no real reason to force every single Covenant to have representation. Maybe Boston (going with the Lovecraftian stereotype of New England) has almost entirely Ordo and maybe Circle membership. Maybe some small town of 10,000 has a single Lancea bishop as its only vampire. There's nothing wrong with that.

              You can certainly do the (in my opinion overplayed) advice to just "do what feels right for the story," but I like the ratios mentioned because too many vampires means it's that much harder to maintain masquerade. A frugal vampire could probably survive off a single human, but when half the population is persistently anemic, the Masquerade is far too fragile. Smaller numbers help the vampires to hide.

                              I don't really see what's unreasonable with 30 vampires in a town of 20000. As Griautis has pointed out, vampires don't need that much blood to begin with, and for the first few decades are fine with animal blood. With the advent of subtle feeding you don't even need to tell people you're drinking from them. A vampire can hide in the attic of the Tanner family and sup quite comfortably knowing that the single men may bring home girlfriends. A gangrel can simply live near a farm and occasionally drink from cows, not requiring any humans at all. A small coterie of vampires could give a lesser bloodbond to and, with use of dominate, ensure an entire apartment building remains theirs and quiet about their presence. If just one person in that apartment building works at a slaughterhouse or hospital, they could have a whole fridge of blood restocked every week.

                              It's not as if in a town of 300 people someone sees someone else looking more pale than usual they're going to think, 'Vampire!' and call the authorities. One of my vampires, Maw, was a patron for a small fishing community. In exchange for blood, he kept larger fishing companies out of their waters. Another just claimed the entirety of the Everglades and would, in a rather Masquerade-breaking fashion, swoop down and pick up travelers down US-1. Another vampire had a deal with werewolves, another subtly fed on monorail travelers, completely invisible. Another had her entire workplace devoted to her, another owned a strip club and partook from employees and guests. There's a lot of ways to work it so you have a balance between supply and privacy.

                                                      It's worth noting that that even if you assume that ratio (from the very first book printed in the line, before it had had a chance to work out its own identity) as accurate, it's still practically useless for the purposes of statistical analysis. It's a simple arithmetic mean, which contrary to what most of us learned in school is not interchangeable with average. Mean is a very good indicator of average when data is normally distributed, but a terrible indicator of average when the distribution is as horribly uneven as it would be when analyzing the relationship between human populations and vampire populations. Cities with a lot of people are going to have a lot more vampires than 1:100,000 and rural areas with a lot of space and not a lot of people are going to have a lot fewer vampires. What you really want to look at is population density. The more people you've got per square foot, the more vampires you'll have per person.
