        I think these two things are a bit related.

        Aspirations reward you beats for doing well. The dramatic failure option exists for making your failures more interesting. There's also a reason why we have people running the game.         Conditions are a mix. Most Conditions are negative, but there are positive ones as well, they reward you either way. And many negative ones can still be the result of "doing a good job." As your characters advance in XP, they'll have a much easier time generating positive Conditions on themselves and others, providing a greater feeling of "reward for doing well," on top of it.

                                            Originally posted by SpiritSponge View Post
                                            Dramatic failures will constantly come up just so they can gain a beat making the characters look like a bunch of bungling idiots and ruin a serious campaign. I want the characters to play the game instead of always thinking how they will get their next beat.
                                            I think it's worth highlighting that you can only take the beat to dramatically fail a roll you were failing anyway. You're not trading success for dramatic failure, you're trading failure for dramatic failure. As the ST you have a huge amount of control over how a dramatic failure plays out. There are lots of tools and options, which means you have a lot of ways to avoid them looking incompetent. They don't even need to see some of them (for example a NPC gaining a positive Condition against the PCs isn't exactly "visible" to them). As long as the results make sense as something that could have happened anyway, it's easy to mix personal failures with external failures to avoid the "bungling idiots" effect.

                                            And the players are supposed to work on getting beats. That's why beats come from the sources they do. Setting and fulfilling goals, resolving Conditions, taking dramatic failures, good RP... these are all things the players should basically be doing anyway. It's what they should be thinking about, which is, "what should my character be doing that advances the plot or is at least making the story more interesting?" Because that's what gets you beats.

                                            There isn't really an issue with "gaming" a system intended to be gamed. The potential issue is players rejecting the spirit of the rules ("make things more fun/interesting and get a reward") and just focus on the reward. But then you get to Charlaquin's point about this needing to be a talk with the group; not a rules issue.

                                                        Um. As said before, you're trading a failure for a dramatic failure. It's not really any more bumbling than it would be otherwise.

                                                        Perhaps more importantly, dramatic failure doesn't have to be a failure on the character's part. Read the example dramatic failures. They can be external things that mess up the characters' plans further. An example is the dramatic failure of Mesmerize, where your target is so much stronger of will than you that they are empowered by their ability to resist. It isn't that the vampire is a bumbling fool, but that their target is a paragon of mental strength, at least in that moment.

                                                        Similarly, if I have a character trying to pick a lock and they dramatic fail, I might not have them break their picks and scream in surprise like a fool. I might just have it be that the 'dramatic failure' means that they can hear a guard patrol coming down the corridor just this moment by chance, so they have to go hide and/or find another way in. They've failed and they cannot continue to roll, but that doesn't make them bumbling fools.

                                                        2e rewards players for allowing interesting obstacles and complications to be injected into the story. It only rewards the players for acting like bumbling idiots if you interpret a failure as the result of being an idiot. Sometimes things just don't work out, even after you do everything right. That applies IC as well.

                                                            If SpiritSponge's crew is already embracing the game's drama/politics, and playing up negative conditions without meta-incentives, then they're right - beats aren't a necessary factor for that campaign.

                                                            If a crew tends to try to maximize their successes and avoid hardship/drama in most situations, beats are a good way to start teaching them that setbacks can also be a compelling aspect to the game. If a crew has a hard time coming to terms with their characters being Mesmerized or Possessed or any of the conditions which have historically been seen as 'removing protagonism' then again, beats are a way to incentivize players to break out of that comfort zone.

                                                            I was worried about the Dramatic Failure clause for a recent game I started, partially because of the possible 'Keystone Kindred' effect, but also because the gaming experience between the players was going to be very different (one first-timer, one average, one ten year vet). I chose to go with group beats and that seems to have encouraged the crew to help each other with aspirations. But I also started with the Dramatic Failure option in my hands as the ST.

                                                            Rather than just let the players declare they want the Dramatic Failure, I offer it during appropriate moments/rolls in a narrative way. For example, when trying to stealth into an old factory they blew the roll and I announced, "Just as you rush across the street, headlights flare on in the parking garage across from you. Is it a normal failure and there's just been a witness to someone sneaking into the factory who might turn over info to a later investigation, or is it a dramatic failure and you just got spotted by a patrol car that's going to start heading your way?"

                                                            To second what Maina said, I tend to frame the Dramatic Failure as turns of circumstance rather than, "You trip and fall as you run across the street and bash your head on the concrete." There's a difference between unexpected complications you have to deal with and you just looking stupid as a character.
