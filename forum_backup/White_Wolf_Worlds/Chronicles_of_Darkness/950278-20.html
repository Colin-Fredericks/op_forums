    Originally posted by The Kings Raven View Post
    What would your intended character arc be for a Genius? What would your equivalent of a Mage's descent into hubris and obsession be?


    Would you keep Genius' current concept that Mad Scientists learn that trying to force the world to conform to their vision is practically impossible and a fundamentally unhealthy goal for everyone (or fail to learn that and suffer the consequences) or do you have a different idea?
    I'm going to start by saying that I think anyone planning on making a game should watch these videos:

    Part 1:


    Part 2:


    You've told me you have a theme--transgression. Everything in the game should point back to that theme. Every single thing. Your abilities, the history, any kind of Magnum Opus you create, and the undercurrents and metaphors in your game (what we've been talking about here). Those videos talk about mechanics as metaphor for video game developers, but I have found everything they discuss very helpful for RPG development too. I highly recommend you watch them, and I highly recommend playing the flash game they link to. It's very short, and I think it'll give you a lot of inspiration.

    If I were making a mad science game, I would base it heavily on the works of Jacques Derrida and Jacques Lacan. Phenomenology and Deconstruction, along with the crash course I gave above. I think a focus on psychological horror and how we define and derive meaning from symbols has just as much potential to be really tragic as what you're talking about, and I would actually posit that it has even more of that potential. It would, in a sense, build off the work already done with Mage, while still keeping itself distinct and respectful of that text. It would fill a void I don't think is as covered in CofD, and if you kept it cohesive, I think it would have the potential to be amazing.

    You could still keep the concept of a battle for understanding. Kind of about who controls public perception, and who controls meaning. Or you could go a different route and make the tragic horror of Genius about trying to control that meaning, only to find that people believe what they believe, and that signifiers are too hard to change once they're established in people's minds.

    I would make the game much more internal, and much less focused on building Wonders. I think Wonders can still be present, there should definitely still be mechanics for them, but the mechanics in 1ed are convoluted and confusing, because they--in my opinion--don't hearken back to a cohesive theme. Start with your theme. You have your theme, as I understand it (transgression). Build out from there.

                                                            #315
                                                            08-23-2016, 01:51 AM
                                                            Earlier today my thoughts came around to some of the arguments I've made thus far as I contemplate continuing on with the Genius review or if I said the mainstay of what needs to be said, and my thoughts came around to questions about whether I was blowing the depth of the misanthropy and the exclusionary nature of the game out of proportion. While I stand by those arguments, the thought occured to me that people could respond that I only made an issue of those things because I was pre-disposed to be biased against Genius, and that in fact you could probably invalidate every argument I made on the subject. While that is a position I can support points on, I feel like it ignores certain realities in favor of a sweet lie. I pondered on this long enough that I figured "Hell, let's talk about it."

                                                            I Slapped God for His Throne-The Myth of the Objective Review

                                                            I actually don't like arguments about subjective opinion, by and large because it ignores that creative works have a craft element to them, that there are, in fact, good and bad ways to do things, and that there are things to learn from it. More on point, I don't like it because the easiest way to dismiss someone's objections rather than consider them is to label it as "just their opinion." However, when it comes to my own commentary in any sort of position of authority, I often make a keen point of reinforcing the idea that no one has to listen to me, because while I have issue with it, there's no such thing as an objective review, and even if there were, it would be useless anyways.

                                                            In case you're not familiar, the idea of the objective review is basically, well, a review that relies on objectivity, on a view point distant from one's self and one's biases and judged purely on what it is. It basically posits that there is an outside authority on the subject that cannot be argued against, a universal decree of what works, what doesn't, what is good and what is bad. This can be handy when you're trying to say something negative about something beloved (like this review) or something positive about something reviled (say, Twilight)-you can shift the blame of unpopularity onto a premise that says "This is the way things are." It's about having authority by not directly claiming authority.

                                                            I imagine you already know the problem-where, then, does this objective authority come from? And you know, that is a hard question to answer, if it has an answer that isn't pure revelation.

                                                            The simple fact of the matter is that, while there might some vague senses of what can make any given project successful, the fact of that matter is that, yeah, shit's too variable. Every project is unique, and people are going to react from a mountain of millions of unique perspective building conditions. An objective review would have to be something that literally every one in the world could agree on. The only reviews I think you could get of that sort might be purely compositional, like "This post is an arrangement of black pixels amidst a wide field of white pixels", and I'm not even going to commit to that.

                                                            Yeah, that's boring and useless. And more importantly, I personally don't want to explain my viewpoint to everyone. I don't have an interest in persuading everyone. I don't have a belief in persuading everyone. If I did, I wouldn't have wasted my time on Genius, because that's not a place I'm going to do it.

                                                            And really, this brings up the big thing I don't believe reviews are for.

                                                            We Are All Individuals-Personal Authority and Poking People in the Eye

                                                            I probably should have titled the section this: "I AM NOT HERE TO TELL YOU WHAT TO THINK. THAT'S NOT WHAT REVIEWS ARE FOR."

                                                            It is not my job, or the job of any reviewer, to tell the audience what to think of any given thing. It is not their job to acquire a flock of like-minded sheep who preach the word almighty about Ghostbusters or American Gods or Majora's Mask or even Genius. There are only two things a reviewer does.

                                                            1) Establish a case for their personal authority and the nature of it, and
                                                            2) Use that authority to provoke discourse that will allow the readers to deepen their understanding in some way.

                                                            Or to put it simply, "TRY TO HELP PEOPLE THINK."

                                                            That sounds a little dismissive, but for a moment here, let's consider, well, this review. Lots of people have come away with an understanding that Genius is shit and could be so much better if some ideas were applied. Lots of other people have come away with a deeper appreciation for how Genius works and appeals to them and avenues of thought it had not revealed to them before. Some people have also realized that their own discussion techniques are overly hostile, while some people have learned that no, they can get even more in your face.

                                                            Genius and I make for a messy, ugly battleground, but god damn if everyone doesn't walk away as much of a winner as they are a loser. Because, in the end, no one is winning against anything.

                                                            This entire thing is a sledgehammer, forged of my expertise, my experiences, my reputation, and, above all else, my biases, refined in the experience of this thing we call Genius, slammed against a community to see what would change. That is what a review is for. In a certain way, it's an experiment-let's tip over the rock and see what squirms there, or let's add fire and see if it explodes or fizzles.

                                                            And I could do that if I wasn't biased. I could try and talk about this game completely without admitting to the fact that I kind of unfairly hated Genius to begin with, that despite all efforts I might exert to give it a fair try, I would inevitably lose to the aquifer of spite that has been filling with each and every jackass who made Genius look less and less appealing, whether directly or vicariously. I could try and leave my concerns about social justice at the door and just talk about this as a game. I could have left out my history and kindly overlook an angle people might never even think of. I could have tried any of that.

                                                            But I would have failed, because I am my own authority-in fact, I am quite proud of several of the ways I have built that authority, and I have pangs of guilt of when I have abused that authority. I cannot rely on anything beyond it. And honestly? Why pull away when those biases, unfair though they might be, provide a way for people to look at something? To look closely at it and go "Really? Are you worth it?". Even if they walk away with a deep conviction of a worth I don't believe in, the examination happened. And that's worth it.

                                                            Still, I don't want to make it sound like all negative reviews are worth reading. They're not. I'm not even going to tell you mine is. it's entirely possible that, yes, I am full of shit. I'm sure there's an number of you walking in who already knew that and don't feel like you need to rediscover it(I even jab some of you for it, because I can). And I'm sure you have your own metric for how you can tell.

                                                            Still, I'm gonna give two main keys I look for in a useful negative review. And, okay, yeah, I'm also going to propose these are reasons why I'm not. I can only admit to possibly being ass for so long.(I mean, don't get me wrong, I'm a jackass, but I'm not ass.)

                                                            Given and Denied-Credit Where It's Due and Reason Where It Stands

                                                            When I listen or read a negative review of something, two things usually mark it of at least some worth to me.

                                                            1) Does it at least pretend to try to understand where the piece was coming from?
                                                            2) Does it at least pretend to decently explain it's points to me so they may be plain?

                                                            Truthfully, I'm a lot harsher than that, but at a bare minimum, I want to see reviewer at least talk about trying to comprehend why a choice was made and as clearly and in as much detail why they think the problem is there. These things together mean to me, if nothing else, that a genuine effort was made to establish the authority worth investing into, to establish that the questions are not just idle throw arounds, but actual conundrums.

                                                            The willingness to postulate good reasons why someone made a poor choice, the empathy to consider that, is a matter of good faith. It may be useful to hate a thing and to let the anger flow, but that anger has to be tempered by consideration. If you ask a question of why a thing was done, it doesn't hurt you to try and answer why you would do that thing and come up with an answer that you could actually conceive of applying to yourself. It saves time and makes you look less like a fool, if nothing else-it's one thing to say "This thing is wrong" and it turns out it was something obvious, where at least "This thing is wrong, even if I can see them doing because of this" is a whole 'nother thing because it fills that "you missed the obvious" with a different thought. Being willing to concede that it may not have started with bad intentions makes people inclined to listen to you, which is important if you want anyone other than those who cheep along with you to at least consider the subject-it puts them in a frame of mind to consider hings themselves. And, honestly, it just becomes a new avenue for discovering problems.

                                                            Meanwhile, the willingness to go in depths and explain your position, to try and account for all the variables and exactly how and why something doesn't work translates into meat and power. The more thoroughly you explain the problems as you see it, the less places it has to go for defense, for justification. Any idiot can dismiss a simple "It's terrible", or even "It's terrible because it has terrible characters, terrible aesthetics, and terrible gameplay." Terrible doesn't mean anything on it's own there, any statement requires building up.* But you say "It's terrible because it's characters are all selfish, one dimensional stereotypes(and here's how, rarble rarble), the aesthetics are a mis-match of eras that defeat each other's intended atmosphere( and here's how, rarble rarble), and the gameplay completely favors these characters with zero incentive to play anyone else(and here's how, rarble rarble)", and all of sudden, just by sheer virtue of mass, a person has to weigh things carefully, if for no other reason than seeing if it's right. An even better argument weaves those points together and demonstrate the intersectionality of these problems, how one feeds into another, meaning one has to address the whole picture. All of this together creates questions, and whether they agree with it or not, they have to own their personal biases and experiences and leverage them into a viewpoint that someone can learn from. Thoroughly exploring and explaining your points means you create a labyrinth of people to explore that, by the time they get through, causes them to have their own labyrinth where there might not have been one before. And that's how dialogue is created, that's how we learn.

                                                            *By the way, the same applies to declarative statements, and even better, this is where you get diversity-even as you build along the lines, you can make clear concessions to other answers once that pillar well and truly stands

                                                            Now, it's a joke, now, just how much I did that second one(23,500+ words, how the fuck do I have that much problem with anything?!), but I don't think any can argue that I at least tried to provide context and view. I explained a hell of a lot about my viewpoint-and a big part of that viewpoint was the inclusion of why some of these choices seemed like obvious things to do. In fact, while it's not present in every section, if you go back and read, I start almost all of my sections with, first, some kind of explanation why doing this thing must have seemed the most obvious thing in the world from the point of view of the mad scientist, and what were the things I was talking about before I going into how they mishmashed. Sure, it's not always perfect-for example, in the statistics section, I explain what the section is doing first and then pull a sort of "got'cha" argument with what that means to someone the world is tilted against, and even though I do go back and explain why that's a thing, it's not the best way to have done it. But even if I flub? I think it's clear I tried something on both fronts. Was it enough? Well, probably not-there's a substantial amount of feedback that suggests the contrary.

                                                            But a review that doesn't have any of that? It's not there to provoke thought or deeper appreciation, it's just... Venting. It's all flash no substance, nothing but steam. And often times, the only thing you'll get from that is burns. Not everyone is worth listening to, it is worth repeating. I may not be worth listening to. I can't tell you that. I won't tell you that. You have a brain, you can decide that for yourself.

                                                            TO BE CONTINUED-Wait, what?

                                                            It seems like I'm wrapped up here, but I think a question remains. Sure, I can argue bias is a good thing. But can it be a bad thing?

                                                            Well, yeah, the answer is yes. The better question is "How widely can bias vary an opinion, and at what point can that bias unfairly swing other's opinions?"

                                                            I'm not going to read Genius again for a while, and really I don't want to read anything, but, you know, I think I have a subject we can talk about. Something that, really, honestly, could be okay or could be terrible, depending purely on your bias.

                                                            See you tomorrow.
