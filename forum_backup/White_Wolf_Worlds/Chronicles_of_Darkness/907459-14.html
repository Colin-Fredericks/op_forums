                                                    Eh. To me, part of the point of every individual table/group/session is that you're never going to perfectly render RPG systems into an actual play where every system is relevant and has the exact impact that their designer wanted. So, IMO, you never play RAW; it's just a spectrum of how much you acknowledge that and how much effort/formality you put into your hack. Tweaking the rules as you go feels very much like part of the point of playing a TTRPG rather than, say, Ocarina of Time.

                                                    But I also developed a hatred from rules lawyering very, very early on and that probably informs a lot of my perspective. My general attitude when approaching TTRPGs is that the text is an inspirational piece of art, much like a novel or painting. The first thing to do is to engage with the text itself and figure out what the author wants to say, because to me, that's the part that matters. The second thing to do is to use your appreciation or criticism of the text as a basis for what you do yourself. It's extremely nonsensical to me that feel entitled to systems designed by someone else that help tell a story they want to tell: my response to a reading of Lord of the Rings might be to argue that Sauron shouldn't have been such a vague, amorphous villain, but I'm not entitled to ask Tolkien to change his text to suit my taste when reading it out loud to an audience; I'm only entitled to tell my own story in my own way and take from his text what I want to keep and discard what I'm not interested in.

                                                    I call the Integrity-analogue the "subjective stat".

                                                        I change things, but I feel rules are there to facilitate play and it is fine changing it if your group wants,but rule changes have to be upfront or players can feel duped. To me, a game isn't fun if I'm the only one telling the story, and because of that, the players need to know what I'm changing. If they signed up for a clear Evil Sauron, than I'm kinda cheating their play by taking that away.

                                                        Our story doesn't have to be entitled to anyone, but I can't view it as just mine or that can ruin the fun for the others. Been in several groups that when the ST changed something dramatic about the world, players would go out of their way to avoid it if not talked about beforehand, because we didn't sign up for that. It can turn into some of the worst railroading, as even shared conceptions are at risk. One ST for WtF wanted to have lupus in the game, but mentioned the fact they were everywhere after the game started, even telling the Blood Talon they had a hard time being non-lupus. We had been open before about finding Lupus weird and uncomfortable, so the game became our wacky adventures of trying to find out where this mass Uratha delusion came from. It was a fun game.

                                                        I feel changing most mechanical rules doesn't have the same effect, but in WoD so much of the mechanics interact with the world. A player may build their character one way to find that their idea is void or other some such. Even something pure mechanics can be frustrating for players. Once made a character who I wanted to be great at classic wrestling, built them in a way that wouldn't give me an advantage. The first time I tried to grapple, the DM told me they didn't like the grappling rules so there wouldn't be any. When I complained, he said I could roll it like a normal attack, but I made the character so I could have a different combat experience and the DM was unfair not to make that clear from the beginning.

                                                        guess what I am saying, is it's important everyone is on board with the rules or there can be disgruntled feelings as the ST forces a story the players may not have signed up forG
