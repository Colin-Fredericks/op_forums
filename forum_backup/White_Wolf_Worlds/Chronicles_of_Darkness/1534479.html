    Compatibility between 1ed and 2ed

    04-20-2024, 02:48 AM
    I was reading the 2ed Core WtF book, and I felt like the lore information on the tribes and auspices was not enough for me, specially on the case of the Storm Lords (and the Cahaliths, but a little less).

    I want to know how much the 1ed supplements could help me from a lore point of view. As I understand it, 2ed made many changes on the tribes, like the addition of favorite prey. How big were those changes? Should I read the 1ed supplements to have a better understanding of the tribe, or are those informations obsolete as of 2ed?

    My biggest grips are the favorite prey (I still fail to see how a Ridden don't fit anymore as a Spirit for the Bone Shadows, and their opinions on the Storm Lords seems to agree with me), exactly what "weakness" means (their story on getting the favor of Winter Wolf felt like a useless and unneeded death), and I'm still unclear on what the tribe strives for. Like, don't show weakness to rule by example sounds like it's not working, since my impression is that the other tribes don't respect them much?

    With the Cahaliths I just got the impression that in the end they are just the guys who want attention because they envy the Rahu.

    I would really appreciate any help, inputs or opinions.

      Werewolf did change quite a bit between first and second edition. For the Tribes they added the favourite prey, which does recontextualize them quite a bit. There was a "Secrets of the Tribes" book planned for 2e at one point, but it never materialized so we have to go with what we have.

      I think the best place to look for more information on the Tribes is the 1e book "Tribes of the Moon". Most of what it says is still relevant. For example, of the Storm Lords it says not to think of them as self-important and arrogant. That can be how the other Tribes see them, and it's a perspective the Storm Lords allow or even encourage. However, within the tribe themselves their viewpoint is something much more like noblesse oblige. They are there to be the rock - unshaken when other Tribes falter. They don't promise Winter Wolf never to fail, just never to let others (especially their pack mates) see them fail. Often this comes across as the Storm Lords becoming the Alpha in the pack - being that inspirational leader who never gives up. But it can just as easily manifest as a hard-as-nails Rahu soldier, who always gets the job done no matter the cost.

      In terms of their favored prey, Ridden is a bit of an odd fit for the Storm Lords. However I see it as a hatred of weakness. After all, to become possessed or Claimed by a Spirit is a weakness, and rather than keeping it hidden, the Claimed wear their host's weakness and failure visibly for the whole world to see.

      Cahaliths do envy the Rahu, but that's because they are all about Glory. The reason for this though is that the Cahalith are storytellers. They care about the stories that will be told about them around the campfire and how their name and their deeds will ring out to future generations. The Rahu, by contrast, care about the deed itself which is why they tend to achieve more impressive feats than the Cahalith.

      Cahaliths are not defined by this envy though. Like I said they are the storytellers, bards, singers, or whatever other form of expression you can think of. This is essential to the Pack. After all, achieving renown doesn't just require that you perform a worthy deed. You need the Lunes to carve the Renown marks into your flesh and since the Lunes weren't there to see your deeds, someone needs to tell your story and make it sound suitably impressive. This is where the Cahalith steps in. They will strip the story down to its most impressive essence and give is a suitable narrative structure and flowery descriptions. They also know when not to let facts get in the way of a good tale. Without the Cahalith, the pack will often struggle to convince the Lunes of their worthiness, no matter how mighty their deeds.

          Signs of the Moon from 1e will likewise be helpful in getting the headspace for the auspices.

          The Ridden are an example of cascade failure to be dealt with; in order for things to get that bad, a spirit had to successfully cross the Gauntlet, find a host, and overcome their resistance to at minimum be a whisper in their ear if not outright subvert the flesh to supernatural expression. If you're a local werewolf and the Urged are in your territory, it is a sign you have likely failed to keep your sacred spaces from being used to cross over and maintain an environment that allows the Herd to be Resolved and Composed — doubly so given that 2e introduces at least a couple of mechanics that translate to "Lunacy makes you more susceptible to spirits." Even outside that context, allowing temporary Resonance to build up fosters fixations and the 1e core made an explicit point in its Storytelling chapter that narratively spirits represent consequences, with special attention given to the Shadow's ability to expose things that go on behind closed doors in the material world, with a specific example relating to their tie to the community:

          Originally posted by WtF Core p225
          As an element of horror, spirits inherently represent consequences. When a murderer stalks a city block, killing again and again without being caught, the dangerous spirits that gain strength from his spree are a consequence. They directly represent the ramifications of the murderer’s actions, but they also represent the consequences of others’ failure to catch and stop the murderer. A community that has a secret history of hate may have engendered a powerful spirit threat, one that emphasizes the enormity of their brutal actions when the community’s quiet façade gives no hint. Most spirits represent the weakness and selfishness of human action, but some also reflect the failings of the Uratha themselves. In the animistic world of Werewolf, spirits are a direct reminder that the evil that goes unseen is just as gruesome a threat. Every action has its consequence, and the pack must be consistently vigilant to fight against those consequences.
          The second story of Winter Wolf concerns endurance as a trait that he values; in case the mechanic of Renown and the recurring line of "this story is true" didn't make it evident, werewolf culture is a storytelling culture with a lot of emphasis on legendary exemplification of virtues. The hunter set out to kill the monster and accomplished it at the cost of a mortal wound, then rebuffed all efforts to make her return easier on herself — in this story Storm Wolf is an incidental bystander to the hunt whose aid would only lessen the impact of her either making it back from the hunt or dying in the attempt. It would be a story with a more pragmatic lesson, but it wouldn't be True to the ideals of the tribe; there is no Forsaken society in the time of the story, and the hunter accomplished the goal she stopped at nothing to achieve — if she had been even better she would have slain the monster without dying herself, which is the incentive for listening Storm Lords to improve or perish, as the Off The Hunt section of the writeup words it.

          (A minor notable feature of First Tongue being derived backwards from Ancient Sumerian and Beast: the Primordial using Family names largely derived from mythological figures from ancient cultures is that one can spot how the Storm Lords' twist on the Oath translates "weakness" from a word that is related to a demon of disease. The necessity of Father Wolf's death and continued efforts to carry out the hunt in Forsaken lore is pretty clearly resonant with the Storm Lords' philosophy.)

          The Cahalith likewise directly link to the storytelling tendencies of werewolf culture, specifically as a tool for motivation and a means of preserving memory — the Shadow talks to itself and humans have an entire branch of the inner cosmology that is mostly barred to the Forsaken, and the Gibbous Moons grant words the weight of narrative, because this is the part where you run away has real impact in a setting built on emotion and established patterns. The tension between being empowered by recognition for your memorable feats and being given the spiritual talents of prophecy and inspiration instead of being empowered by your adherence to the central pillar of your culture's values and given spiritual talents that facilitate the most prominent action in the cultural duty is where that cliché of Cahalith envying Rahu comes from, because stereotypes are pervasive and there's a marked difference between being the hype man for the less-flashy gifts of the "support" auspices and watching the destined living superweapon carve through a horde of spider-people. It doesn't consume the Gibbous Moons' thoughts by any stretch of the imagination, but it's hard to deny the pieces of the dynamic are there.

                  Originally posted by Ulbrecht View Post
                  As social creatures, the Uratha should be covering each others weaknesses, not hiding it from allies.
                  Remember that this is a setting with no small degree of conspiracy and a game built with character flaws as a mechanically enshrined aspect of play — the Scions of Urfarah are werewolves, with all that implies, including "flaring Renown invites witnesses to challenge you," "if you don't hunt regularly you will be gradually cut off from the spirit world and rendered more prone to berserk fury," and "you likely hunt things that can kill the kind of people you grew up around."

                  The story is an extreme example of dedication to the principle, but as a game mechanic it's there to make it so that Storm Lord characters will strive to eliminate discernible weaknesses from themselves and be image-conscious (their affinity Renown is Honor for a reason) or grapple with whatever inadequacies they didn't manage to hide through their initiation with or without someone else's help.

                  The Uratha are social creatures, yes, but they're also not-quite-human wolf-people hardwired to hunt and kill gods, reborn in a physically and spiritually traumatic moment that may have killed one or more of their loved ones, bonded with other monsters they might not have known before through shared adversity, and tied their essence to the fixations of one or more ephemeral obsessives from a world where dog-eat-dog is the law of the land.

                  Everyone's got their core tenets to cling to in the face of a dubious and fearsome world, and other werewolves and loved ones are not less frightening to trust with vulnerabilities in a world where haunting spirits can read their minds. It's not too out there as a coping strategy for a werewolf to decide hardening themselves is the best way to ward off trouble.

                  I still have too many problems with the Ridden though, and since their part as favorite prey of the Iminir was not present during the 1ed, the reading has not helped with that. In the end, they still feel like they're just an specific type of other tribe's prey.
                  Ridden are broadly a prelude to Claimed, which present a different set of problems to spirits.

                  The Bone Shadows' favored prey respects a metaphysical framework built on give-and-take and even the ones who jump the fence can often anchor themselves without parasitizing another sentient being. The Seekers can hunt their favored prey without ever entering the Flesh, which tilts their vibes and practices in a different direction than the tribe who thinks creatures possessed by demons represent a greater danger.

                  The Storm Lords' favored prey is a violation of boundaries in two acts that at bare minimum puts an unallied supernatural infiltrator with a cheat-sheet among the Herd and may further give an overreaching spirit-noble the ability to come and go as they please with powers they did not originally have. It's like the Hosts with less identifying mess.

                  It keeps bothering me that the Cahalith are seem as a strictly inferior version of the Rahu.
                  They're completely different roles. Any given auspice is going to look inferior at the job of the other auspice without dedicated effort. The Rahu just happen to be the most visible to the degree that if not for the Pure they'd be "the werewolves everybody thinks of when you say 'werewolf'" because their role is specifically violent and they draw strength from the full moon.

                  The Gibbous Moons are who you get when you want a werewolf that is a monster from a horror story. They're the morale and psychological warfare specialists to the Elodoth's diplomats and logisticians. You bring them in to win hearts and minds and freak the enemy out on purpose.

                  It gets to the point that I feel it turns people away from playing them.
                  They're one of my favorite auspices, at least, and I know I'm not alone in that.

Mechanically, breaking a tenet of the Oath is a breaking point toward Flesh that can be compensated for by taking steps to reconnect with one's spirit half and make restitution for the loss of face — at a sufficiently dramatic scale you may even be able to spin the conflict into a basis for earning Renown.

                              The comparative lack of 2e books for Werewolf specifically causes problems on this front, but the introduction of the Hive-Claimed and the fact that idigam can make Claimed as part of their general disruptive toolkit.

                              The important things for the Storm Lords, as concerns their tribal ban and the general overview, are that the Ridden and the Claimed represent a worst-case scenario as far as a pack's housekeeping optics and every single possession-oriented Manifestation turned on a living being allows the spirit to read the host's mind.

                              If a duguthim has shown up, you do not have your territory under control. If your packmate or loved one knows your weaknesses and something Fetters itself to them (or worse), a spirit now knows it too, and the well-documented near-immortality of spirits means even after you kill or exorcise the host you have an ephemeral little bastard who can share and/or exploit that knowledge like the vengeful ghost of the monster it turned your buddy into. Father Wolf's death and the decision that killing him was necessary is the foundational myth of Forsaken culture and the reasons for that decision are that he was getting weaker and the prey he'd previously kept in check was running rampant because of it; the Storm Lords take that lesson to heart.

                                              Originally posted by Ulbrecht View Post
                                              If the Ridden/Claimed appearing is a worst case scenario, should I assume that the Iminir spend more time hunting other preys that are not it's favorite? Theoretically speaking, if the Bone Shadows and the Iron Masters "did their jobs right" the Storm Lords wouldn't need to worry about the duguthim?
                                              The thing to remember about the introduction of favored prey in 2e is that werewolves still hunt other things. You can be a Blood Talon pack and never call the Hunt on another werewolf, you can be a Meninna and never run afoul of the Hosts, and if you're a Storm Lord and you never see Ridden on your territory you're probably doing something right with your patrols and networking.

                                              Keeping humans and spirits from coming into close contact with each other is fundamental preventative goal for both carrying out the Forsaken's duty in general and keeping spirits from Riding and Claiming the Flesh in the first place, but the nature of the Shadow and hidden spaces in general is such that the Storm Lords will probably still worry about the duguthim on general principle — vigilance and image-consciousness are both big parts of Honor as a duty-bound territory-keeping predator — but yes, if your pack's resident experts in hunting humans and spirits have a handle on their respective specialties you should be able to focus on setting a good example and honing yourself.

                                              In the book there is a commentary about the Geists being a type of Claimed. Is that a larger view between Uratha in general, just among the Tribe, or just a pack/indivudual interpretation?
                                              It's a somewhat common interpretation by Storm Lords, partially because Claimed are weird and werewolves are busybodies and partially because from a raw metaphysical standpoint the distinctions can get kind of dicey — the Bound don't have a spirit blending with their soul on account of how dying means they don't have souls anymore and a geist is a ghost rather than a spirit, but "partial physical/mental merger between an incomprehensible ephemeral being and what was once a human, with overt supernatural powers included" is something werewolves have been interpreting as a Claimed since Signs of the Moon.

                                              There's a type of supernatural being whose situation bears some conceptual similarity to the Bargain in Skinchangers referred to as the Siten Uzu, which is described as "the exact opposite of Claimed" but nevertheless uses many of the same systems terms used for Claimed in Predators, and looking through Werewolf 1e's booklist is in some ways an exercise in spotting the ways werewolves will interpret a supernatural phenomenon as something within their usual categorization schema to justify messing with it, most commonly in the form of describing an unfamiliar monster as an unusual type of Claimed or Host.

                                              It's a thing the Uratha do in general, much like changelings and demons may speculate that some local occult weirdness has its roots in the Fae or the God-Machine at enough of a remove that it doesn't ping the normal senses for those phenomena. This is what happens when you're a monster in a world full of different monsters and your main storytelling convention is one or two degrees of separation from the framing devices for urban legends.
