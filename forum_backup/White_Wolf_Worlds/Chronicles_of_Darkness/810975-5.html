        The best use of Tiers as a useful concept to players and Storytellers I've seen was one about 1e New World of Darkness splats from a while back on rpg.net. The basic idea of the thread was a grouping roughly under an idea of raw mechanical power as a way of helping guide people when it came to potential cross overs. The idea being as long as you were within one tier up or down in a cross over you were in a general sense ok without the sheer mechanical prowess of a particular splat running roughshod over another. Where as if you got into the territory of 2 or more tiers of difference you were going to start to run into issues of player agency.

                            Originally posted by PenDragon View Post
                            Difficult? No. I'm saying it's flat out impossible. I mean, sure you can make a tier list, and maybe it will be valuable to a handful of people. But it won't mean anything, not practically. Making a tier list for CofD is kinda like trying to tier True Blood, The X-Files, Supernatural, The Invisibles, and Once Upon A Time together.

                            What metric do we even use? Versatility? Mages are gonna rock the house there. Combat? That almost certainly means white rooms, and grotesque Werewolf overkill. Will the tier list account for different experience levels? Integrity-stat degeneration? Will it address different game types? Because a vampiric politics setting is gonna have a mighty different tier list than an on-the-run Demonic spy-thriller.
                            Like all tier lists you have to define the metric you're using. This isn't that complicated. Have you seen the 3.5 Tier list?

                            Tier 1: Capable of doing absolutely everything, often better than classes that specialize in that thing. Often capable of solving encounters with a single mechanical ability and little thought from the player. Has world changing powers at high levels. These guys, if played well, can break a campaign and can be very hard to challenge without extreme DM fiat, especially if Tier 3s and below are in the party.

                            Tier 2: Has as much raw power as the Tier 1 classes, but can't pull off nearly as many tricks, and while the class itself is capable of anything, no one build can actually do nearly as much as the Tier 1 classes. Still potentially campaign smashers by using the right abilities, but at the same time are more predictable and can't always have the right tool for the job. If the Tier 1 classes are countries with 10,000 nuclear weapons in their arsenal, these guys are countries with 10 nukes. Still dangerous and world shattering, but not in quite so many ways. Note that the Tier 2 classes are often less flexible than Tier 3 classes... it's just that their incredible potential power overwhelms their lack in flexibility.

                            Tier 3: Capable of doing one thing quite well, while still being useful when that one thing is inappropriate, or capable of doing all things, but not as well as classes that specialize in that area. Occasionally has a mechanical ability that can solve an encounter, but this is relatively rare and easy to deal with. Challenging such a character takes some thought from the DM, but isn't too difficult. Will outshine any Tier 5s in the party much of the time.

                            Tier 4: Capable of doing one thing quite well, but often useless when encounters require other areas of expertise, or capable of doing many things to a reasonable degree of competance without truly shining. Rarely has any abilities that can outright handle an encounter unless that encounter plays directly to the class's main strength. DMs may sometimes need to work to make sure Tier 4s can contribue to an encounter, as their abilities may sometimes leave them useless. Won't outshine anyone except Tier 6s except in specific circumstances that play to their strengths. Cannot compete effectively with Tier 1s that are played well.

                            Tier 5: Capable of doing only one thing, and not necessarily all that well, or so unfocused that they have trouble mastering anything, and in many types of encounters the character cannot contribute. In some cases, can do one thing very well, but that one thing is very often not needed. Has trouble shining in any encounter unless the rest of the party is weak in that situation and the encounter matches their strengths. DMs may have to work to avoid the player feeling that their character is worthless unless the entire party is Tier 4 and below. Characters in this tier will often feel like one trick ponies if they do well, or just feel like they have no tricks at all if they build the class poorly.

                            Tier 6: Not even capable of shining in their own area of expertise. DMs will need to work hard to make encounters that this sort of character can contribute in with their mechanical abilities. Will often feel worthless unless the character is seriously powergamed beyond belief, and even then won't be terribly impressive. Needs to fight enemies of lower than normal CR. Class is often completely unsynergized or with almost no abilities of merit. Avoid allowing PCs to play these characters.

                            Now to be fair, the bottom tiers are almost certainly not going to apply. Even taking into account Minor Templates and base mortals I don't think anything is likely to ever hit lower than Tier 4, probably even Tier 3. This game simply lacks the downward power variance of 3.5, which is why a dedicated tier list for this game should probably have more focused tiers than 3.5 did due to having less power variation. So no, it isn't impossible. Hell, while not ideal, even this could work. Looking up the 5e Tier list, they've not got anything below Tier 3, so it isn't like knocking off a couple of tiers or a tighter power variation somehow makes this impossible.

                            Those games have social situations with just as many potential variables as this one and they managed to pull it off, so I don't want to hear about it being somehow impossible here. You may not want to spend the effort in coming up with it, which is fine, and is your choice, but don't try to pretend it just can't be done.

                                The D&D tier list, which I'm very familiar with, is built on the assumption that all the characters will form a party, and run through combat heavy, D&D style encounters. And there's nothing wrong with that, that's how D&D is expected to be played. Mind you, I've played games with Wizards who were considerably worse than the party Fighter, and Rogues who were on par with the average sorcerer. But if what you're looking for is a Tier list that ranks the splats based on the assumption that they are all collectively working as a party to defeat Storyteller placed obstacles, I can probably gander a decent one:

                                I'm gonna put this in spoilers, so people read my post before they assume I've suddenly flipped to the idea of Tiers.

                                Tier 1 (The 'Demigod' Tier): Mages/Demons - Both Mages and Demons have supreme flexibility, Mages through Creative Thaumaturgy, and Demons through their more open ended Embeds and Exploits (Looking at you Shift Consequence & The Word). More importantly both splats can solve challenges without ever going near them, and are difficult to find even if you are another Mage/Demon. Both have the potential to invite cosmic superpowers onto the field that other splats would struggle to contend with, and have Masquerade effects (Quiessence & Spoofing) that passively hide them from the public eye.

                                Tier 2 (The 'Oops, Bye' Tier): Changelings/Sin-Eaters/Beasts - All three splats are nearly invisible, indistinguishable from normal humans until placed under considerable magical scrutiny. Each is also equipped with a host of fantastic template traits, and few self control issues. Most importantly, while not as versatile as Mages and Demons, all three are equipped with powerful Get Out of Jail Free effects, and have mostly free reign with their supernatural powers.

                                Tier 3 (The 'You Won't Like Me When I'm Angry' Tier): Werewolves/Mummies - Basically the same as Tier 2, save that these splats come with stupendous drawbacks (Kuruth & Descent) that constantly threaten to force the character into dangerous situations. While both lack run-away buttons on the level of Tier 2 (from a utilitarian perspective Mummies lose more from dying than a Sin-Eater does), they are extremely durable when pressed and capable of incredible burst power. Both Werewolves and Mummies also assume the presence of copious NPC allies, which in a normal game is certainly a boon, but in a tier list measuring their individual power among a group of supernaturals is mostly a setback.

                                Tier 4 (The 'If It Weren't For All These Banes' Tier): Vampires/Prometheans - Both splats, while possessed of formidable supernatural abilities, suffer from drawbacks so extreme as to dramatically limit their utility (again, in an objective based cohesive party game). Vampires suffer during the day, and Prometheans suffer in crowds. Both also labor under the 'anger issues' clause of Tier 3, but without the astonishing mechanical overdrive that makes Kuruth so powerful.

                                I won't bother with the many mini splats (though I'd probably put the Purified up in Tier 2, with the other splats good at taking multiple tries to solve a problem), or Hunters (who would probably kick Vampires and Prometheans down to Tier 5, thanks to not suffering terrible banes).

                                There. IMO that's a pretty convincing D&D style Tier list, and one I'd argue is largely accurate...assuming everyone is playing a utilitarian problem solving machine and not an actual human being with hopes, fears and interests.

                                        IMO that's the value of a tier list. It's easy to think of challenges and stories for same-splat chronicles. However, there are some pitfalls involving crossovers that aren't immediately apparent.
                                        I'll agree with you that Tier lists tend to highlight this issue (and blow it wildly out of proportion), but frankly that is something a Storyteller should already be doing. A socialite Carthian is going to be just as out of place in a game of cruac wielding crones as he is in a game of Mystagogue lore divers. Sure the exact nature of the issue will be slightly different, but no less prominent.

                                        For crossover one would be far better served by a guide to the themes, expertise and restrictions of each splat (ala MCN's post on page 4) than a list rating them on a 1 - X scale.

                                        Edit: If I were constructing a tiering of splat based on ability to ruin everyone's fun it'd probably look like:

                                        Tier 1: Beast - They are literally encouraged to fuck with other splats, driving them towards the same bestial depravity Beasts themselves glorify. How this was ever deemed the crossover splat I'll never know. This falls to Tier 6 if the player plays a particularly bland, unoffensive beast.

                                        Tier 2: Werewolf/Promethean - One bad Kuruth or a little too much Disquiet can collapse into total, involuntary party murder. Otherwise great team players, but it just takes one bad roll.

                                        Tier 3: Mage/Demon - Both can run rampant, but that's more or less a player problem. And unlike Beasts they aren't encouraged to fuck over their fellows. True they have the potential to invoke cosmic horrors, but those risks can be consciously mitigated and the antagonists are storyteller controlled.

                                        Tier 4: Mummy - Mummies aren't equipped with the same accidental gamesplosian powers as other splats, but their extremely objective oriented nature and bizarre powerstat can make them difficult to integrate and shift the focus of the game towards their own descent.

                                        Tier 5: Vampire/Changeling/Sin-Eater - All three can upend the game, but unlike Beasts they are not encouraged to, and unlike Tier 2-3 it's unlikely to trigger on them involuntarily (Frenzy maybe, but that's easier to weather without everyone dying).


                                        Edit #2: And if I were making a tiering for which splats can be the best crossover buddies...

                                        Tier 1: Mage/Demon - The same flexibility that makes them the utilitarian power gamer's best friend, also shines in the hand of the party friendly player. Used to supplement their allies, instead of invalidating them, Mages & Demons can shower their friends with buffs, merits, and tools to ease their way through life.

                                        Tier 2: Werewolf/Mummy - Who doesn't like a friend who also comes with a host of allies/cultists, protected territory/tomb and neigh invulnerable meat shield. They can also protect you from Spirits and Ghosts respectively, which is nice.

                                        Tier 3: Changelings/Beasts - Pledges, family dinner, dice boons and social smoothing. What's not to like. They can also sneak you out of a tight corner, albeit into a no less dangerous corner (the hedge/their lair).

                                        Tier 4: Vampires/Sin-Eaters - Vampires could easily be tier 2, with a cozy haven and loyal bodyguards. Same with Sin-Eaters and their ghastly (meant here as a complement) krewe. But as neither are base assumptions of the splat, they sit down here.

                                        Tier 5: Prometheans - Prometheans are shitty friends. They make your house smell and your friends avoid you. They are probably stealing from you as well, and talking behind your back. Why do you even talk to them? Hell, why do you suffer them to live? Burn them. BUUUUUUURN!!!!!
