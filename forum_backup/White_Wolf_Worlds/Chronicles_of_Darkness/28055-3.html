                                            It's very easy to win, in these cases. Don't write token characters. Almost all of these are about tokenism in one form or another.

                                            Well, for token: You can write minority characters without including them only because they're minorities. You can write them without being stereotypical.

                                            I don't think it's too much to ask nor particularly hard.

                                            For Minority Decay, don't kill off all the people of color or banish them unimportant land. Emphasis on all because that's the key point. When practically all the people of color or LGBT characters get killed off, leaving the white cis whatever protagonists, that's where one might call foul. The two feed into eachother as far as Tokenism goes, because Token characters aren't terribly compelling.

                                            Inclusion Cookies is when the token character was included specifically to address complaints about erasure or the lack of diversity. I don't really think this one needs to be explained, but it's basically, if you've been told that your show or book lacks diversity or whatever, including a character who is a stereotype and solely for the purpose of assuaging those complaints is tokenism and insulting.

                                            Bonnie: This one might be a bit trickier, but it's essentially the idea that diversity of skin tone with no acknowledgement of culture or background or anything like that is just another form of tokenism, and that if you're including people of color, it pays to acknowledge the fact that they are people of color, without reminding the reader of it every five seconds. I feel like this one can be a pitfall, because if you think too hard about it, you'll feel stilted, but if you don't think about it at all, it's paying lip service to the idea of diversity rather than actual diversity.

                                            I really think there's a tendency for people to overreact to this sort of criticism as "It's impossible to please you people!" but that's a mistake. You wouldn't want to write a cliche character for any other reason, so why would you want to write one who is LGBT or a person of color?
