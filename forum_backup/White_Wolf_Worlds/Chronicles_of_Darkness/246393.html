    Fairy Tales and Ghost Stories - Shared Likenesses of Fae and the Dead

    09-01-2014, 06:45 PM
    Lacking the mental energy to arrange these into discrete paragraphs, found below is a bulleted list of some observations of similarities I've accumulated in the course of reading Changeling, Geist, and other instances of ghosts throughout the WoD.

    • Glamour stemming from both emotion and supernatural bonds/bindings - anchors provide Essence and can be either things that emotionally resonate with the ghost or things that some supernatural agency have bound it to (ostensibly under the same clause that gives some anchors the subtag of "unfinished business").
    • The Underworld and the Hedge both utilize portals as their primary means of entrance and egress, in contrast to the Shadow and the Astral's more typical "change of perspective" mechanisms.
    • Trods, for what little mention their Earthside manifestations have received, are framed as founts of Glamour - an open Avernian Gate gives ghosts within its proximity enough Essence to last the scene without being drawn back inside.
    • Mirrors and false images as a stock in trade.
    • Book of the Dead posits a strange predilection among the dead for games - chess with Death, etc?
    • The sometime consequences of bringing a person back from the Underworld resonate with the subtle differences between a fetch and its basis.
    • Mazes and labyrinths between gods and men.
    • Cruel and exacting gods who deal in "equivalent" exchange, often of the bizarre or terrible variety.
    • A predilection toward enslavement of "lesser" beings, including mortals.
    • Powerful passions and emotions as a means of staving off fading out of sentience.
    • Described impact of Fae Dwindling similar to examples of ghosts without emotional or supernatural investment.
    • The effects of disuse upon regions of Faerie are that the place takes on an aspect of death and decay - the Underworld's denizens bear their deathmarks more overtly than their anchored cousins.
    • Gravewights and burial mounds and Maya Sharptongue's point of egress.
    • The Grave Dream, Et in Arcadia ego.
    • One mythic origin of the Nosferatu in their clanbook says they became what they are (rather than just vampires) by consumption of the essence of the beings in the Underworld; the same book includes a changeling calling one of the Dragons his "second Keeper" and makes note of Nosferatu feeding habits including the draining of blood from sleeping persons.

    There are almost certainly others. Anyone care to note them or comment upon the above list?

      A latecomer or three:

      • Keepers and Kerberoi weaker nearer to the mortal realm.
      • Formation of a Court or krewe giving founders a free dot of Mantle/Status, irrevocably tying them to the group, and removing some degree of free will through the involvement of fate.
      • Ruled By Passion and Vice Addiction/2e ghost WP recovery mechanics.
      • Gentry and the Kerberoi as extensions of their domains.
      • Definite or theoretical use of intruding supernaturals as a means of expansion (Becoming Nightmare and the Kerberoi-as-former-Sin-Eaters theory).
      • Briarwolves and the posited pedigree of some of the guardians of Tartarus.
      • Explicit ontological ambiguity of Fae and Kerberos.
      • Existence of an overt threshold between deep Hedge and Arcadia loosely analogous to the first river of the Underworld separating Erebus from Tartarus (explicit example of a threshold guardian to the Fae is a Cerberus).

                          Leshy protects the animals and birds in the forest and tells them when to migrate. He can shapeshift into many different forms. As a human, he looks like a peasant with glowing eyes, and his shoes are on backwards. A person who befriends a leshy can learn the secrets of magic. Leshies aren't evil: although they enjoy misguiding humans and kidnapping young women, they are also known to keep grazing cattle from wandering too far into the forests and getting lost. Sometimes cow herders will make pacts with a leshy by handing him their crosses from around their necks and sharing communion with him after Christian church gatherings; these pacts are said to give the cowherds special powers.

                          Sometimes more than one leshy inhabits a forest, and then they will fight for their territory, knocking down trees and scaring animals.                                   • The End of the Road is theorized to be an (if not necessarily the) Underworld and effectively turns the player characters from living to dead without the intervening step of actually dying; its guiding intelligence is described as inherently mean-spirited and childlike, it "dimly and instinctively craves stimulation, a feeling of belonging and participation in something greater than itself," creates flawed doppelgängers of the PCs, is capable of retrieving the characters from their escape attempts "as though they walked away in a dream and [woke] up in the exact spot in which they fell asleep," and causes anything in the scene that isn't a player character or a doppelganger thereof to gradually begin to look decayed or degraded when the PCs aren't paying attention to them.

                                          Mostly I just made this thread as a collection ground since a couple months ago it became readily apparent that my primary method of highlighting these sorts of connections is not terribly secure against accidental total deletion.

                                          A more esoteric one:

                                          • Mummy's Guildhalls of the Deathless chapter on the Mesen-Nebu (i.e. the alchemists' guild) talks about how alchemy is payment, and the sidebar on the Subtle Bazaar mentions how after the 10th century writings on the supposed allegory reference it as though it were a literal place, managed by jinn, trading strange things and running the risk of getting what they wanted with a nasty twist if their bargain was bad. Geist's four Elemental Keys explicitly draw from alchemical symbolism and share a Key Skill with the powertype revolving around one of the most basic kinds of supernatural prices (as the vampires know Damned well), to say nothing of the earlier mentioned chthonic equivalent-exchange laws.

                                          Mummy's corebook section on the alchemists also talks about a curiosity of the force their alchemy manipulates:
                                          The lords of Irem made one house out of its smiths, jewelers, and mine overseers. The Shan’iatu revealed that they all unlocked the alchemical essence: Dedwen, or “prosperity.” Modern philosophers might call Dedwen “utility” because it symbolizes an occult principle of value that transcends particular applications. The alchemists knew that all material creation harvested Dedwen and urged it toward a particular manifestation.
                                          […]
                                          The alchemists suffer from a weakness for beauty, for it is the most enigmatic form of Dedwen. Material strength, wealth, and political prestige have straightforward uses. Esthetic properties are powerful for reasons that lie at the edge of the alchemists’ understanding. […] One Born of Gold also appreciates the beauty of skillful actions. An archer striking her mark creates Dedwen in excellence. It is said that high alchemy manipulates esthetic value along with material properties—that in sacrifice, a beautiful servant or great swordsman makes a blade sharper.
                                          Cut back to Changeling, and…
                                          Originally posted by Introduction: A Game of Beautiful Madness
                                          Faerie is beautiful. It isn’t kindly, or nurturing or benevolent, but it is wondrously beautiful. The same is true for its children, both those who were born of its unreal matter and those mortals who were abducted and nursed on its magic. Even a hideous Ogre may have some strangely sketched artistry to its asymmetrical features, and even a Darkling of disturbing mien may have an elegantly hypnotic grace or cold, frank sexuality. But as the Lost move among the mortal world, trying to recover their old lives or draw enough Glamour to sustain themselves, they become aware of the beautiful things that mortals often take for granted. To a changeling, there is beauty in the grief hanging over the funeral of a good man, or in the awkward way a young girl twists her hands at a school dance. They see things nobody else does — not simply because they can, but because they try.
                                          Originally posted by Chapter 4: Storytelling
                                          Beauty can come from the strangest places, or things that don’t normally suggest beauty can in fact become beautiful in their function. Consider the savage beauty of a shark or lion, for example, and recall the “terrible” beauty of the witch-queen Jadis from The Chronicles of Narnia. Think about the beauty of loss — a virtuoso composition of a dirge, a masterfully painted memorial of a hero long gone, the turning of the leaves in autumn before the dead season. Recall a significant break-up: It was hell to go through, but aren’t you glad she’s gone now?
                                          The diversity of resources the fae can draw upon and ask for is always a fun balancing act to contemplate, I think.

                                          And while we're on the subject of similarities not specific to the World of Darkness…

                                          • "Don't speak ill of the dead" is curiously similar in practice to the conventions of not calling the fae by unflattering names.
