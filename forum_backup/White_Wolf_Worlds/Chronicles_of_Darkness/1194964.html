    How is CoD played?

    03-13-2018, 04:37 PM
    I stumbled across this and I agree.

    https://forum.rpg.net/showthread.php...pically-played

    Specifically the fifth poster:
    By what the books claim to want, Vampire is intended to be these character-focused stories about struggling to avoid becoming a monster, while navigating and manuevering through a dangerous political landscape.

    But then the game fails to support any of that mechanically in any way that really makes it usable without the group having to do all the work themselves.

    And about half of the clans are combat-oriented.

    So typically, Vampire just kind of turns into a bunch of violence (often involving like gangs and the Sabbat and stuff), basically DnD-style murderhobos, but in a dark and broody modern city instead of in a dungeon.

    Vampire is kind of tbh a really big example of a total failure of game design, because of the fact that the game wants something, fails to reinforce that thing through anything but words and lore dumps, and then people play it differently and the designers are confused on what they did wrong.



    Unfortunately yes. The nWoD or now the Chronicles of Darkness just does this with a more streamlined and coherent rule-set. The latest 2nd edition books for Vampire the Requiem, etc., incorporate some narrative game elements. But they feel pasted on top of the existing Storyteller System rules. I'm talking about the new Aspiration, Beat, Conditions system. I think the additions are a step in the right direction in terms of fulfilling the promise in the prefaces of all the games.

    Here is literally the first paragraph out of character in the New World of Darkness Core book.

    This book presents rules for playing a type of roleplaying game called Storytelling. In this type of game, the traditional elements of a story -- theme, mood, plot and character -- are more important than the rules themselves. The rules serve to help you tell stories about your characters in an interactive experience. They help prevent arguments and provide a solid basis for handling elements of chance, but they don't overshadow the story itself. The triumphs and tragedies of your characters as they try to survive and even thrive in the World of Darkness are the main focus, not dice rolls or lists of traits.

    It then proceeds to give a wealth of crunch dealing with firing guns in close combat, etc.
    How would people here agree or disagree with this?

    Thanks!

              One of the things about the CofD, is that it exists (and I think most of us fans like it here) in a strange place most RPGs reject. Most RPGs are either focused on a very specific playstyle (from good old D&D to something really different like Dread), or try to be as general as possible (GURPS or Fate are both designed to be whatever you want them to be if in very different approaches). The CofD, and the WoD before it, have always tried to balance a strange medium between those two places. The CofD doesn't try to be everything. There's game styles it clearly doesn't cater to. But it style aims to be a bigger tent than more focused games. The CofD can do a lot of different modes of play well, as long as you stick somewhere in the horror/urban fantasy/maybe low fantasy alternate setting areas.

              This does, however, make it hard to answer the question of "how is CofD played?" It's designed to be played in a bunch of ways. For all that some people don't like certain parts of 2e, there are elements in the game to help guide the group in where to go. Aspirations are one of those. With good communication between the ST and players regarding Aspirations, you can really see how a given game is at least going to start out in play.

                  I really am curious about this and I think these answers, while appreciated, missed the mark.

                  How do the RAW of oWoD, nWoD, CoD, support the bolded part of my post? Where in the rules are the rules for how to achieve the theme of a vampire fighting against his humanity?

                  Let me look at VtR for my examples.

                  Beast: The inchoate urges that drive vampires away from the Man.
                  Man, the: The humanity a vampire maintains (or tries to); the spark of mortality that distinguishes him from the Beast. VtR p 85

                  Humanity: How the Kindred stave off the wiles of the Beast Within, and the toll its actions take upon their sense of morality.
                  Derangements: When a Kindred’s mental or moral state erodes, she sometimes exhibits dangerous psychological quirks. VtR p157
                  Okay, let's start with this. An earlier description tells how mechanically, Humanity replaces the CoD Morality. However, a Vampire's Morality starts at seven. SEVEN! On a ten point scale, that seems high for a vampire. Character creation mentions it on p82 and that they can start lower for xp, down to a five. Still at half, for a creature that has a Beast that only wants what it wants, in the description of the Beast.

                  Humanity, even though it is talked about a lot, isn't fully defined until p181. In it, we find that a seven rating means that a Petty Theft will require a roll to lose humanity. So, we have, in the rules, a character who is defined as a monster with a Beast but is pretty high on the humanity scale.

                  I would posit that the system is already not doing well in portraying Vampires as fighting against a darker nature. If they started at a Humanity of 4 and could buy it up, that would indicate that for me. At a 4, they can commit manslaughter, you know that thing you might have to do to get blood.

                  If they do have to make a Humanity roll and it's a degeneration, they might suffer a derangement. Those start on p186 with some examples on p 188. These are good examples. If a vampire does gain one, these are good. They have a description but then a game effect that can and should be enforced. However. I have played a long time and giving a character a penalty like this, imx, doesn't work. The player will merely have their character avoid situations that would call for a roll. Further, a lot of it could be situational. Delusional Obsession, for example, gives an example of a gardening obsession. (How many have played Vampires with that?) And then an obsession with anything to do with the Moon. They are really cool and evocative but it's part role playing and it sets up GM v player, imo, rather than helping the story. I'm not arguing that the player could bring this up themselves. I'm saying that the RAW gives them no incentive to do so!

                  While the derangements are good, there are no good examples of Humanity loss. Where does feeding fit? If they don't kill, or intend to kill, do they get to keep their starting 7? Doesn't that seem high? I have read some people like to get into the social and political side of it. Where is a white collar crime? Robbing a bank on the scale of hacking, not a gun robbery? Insurance fraud? Extortion? Manipulation? What about slavery or human trafficking? Some of those are implied to be on there but by not being on there, it leaves those in a grey area, which again goes back to GM v player.

                  Willpower is powerful. It seems to let you overcome bad things. If the chronicle is an action packed thing with something happening every day, I could see Willpower getting low and then not having it to be able to resist something that would stop humanity. Is this common? I ask because the game sets up a long running campaign with immortal characters. Basically, this is the five minute adventure day that most systems have. Does the GM need to keep the pressure up constantly? Or can the players have their characters rest until any Willpower is full and no chance to lose Humanity? Or, anything? Can they rest until fine, whether that's days or weeks, and then get back to their plans?

                  While I grant that combat gets a lot of attention because it can take a while, I found combat in Mage to be some of the fastest and best dramatic system out there. Further, for a game of socializing, where are the social combat rules? Where are the status rules? The setup of a city literally fills all of the positions in the "government" of the Kindred, forcing them to accept they are the lowest or to move to someplace else. Conflict is purely physical to climb the ladder without social rules. Again, a group could role play this! I'm not arguing that. I'm asking where in the RAW this is shown?

                  Finally, I don't see any system about the Beast. Just that losing control causes the Beast to emerge, perhaps in a Frenzy. There is not even a listing for Beast in the index! For something that seems to be central to the game, it's got a definition and then is mentioned with other things but is left vague itself.

                  So, that's my point about playing a Vampire. The RAW doesn't seem to support a horror theme. In contrast, FATE allows you to put your character into a bad situation to gain a Fate point, which can be used later. Mutants and Masterminds doesn't give the characters a Hero Point until they have felt the disadvantage! No freebies there.

                  I do see a lot of talk about humanity but the system of losing it, or the Beast, just isn't well defined. Further, ratings are such that each point matters and so players are going to resist as well as they can, using ideas above and I'm sure other things, to not risk "losing" their humanity. And that point, it doesn't seem to be a horror game to me.

                  While I have my own problems with Exalted's Limit Break, and don't know 3E very well, Exalted's Limit Break idea would seem to be a better starting point. It's still left vague but a meter that causes the character to truly make tough choices would be better than what I have seen in my time with Vampire. Imagine a system where anytime they are under pressure the meter fills. Slowly they watch that meter rise, knowing they will have to make a tough call. Further, couple it with combat (or social combat), so it's not something that will ever happen off screen or in downtime. It will happen at the worst possible time. Is that great drama? And where in the system is that?

                  I hope this better explains my thought process on why I agreed with the post I had. I do appreciate the feedback and agree that a game can be whatever the group makes of it. I want to know what the RAW does to create the mood, theme, plot, and character it set out to do.

                  Thanks!

                              Originally posted by evildmguy View Post
                              However, a Vampire's Morality starts at seven.
                              This whole thing confuses me.

                              So what? Seven is a starting point, largely because it's where humans on Morality start, because VtR starting level vampires are by default fairly new to the whole vampire thing. You're not making someone that's been stalking the nights for a few centuries (hence the optional starting XP chart for making more experienced characters), you're making a character that's somewhere between a pretty competent person that was Embraced very recently, or a vampire that was more of an average person but has had some time to adjust and learn about vampire society basics (a lot of this will be in things like Merit selection).

                              The starting Humanity level on the higher side leaves their room for it to fall before the character's are either stable with their condition or falling hard. If you started Humanity at 4, the actions that even put it at risk are atrocious enough that avoiding them isn't that hard. You'd eliminate struggling with the Beast completely. The Humanity 4-7 range is largely where you're going to see the most struggling because it's were there's the most room for moral compromises that people are willing to risk. If you changed VtR 1e to start at Humanity 4.. you might as well just ignore Humanity change completely and set anything that treats Humanity are a variable to 4; regaining Humanity is difficulty and expensive, while losing it from there requires just giving in to being a monster.

                              Humanity is pretty much going to down to at least 4 for most players over time unless they're actively throwing XP (and a lot of it) to keep it high. Eventually they're going to fail rolls.

                              They are really cool and evocative but it's part role playing and it sets up GM v player, imo,
                              And this whole bit is too subjective. "GM v player" is an attitude of the group. All RPGs set up the GM/etc. in the role of providing the challenge of the game for the players. Not matter how much a gaming book encourages an antagonistic relationship, or a cooperative one, having a GM/etc. cannot be mechanically forced into a cooperative style. I've seen people make D&D feel super-collaborative because the DM loves to see the PC's shine and sets up really fun scenarios with player input to make that happen. I've seen Fate get super antagonistic over the GM's approach to compels.

                              It's a position that's only as problematically antagonistic as the group makes it. Even with mechanical incentives (which there are, even if not as blatant as 2e makes them) some players avoid this stuff. Even without them, some players love it.

                              Where does feeding fit?
                              Where ever is appropriate based on the method of feeding. Feeding a small amount from a willing donor is obviously not the same level of Humanity sin as grabbing a random person of the street and feeding to the point of killing them. Though the feeding rules do make it difficult to never screw up and overfeed (and other problems as Blood Potency rises).

                              Willpower is powerful.
                              It's a limited source of +3 dice to rolls, or +2 to Defense/Resistance. It's not that great. Also there's things you can't spend it on; Humanity checks being a fairly important one to note here (most of your point seems to have missed that). It's also spent on a bunch of things besides general roll boosting. Even if you're playing a game with a relaxed enough time frame to regain Willpower to full easily... it's still limited enough that players are going to weight spending it rather than spamming it, but not feel too bad if they spent it all and can't push one roll a bit higher.

                              Finally, I don't see any system about the Beast.
                              The Humanity. hunger, and Frenzy systems are about the Beast. The Beast is defined by how it's resisted.

                              Imagine a system where anytime they are under pressure the meter fills.
                              And, IME, these things suck for horror games. It works for Exalted (actually it really didn't work that well for Exalted until 3e) because Exalted isn't a horror game, and that build up is to highlight that the Exalted are flawed beings and evoke inspirational material where heroes frequently flipped out and did grandly irrational things to match their grand heroics. In horror games, it just turns into, "count down until the game screws me no matter what I did." It's kinda fun in Dread, but Dread is a horror-themed party game with some RPG-like elements. You're not going to play a long running campaign of Dread and have it keep the tension of the primary mechanic fun.

                              Long term horror RPG games need to focus on character choices and player agency to maintain a sense of drama. Those generate player buy-in, which generates engagement with the story, which generates horror themed drama. Otherwise horror quickly devolves into resource management
