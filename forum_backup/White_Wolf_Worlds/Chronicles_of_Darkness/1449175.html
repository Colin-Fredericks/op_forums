    What's wrong with 1e Pledges? Should I use them?

    05-21-2021, 04:40 AM
    I always thought that both 1e and 2e Pledge systems had their pros and cons. Second edition pledges are simpler and quicker to use, hence they're great when you don't want to go too much into details or slow down the game. First edition pledgecraft however allowed for more detailed and accurate pledges.

    My idea was to use 2e pledge system most of the time but to still use the 1e system for those occasions when the Pledge was a central element of a story, when the details mattered, and when the players would want to customize the terms.

    Then I recently stumbled upon this post by Satchel in another thread:

    Originally posted by Satchel View Post
    If what you're instead wondering is why pledges changed to mostly grant boons that you actually had access to, it may have something to do with the multiple times people asked why every changeling wasn't filthy rich and living on easy street despite 1e's implication that the Lost tend to use looser more-exploitable language in pledgecrafting with mortals. There's not much point in being a game about being a fugitive trying to rebuild your life while sticking to a consistent locale and working with magic that tries to keep the books balanced if everyone can wordsmith their way to comfort at character creation; not for nothing does Demon use its similar system with an Integrity analogue that lets them cut and run with increasing frequency; not for nothing does Deviant's power structure include an explicit tagging system for "this game element will attract unwanted attention to you."
    That made me wondering: what are the drawbacks of 1e Pledgecraft (other than its complexity)? What kind of pitfalls should I be aware of if I wanted to use it in 2e and how could I avoid them?

      Drawbacks of 1e Pledgecraft: it's easy to abuse, the benefits of the pledge come from the Wyrd rather than actually coming from either of the parties involved, it has to be "balanced".
      • Easy to abuse: it's not difficult to put together pledges that can grant significant bonuses that are effectively permanent with drawbacks that will never come up in play.
      • Benefits of the pledge come from the Wyrd: you can build pledges where the benefits are completely disconnected from the tasks.
      • It has to be "balanced": this is problematic for two reasons: it's athematic (faeries aren't noted for playing fair) and it removes agency from the players by not allowing them to decide what they'll accept (not everyone views the value of things the same).



      If forging the Pledge is a major focus I would instead use the Social Maneuvering rules. Each party makes an initial offer of terms, this sets the baseline for the Pledge and can modify Doors (or be a complete non-starter). Offering additional services counts as soft leverage, opening all Doors means the other party accepts the Pledge. The PCs can decide whether they accept the Pledge on their own, without the need for social maneuvering (they can also choose to let the NPCs engage in social maneuvering against them).


      Originally posted by Example
      The PCs have offended the Summer Queen and are trying to hide from her lackeys in the Hedge; the offense was minor and if they can avoid her for a week she'll calm down and the whole thing will blow over. They flee to Cedric Scourwind's Hollow to hide, offering him a Pledge for their protection. Cedric's side of the deal is to hide the PCs for a week, the PCs offer to take care of Cedric's house and grounds while they're there. Cedric has a Resolve of 3 and Composure 4, so starts with 3 Doors. He hates company (+1 Door) but has a personal grudge against the Summer Queen (-2 Doors), leaving him with 2 Doors. The PCs' impression level with Cedric isn't high enough to allow more than one roll before trouble comes knocking, so they're in a bit of a bind; while an Exceptional Success on their roll would get them in (opening up both doors), it's likely they'll have to offer one (if they get a Success) or two (if they fail) additional favors to Cedric before he'll agree.

      With this system there's tension, room to jockey for an advantageous position, and the ability to either get in over your head or get a lot for very little based on skill and cleverness.

          Originally posted by moonwhisper View Post
          That made me wondering: what are the drawbacks of 1e Pledgecraft (other than its complexity)?
          I mentioned Demon's pact system being similar, but the differences there are notable:

          1. Demons can give mortals vast riches and other benefits that they don't personally possess and have no relation to the agreement besides being things the other party wants with pacts (and in fact that's one of the main bargaining chips it provides), but the only way they can personally benefit from these is to use soul pacts to take over a client's life. Changeling 1e pledgecraft has no such limitation; its main distinction is that conditionals are baked in and you can't just outright offer a one-sided deal like the Unchained can.

          2. Demons occupy a gameplay setup where exposure is more all-or-nothing and pacts are literally one of an extremely small handful of ways to regain Cover; breaking pledges imperils a changeling's Clarity, and making them offers some extra personal security, but pledges aren't going to help you bounce back from penalty-level Clarity, and being able to be out of your mind and still able to hand out Skill bonuses and Striking Looks as an innate template feature is more appropriate for monsters with a literary niche closer to the True Fae than the fugitive person-made-set-dressing trying to gradually rebuild their life in the mortal world. The Unchained are all about being destabilizing influences who can just disappear into the background and pick up the pieces elsewhere, but the Lost in 1e do poorly by being able to set themselves up in comfort when their relative inability to handle new environments is brought up multiple times.


          It's telling that the other place an analog to 1e's pledgecrafting system shows up in is the Mage book Summoners, where it's part of a system of bargaining for boons from summoned entities — and the boons come from the summoned entity, who is generally not a mortal-personlike actor in the story.

          Pledges in Changeling have always been set up such that the consequences of breaking them are more important than mystical benefits derived from them, hence Sealings being a universal fae ability in 2e. The main benefits of swearing an oath to join a motley or a Court or a freehold is that you belong to a group whose members face immediately enforced consequences for betrayal; the bits and bobs you get from strengthening your Mantle are a side benefit to further aligning your behavior to suit your Court's patron or the purpose of the group, and Contract knowledge is a one-time payment for the ability to make a recurring transaction (doubly so for Goblin Contracts).

              Because of the way 1e assigned value to different aspects of the pledge, and because those values had to sum to 0 with no further stipulation on how they interacted, you could make pledges that had very strict requirements with slap on the wrist penalties for failure, or assign yourself "tasks" that were nearly impossible to fail with absolutely crushing punishments and reap substantial ongoing rewards in either case.

              Also it was weird that shorter duration avoidance pledges gave you more benefits than long term ones. Other than the (admittedly not trivial) cost of renewing the pledge each day you could reap more benefits while knowing that you had the option to renegotiate each morning if you wanted.

              Back before 2e came out I had a homebrew that used 1e basics with these changes: 1: Duration does not have a numerical effect 2: The value of boons is equal to the greater of tasks or penalties, not the sum

                  Let me state up front I vastly prefer 1e's pledge crafting. It requires a "always watch your words" mindset and rewards cleverness in ways the second edition just doesn't. The way a quick thinking changeling can intentionally warp a conversation and then produce boons or sections out of nowhere fits well with the fairytale inspirations of the game and the fact that your enemies wield the same power only heightens the paranoia. Tomorrow when you show up for work one of your keepers minions could suddenly be your boss, after all.

                  The biggest drawback of 1e's pledges is the bookkeeping. As ST you need to write down and remember every pledge your players make as well as those of important NPC's. The need to maintain those tasks is the price of those boons that are so powerful and, in my experience, even the simplest task can become complicated or be accidentally violated in the chaos of game. This becomes doubly important and doubly difficult the second somebody decides to set a trap in a pledge with the intent of cursing the other party.

                  One pitfall, mentioned above, is that pledge craft is powerful. Easy access to Status/Allies, in particular, can just blow through many situations which would be challenging obstacles for others. In a recent game, for example, my character was trying to recover an item from the police so they casually promised to get it back "may the Wyrd grant me the authority to recover it" and with their newly minted Police Status they just walked into the station and checked it out in the ordinary fashion.

                  The solution, such as it is, is to remember that this is just what changelings do. Vampires can enslave any mortal with the blood bond and changelings can gain access to mortal institutions with a few words. Bear in mind also that they are, primarily, fugitives from the Fae, not from the human world. Three dots of Resources "for free" doesn't really offer much protection against the gentry, it just makes their life nicer while they are hunted.

                  Originally posted by Satchel View Post
                  There's not much point in being a game about being a fugitive trying to rebuild your life while sticking to a consistent locale and working with magic that tries to keep the books balanced if everyone can wordsmith their way to comfort at character creation;
                  This is actually something I’ve always liked about the game. It brings to a head, right from session one, the question of what life you want to build. You can just form a motley and get Resources and a new ID right out the gate but it’s not your life. It’s not the people and things you left behind. Do you just pledge for your old job back or do you kill your fetch and step into their place or do you try to earn it the same way you did the first time?

                  As general advice/house rules I do a few things that help with pledge craft.

                  1) The Wyrd can ballance the pledge with Boons and Sactions. This lets the characters only worry about the Task and Duration and you just ballance it behind the scenes if the math dosn’t work out, instead of dropping our of character to calculate everything on the fly.

                  2) Negative Merits. I use them as a thing. More or less a Flaw version of a given Merit but this can come into play when a character uses a Blessing, looses it in some mundane way, and then the pledge runs out. A character that gaind a few dots of Police Status and then lost a dot stealing from evidence might find themselves now a wanted fugative when the loss of the plege-granted dots leaves them with less than zero Status.

                  3) Poison the Boon. I use negative Merits with it and allow for poisoning of boons the partisipants didn’t even receive. This may not be to your taste but I like allowing the players freedom to apply bassically any curse they can think of on a pledge, from “your fortunes will run dry” (-Resources) to “we’ll all see how ugly you truly are” (-Striking Looks). I actually just use this entirely in place of the Flaw Sanction.
                  This may not be a good idea if you’re concerned with how powerful pledges already are - Adding the persision targeting of Merit removal via pledge traps is a whole extra layer of power ontop of everything else you can already do…

                          Or, another example from my past games, I had one player who swore to purchase a small gift for their motley mate every day* in order to get three dots of Resources. Take that away and changelings really can get near unlimited dots of Merits for absolutely nothing.

                          * This actually reminded me of another potential pitfall I house ruled over - Pledges last until their done.                           Hence doing the purchasing every day. And the player really thought her bases were covered because she swore to purchase a gift every day, not give it, so if they were separated she could buy up souvenirs to give when she got home.

                              The main downside of 1E Pledgecrafting is they are a minmaxer's dream.

                              Played as written it is very easy to take on lots of downsides you won't actually be hindered much by(minimizing the downsides) while taking upsides that are really powerful to you (maximizing the upsides).

A good ST could certainly overrule a lot of that, but, well, a good ST can overrule any broken rule system so that doesn't say much.
