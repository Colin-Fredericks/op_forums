    Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
    Let's look at a fairly simple and direct comparison: Rock & roll vs. hip hop.

    Rock & roll and hip hop are both musical genres that were created by black American subculture.

    Rock & roll was heavily appropriated. First by white American culture, and then by white culture in general, and by global culture. Extremely few of the creators of rock & roll got rich, while a lot of white people got rich off them. And rock & roll didn't get dynamic for it, it got static. For the first 30 years or so of its popularity, with the originators pushed out, the appropriators were just remixing the same material over and over for pop audiences. When you think of the revival of rock & roll's creativity in the 70s and 80s, it wasn't because global exposure to the music led to all sorts of innovations... it's because white people starting adding black and latin innovations to their work. The only reason we have hard rock and heavy metal, is because white people starting appropriating the blues to spice up how dull rock & roll had become in their hands.

    The hip hop and rap artists grew up with that history. So they fought it. They pushed to keep control over their work, push against appropriation, and to make sure that other artists that wanted in "earned" it instead of took it. Are there non-black hip hop groups? Of course there are. But it never stops being a black cultural expression that other people are knowingly engaging in. The pioneers of it are acknowledged and elevated instead of shoved aside for more mainstream faces. Black people haven't been shut out of the economic and social success their creativity generates. Hip hop didn't stagnate into some static art form. It's constantly evolved, and frequently from positive cultural exchanges rather than apropriative ones.
    Do you think white people would have bought so many records at the time, if they weren't made by other white people?

    Suppose you have one community of 10,000 who have some sort of cultural product. And suppose that somebody from a more powerful community imitates it, popularising it to 90,000 people. Only the 10% who started it have the real thing, but what's stopping them from keeping it and developing it amongst themselves, as they did before it was copied?

It's just what I do to feel good about my content and I don't really discuss any of it with my players.         Secondly, the damage is mostly already done by the publishers of those books. None of us are responsible for that. We're only responsible for the way we handle that content and the content we create ourselves.

Like the golem or the wendigo examples described above, through careless handling and willful ignorance, the popcultural machine has stripped away a lot of the original identities and spat out two new things that have replaced the originals for most people.

            So, because I'm apparently feeling a bit chatty, and maybe a tad cynically ironic...

            I want to explain what a golem really is. When I say golem, everyone thinks about magical constructs that have a habit of rebelling against their creators. Those aren't really golems. They're called golems in reference to what a true golem is.

            We, as in humanity, are golems. Or at least that's how we start. A golem is a person of ignorance. Someone that doesn't even understand that they don't know. In Jewish mysticism humanity is troubled by various failings that keep us from spiritual enlightenment. Being a golem is the first. You cannot seek enlightenment without knowing you lack it. We all have to have a moment of inspiration; an experience that makes us wonder in awe of the vastness of the universe around us, and sparks in us a desire to understand it. In the moment we cease to be golems, our ignorance is shattered and replaced by a drive to start down a path towards a higher state of being.

            In Jewish mysticism, it's believed that all of the failings of humanity are told in the story of Eden (lots of different ways to count them and differentiate them abound). If overcoming that first ignorance is in the story of Eden... it comes first. And the first failing of Adam is that unlike all other life in Eden, he was not simply created once. At first, he was shaped into a lump of dirt/clay. As such, he existed as a golem, a being unknowing even of itself. Only with the first divine breath does Adam get created a second time, as a knowing human, who then begins the process of understanding his world (the naming of all the things in Eden).

            Of course, the connection to the golem-as-creation of man should be more obvious now. When a Jewish mystic creates a golem, they are copying the creation of Adam. They're called golems because they're stuck in that state: created once, but not being granted the divine breath that would free them of their limited state. And why they don't get that second creation is down to the different golem stories (there are many) and what the storyteller is trying to convey. Golem stories in this sense are cautionary tales about the other failings along the path to spiritual enlightenment. When you have progressed far enough, you have the knowledge to create ignorance, and deny awe, and that power is like all power; dangerous. Humans are not meant to be stuck in that state, and they will push against such wrongful imprisonment.

            So, when I look at a game like Promethean... of course I'm comfortable with how golems are used. Promethean is a game all about what the true point of golem stories are supposed to be about. It's a mystic journey to become a full and true human... to cease to be a golem... it's about as good of a secularized version of things as I can imagine.

            But when I look at a monster manual with a golem reduced to a clay monster (or worse golems of other materials), not only is it disrespectful to my culture and traditions... I'm looking at some bland pointless thing taking the name of something deep and spiritual. Golem stories don't exist to have a monster to fight, they exist to keep us humble on our path towards being better people. They exist to remind us of where we started and no lose sight of that. Calling a statblock that gets bonuses because it's made of clay, and can be killed if you target it's forehead just right a golem is denying yourself a far more fulfilling story.

                                    Ditto, while I knew the story and presumed there was an allegorical meaning in the sense Heavy Arms mentioned, I wasn't aware it was a general concept used.

                                    A bit further from CofD, but it gives a bit of insight to something in Magic the Gathering; early sets had a pretty standard D&D style of golem, but later ones made it clear golems could have personality and were basically a fantasy version of a sci-fi robotic AI, whereas things that were merely clever machines were usually typed Construct.

                                    One of the first turning points was in the Weatherlight Saga, which features a silver golem named Karn who is portrayed as a fully fleshed-out character (by the standards of card games about wizards summoning beasties to throw at an opponent); created by one of the greatest artificers, a sworn pacifist after an action of his harmed someone, and eventually becomes an accomplished artificer and planeswalker in his own right. Popular character to this day.

                                    And one of the creators of the Weatherlight Saga storyline is Magic's current lead designer, Mark Rosewater, who is Jewish. And now I wonder how much one might have to do with the other, re: treating a golem as a person as opposed to just an animated statue monster.
