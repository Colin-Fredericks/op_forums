    It is not just semantics though, saying that Breaking points means something other than what it does has a lot of impact. It presents as if every Breaking Point was automatically a loss of Integrity, which it isn't. That's like saying that a Vampire feeding always kills a person rather than that sometimes when a Vampire feeds they kill the person. One will be more common than the other, and they should be.
    Unless he suffers frenzy, a vampire can ensure he won't kill his victim by being careful to feed in small doses and not make a roll to be sure. Difficult, yes, but possible; that's how they make Herds. With a Breaking Point, there ALWAYS is a risk you'll lose Integrity. That's kind of a big difference.

    On this point specifically, not really. If something is traumatic than doing it a bunch doesn't make it less traumatic, either they are good at coping with the trauma in the first place (they pass the tests) or it affects them (they lose Integrity).
    Yeah, about that. Finding out someone died because you spared a werewolf is traumatic. Having your spouse turned into a vampire is traumatic. Finding out the changeling you have caught and torured all night was innocent is traumatic. Calmly studying a supernatural's past through observation and deciding without much dilemna that he deserves to live because he did nothing wrong is NOT a traumatic experience. At least not in itself; sure, you can suffer Breaking Point by finding out about all the messed up stuff he did, or getting too involved in his cult. But the act itself is rational and not something that should have the potential to induce a trauma.

    Still not saying that that specific example should be a Breaking Point or not. I think it is too general to say. If it's just making the determiniation in a vacuum then probably not, if it is constantly deciding whether you should kill a sentient being because they might be 'harming' others and trying to decide if it's just 'in their nature' or a choice, and what the 'correct' choice then is for either option then yeah, that's gonna wear on you.
    Yes, that's kinda our point; it's too vague and can cover situations where it doesn't fit. There needs to be a better distinction.

        Investigating monsters doesn't inflict any penalty or breaking point. Unless you let them go free and they kill someone, or you kill them after finishing your investigation.

        The real problem with Integrity loss is that while each breaking point gets you a beat, it takes 15 beats to get that integrity back (by core.) So if you have 10 dice average on your breaking point roll, you have an average of 0.7^10*100%=2.82% chance of failing each roll, with a (1-(0.7)^10)^15*100=65% chance of passing them all with a net outcome of success. Someone check my math, its getting late. At 7 dice average, that is only a ~28% chance of passing them all meaning you will most certainly lose "Experience Value" (considering each point of Integrity to be worth 5 Experiences) by taking breaking points. This means that, while Breaking points may look like they make your character stronger, you would lose "Experience" overall unless you are rolling lots of dice every time. At 9, the odds slightly favor success, meaning that you would probably gain Experience in the long run purely from breaking points.

        Of course that is a super meta munchkiny way of looking at things, and the story changes if you don't consider Integrity having inherent value. If that experience is spent elsewhere instead and you take the Integrity hits, then it all becomes murkier than I can work out right now.

        Edit: Woops, forgot to take in the possibility of multiple failures in that math. At 9 you have a ~54% chance of succeeding them all, but you don't have a ~46% of losing once. The possibility of multiple failures means that it still probably isn't even yet. I told you it was late >_>

        Edit2: Grumble Grumble. So to pull out some real math, it should be a ...(15!/(13!2!)(1-0.7^9)^13*(0.7^9)^2*100=10% chance of getting two failures, 1.82% of getting 3 for d9. And a 34% for one failure. So quick and dirty estimating, you will still have a net loss at 9 dice. Its *10* dice that you would start to gain at. 28.36% failure, 5.77% two failure, 0.7% three failures, 65% all successes. But please take that with a grain of salt at this point :P.

            Originally posted by Elfive View Post

            A hunter stares into the abyss. For them, high integrity is about compartmentalising. Separating the hunt form their everyday life. If you go empathising with the monsters then of course that's gonna make the two sides blur together.

            I mean look at how you phrased it. High integrity is just "Bam, done, what's for lunch?" whereas the other guy meticulously goes around background checking, probably staking them out or poking their nose in weird cults. Who's putting in more effort there? Whose sanity is more likely to crack?

            It looks backward form a distance, but get up close, think about what it involves and suddenly it makes sense. Just like with non-hunters, high integrity is born of looking at the shadows and not thinking too hard about it.
            There is definitely a point to be made that a simpler us/them worldview would be more resilient to cracking, but I present the following counterpoints.

            1) Hunters are not mechanically optimised vigil machines. They are the product of a society and upbringing that says Nazi-ish dehumaniseing ideals are evil. Many Hunters, arguably more than half of the core 12, would choose a code that judges monsters on their actions over one that thinks all monsters do not deserve to exist even if the other led to higher Integrity.

            2) While a binary us/them may be better at keeping your integrity up I think it would be worse at keeping your two lives separate than a code closer to social norms. imagine a Hunter going to a dinner party and his friends daughter emerges from her room wearing lots of black and silver pentacle jewellery. A hunter who believes all witches are subhuman and deserve to die (even if he only suspects she might be a witch) is more likely to creep people out by the way he acts than one who thinks witches are human and only deserve to be hunted for criminal actions.

            3) What's the Hunter's goal? Unless they genuinely believe the extermination of anything supernatural is a goal onto itself allying with the right monster can be an enormous asset. If your goal is to keep your community safe you want the witch who doesn't threaten anyone on your side. A Code that says she's not human is going to make it much harder if not impossible to win her trust.

            4) Thematics. Saying that the default position for an oppressed group organising to fight back is for them to go full Nazi is a thematically weird message.

            For these reasons I think it's best to avoid a default code that deny's monsters even have the right to exist.

                    ElvesofZion You can't win. The sole issue with the Code is reader bias. You can't debate someone that reads the preview and sees "humans good, monsters bad" as a simplistic representation of a view, not being dismissive. If someone thinks the Code suggests that monsters have no right to exist then there can be no discussion with someone that like me reads the same thing as "human life is more valuable than monster life". It isn't a left-right argument, more like up-left as in no relation to each other.

                    To others.

                    I will skip the game talk and go to a real world type of example to explain how I saw the Code. I will switch out monsters with dogs. I like dogs. I prefer cats but dogs work better for this. I like the average dog more than the average human. If I see a dead dog on the side of the road, I think poor dog. If I saw a human instead, I would want somebody to clean that mess up. However, I accept the universal morality of humanity that says a human life is more valuable than a dog's life. A dog should be killed if it bites a human outside of defending itself or family. Given the choice of saving the life of a human or a dog, I choose human.

                    It is instinct. I don't consider the quality of the lives. I assume the human leans towards ass-hat and the dog is likely a good dog. I still choose human. It doesn't matter if the human has a year left to live and the dog could go on for 10 or 15 years. There is no way I would choose a generic dog over a generic human. A known example of one or the other complicates the equation.

                    The Code is supposed to be messy when dealing with known baddies. Human may not be good. Monsters may not be bad. However, human life has more value than monster life. That does not mean destroy all monsters. It definitely means when there is a choice between human and monster a hunter should choose human. Some hunter groups blur lines, but the hunters that work for those groups take up the Vigil to benefit humanity, and work to the detriment of monsters (some) in their effort. These far reaching changes to the Code belong as house rules because the problem exists solely within the house.
