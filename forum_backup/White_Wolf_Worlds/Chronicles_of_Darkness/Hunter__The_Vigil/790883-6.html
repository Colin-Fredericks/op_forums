                                            So it seems this discussion is now focused on the sexual violence aspect of the Abbey. So my views:

                                            I don't think the Abbey has institutionalised sexual violence. Quite the opposite actually, the Abbey's membership is mostly uncomfortable with sexual violence but being the worst sort of libertines their institutional culture also prevents them from expressing that disapproval.

                                            I think the section beginning "Make no mistake" - oft quoted to support the idea that the Abbey institutionalise sexual violence is actually saying the opposite and agrees with the above. When the narrator says "It’s not about sex" what the narrator means is "The Abbey is not about sexual violence". When the narrator follows that up with "though it can be" what the narrator means is "but if it's your thing I won't object". Which is what I said in the preceding paragraph.


                                            Still, whether the 1E Abbey has institutionalised sexual violence is not really that useful a discussion. The question is, should the 2E Abbey? And I think the answer is no. After all, the 1E Abbey didn't. I don't think you can say that the Abbey has an institutional policy of forbidding it's members from sexualised violence without breaking charachter, since "no ethics" is core to their charachter. But you can certainly take the spotlight away and give it to the Abbey members doing stuff like finding ways to fly or crashing Spring Court parties.

                                            Taking the spotlight away would probably require not mentioning sexualised violence even once. Even a single mention would most likely stick in the mind.


                                            I was also thinking a bit about how an Abbey Hunter could go from novelty seeking hedonist to a creature-of-habbit Slasher and I think I've identified two likely paths.
                                            1) They find a kick they like so much they stop seeking novelty and seek more of the same. If there aren't enough monsters to supply their fix, they might turn to regular humans. E.G. An Abbey hunter who enjoys betraying others too much might run out of monsters, and become a Charmer.
                                            2) The mortal world reminds the Abbey that they're not above the rules, and their rage overtakes their hedoism. E.G. A witch simply calls the police, an Abbey Hunter gets arrested, and breaks out to become an Avenger against every officer and lawyer who put him away.

                                            That said, I still think Claimed by Infernal Demons needs to be highlighted as a way Abbey hunters fall.


                                            Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
                                            I don't trust the average roleplayer with a nuanced masterpiece of storytelling.
                                            I oppose on principal any argument that begins with not trusting your audience.

                                            Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
                                            This sounds frighteningly like the "racism has a place in the World of Darkness, because racism is dark!"
                                            Racism does have a place in the World of Darkness! Without racism I can't tell some of the stories that resonate with me. (I do this a more often than Hakollel. Look how often the theme of "home is a special place" crops up in my work).

                                            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                            Ashwood criticizes the justifications of Hunter flat out, and asks players to ask questions about play experiences: why do we enjoy a game about hunting monsters, tearing apart NPC's and our PC's both physically and emotionally, and why we stop where we do, and in facts asks us if we're honest with out own reasons. It's a chance to realize terrible things about ourselves, and thus gives us a real chance to grow and develop.
                                            I think you raise a very good point here. There's an awesome bit of meta to the Ashwood Abbey. The Abbey hunt monsters for fun, which is exactly the game we're all playing.

                                            I don't think we realize "terrible things about ourselves", because unlike the Abbey we have a cast iron justification. We're only hurting fictional people, and we know why we do it. We're descended from aggressive apes with all sorts of bugs in the wetware, and thus we require an outlet and fiction has the immense advantage of not hurting real people.

                                            But still, the meta element is there and it's awesome. It would be a shame to loose it.

                                                    This thread has gotten really long so forgive me if this has been brought up by a previous poster that I missed while skimming.

                                                    Hunter as it is now has a bit of a problem with violence being treated as the norm in a way no other CofD game line has. It makes sense to a degree, after all the game is called Hunter not "Aware Mortals". Still the basic goals of most compacts and conspiracies as they exist now is to capture or kill whatever supernaturals they find causing problems in whatever venue they focus on (with Network Zero being the main exception in the corebook). Even the non-violent sub-groups within the larger compacts are largely portrayed as support for violent field teams.

                                                    I'd like to see more of an expansion on non-violent and less directly violent takes on the Vigil in the coming 2E.

                                                    For Example:

                                                    As it has been mentioned you can tone down or outright remove the sexual violence from Ashwood Abbey and reframe them as thrill seeking hedonists. Folks hunting down Mages to just to find out what various enchantments feel like, inviting themselves to Summer Court soirees to get a chance to taste goblin fruit or to have sex with a living ball of flame.

                                                    TF:V could be framed as less genocidal towards monsters and more towards wanting to control them. Having intelligence agents act as liaisons to the entrenched supernatural organizations (Vampire Covens, Mage Orders, Werewolf Packs, etc) attempting to make arrangements with them to better serve the interests of the United States government ("Drop some nasty magic on this remote village and we'll keep the other conspiracies off your back until we need you again), stepping in with the advanced hardware only when the monsters start to step out of line.

                                                    The Union could tie more into the history of organized labor and civil rights movements. Putting more of an emphasis on community building with the Supernaturals to keep everyone safe (until other Hunters or rogue Supernaturals mess it up naturally). Rather than the rather nasty reactionary form they've taken in 1E that feels much more like one of those border guard militas than a union.
