    Bits of a New Conspiracy: The Foundation for Advanced Psychiatry

    02-07-2015, 06:28 PM
    I'm working on a Hunter Conspiracy; it's far from done but I thought I'd post some of my notes on it here for amusement/interest.
    The human mind is a hideously complex series of mechanisms; many of its workings are still only poorly understood. Dreams, for instance, are an enigma—What purpose do they serve? Is there any meaning that can be derived from them, or are they merely electrical misfirings in a tired brain?

    Why did every person in the city taking that experimental new antidepressant have the same dream of a faceless woman offering them a rotten apple on the same night? Why does one of my patients have dreams of sexual encounters with people he’s never met who are found strangled exactly a week later? Why does my daughter keep having nightmares about “bad men made of sticks” living in the basement of the house next door?

    Monsters leave scars. Some are obvious, claw marks across a chest, a missing ear, a portable dialysis machine. Others, not so much. They lurk beneath the surface, tormenting their bearers until they succumb.

    In the World of Darkness exists a group of academics, doctors, and therapists who wish to both study the psychology of monsters and alleviate the supernatural's detrimental effects on the human psyche. Their methods combine orthodox talk therapy and medical prescriptions with outdated psychoanalysis (Jung is quite popular), arcane rituals borrowed from cultures around the globe, less-than-legal substances, and, occasionally, ethically dubious surgeries (what does it really mean to lobotomize a walking corpse, anyway?).

    The psychotherapists of the Foundation for Advanced Psychiatry hold their Vigil in the realms of human consciousness, fighting against the hobgoblins hiding in the brain’s nooks and crannies. They’ve dedicated themselves to opposing monsters on the psychological front, using group psyche as a weathervane, healing those who’ve grown deranged in the face of what’s out there, and sifting through dreams to find the answers they seek.

    The Enemy

    Foundation behaviorists have come to recognize a handful of psychological patterns indicative of monstrous incursion. Though someone suddenly acting out of the ordinary is certainly worth investigating, what’s even more troubling, often, is when someone prone to erratic behavior or mental instability suddenly takes an unexpected turn for the better, becoming serene, more confident, better put together. Hypnotherapy sessions have revealed subjects claiming to have signed away their troubles to fallen angels, let alien spirits enter their souls, or even come to the realization that they’re not actually human, but merely a replacement for someone who’ll be coming back soon.

    Mass hysteria can often be spotted in advanced, especially when local therapists decide to break patient-doctor confidentiality and compare notes, and, lo and behold, an impossible number of people have confessed to fantasizing about murdering the prostitute who hangs out on State and 14th. Sudden upswings in religious convictions are also notable, but going wisdom is to proceed cautiously and prepare to abandon ship should any patient start waxing poetically about the Fertile Crescent (though damned if anyone in the Foundation can figure out why).

      Hunters
      You’re a psychology professor at a small university. You’ve always kept in contact with a college friend of yours who’d decided to take the psychiatrist’s route instead. One day you get an email from her about a strange man who claims to have gone to Mars. Then the messages stop. Then one day, out of the blue, she sends you a rambling, near-incoherent missive about discovering the secret of immortality, confessing to horrible, atrocious acts in the pursuit. Your replies go unanswered, and, deeply concerned for your friend, you reach out to your academic contacts to see if anyone knows anything about this kind of pathology. Soon after you get a response, from a research group you’ve never heard of before; there’s nothing that can be done for your friend now, but, if you’re willing, they can show you how to keep the madness from spreading. It doesn’t hurt that they’re offering you funding, either.
      When you started your internship with the psych group, they gave you a bunch of required reading, including scans of Jung’s private journals, nigh-incoherent anti-psychiatry screeds, even an annotated copy of House of Leaves. You thought it was some sort of prank on the new guy at first, but now you know they’re deadly serious.

      You work as a sales rep for a pharmaceutical company that specializes in mood stabilizers. You can practically feel the waves of loathing coming off the doctors when you hang around their hospitals for too long, but you’re not just profiting off human misery; you’ve been taking notes on who’s taking what and how it’s affecting them. You’re saving lives and not even taking the credit. You’re basically a martyr, you know.

      You were an orderly at a severely-underfunded state asylum, and you’ve seen some shit. Not just the way the patients were neglected, or the permanent air of uncleanliness that hung over the place, but you’d heard stories. Stories of nightly abuse. Stories the doctors wrote off as mass delusion. Sometimes you wish you hadn’t stuck around that night and seen that pale man bite into the neck of a strapped-down inmate, but at least you’re not changing bedpans anymore.
      You studied philosophy, and pretty much everything you knew about mental illness came from Deleuze and Guattari. You were doing research for your senior thesis on Anti-Oedipus when you came across the strange book stashed behind the encyclopedias. It was some arcane text from the fifties about things like “Dreams of Lilith” and “The Ruinous Prince.” There was no frontispiece, no ISBN, just a mailing address written in minuscule letters on the back of the title page. You were fascinated and decided to write your thesis on that instead. When you were all but laughed out of your defense, you decided to send a letter to the address. Now who’s laughing? Not you, definitely.

          Status
          ● You’ve been approved as a member of the Foundation and can purchase dots in the ??? Endowment.
          ●●● You’ve experienced enough of the supernatural by this point, either firsthand or through your patients, that you’ve trained your mind to resist outside influence. Gain the Indomitable merit. If you already have the merit, or if you purchase it later, increase its bonus by 2.
          ●●●●● Once you’ve reached this level of prestige within the Foundation, you gain access to the connections and inroads the Conspiracy has fostered with psych institutions around the globe. Whenever you make contact for the first time with an institution connected to psychological research or mental health—such as a university, hospital, or insane asylum—you gain access to four merit dots to divide among Allies, Contacts, Library, Mentor, Resources, Retainer, Safe Place, and Staff as you wish. Once these dots are in place for any given institution, they can’t be reassigned without significant social effort.
          Example: Doctor Felipe Wu’s theories of the mind are renowned among both the Foundation and the wider scientific world. When reports of strange and erratic behavior among a large number of inhabitants of Sacramento cross his desk, he decides to go investigate firsthand. He initially checks in with the University of California’s psych department to get a feel for what’s going on. Thanks to the efforts of local members of the Foundation, Dr Wu can take advantage of some bits of the department’s infrastructure; he gains four merit dots to allocate. His player decides to put two dots into Library (Science), representing a berth of books provided for him, one dot into Safe Place, as an office the department allows him to use, and one dot into Retainer, representing the psychology undergrad Rebecca Schulz, who’s excited for the opportunity to intern with one of her chosen field’s great minds.
          Later, following up on a lead, Wu goes to the city psychiatric hospital. Again, he gains four merit dots; this time his player puts all four into Mentor. The director of the hospital, Dr Angela Harper-Roth, turns out to be another member of the Foundation, and is happy to give Felipe Wu any advice she can about Sacramento’s goings-on.

              Endowment: ???
              Mental Health (● - ●●●●●)
              Those affected by the supernatural often require specialized care. With the Mental Health Endowment, a member of the foundation can provide her patients with preventative measures and follow-up therapy for traumatic brushes with things lurking in the shadows. A therapist can have a number of patients in her care equal to her combined dots in Mental Health + Status (Foundation)—maintaining patients requires time and resources, after all. Bringing a new patient into one’s care requires a point of Willpower and a successful Intelligence + Medicine/Occult/Science roll to get a bead on which treatments would be the most effective. Most Foundation psychologists use a combination of talk therapy and prescription medication, though others incorporate more arcane or unorthodox practices into their sessions, such as Jungian dream interpretation, Buddhist meditation, or entheogenic rituals. Anyone under the therapist’s care receives the Foundation member’s dots in Mental Health as a bonus on rolls to resist mental or emotional coercion, such as a vampire’s powers to rewrite memories, or a faerie’s uncanny ability knack to manipulate desires. If the power in question is resisted rather than contested, subtract Mental Health from the monster’s roll. Dots in Mental Health are also applied to Breaking Point rolls directly caused by a supernatural source.
              Drawback: If a member of the Foundation isn’t available for sessions, patients might suffer a relapse. After a number of weeks equal to the therapist’s dots in Mental Health have passed without a session, a patient loses access to the Endowment’s benefit.
              Shock Therapy (● - ●●●●●)
              Out in the field, there’s not always time to talk it out. Sometimes what someone needs to snap out of hysteria is a moment of moral support, a quick dose of morphine, or maybe just a slap in the face.
              Cost: 1 Willpower
              Dice Pool: Wits + Medicine + Shock Therapy. If the Condition in question was caused by a supernatural power, then a Clash of Wills ensues.
              Action: Instant
              Roll Results
              Dramatic Failure: You’ve managed to make things worse. Choose one: You gain the same (or a similar) Condition you were trying to fix, or the patient gains a second Condition of the Storyteller’s choosing.
              Failure: The quick fix isn’t working. You can try again, but each successive attempt nets a cumulative -1 penalty to the roll.
              Success: You get the unbalanced individual to snap out of it. Success on the roll either completely absolves someone of a temporary mental or emotional Condition (such as Shaken), or suppresses a Persistent Condition (such as Addicted) for a scene. Removing a Condition this way does not garner the victim a Beat. A Persistent Condition can only be subject to Shock Therapy if the possessor is currently actively suffering from it; if an alcoholic isn’t currently being tempted to drink, then there’s nothing she needs to snap out of.
              Exceptional Success: The patient gains the Steadfast Condition.
              Note: Shock Therapy works equally well on positive mental/emotional Conditions like Inspired.

                          Stereotypes

                          Hotel Mascaron: I was treating a woman once who’d been suffering from paranoid delusions and fugue states. She was convinced that there were alien invaders from another dimension kidnapping people and replacing them with identical copies. And whenever she dreamed about the invaders, she’d always wake up miles away from her house. My treatments didn’t seem to be working for her, no matter what I tried. Then, she just stopped coming to sessions. After the third missed session, I got a phone call, late at night; there was a lot of static on the other end, and a strange voice said that my services were no longer needed, but thank you anyway. I thought I saw the woman again, once, but it was dark out, and she was wearing some kind of fox mask. I think she had a baseball bat with her, too. I don’t know what’s going on, but I think I’d rather just wash my hands of it.

                          The Loyalists of Thule: Guilt complexes are sometimes associated with post-traumatic stress disorder, but I’m starting to wonder if my contacts in the group are hiding their true motivations for the work they’re doing. They have the most awful dreams, too: mass genocides, brutal totalitarian regimes, an ancient island holding long-forgotten horrors rising from the seas...

                          The Season Letters: It’s funny, the only reason I got involved in all this is because of a wrong address. Back when I was an undergrad, I got a letter from someone—an anthropologist, I think—claiming he felt he was on the brink of making an important discovery about some sort of tribe out in California. He’d included some newspaper clippings about Humboldt squid swarms and weird graffiti that was attributed to a new gang in the area. I wrote him back saying that I’d received the letter by mistake, but he never replied. I wonder what happened to him? And why do some of my patients have dreams with that same graffiti?

                          VASCU: I'm apparently on some sort of government file, because these guys keep getting referred to my clinic. I'd be happy to help if they ever actually let me inside their heads; they're always so cagey about their feelings. High-strung too. A lot of them suffer from imposter syndrome, recurring nightmares, and nosebleeds, for some reason I can't quite figure out. But what's much more interesting, one of them let slip that there's apparently a high-security prison out there somewhere full of mystical serial killers. What do you think their psyches are like? I smell tenure.
