    Hunter versus Mortal games - Differences?

    02-27-2016, 11:46 AM
    On another thread, I discussed how I felt about the core mortal game "eatting" a lot of Hunter stuff. Professional Training, Cult-like merits, Horrors. To me, that was always what mortal games were about, and HtV simply took all that to the next level. The major advantage of a HtV game was that it actually focused on mortals as a real power in their own right. Thus, Risking Willpower and all the organizations in the know. Mortals are dangerous when they unite, and thus Tactics. Some really great ideas here. Meanwhile, after the core book? There's really not a lot of mortal-specific stuff that comes out. Sure, there was the generic Second Sight and Reliquary books, but those were self contained mini-games, not for core mortal stuff. Same with Inferno, Immortals. And the Armory books and Book of the Dead/Spirits were generic for the entire nWoD. Hunter was the book that actually took mortals, gave them organization and abilities, and set them lose on the world. It was the closest thing to a mortal-specific release after the core book.

    And, yeah. Mortal 2e did "eat" a lot of Hunter stuff. A lot of people actually called Hunter mortal 2.0 back when it first came out for a reason - it included almost all the stuff you might want for a mortal game. Really, I honestly feel like HtV basically is still just that. Mortal games. It was a focus on mortals that the core book simply didn't get - the original core book was simply too focused on providing generic stuff for all the games instead of a focused mortal. The big thing that Hunter brings is more things that mortals can do. We have example organizations, we have better teamwork rules (tactics), we have better uses for willpower, we have rules for Coding up Morality/Integrity. Those aren't touched on mortal rules, because the mortal rules don't assume supernatural organizations for mortals.

    Because of all the above, I've never really thought of Hunter being a fully separate gameline from mortals. Hunter is mortal taken to the next level. They do all the same things that mortals do. They have the same worries and concerns. Mortals who explore the darkness. The only difference is that hunters have decided to do something about it. Study it, hunt it, socialize it; the different ways the Conspiracies look at supernaturals. (Vision, Zeal and Mercy to use Reckoning terms). I know that a number have classified Hunter as the Power game, and Geist game as the "pick your own stat" game, but for me its always been the opposite. Mortals and hunters are the center of the CofD - they're the (0, 0, 0) starting point for me for touching on the themes and ideas of all other games out there on some supernatural graph. You touch on Mage's study of mysteries, you have your own Hunt, you deal with social situations. There's some Promethean-esk qualities of soul searching and what makes a Monster from a Man, and dealing with the madness of whatever led you to the Vigil. The books of HtV have a heavy focus on other supernaturals rather than their own stuff for a reason. They scaled from street level cells to world spanning conspiracies to adapt to any game you wanted. I know Geist was meant to be the bare-bones, build your own game, but HtV always filled that niche for me better than Geist ever did.
    At least, all this is my opinion on the matter. I know some of you disagree with my opinion, and feel Hunter is actually a pretty different game. Some others might agree, but have a different way they reached a similar conclusion on the matter. So, here's my question that I would like to put forwards. How do you feel about all this? Are they the same? Different? Should we think of them as different games as Vampire is from Werewolf, and if so, how should it be done?

    Discuss! Lets hear it!

      I think Mortal and Hunter are different games. Mortals deal with whatever they're facing and then try to go back to their normal lives. Hunters can't turn back. Their former lives are over. The Vigil is their new life.

      To put it as an example: In the Resident Evil series, the classic characters (Jill, Chris, Leon, Claire, Barry) are Hunters (though after RE5, Jill is probably a Deviant instead). The ones that never were seen again (such as the entire cast of the Outbreak games) are Mortals instead.

          For me, the big difference between a blue book game and a Vigil game is that a blue book game has an expiration date. It doesn't necessarily have to be a one-off, but the core story of a base mortal game is regular people being forced to confront the supernatural. After a while, that story has to end. Even if it doesn't have a pre-planned end point and the characters don't all get killed by horrible monsters, at some point they stop being ordinary people. Once the supernatural stops being the thing they all try to avoid and pretend doesn't exist and starts being something they know exists and fight against, it's no longer the same story. It's a story about people who have faced the darkness and made the decision to push back against it. It's a story about Hunters.

          Accordingly, I use mortal for one-off games, or for the starting point of longer running games. I use Hunter as the continuation of that start point.

                  I disagree. For me, a Mortal game is like playing Call of Cthulhu (not exactly, but similar). Mortals are weak, vulnerable, and scared for their lives. While they might do the same thing as Hunters and actively try to destroy supernaturals, that's not the focus. The focus is how the Darkness chips away at their lives, robbing them of their sanity every time they encounter it. Hell, mortals may not even interact with the supernatural directly or even not at all. Here's a bit of an interesting take: perhaps the supernatural doesn't exist. I don't mean that it definitely doesn't, just that its existence is ambiguous. Both the characters and the players aren't quite sure, which can create some real nice dramatic tension, especially for experienced players.

                  A good friend of mine who has run tabletops for decades gave me this advice: subvert your players expectations. Do they think the supernatural exists, give them evidence it doesn't. Do they think the supernatural doesn't exist, give them evidence it does. That way, the game is unpredictable to them, you keep them guessing, and you keep the game hella fun.

                  With Hunter, you can't do that sort of thing. Hunters hunt after all, so they need something supernatural to deal with. Mortals avoid the supernatural as much as they possibly can, only confronting it when they're cornered. Hunters confront it to begin with. They drive assaults, they build arsenals, they study, they plan. The horror in Hunter is also different. There's a reason only Hunters turn into Slashers when they lose Integrity (or whatever the equivalent is gonna be in 2E). It's not about how the Darkness destroys their lives, it's how the Darkness corrupts them, turning them into one of the monsters they hunt.

                  TL;DR Mortals avoid the supernatural AMAP, Hunters confront the supernatural until they die a hero or live long enough to become a villain.

                          I've always been fond of the idea that blue book mortals are victims or drawn in by coincidence, while Hunter is about those obsessive people who actually seek the horror out. There's a shift in mentality between a character who is curious about the supernatural in the abstract and one who continues searching for it even after some monster nearly rips his arm off, and the crossing of that line is when a mortal becomes a Hunter.

                          Of course, this interpretation works best with the whole "humanity knows the supernatural is out there, but keeps its collective head down and tries to forget" meta-idea; in this context, Hunters are those that cannot keep denying it.
