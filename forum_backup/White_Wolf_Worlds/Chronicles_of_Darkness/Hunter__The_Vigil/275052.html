    October vampires!

    10-07-2014, 07:56 PM
    For my own amusement, I have been doing a series on Facebook spotlighting vampires from different cultures at a rate of one a day, planned to continue through October. I'm doing Internet research and usually spending about an hour per vampire just on research, putting some degree of effort into using sources with reliable knowledge of the culture's traditional beliefs. I think that's not bad, but I don't promise to get anything perfect.

    A friend of mine said I should post these in the Vampire: The Requiem forum. I've decided to post these, but screw the vampires! I want people to know about these things so that people can hunt them! So I'm putting them here, in the Hunter forum.

    Since it is currently October 7, I have already posted seven of these to Facebook. For today and the next five following days, I will post two vampires a day here. That will get me caught up. After that, it will be one a day.

      Vampire 1 - Adze
      Myth origin date: Unknown, but this is a very old myth
      Myth origin location: Ewe folklore, modern day Togo and Ghana

      Modus operandi:
      Adze dwell in the wilderness in the form of insects, especially fireflies. A single adze may shapeshift between multiple forms. In insect form, an adze is very dextrous and almost impossible to capture or keep at bay with nets, magic, or pesticides. Adze consume the blood of children by landing on the child's thumb or lips (sources vary) and drinking the blood through a long, elephant-trunk-like nose. They have been known to drink until a child is lifeless. Additionally, their victims, and potentially even the families of their victims, can become ill from their attacks.

      If an adze is unable to obtain sufficient blood it will become very weak and eventually transform into a human form or human-like hunchback form with talons (sources vary). After such a transformation, adze go on a frenzy seeking to consume the heart and liver of a child or their captor (sources vary, and in the "captor" variant it's possible that they just need anyone's heart and liver and the captor is closest).

      Adze have the power to possess humans using magic, with some sources claiming that they can only do so while in human form. Some sources claim that sorcerers have been known to invite such possession in order to gain the adze's power to shapeshift into any form, but other sources more credibly suggest that adze preferentially possess sorcerers in order to gain the sorcerer's magic. Victims of possession grow ill and eventually die.

      Apotropaic magic:
      CAUTION: An adze in firefly form cannot be harmed by any means and is very, very dextrous.
      An adze that is captured in firefly form immediately reverts to human (or quasi-human) form. In this form it can be killed. Capturing them is very difficult due to their dexterity, but adze are partial to coconut milk and palm oil (they have been known to drain an entire village's supply in a single night), and using these as bait for a trap has been successful in the past.

      Influences:
      Adze, flying as insects with long noses that they use to drain blood, causing victims to become sick, bear a striking resemblance to mosquitoes spreading diseases such as malaria, and were likely an early attempt to describe the pattern of an insect bite followed by illness.

      The dynamic of possession allows people to claim that those they do not care for are possessed by adze.

          Vampire 2 - Soucouyant
          Also known as: Soucriant, Ole-Higue, Loogaroo
          Myth origin date: European colonial period
          Myth origin location: Caribbean

          Modus operandi:
          The soucouyant is a jumbee, a malevolent spirit. She is universally an old woman. At night she strips off her skin, becoming a ball of fire. She may also take the form of a fiery insect or hairless bat, though human in size. At this time she stores her skin in a mortar, cauldron, or jar, potentially painted black with runes (sources vary, with mortar being the most common). This hellish creature then stalks the night, entering homes through keyholes, cracks, stove pipes, or similar apertures and feeds on the blood of her victims (preferentially sleeping children or pregnant women) by biting the victim's limbs or soft regions of the body. In some variant myths she materializes as a fiery, skinless woman or physically distorted woman in order to do so. Victims of her bites become ill, and those she kills by direct blood loss become soucoyants in some variations of the myth.

          The soucouyant gains power through her feeding, allowing her to cast black magic and vodoun magic. She can shapeshift into insect and animal forms by way of her magic. By day she probably lives among normal people, blending in. She may also associate with other soucouyants, in which case there will likely be a coven leader more powerful than the others.

          Apotropaic magic:
          The soucouyant cannot be directly harmed in fiery form, and though she is vulnerable in her human form, her magic makes her a formidable foe. However, she must put her skin back on at the end of the night (sources all agree that she simply does this; there is no suggestion of an option not to do so or a consequence for failing to do so). The most common way to kill her is to find her skin and render it unusable to her. The most commonly suggested means of doing so is to fill or line it with coarse salt so that she will either die (most variants) or be subjected to excruciating pain and discomfort, revealing her and rendering her vulnerable, when she puts the skin back on. Variant myths suggest that red peppers might be suitable instead of salt, or that the skin must be boiled non-stop in holy water (which will need to be frequently replenished) for 13 days. The latter variant suggests that the soucouyant will attempt to regain her skin by attacking those guarding it.

          One effective defense against the soucouyant no matter her form is exploitation of her compulsive arithmomania. If she is exposed to salt or rice that has been spilled onto the ground, she must collect every grain, despite the difficulty of doing so or the vulnerability it causes her. This can also be used as a means of exposing the identity of a soucouyant. Note: arithmomania involves counting or an obsession with numbers; The soucouyant myth makes no mention of counting, but I characterize the soucouyant's behavior here as arithmomania because of similarities to other vampire myths which will become evident as this series continues.

          In some variants of the myth, a slain soucouyant uses her last magic to permanently take the form of a blood-sucking organism such as a tick, leech, mosquito, or vampire bat.

          Influences and similarities:
          The soucouyant is believed to be a myth that evolved as a combination of French vampire mythology and the beliefs imported by African slaves. Her names belie this heritage: Soucouyant and soucriant have both been suggested as bearing etymological relationship with either the French verb "to suck" or the Fula and Soninke words for man-eating witch. Ole-higue is thought to be a corruption of "old hag". Loogaroo is a likely derivation of loup-garou a mythological French werewolf (consider the similarities in shapeshifting).

          The soucouyant's propensity for taking the form of a fiery insect bears resemblance to the Adze's firefly form. Also similar to the adze is the preference for consuming the blood of children and the propensity for spreading disease to victims. And of course, like the adze the soucouyant shapeshifts and casts magic.

          As in the case of any myth involving a being taking the form of a fireball at night, the possibility of atmospheric lights having inspired or influenced the myth should be considered.

                  Vampire 3 - Penanggalan (Malaysia), Balan-banal (Sabah), Krasue (Thailand), Leyak (Indonesia and Sri Lanka), Ap (Cambodia), Kasu (Laos)
                  Origin date: Unknown (old)
                  Origin location: Southeast Asia

                  Modus operandi:
                  The penanggalan is universally female in most myths, and predominantly female in one myth. In some myths she is young and beautiful, while in others she is aging. In all myths, she craves the blood of young children and pregnant women.

                  By day she appears to be a normal human. She will often work as a midwife in order to be near to pregnant women, probably in order to make her nightly hunts easier. At night her head detaches from her body with her lungs, heart, and gastrointestinal system dangling from her head and dragging across the ground. In this form, her body is lifeless while her head grows fangs (myths vary from two to a mouthful) and she stalks pregnant women and young children, with a special interest in the blood of newborn children and women who have just given birth. As she moves, her entrails sparkle or glow and are said to resemble fireflies. The penanggalan will sometimes wait on the roof of a house in which a woman is giving birth and let out a shriek when the delivery is complete.

                  Those that are bitten by a penanggalan contract a wasting disease which is almost always fatal. Additionally, in some variations of the myth contact with the entrails of a penanggalan can cause sores which will not heal without the help of a shaman.

                  The origin of a penanggalan varies widely by specific myth. Most myths accept that a woman can become a penaggalan through choice by use of black magic in order to permanently preserve her beauty, or by curse in an act of foolish witchcraft such as a botched spell to fly (where only the head flies) or a pact with the devil that is subsequently broken. In some myths a woman can become a penanggalan through the actions of another; a powerful curse can impose the condition, and the daughter or granddaughter of a witch may become a penanggalan. There are also a number of myths of the origin of a specific penaggalen usually involving a wronged woman, such as a woman who is bathing turning her head so quickly toward an intruding man that it comes off, or a woman being burned alive but then rescued before her head or innards were burned. If the origin of the penanggalan has to do with her own witchcraft then additional care should be taken, as her magics may be dangerous, even aside from her penanggalan nature.

                  Apotropaic magic:
                  A penanggalan is dangerous, but can be defeated by normal physical damage to her head or viscera.

                  A penanggalan must return to her body before sunrise. Failure to do so results in death. If the body is found, it can be exploited to kill the penanggalan. Various myths espouse filling it with glass to shred the innards when the penanggalan reattaches, crushing or burning the body so that the head cannot reattach, physically preventing the head from reuniting with the body, or most comically, turning the body upside down so that the head accidentally reattaches to the anal-genital region of the body (this will not kill the penanggalan, but it will reveal the penanggalan for what she is and make her less dangerous).

                  The penanggalan can be deterred with sharp objects that might harm her trailing viscera. Traditionally pineapples may be planted beneath the house of a pregnant woman if the woman lives in a house built on stilts. Also traditionally, the thorned leaves of a mengkuang plant are placed on window sills and thresholds to deter a penanggalan from a house where a woman is pregnant or has recently given birth. In one variant of the myth, penanggalan fear betel nut cutters and scissors, and either of these items may be placed under the pillow of a pregnant woman or new mother as a deterrent of last resort. Finally, if the placenta from a new birth is removed from a house and buried, this may prevent a penanggalan from finding the house (perhaps the creature can track by scent, and this measure is intended to prevent that).

                  In more than one myth, penanggalan can be identified by a scent of vinegar. In one variation of the myth, the penanggalan will routinely soak her organs in vinegar before reattaching to her body in order to shrink them so that she can more easily get them inside her body. In another variant, the body is left lying in a tub of vinegar when the head detaches, as it will rapidly decay if this is not done. In either case, the vinegar so saturates the penanggalan that she cannot escape its odor.

                  Influences and similarities:
                  The image of a penanggalan floating in the darkness but visible as a cluster of fireflies immediately calls to mind the adze especially, and to a lesser extent the soucouyant.

                  The idea of a creature that leaves part of its body behind, vulnerable, at night and needs to return to that part of the body at day strongly resembles the soucouyant.

                  Like the adze and the soucouyant, the penanggalan prefers the blood of children. Like the soucouyant, the penanggalan prefers the blood of pregnant women as well. Like both, the penanggalan spreads disease with its bite.

                  Like both the adze and the soucouyant, the penanggalan may practice black magic even while in human form.

                      Vampire 4 - Strigoi
                      Origin date and location: Positively identified to no later than 1656 in Istria (a peninsula in modern-day Croatia). Purported to date back to the Dacians, which if true would put the origin no later than the fourth century CE, probably in modern-day Romania. They myths exist today primarily in Romani and Slovenian folklore, but exist to a lesser extent in other cultures in southeastern Europe in the area between the Black and Adriatic seas.

                      Modus operandi:
                      Strigoi are predominantly male in Romani mythology and predominantly female in Slovenian mythology. In either case, they are true undead, having once been living people, then died and risen in a mockery of life. They appear to be corpses, though over time they slowly gain a semblance of life until after seven years they appear to be alive. They must sleep in their own graves, although some myths state that after seven years this becomes a less restrictive need to sleep in any grave, and only on Saturday. Some sources instead say that during this time the strigoi gathers with other strigoi to perform sorcery or for social purposes. After reaching seven years of age they become capable of breeding; the children of two strigoi are moroi (future subject of this series) and the child of a human and a strigoi is a dhampir (future subject of this series). They possess two hearts, the extra one near the normal one. The extra heart stores the vitality of the strigoi.

                      Strigoi need to drink blood, which in some myths they do directly from the hearts of their victims (in some myths they consume the heart entirely). In other myths they will transform into a dog, cat, sheep, bat, tick, or insect to consume blood from people.

                      In some myths Strigoi return to their loved ones and attempt to live with them as though nothing is unusual (although they still must abide by their unnatural needs). Loved ones living with a strigoi invariably lose energy and die.

                      Bulgarian mythology states that strigoi cannot be seen (but see Apotropaic magic).

                      A person who dies has a chance of becoming a strigoi if one or more of certain conditions are met. The more of these conditions are met, the higher the probability of becoming a strigoi. The condition vary a little by source.
                      * Born with a caul (placenta covering face).
                      * Red hair with blue-eyes.
                      * Lived a sinful lifestyle.
                      * Unmarried male who died by execution for perjury, by suicide, or after having been cursed by a witch.
                      * Just an unmarried person.
                      * Just a suicide.
                      * Mother didn't eat salt while she was pregnant with the person.
                      * Born with a tail.
                      * Born out of wedlock.
                      * Filled with pain and regret at time of death (may be the cause of suicides becoming strigoi, rather than the act of suicide itself).
                      * Seventh child of the same gender in a family.
                      * Died without having been baptized.
                      * Bitten by a strigoi or moroi (future subject of this series).
                      * Excess body hair.
                      * A third nipple.
                      * Died an unnatural death
                      * Corpse was walked on by a cat

                      Apotropaic magic:
                      Strigoi do not appear to be invulnerable to traumatic injury, although sources are strangely quiet about this issue. At any rate, a strigoi would be a dangerous enemy to face in combat.

                      Corpses can be identified as strigoi by any of the following signs:
                      * Unnaturally well-preserved
                      * Bloated and black
                      * Foot is turned inward
                      * Red in the face
                      * Small hole near the headstone (used by the strigoi as a passage to the coffin)
                      * Lying face down in the coffin with blood near the body
                      * Traces of corn meal on the coffin

                      If a strigoi is invisible (Bulgarian mythology), a twin brother and sister wearing their clothes inside-out on a Saturday can see it.

                      According to some myths, strigoi may be repelled by any of the following:
                      * Garlic.
                      * A cross painted with tar on each door of a house.
                      * A house with all the lights on.
                      * Thistles on the window sills.
                      * Thorns across the threshold.
                      * Lit bonfires.
                      * Prayer.

                      Preventative measures and destruction practiced upon the corpse - all of these methods involve either taking this measure at the time of burial or performing an exhumation during the strigoi's inactivity:
                      * Nail the coffin shut.
                      * Drive a stake of iron or wood through the heart or navel (sources vary on effect; this may be to pin the strigoi to the coffin, or it may immobilize the strigoi on its own. Do not remove the stake as in many or most myths this does not kill the strigoi in itself).
                      * Place garlic in the mouth. Some sources state that the garlic must be under the tongue.
                      * Place iron or steel in the mouth, and bits of the same over the eyes and ears or between the fingers. Drive a hawthorn stake through the legs.
                      * Various methods of destruction by fire, often practiced in combination:
                      * Cut out the heart and burn it.
                      * Burn the corpse. Traditionally the ash is mixed with water and consumed by the family of the strigoi.
                      * Decapitated the strigoi. Burn the head.
                      * In combination, cut out the heart and cut it in two, drive a nail through the forehead, place garlic under the tongue, smear the fat of a pig killed on St. Ignatius' Day on the corpse, and re-inter the corpse.
                      * Bury a bottle of whiskey with the corpse.

                      Influences and similarities:
                      The strigoi is capable of shapeshifting, like the other vampires we have discussed so far. Like the soucouyant and the penanggalen, it suffers a periodic time of vulnerability, but unlike those vampires, this is when it is NOT hunting.

                      The large number of risk factors for becoming a strigoi allow the myth to be used as either a warning away from certain behaviors or as an opportunity to gossip and judge others.

                          Vampire 5 - Mosquito Man
                          Myth origin location: North American Native American tribes, probably tribes from the northwest coast.
                          Myth origin date: Unknown (old)

                          Modus operandi:
                          The mosquito man appears to be a normal man and is capable of interacting with humans normally. However, he is capable of rapidly unfurling from his mouth a long proboscis which he will use to attack humans. Northwest coastal tribes' myth suggests that he uses this to puncture the skull and suck up the entire contents of the cranium (this variation of the myth does not qualify as a vampire for the purposes of this series), while a variant myth suggests that he drinks human blood and typically leaves victims alive, though with an itchy bite mark (this variation of the myth does qualify as a vampire for the purposes of this series).

                          In all variations of the myth, the mosquito man's attack is rapid and stealthy, so that the victim may not realize what has happened even if he was looking directly at the mosquito man.

                          The mosquito man prefers stealth, but if he is willing to abandon it (perhaps for having already been identified), he can unfurl mosquito wings from his back and take flight. A mosquito man in combat is very dangerous for its ability to fly and rapidly kill with its bite.

                          Apotropaic magic:
                          A mosquito man suffers all of the vulnerabilities of a normal mammal, and can be killed with any common means.

                          There is unfortunately no recorded special means of identifying, repelling, or harming a mosquito man.

                          Influences and similarities:
                          The mosquito man's insect theme is reminiscent of the adze and soucouyant particularly. There have been no vampires covered by this series so far that did not possess the ability to transform into at least one kind of bloodsucking insect or arachnid under at least some circumstances.

                          The mosquito man may be an attempt to explain mosquito bitemarks that occur from bites that are not noticed, especially if those bites are severe. It may also serve as a cautionary tale against befriending strangers too closely.

                          Like other vampire myths, this tale allows a gossip to accuse anyone of a stigma that is difficult to disprove.

                              Vampire 6 - Edimmu
                              Myth origin location: Babylonian, Sumerian, and Akkadian. Modern day Iraq and Iran.
                              Myth origin date: The oldest written record of a vampire known to exist is of the edimmu. That record is more than 6000 years old.

                              Modus operandi:
                              Edimmu (roughly "demons") are a type of utukku (spirit). They are unquiet ghosts - it is important to note that the ancient Sumerian cosmology did not differentiate much between ghosts, angels, demons, and gods, seeing them all as the same sort of being in a metaphysical sense.

                              Edimmu came into being when their corpse's funerary rites were willfully neglected, when they were summoned from the underworld by a necromancer, or in circumstances when the edimmu was unquiet or restless, perhaps due to a violent death or just because they were wicked in life. Edimmu that came into being due to improper burial would focus their harangue on the individual who had been responsible for their burial and that person's family, but all edimmu were wicked in nature and severely violent toward all people in general.

                              Edimmu were believed to travel as wind, enter a home through a hole or crack, and work all manner of harm, including causing illness and disaster, tempting to criminality, draining life energy from occupants (especially children), and possessing people. This aspect of the myth sounds much like a demon of Christian mythology.

                              Edimmu would sometimes also manifest physically and attack people, devouring them or drinking their blood. When they manifested, they sometimes chose to do so as a hyena, although at other times they were humanoid in appearance. They would sometimes sit on the chest of a sleeping person, and from this position draw blood or life force (this resembles to some extent certain demon myths of Europe, such as the incubus, succubus, and sleep thief.

                              Apotropaic magic:
                              Edimmu could be fought by other utukku or gods, but not by normal people.

                              Edimmu could sometimes be appeased with proper funerary rites and offerings (this is probably related to the belief that they might come into the world due to improper funerary rites).

                              Spinning black and white yarn around the bed of a person being harangued by an edimmu would protect that person while they slept.

                              The Babylonians and Akkadians developed multiple exorcism rituals to cast out edimmu. In fact, the Utukku Lemnutu is a surviving ancient document recording an exorcism ritual in both Babylonian and Akkadian, making it important for linguistic study. One shorter exorcism rite is the chant reproduced below:

                              Evil utukku, evil âlû, evil êdimmu, evil gallu, evil god, evil rabisu, labartu, labasu, âhhazu, lilu, lilithu, handmaid of lilu, sorcery, enchantment, magic, disaster, machination which is not good–may they not set their head to his head, their hand to his hand, their foot to his foot–may they not draw near. Spirit of heaven, mayest thou exorcise, spirit of earth, mayest thou exorcise.

                              Similarities and influences:
                              It is highly probable that this myth spread to Europe, India, and other parts of Asia. It may have even traveled back into Africa. Similarities can be seen not only in vampire myths, but also in the myths of various other supernatural beings. It seems likely that edimmu myths diverged into myths about different types of supernatural creatures, each having some of the characteristics of an edimmu.

                              Among vampire mythology, the blood drinking, shapeshifting, predilection for preying on children, spreading disease, sapping vitality, and traveling in a non-corporeal form may have been transmitted to other cultures. This doesn't seem to explain the insect and especially firefly similarities, though.

                              Regardless, the myth appears to have been a catch-all explanation for all manner of negative events, from medical problems such as infectious disease, loss of vitality, sleep apnea, and so on; to natural disasters; to criminal behavior and general sinfulness.

                                      Vampire 7 - Jiangshi
                                      Also known as: Chiang Shih, Ch'ing shih, Chiang-Shi, Giang Shi, Kiang-Kouei, Kiang Shi, Kiangshi, Kouei, Kyonshi, Xianhshi, and outside Asia as "the hopping vampire"
                                      Myth origin date: No later than 1700s
                                      Myth origin location: China

                                      Modus operandi:
                                      In traditional Chinese theology, each person has at least two souls (in some theologies, more are present), a good "higher soul" called the hun, and a wicked or at least selfish lower soul called the po. The most common view of a jiangshi is that the po of a decedent has remained in or returned to its corpse, absent the hun. More rarely, the jiangshi might be seen as a body possessed by a demon. In either case, the myth remains almost identical. The dreaded hopping vampire is a true undead human that has been raised to a mockery of life.

                                      A jiangshi might come into being by any of the following circumstances (most of these would not these consistently create jiangshi, and it would seem likely that meeting more than one of these criteria would be a warning sign for unusually high risk of becoming a jiangshi):
                                      Improper burial, which might be as innocent as failure to bury a piece of the body while the rest was buried properly
                                      Failure of descendants in duties of ancestor worship
                                      A cat jumped over the body before it was buried.
                                      The body did not decay properly for some mundane reason. In this case, the fingernails and teeth can be seen to grow (actually a sign of normal dessication and decay as gums and skin recede and are mistaken for nail and tooth growth)
                                      The decedent died of suicide, drowning or being buried alive
                                      Deliberate creation by the sorcery of a living person
                                      A corpse absorbs enough yang qi
                                      The person's po just wants to cause trouble
                                      Viral transmission from a jiangshi bite (modern fiction only)

                                      Jiangshi are nocturnal, sleeping during the day and in some variations of the myth sleeping on moonless nights as well (the thought being that the light of the moon gives them energy, which alone would not explain inactivity during the day, when the moon is often present). They must feed on the blood of humans, preferentially men, in order to gain energy. These attacks are brutal and do not leave the victim alive unless the victim escapes. They possess elongated nails or talons, probably in reference to the mistaken idea of nails growing after death, which they have been known to use to rip heads off bodies. As if they weren't already bad enough, they are also known to rape women.

                                      Jiangshi are afflicted with rigor mortis and unable to move their limbs much. Their arms are typically stiffly held straight out in front of them, while their legs cannot bend sufficiently for normal walking, forcing them to hop. Despite this, their long, talon-like nails and powerful bodies make them extremely dangerous. In some variations of the myth, they are blind, yet still somehow able to find their victims. Their locomotion is assisted by their ability to turn into a ball of light and move about in this form.

                                      Jianshi can be identified by their red eyes and by the hair which begins to grow all over their bodies (most variations of the myth) or at least on their heads (the remainder of the variations of the myth) when they first rise. This hair grows a greenish color, but as they age and mature, the color becomes more white. When the color is pure white, the jiangshi is fully mature. At this point, it can fly through the sky in its human form, and it can also shapeshift into a wolf. A mature jiangshi is extremely dangerous.

                                      Apotropaic magic:
                                      Jiangshi that are raised by failure to perform ancestor worship may be returned to normal lifelessness if ancestor worship duties are observed.

                                      Jiangshi that are raised by improper burial may be returned to normal lifelessness by receiving a proper burial (you'd better bury the corpse during the day!).

                                      In some variations of the myth, sunlight destroys them.

                                      It is said that a broom can be used to sweep them back to their grave.

                                      Jiangshi can be destroyed by having jujube seeds nailed into the jiangshi's seven acupuncture points from its back, by fire, by having a Buddhist or Taoist death blessing that has been written down pressed to their forehead, by being shot with a bullet, or by the sound of a loud thunderclap too near to them (which would usually only happen while one was flying during a thunderstorm).

                                      Jiangshi fear thunderstorms. They also fear their own reflections, making mirrors an effective way to repel them. They are also repelled by items made from the wood of a peach tree and by a rooster's call. They cannot cross running water, nor can they cross over iron fillings, red peas, or rice. Barriers of these things can encircle and trap them. They suffer extreme arithmomania, and are compelled to count any spilled grain regardless of the circumstances.

                                      It should be noted that many say that a mature jiangshi cannot be destroyed except by a bullet or thunderclap. Other methods of destruction listed above do not work.

                                      Influences and similarities
                                      The hopping vampire may be related to a practice called Xiangshi ganshi, literally "driving corpses back to Xiangshi". In the past, many migrant workers from Xiangshi would leave to work elsewhere. When they died, it was thought that they might become homesick if buried elsewhere, so they were transported back to Xiangshi. This was often done by arranging them together in a cluster, each corpse vertical and tied to bamboo for physical support. Since bamboo bends, they would bounce a little as they were transported, hence the appearance of hopping. They would be transported at night to avoid contact with other people and because the temperatures were cooler at night (transporting bodies without refrigeration does not make for a pleasant odor!).

                                      The green coloration of a young jiangshi may be a reference to a fungus known to grow copiously on the cotton burial garments once used in China.

                                      Traveling as a glowing ball of light calls to mind numerous other vampires we have seen so far (adze, soucouyant, penanggalen). Shapeshifting into a wolf continues the ubiquitous theme of shapeshifting into animal forms and specifically calls to mind the strigoi. Arithmomania is shared with the adze. Origin by improper burial is common with the edimmu, and several other methods of creation, including "just being bad", sound like the edimmu or the strigoi (the other true undead we have encountered so far).

                                          Vampire 8 - Manananggalen
                                          Sometimes confused with: Other types of Aswang (monsters or witches), especially the tik-tik and wak-wak.
                                          Myth origin date: Unknown (old)
                                          Myth origin location: Phillipines, but also known in other parts of Southeast Asia such as the Indonesia and Malaysia.

                                          Modus operandi:
                                          This creature is univerally or predominantly (sources vary) female. In human form, the manananggalen is beautiful. She may be aging, but she is aging well. She remains in human form all day, and is generally normal.

                                          At night, the manananggalen (literally "one who separates itself") separates her upper torso from the rest of her body. The lower portion of the body remains in place and inert, while the upper portion transforms into a bat-like creature to hunt, and viscera continue to dangle down from this creature's open torso. It needs to feed upon the blood of an unborn fetus, and so it hunts pregnant women to pierce their abdomens with its proboscis-like tongue. It sometimes perches on the roof of a pregnant woman's house.

                                          Less common myths suggest that people become manananggalen through simple heredity or be consuming human flesh or the bodily fluid of a manananggalen. However, the most common variant suggests that each has a live, black chick inside of its throat, which grants it immortality and power but must receive the blood of fetuses. The chick is sometimes passed on from a mananggalen to its descendants, which allows the original manananggalan to die.

                                          Apotropaic magic:
                                          A manananggalen is destroyed if it fails to unite its body before dawn. It can be prevented from doing so if, while it is hunting, the lower portion of its body is found and the inside of lower portion of the body is sprinkled with salt, crushed garlic, or ash.

                                          Other than this, the only way to destroy one of these monstrosities is to remove the chick from its throat, which can be done by tying it upside-down and smoking it or by spinning it around until it vomits up the chick.

                                          Some people try to use thorny leaves on windowsills to deter manananggalen. This may be confusion with penanggalan, but as the two are so similar, this should probably be considered a deterrent to the manananggalen in that creature's myth.

                                          Influences and similarities:
                                          The manananggalen bears striking resemblance to the penanggalan, which is from the same reason. It seems obvious that they are variant myths of one another, either from a common source or one having spawned or heavily influenced the other. However, they maintain distinct mythologies concurrently in largely the same region. Similarities include leaving a part of the body behind to feed, chosen prey (newborns or pregnant women for the penanggalan, blood of the unborn for manananggalen), and some of the related apotropaic magic. However, due to distinctions in genesis, appearance, details of transformation, and destruction, the two are distinct.

                                          Separating part of the body from the rest is shared with the soucouyant. Taking a bat-like form is the least ability of shapeshifting we have seen so far, but still evokes the strigoi and probably other versatile shapeshifters among the vampires examined so far. Again, we see an obsession with vampires preying on children or pregnant women, common to may other vampires. Notably, salt is used as an apotropaic again, and garlic is a shared apotropaic with the strigoi.

                                          It's possible that the predilection of this and other vampires to prey on the blood of newborns or pregnant women is based on "the blood is the life" concept common to many cultures, with newborns representing the freshest life and thus the life most able to sustain another if it is stolen.

                                              Vampire 9 - Moroi
                                              Myth origin date and location: Probably originated in the same culture and about the same time as the previously-covered Strigoi, so possibly 4th century or possibly as late as 17th century, somewhere between the Adriatic and Black seas.

                                              Modus operandi:
                                              The less common variant of the moroi holds moroi to be living, relatively peaceful vampires. They are often born as the live offspring of two strigoi, or of a moroi and a human. The seventh son of a seventh son may also be a moroi, as may the illegitimate child of two illegitimate children. Regardless of genesis, they can be identified from birth based on any or all of the following criteria, visible at birth:
                                              * Unusually hairy
                                              * Born with a caul
                                              * Has a tail
                                              * Has an extra nipple

                                              These living vampires drink some blood from their sexual partners but do not generally kill. They are predisposed to practice witchcraft. When they die, they are predisposed to become strigoi. They can also become strigoi by drinking the blood of a human and killing that human.

                                              The more common variant of the myth is substantially different. In the variant, moroi are the undead, restless ghost that results when an infant dies before baptism. A variant myth is more specific, demanding that the parents conceive the child illegitimately and then murder it before it is baptized. This variant may be based on a specific tale and would satisfy the more general version of the myth. These restless beings may prey on the blood of livestock or on the blood of their relatives, even eating hearts. They may harass their parents with tempests or hail storms or attempt to lure their parents into the grave. They can shapeshift into adults as well as dogs, cats, horses, sheep, toads, and bloodsucking insects. At night they cry, asking for their baptism. They can also possess the bodies of the living, usually of living bears.

                                              The body of such a moroi, if exhumed, will be found to be bleeding from the ears, eyes, nose, and mouth.

                                              A final variant of the myth is of a simple witch who steals livestock. This is not a vampire for the purpose of this series.

                                              Apotropaic magic:
                                              The living variant of a moroi is a mortal which can be killed by physical trauma, disease of any kind, or anything else that might kill a human. Precautions should be taken, lest the being rise as a strigoi (see the strigoi entry, apotropaic magic section).

                                              The unbaptized child variant can be prevented from forming or may be quieted by any of the following:
                                              * A relative carrying a candle (in some variants the candle most recently used by the moroi in life - perhaps it was near the child), walking around the grave three times.
                                              * Burning resin every Tuesday and Saturday night.
                                              * Burning the clothes of the moroi
                                              * Stabbing the heart of the corpse with a pin, needle, or nail

                                              Similarities and influences:
                                              The direct relationship to strigoi is obvious.

                                              This is a rare instance in which we see an infant vampire. Usually we see vampires preying upon infants. Even so, we do see a vampire harassing its relatives (as in the cases of the edimmu and jiangshi). Also reminiscent of these vampires, as well as the manananggalen, is the failure of relatives to adhere to a religious duty (in this case, baptism). Ability to cause ill weather is reminiscent of the edimmu, as is focus on an improperly observed. Shapeshifting is ubiquitous as ever, and possession either reflects the edimmu or is derivative mythology based on shapeshifting.

                                              Moroi mythology may have been an attempt to classify various birth abnormalities as well as to explain erotic vampirism practices. The idea that relatives are being punished for failing or potentially murdering an infant allows neighbors to blame a couple for their own misfortune, potentially as a means of abdicating responsibility to charity.

                                                          Vampire 12 - Draugr
                                                          Also known as: Draug, draugar
                                                          Myth origin date: Documented to no later than the 13th century
                                                          Myth origin location: Iceland. The myth spread among all Norse populations in Northern Europe and remains a staple undead in the fiction of some nations today.

                                                          Modus operandi:
                                                          Buckle your seatbelts, kids, because this thing in a nightmare that makes the edimmu look like a vampire pumpkin.

                                                          The draugr is a malicious revenant that consumes people whole, consumes their flesh, or drinks their blood, the latter of which qualifies the draugr as a vampire for the purpose of this series. It is a true undead. It's body does not decay, but it does turn black or dark blue. Draugr are hellishly malicious and do not value the affections of their former lives at all.

                                                          A person who dies may rise as a draugr if they were mean, greedy, or selfish in life. However, in some variants of the myth, even dating back to some of the earliest documented accounts, this is a vampire that collaterally reproduces. Any living person slain by a draugr becomes one. No special wound or method of killing is required to spread this terrible curse. Which sucks.

                                                          Draugr are nocturnal hunters, and are most active in midwinter. They stalk the night seeking any victim at all. Shepherds are one common target in ancient stories. They typically dwell in their barrow or tomb during the day.

                                                          Draugr are possessed of a wide range of powers. Principally, they are physically terrible. They are unnaturally strong and heavy. They also have the ability to enlarge themselves at will. Fighting them in physical combat is extremely difficult.

                                                          Draugr also cause madness. Any animal that approaches near the barrow of a sleeping draugr will go mad and howl itself to death. Draugr are capable of using psychic attacks to cause madness in humans, and this madness may cause suicide. This attack does not seem to be 100% effective, and is moderately more effective against women than against men, although both genders are susceptible.

                                                          Unfortunately, this is not the end of the draugr's power. Draugr can swim through earth as through water, and can create hours of darkness in day. They can also put a curse or doom upon any human. Finally, they possess trollskap, the magic of witches, which they can use to control the weather, divine the future, or shapeshift into animal and monster forms. Creatures they shapeshift into are often flayed. Forms seen in ancient mythology include a seal; a great, flayed bull; a horse with a broken back and no ears, a cat that sits upon a sleeper's chest and grows steadily heavier until the sleeper suffocates, and a troll. Finally, draugr spread disease in the communities they harass.

                                                          Draugr SUUUUUCK.

                                                          Apotropaic magic:
                                                          Step 1: Be a badass Viking warrior...

                                                          No, but seriously, the primary method of dealing with these things is to be an epic hero and to barely win in a fight against one, WHICH ISN'T EVEN ALWAYS EFFECTIVE FOR THE EPIC HEROES.

                                                          Let's back up.

                                                          If a corpse is found vertical, upright, or just in a weird position not laying down, that is a warning sign that it will become a draugr. Of course, a person killed by a draugr will also become a draugr. In such cases, there are various methods of prevention, which can also be applied liberally to any corpse even if it does not display warning signs (listed in order of efficacy):
                                                          * Burn the corpse. In fact, some believe that the iconic Viking funeral of burning a warrior in a boat was intended to protect the community from the depredations of draugr.
                                                          * A special corpse door was built in the barrow. The corpse was brought into the barrow through this door feet first. The door was then sealed with brick and in some cases grave binding runes were carved on a stone within the barrow. The belief was that the draugr may rise but would be unable to leave through any door other than the corpse door, and that would be sealed so that it could not be used by the draugr.
                                                          * Special preparation of the corpse could prevent it from rising as a draugr. This included placing a pair of iron scissors on the chest, hiding twigs among the clothes worn by the corpse, and either tying the big toes together or sticking a pin into the sole of each foot to prevent it from walking.
                                                          * The coffin could be lifted and lowered in three directions as it was carried to the barrow in order to confuse the sense of direction of the draugr, making it difficult for the draugr to return to the community burying it.

                                                          But let's say somebody didn't burn a corpse and either didn't do the other stuff or it didn't work, so now there's draugr. First, you have to find the damned thing. If you see a barrow from which emanates a great glow, as of foxfire, that's just the barrier between the world of the living and the world of the dead, made manifest in the burial mound. Your draugr miiiiiiiiiiight be there (hint: it is totally there).

                                                          So then back to step 1...

                                                          If you are fighting a draugr be aware that they are not actually harmed by sunlight. They are nocturnal, but this is more like "daytime hours are not my preferred period of activity" and less like "sunlight will turn me to dust". Rely on sunlight if you want to die.

                                                          So what do you want to do? Remove its head from its body. This will incapacitate, not destroy, the fell creature, allowing you to then cremate it, which WILL destroy it. In order to be sure that the creature will not return, the ashes must be scattered in the sea (again, Viking funeral).

                                                          That might be a challenge, however, since in the case of some draugr, NO MORTAL WEAPON CAN HARM THEM. Your solution in this case is to wrestle the draugr back into its grave, where it will then be compelled to stay. After that you will have an opportunity to decapitate and burn it. Bear in mind you are doing this while it is growing, turning into a troll, attacking you with super strength, making you go insane, casting magic on you, and trying to swim away through the ground. I AM SURE YOU WILL BE JUST FINE.

                                                          In one recorded instance, a house was infested with draugr, and a ritual was used to force them to return to their graves instead. An ancient legal procedure allowed a lender to set up a court in front of a defaulted debtor's house, far enough removed from the front door that the debtor would have space to set up a counter-court, with enough space between for a wagon laden with wood to pass between comfortably. This was called a door doom. In the case of the infested house, a door doom was set up outside the house and one-by-one, each draugr was summoned to the door doom, where pronouncements against its behavior were made and it was convicted, then sentenced to return straightaway to its grave. Each draugr obeyed based on ritualistic compulsion, and there they remained. The house was then blessed with holy water to prevent return of the dead.

                                                          Influences and similarities:
                                                          Iron as an apotropaic is reminiscent of the strigoi, as is piercing the feet to prevent the dead from walking.

                                                          Scissors as an apotropaic calls to mind the penanggalan.

                                                          The similarity between draugr and dragon is a false cognate. Draugr is related to the Old English "dreag", meaning "ghost". As such, their is no etymological relationship with Vlad Dracul, as Dracul means dragon.

                                                          Cremation as a means of destruction, the spreading of disease, and shapeshifting have been seen in many past vampires.
