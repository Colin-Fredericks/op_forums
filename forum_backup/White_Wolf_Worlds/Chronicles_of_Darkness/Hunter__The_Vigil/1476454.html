              I'm generally reluctant to share a lot of my criticism of the books on these forums. Folks here can get heavily invested and very thorough in their readings and rebuttals. I don't mean in any way to hit and run, but I'm probably just going to leave my thoughts here and move on.

              I'm quite disappointed in Hunter Second Edition. I don't think it makes for a bad game book, but I think it hits mediocre at best. Vigil First Edition set a very high bar to meet, and I don't think it had to hit the same mark of writing and design perfectly to be a satisfying and exciting book to get. But in addition to a lot of little things and frustrating decisions that build up in the aggregate, the spirit isn't there.

              Vigil's 1e core had a murky hoariness to it, a sense that the shadows were thick and pretty much anything was waiting out there. As iconic as a lot of the compacts and conspiracies were, they felt like elements in a vast ocean with a lot of room for other things, and much of the information about them that later books rolled out raised more questions than it solved. Apocrypha was rife and contradictory. The first tier was everpresent, and its spirit was present even in higher tiers, with a street-level vantage point concerned with the immediate experience of hunters on the ground. Second Edition feels at once too defined and not focused enough, too invested in details carried over from past books and built on and interconnected, and not enough in what purpose they serve. The first tier feels less present, and the vantage point feels raised, with more attention given to movers and shakers and organizations and connections between defined and listed compacts and conspiracies. It sometimes feels like a Second Edition book written for readers already familiar with First Edition to know not only who these people are but why they should feel invested in them. Less is more, and Second Edition is too "more" and not enough "less."

              Smaller, more individual points:

              The treatment of Null Mysteriis is more coherent than in First Edition.
              The treatment of the Cheiron Group loses its strongest thematic element around which everything else neat and strange about it was wound, that the Field Projects Division was in some sense secondary to a large medical conglomerate, a way of recouping investments in failed experiments. It was most centrally an industry demonstrating the capacity for monstrocity of human society, not through alien invaders but through the simple logic of profit. Cheiron wasn't truly scary and faceless because rumors spread about the ancient lives and many names of the Board of Directors. It was scary and faceless because the corporation wasn't sending you to risk your lives against vampires so that they could see through the devil's eyes, but so that they could catalyze a binding agent that reduces production overhead on medical adhesives by 3%.
              The treatment of VASCU just misses the mark completely.

              I like that attention is given to supernatural objects and locations. I don't like that hunters now have a much more distinct Integrity trait from non-hunters, I don't like the way it works, and I especially don't like that despite not working like Integrity works, it is still referred to as Integrity.

                  ^this, but adding the personal to it:

                  Disappointed might not be how I word it, but Second Edition definitely didn't punch as hard as First Edition. Indifferent might be the expression of my feelings.

                  I'm fairly sure I've bitched about this before and else where, but for me the biggest cause of this comes from the way that Chapter 2, where a lot of the feel of Hunter 2nd is established, it a lot more concerned with the world of hunters on an "organizationally" internal basis, where a lot more of the text is interested in the operations hunters within their own structures, how those structures interact with each other, and how it plays out on the chess board arrayed against the night-all of this in contrast where Hunter 1st spent a lot of time both establishing the emotional backdrop and drive of individual hunters and how the sort of nightmares out there form the basis of relationships hunters have with the Vigil. 1st Edition is a lot more streetbound, focused on the individual action and consequences, a lot more casually paranoid. 2nd Edition is more top down and disassociated.

                  Another casualty of this is the way that the shadows, grime, and muck of it all don't really feel like they work their way into a hunter's life, attitude, and soul. It's addressed, sure, but because 1st Edition took a more intimate and causal relationship with the acts that make up the Vigil, that version makes it easy to understand how the Vigil warps and eats away at a hunter, how bringing yourself to match the monsters burns away at humanity to keep up in kind. 2nd Edition makes it feel more....professional, even in it's duress. Maybe that works in the age of the hustle and gig economics, but if I really wanted the vibe of the dehumanizing and unrewarding aspect of that sort of stuff expressed in monster hunting, I'd go play #iHunt(and I encourage everyone to go check that out).

                  On the whole, Hunter 2nd feels cleaner and more depersonalized than it probably should. I'm still more or less a fan of it, because I'm a fan of 2nd Edition on the whole even as time is showing it's wear and tear, and Hunter 1st is a hard game to one-up because of how well executed 1st edition was, but of the 2nd Editions, it's undoubtedly the one I'm coldest towards. I'd recommend it to keep the gameline up to date with everything else, and that's pretty worth while-but I can't say there's a whole lot that excites me about it.

                      The "To Be Fair" counter-argument:

                      One of the major problems with doing a second edition is that Hunter's Core, much like the Werewolf line on the whole, didn't really leave a whole lot of room to grow or build or do something. It was a really solid core rulebook, and it's a hard thing to follow up on.

                      It didn't really have a tone problem or a missing cool kids factor.
                      It didn't have a muddled focus or direction, a problem of understanding what it is the players do, or why and how and it's larger thematic implications.
                      It didn't really have a mechanical disassociation from the rest of it's narrative.
                      And it didn't really have implied spaces that begged for detailing and expansion.

                      (If you look at all the other games that made the transition from 1st Edition to 2nd, you can find one or more of the above things that becomes clear with the shift.)

                      Hunter was-still is-a hard game to follow up on. About the only thing you could really do more of with the 2nd Edition is lean more into the locality of the Vigil much like how there was a general push to dig into the locality of every other gamelines, getting how specific places with specific histories and supernatural landscapes leads to the particular shapes and forms of hunter responses and organizations (and honestly, some of the ways they did that are very much appreciated, Like SWORN or the Six Stars), and you can only do so much of that before you get 50 flavors of the Union or such. Otherwise, how do you make Hunter 2nd that does something new/expansive while still keeping the game what it is?

                      A lot of the answers I have on hand for that are more things that could be served to be baked into a hypothetical 3rd Edition of Chronicles on the whole. For Hunter specifically, most answers I have come out "Do more of what it was doing already" which is not the sort of thing that gets people excited and full of praise the same way they did for their peers. I imagine people who actually had to work on the development of Hunter ran into the same issue, and did the best they could with what they could.

                                  A lot of the perceptions of 2e being vast improvements is a lot to do with how much improving some of the cores really needed. The "Big 3" of Vampire, Werewolf, and Mage all had a lot to two in 2e: dump a lot of the hold over stuff from WoD that didn't really serve the CofD, clean up a lot of mechanics, clean up a lot of lore/presentation, and so on. Promethean, Changeling, and Geist had less tonal/thematic issues to address, but benefited from some serious redesigns of core mechanics to better capture what the games are about. Mummy is somewhere between those.

                                  There's a reason why the biggest debates on the forums around HtV 2e were what to do with Ashwood Abbey... that says something about how much 2e was going to be more of a tweak than a redo.
