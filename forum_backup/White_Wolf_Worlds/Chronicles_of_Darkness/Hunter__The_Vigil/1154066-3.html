                Originally posted by White Oak Dragon View Post
                As the Code stands now, I see several problems that would be encountered in my games. Namely, one of the major recurring themes in my games is that for all the power possessed by supernatural creatures, the greatest monsters are often human.
                "The true monster is Man!" is generally not going to be a theme supported by a set of games that have been repeatedly emphasized as humanist in tone.

                Another scenario: Hunter B's best friend escapes from Arcadia, and pours out the whole ordeal over a couple drinks. Hunter B gives a shoulder to cry on, and promises that he's not alone anymore. He even helps his changeling buddy to deal with that pesky Huntsman that just won't leave him alone. Then one day, he comes across another hunter that's beaten his changeling buddy to within an inch of his life. If his buddy manages to turn things around and takes out the guy, Hunter B will suffer a breaking point because a supernatural creature that he refused to harm ended up hurting someone. Perhaps I misunderstood, but it didn't sound like self defense was a valid justification.
                I'll be completely frank: This is a terrible example. Let's put this into the context of the world and an actual story/game to diagram out all the major failure points.

                First and most pressingly: Crossover like what you've implied by direct reference to several Changeling-specific narrative elements being interacted with by this hypothetical hunter is inherently going to run into difficulties — Hunter's mechanics not playing well with the abilities of actual characters in other gamelines without making judgment calls as an ST was a remarked-upon issue with running the game in allied crossover play in 1e. "Your mortal monster-hunter character is going to encounter some narrative/psychological tension maintaining friendly relations with monsters" is one of those things that seems like an expected problem that you either accept or houserule around if you want to press onward with it, much like the nocturnal constraints of Vampire or the risk of Disquiet in most meaningful interactions Prometheans have with people.

                "The thing I changed about myself so that dunking myself in exposure to magic doesn't make me a complete wreck of a person pulls in ways that I rationally and intellectually and surface-emotionally may not be comfortable with when confronted with the problems keeping up a friendship with someone a mad god turned into a monster" is a point of drama that informs the setting and affects how those characters interact in a way that underlines the themes of the supernatural in the Chronicles of Darkness, which is not a generic system where magic is harmless and monster-hunters exist entirely out of unfounded paranoia and prejudice.

                Second: Okay, your buddy was changed by the Fae and made his way back. One — Believing this at face value is being overtrusting verging on gullible, as is volunteering this information. General trends being what they are, if your friend is a changeling, he's not the only one in town, to say nothing of what fetches do to hike up the threshold for trusting people to be who they seem to be. If these characters either don't exist or haven't already been antagonists in the story, then you have brought a lone changeling into your Hunter story, presumably for the purposes of underlining the problems with how that character living in the same town as you with magic powers, which indicates a certain degree of stewardship over the buddy on your hunter character's part, which the Code covers through its Persistent Conditions and the fact that allowing harm to come to people because of the supernatural is a breaking point.

                Meanwhile, if those characters do exist in the story and your Hunter character inhabits a setting populated in part by the setting's most notorious imposters and the pod-people their erstwhile masters left behind — the former of which often have the ability to mess with memory and emotion or change their features or invade people's dreams, never mind trade and sell roles and information like the things that might confirm your supposed changeling buddy's identity — then you have to deal with not only a breaking point every time your buddy develops a new magic power and demonstrates it to you as an exercise of trust/full disclosure, but you have to deal with the fact that the people who understand what he's been through are generally invested in keeping the things they got away from out, which is generally best managed by getting more magical powers and/or interfering in people's lives, which you and your cell may take umbrage with and which will generally be a reason for your changeling buddy to keep secrets from you.

                Two — Changelings are trouble-magnets. Even if your buddy and his support group behave themselves completely as model citizens and don't ever pursue resources to enhance their own security (to say nothing of just deciding to flee the coop through the dread dimension they can all make a lasting portal to at minimal cost), the fact is that there are certain temptations they all have at their fingertips (to say nothing of the more particular fixations some of them may have developed during their time in Arcadia) and ensuring that they do not seize those temptations requires a level of scrutiny and surveillance that is comparable to imprisonment — i.e. the thing they all have pretty strong feelings about. And that temptation to gird themselves against aggressors is only going to get stronger when the world they inhabit is one where most of them are on the run from fate-twisting god-monsters and the lesser horrors that seek to court their favor — going after the Lost's friends and loved ones is a time-honored tactic among their enemies, and most of those aren't going to be hunters. Do you put your friend above your family, his family, your entire town and everyone you love?

                Third: Huntsmen aren't early-game antagonists in either iteration of Changeling 2e — the Fae don't send one out for a fresh-from-the-Hedge fugitive, which means you and your buddy with the heart-twisting magical powers either went through a lot of stuff that imperiled your worldview as someone who tries to keep the supernatural from harming the mundane or reconnected a long way into his time as a faerie. Neither speaks well for your Integrity with or without the Code.

                Hunters, by a similar metric, generally aren't going to go after a monster without a reason — sometimes that reason may be petty or banal, but "a hunter comes after your changeling friend" is inherently not something which Just Happens. This holds especially true for changelings, whose innate disguises are helped by the relative manageability of their personal weaknesses — they don't have Death Rage or the daysleep or even the flickering of their mundane disguises with power use to worry about giving them away, which means either you didn't keep a close enough eye on your buddy or something that wants to get at him tattled to this other hunter (who you've apparently never heard of despite operating in the same region, if the lack of detail is any indication) to call in a hit.

                Likewise, if you are present for a conflict between your changeling buddy and a hunter and you do nothing to intervene, then of course you're going to get shaken up if your buddy dies or your buddy with supernatural powers kills a guy. The workaround for that is to step in before it escalates to the point of murder, broker an understanding, and make it clear that your changeling buddy is not going to hurt anybody on your watch. "Monsters hurt people" is something that regular Integrity normalizes by not looking at it — a direct confrontation with that facet is inherently going to be a problem for a monster-hunter who is friends with a monster.

                Your buddy attracting a conflict with a hunter due to his state as a supernatural being does not get a pass from consideration to Integrity just because he's your buddy and to assume two members of differently-regularly-paranoid splats having an unconditionally trusting relationship without difficulty as the goal of a core narrative widget in a monster-hunting game diminishes the themes of both Hunter and Changeling.

                As written, it seems like "Make sure you have the right weapon, make sure you have no witnesses, make sure it's really dead, make sure you leave no evidence," is always a safe approach to the Vigil, at least as far as Integrity is concerned. If you want to maintain a high Integrity on the Vigil, be a speciesist bastard and sentence everything touched by the supernatural to death regardless of actions or context.
                In other words, the folks who can manage the occasional doublethink of being a serial killer or a mass-murderer because they get enough of a reprieve from the supernatural to cool down and reinforce their ties to humanity sleep better at night than the folks who give the monsters a chance and keep tabs on every one that they know about in an environment where the monsters are not universally well-behaved.

                You maintain a high Integrity on the Vigil by strengthening your Resolve and Composure, maintaining multiple Touchstones, being self-sacrificing and adhering to your Virtue, regularly engaging positively with people you trust, and not letting monsters hurt people while you're around. Killing every monster you see happens to have a more long-term success rate in this regard than babysitting an entire motley or freehold, but as has been underlined multiple times in other games, a high or low Integrity rating does not translate to a "good" or "bad" character, and as has been highlighted within this very discussion, a breaking point roll is not synonymous with Integrity loss.

                A high-Integrity hunter who burned down a house full of ghost-children is no more a contradiction in terms than a high-Wisdom Seer of the Throne who makes liberal use of hard mind-control. Meanwhile, the Integrity 5 werewolf-watcher is going to be growing a lot faster thanks to the Beat-engine strapped to her psyche, and she'll have less cleanup to worry about in the long-term even without membership in a clandestine government agency or a debauched secret society to lighten the load.

"The part of me that resolved to be a monster-hunter means I'm going to run into some trouble when I identify more with the ethos of a monster than with a human they're preying upon" is a problem that the game puts forth as a problem for characters to struggle with. The core character concept is not one that gets to avoid these complications.

                            If you aren't willing to make the buy-in of "I'm hunting monsters and some of those monsters are an awful lot like people and this forces me to confront some unpleasant questions about who I am and what I'm doing hunting monsters unless I draw some hard boundaries about the things that can change shapes or control minds and hurt my friends' heads to look directly at" then there's not much point in talking about what the game should and shouldn't do in the name of notional flexibility based on a rigid reading of narrative mechanics.

                            To me, Chronicles of Darkness, and Hunter especially, always had as a major theme that no, being human doesn't automatically makes you a good guy,
                            Not positing that it does! But if your position is "humans are, on the whole, worse than monsters that regularly steal blood, ruin lives, break reality, shatter sanity, and attract the attention of eldritch horrors from beyond mortal ken," then a set of gamelines where being a conscious and responsible supernatural being often means interfering with humankind as little as possible while maintaining a limited number of strong personal mundane relationships is perhaps not the best place to exercise the assertion that that position is equally valuable to the game as the one where knowing who you are and what your life is depends on applying a certain degree of compartmentalization to the horror of those parts of reality that strain the mind to encounter.

                            Humanity does not need to be unanimously virtuous for mundane criminals to be a different category of issue from sanity-melting creatures of variable ethics and cognizance, nor does an instinctive understanding that the supernatural is naturally incentivized to keep its distance from mundane humanity translate to anything that merits a prompt for Godwin's Law.

                            If your Hunter characters are in a setting where the monsters only go after bad people and their attentions are obvious enough that monster-hunters are present and active, your Hunter characters are going to have to make decisions that influence their place in the narrative. What happens when the monsters run out of bad people? What is your cell's plan for bad people in the absence of monstrous interference? Why should poking at the dark underbelly of the world in either sense of the word not be a strain on your self-image? What does being a bad person actually have to do with vampires and why is letting a bad person get eaten supposed to be better for your sense of self than letting a bad person try to become a better person in the absence of bloodhungry undead interference? Is handling these issues their job or can they just stick to keeping monsters from messing with human lives and systems?

                            These are things a Hunter game that doesn't deal with the monstrous equivalent of wild animals attacking complete innocents has to handle, and a monster hunter who makes an exception is one who has trouble coming down the line one way or another. Humanity as a general phenomenon and at least in the particulars of the people you take up the Vigil for has to be worth it for stability to be a thing. You find out your neighbor's a serial murderer who's not a slasher? Kill him and deal with the consequences, let the prospective-hero vampire have him and deal with the consequences, hope he becomes a slasher or a vampire and deal with him then, or alert the authorities and let them deal with the consequences. This isn't a situation where being a monster-hunter gives you an advantage or an excuse and Hunter would be a very different game if it was.

The most important difference is a subtle one, but crucial to understanding the slasher. Serial killers take human life to inflict pain, to elicit emotion or sensation in themselves, or to lash out at their abusers in symbolic fashion. For a slasher, the kill is the point. Some varieties of slasher do espouse higher or more complex agendas, but faced with a choice between killing and not killing, a slasher will always kill.
                                                        This is the frightening truth: sometimes, people kill. And not just out of passion, not because of poor judgment or misguided revenge. No, sometimes they kill because they want to, they need to. Maybe they’re born that way. Maybe they’re made. Some are driven to the point, while others are programmed.

                                                        They can’t just kill one person. Oh, no. They kill two, three, ten, and they keep going until the body count mounts or until they’re stopped. They become compulsive about it. It must be done in a proper way. A certain organ must be harvested. A cipher needs to be painted on the ceiling above the body. The kill must be completed with a homemade weapon: a set of teeth made from bone spurs or a makeshift ax forged out of junkyard metal. Some have their victims and will not stray: they only kill liars, lustful teens, corrupt cops, beautiful people, pedophiles, perverts, prostitutes, cops, or housewives. Some just don’t care. They’ll kill anybody. Anywhere. A horror house with bodies stacked to the goddamn rafters.

                                                        Sometimes, the killing changes them. It’s like a switch. Except, instead of turning something on, it’s turning something off. A light, maybe. A part of one’s self, of one’s soul. A place inside goes dead — or, hell, maybe it was dead long ago. Maybe it was never alive in the first place.

                                                        And out of that darkness rises power. From the void comes secret power that couples the endless urge to kill with supernatural might or monstrous cunning. Soon, the killer cannot be stopped by bullets. Or maybe his words become truly honeyed, sliding into the ear canals and the brains of his victims like a little snake: could be that, as long as he uses his trusty crowbar, his kills are always perfect, always immediate.

                                                        The slasher. A perfect predator. Society’s modern bogeyman. Part human, part… something else.

                                                        Resident Lore-Hound
