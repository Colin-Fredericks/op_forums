                            Adrenaline is ecstatic. Honesty is refreshing. The risk of losing sight of the lines is the stuff of epic stories.

                            I like the AA, despite everything that makes me squick-and this is coming from a guy who goes from chill to violent at the suggestion of sexual violence, mind. But Ashwood gets to certain ugly truths that are at the core of any Hunter, and gets right to the hard questions: not only why people become terrible, but how we handle desire and power fantasy. If anything, Ashwood criticizes the justifications of Hunter flat out, and asks players to ask questions about play experiences: why do we enjoy a game about hunting monsters, tearing apart NPC's and our PC's both physically and emotionally, and why we stop where we do, and in facts asks us if we're honest with our own reasons. It's a chance to realize terrible things about ourselves, and thus gives us a real chance to grow and develop. I like them for some of the same reasons I like Beast.

                            Which, as a guy who pushes RPG's as art, art as communication, and communication as a spark for improvement, is a big deal.

                            EDIT: I also like how, despite everything, they are among the most likely groups to treat monsters as people, rather than just prey.

                                            I think the problem with what you and ArcaneArts are saying as positive thematic role the Abbey has to play, is that it is inherently flawed from the get go: they're 1% elitist snobs taken to near-cartoonish Hellfire Club shenanigans.

                                            There's simply too much going on with them to really achieve them as a narrative foil due to their lack of ideology beyond thrill-seeking. They would be far better for challenging the justifications of other Hunters, and providing challenging role-playing to players, if they weren't wrapped up in the hedonistic aristocrat deal (even discarding the sexual squick). The fact that they're wrapped up in all sorts of cultural baggage that the vast majority of the world's population both can't really relate to, and actively resents for it, it's too easy to dismiss them.

                                            Though I find the use of the words, "honest," and, "pure," about them exceedingly off-putting.

                                            If I want a group of Hunters that challenge all those things, I need ones that can't be dismissed as rich white people solving rich white problems with money, influence, and power. All of that taints their ability to really be challenging in-narrative or as a player. I'd much rather have a Compact like Project Mayhem from Fight Club if I wanted to do this (which works even better with the GMC side of things really).

                                                Originally posted by Teatime View Post
                                                As things stand now, I consider the Abbey a bit of a missed opportunity. While the other Hunter organizations have aspects that can cast them in both good and bad light, Ashwood Abbey's presentation doubled down on the negative. I think "Compacts and Conspiracies" made a step in the right direction, by showing their thrill seeking as not necessarily repugnant.

                                                There aren't many hunters who would be willing to actually socialize with the monsters or to spend the night in a haunted locale just for kicks. Trying to make friends with monsters opens up many possibilities plot-wise, which trying to kill them - doesn't.
