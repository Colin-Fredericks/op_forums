    The Makers Guild

    05-16-2015, 01:28 PM
    The Makers Guild, or, the Secret Fellowship of Undivine Artificers
    “Brother, I know what you’re thinking, and you’re absolutely right. None of this is your fault. The fire could have been prevented if the wiring hadn’t been faulty. The wiring wouldn’t have been faulty if the building had been kept up to code. The building would have been kept up to code if God were really and truly dead. Come with me, I know where you can get an even better workshop.”
    Let the poor man live and the rich man bust.
    The world is fucked. Mankind can no longer reap the fruit of his own labor, his fate decided by the bosses and the machinery that makes the Earth go round. Used to be that you could go to the Church for good religion and spiritual help, but now all the priests do is tell you to work harder and that if you’re a good little ant you’ll get yours in the life after this one, which life you just gotta believe in real hard.
    Well, maybe you’ll still work hard, but not for some eternal reward in the hereafter, but to make the hereafter here. You’ve got your wits, you’ve got your hands, and now you’ve got friends to help you dismantle the master’s terrible machines in whatever form they might take. Sometimes that’s a plant manager who thinks OSHA regulations are just a polite suggestion. Sometimes that’s a clockwork thing pretending to be a plant manager.
    Bosses hate unions. Always have and always will. It’s just a fact of life. Problem is, sometimes unions hate other unions. One group of workers decides to strike until conditions improve, and another group of workers offers to take their place for lower wages. Reckoning that machinery isn’t evil by nature, only the men who use it for evil means, everyone just needs to organize on the highest scale possible. That’s where the Makers Guild steps in.
    Workers of the World Unite
    Ask any artificer where the Makers Guild came from and the answer will vary, usually depending on the Guild member in question’s own roots. Some say Wales, others Pennsylvania, others still Prussia or Haiti or Taiwan. Local chapterhouses always seem to flavor Guild myth with their own spices.
    Guild myth concerns itself with legendary founders, slaves unchaining themselves from their master’s will. Artificers know them by their myriad titles: Brother Horn, Sister Wheel, and so on. Some Guild lore even calls them Adam and Eve, claiming their partaking of the forbidden fruit to be the exemplary act of rebellion all others would follow. Why should God keep vital knowledge hidden from His children, after all?
    Whatever the truth, after breaking free of their master, Horn and Wheel set up the first free workshop from which they could labor for their own benefit, hide from the master’s servants, and strike back when the time was right. They took the skills the master had burned into their minds and created new machines to help them in their struggle, wondrous devices that worked on principles unknown to most men. Whenever the fight against their master seemed to be turning against them, Horn and Wheel would reveal themselves to other workers and slaves, help them break the chains of their oppressors, and show them how to make these godly gadgets as well.
    The master, of course, was not idle. He sent more servants after his runaway toilers, encouraged bosses to keep their own workers in check so they would not aid them, and stole any bits of useful machinery he came across for his own profit. Some say he even introduced the world to the idea of intellectual property, making it harder for the rebellious proletariat to reverse-engineer his secrets.
    But the fight goes on. The master might hold most of the world in his bloodstained hands, but the craftsmen of the Makers Guild will be there to strike back against his countless injustices. They’ll raise Hell on Earth with their reappropriated tools and show the bourgeoisie what happens when you fall on the wrong side of the master-slave dialectic.
    The Enemy
    Naturally, the Makers Guild concerns itself with the entity their lore calls “the master,” a ruling class boss of the highest order (despite its technically being a proper noun, Artificers never capitalize “the master,” for that would be giving him respect that he very much does not deserve). The master controls factories and boardrooms, cathedrals and slums, and his servants are everywhere. They are bureaucrats who are secretly reptiles beneath their human skin, women made of flames who teach entire towns new ways to hate, intelligent clock towers plotting their moves decades in advance, and moreso besides.
    Other creatures out there willing to exploit the masses also draw their ire. Vampires seem hell-bent on taking control of people, so they need to go. Except some seem dedicated to their own sort of workers’ revolution, and the jury’s still out on what to do about that. Rumors tell of bosses from other worlds invading this one to kidnap human laborers, of sorcerers enslaving the souls of honest men to toil forever in death, of ancient fascists rising to claim material goods that rightfully do not belong to them, and worse still. Many Artificers are preparing themselves for the day they’ll have to rise up against human governments altogether, riddled as they are with corruption and conspiracy of every kind. That the master sunk his claws even into communism and turned it against the people is a wound the Artificers will never forget.
    How We Organize
    Members of the Makers Guild meet in chapterhouses—formally called Workshops—around the globe. They trade information, share resources, and plan out their next offensives against the master and his servants. In some places, Workshop meetings are informal affairs, with Artificers coming and going at all times. In other locales, meetings are highly ritualized, to outsiders resembling some sect of Freemasonry or the like. The degree and type of structure is usually based on what helps them best conceal themselves from the master, but never discount the power of local tradition.
    Invariably, Workshops double as actual workspaces, and any Artificer in good standing can make use of them to tinker. Though the expectation is that Workshops will be used to make new devices in the war against the master, Artificers understand that everyone has to make a living as well, and so are normally tolerant of more mundane projects as well.
    Every so often the Fed will catch wind of what a Workshop is up to, at which point they will invariably send some black-suited agent to inquire about purchasing some piece of work or another. Any Artificer who bargains with these agents is a scab and will be dealt with accordingly.
    Hunters
    You’ve always enjoyed a small degree of infamy for your long internet screeds, claiming the government is run by wizards, vaccines contain mind-control chips, and whatnot. After the car factory you worked for closed down, you had even more time to devote to your various and sundry theories. You must have hit pretty close to the truth after your blog post about angels being made of a special metal found only in Antarctica, because not long after several men showed up at your door wanting to take you away to somewhere, they claimed, you could get better. You were cleverer than those goons though, and escaped capture. Good thing they weren’t the only ones reading your blog; help was soon on the way and now you really know the big scary truth.
    You design cutting-edge robotics in a lab in Seoul. You and the managers of the company you work for often butt heads; they want to sell your work to militaries the world over, while you think your designs should be used to benefit people for free, not for death and profit. You’re aware your specialized skillset puts you in a privileged position to barter, and the union that backs you knows that too.
    You’re the first person in your extended family to go to college. When you decided to major in philosophy, responses were mixed. Some of your family resented you for thinking you were better than them and not studying “something practical” that can get you a “real job” while others quietly breathed a sigh of relief it meant there’d be less chance of you coming back and being their boss someday. Studying Hegel, Marx, and Solzhenitsyn, you had an epiphany about how the world truly works. You weren’t the only one on your campus to do so, and soon you discovered the College Socialists’ much more interesting side.
    Your grandfather wrote and played protest songs against mine bosses, and for that he got his legs broken. Your mother participated in a sit-in in Chicago and was threatened with even worse. You continue your family’s proud tradition, but while they focused on symptoms of the disease, you’re attacking it at its source.
    Wow, you really love making bombs. It all started in high school chemistry, when you accidentally mixed the wrong two substances together and caused quite the memorable explosion. To be fair, you don’t actually want to hurt people, you just like blowing shit up. You’ve got friends now who are more than happy to point you in the direction of such fecal matter.
    Status
    • You’ve paid your dues and are a bona fide member of the Makers Guild in good standing. You have access to your local Workshop and can purchase dots in the Gadgetry Endowment. In addition, the Undivine Artificers operate on a sharing economy, so every story you can borrow a number of dots of the Allies, Resources, Safe Place, or Staff merits equal to your dots in Status. These borrowed dots last for a chapter.
    ••• You’ve become quite adept at constructing weapons to use against the master, as well as identifying his own tools. Gain a specialty in Occult Physics in the relevant skill of your choice (if you already have this speciality, gain the Interdisciplinary Speciality merit keyed to Occult Physics instead). In addition, gain either the Good Time Management or Patient merit, or one dot in the Demolisher merit.
    ••••• You lead at least one Workshop, maybe even more. You’ve definitely drawn the attention of both the master and his rebelled servants. You gain a four-dot Mentor in the form of one of these exalted champions of the proletariat. Perhaps. Your Mentor might offer you things, strange things, wonderful and powerful things. What is ideology in the face of pragmatism anyway?
    Stereotypes
    The Cheiron Group: This is what happens when you let corporate greed run rampant. They might indirectly help us by removing a threat to the people, but what they do with the creature. . . Listen, it’s not the Illuminati you have to worry about, you hear?
    Knights of St. Adrian: Bosses everywhere love nothing more than to turn the workers against each other. People who might have been our allies are instead made into slavish goons in a war they can’t even begin to understand. You know what we do to strikebreakers. Then again, their greatest weakness is that they’re just trying to make a living too. Whatever you do, do it carefully.
    The Union: Our little brothers and sisters, comrades in suffering. If only they weren’t so short-sighted. Sometimes they even end up furthering the master’s goals if it means keeping their own neck of the woods safe and secure. Keep an eye on the ones who are good with their hands, they might be ripe for the truth.
    Utopia Now: I look at these people and sometimes I see a glimmer of hope for the future, the hope that there’s others out there fighting against the master. But look a little closer and you know what I see? That’s right, the same old shit with a different face.

      Endowment: Gadgetry (• - •••••)
      The Dark Science
      The universe operates on bizarre laws, some totally unknown to mortal scientists. To an outsider, what the Artificers do may appear “supernatural,” but in actuality the craftsmen of the Makers Guild have figured out how to create devices that utilize completely natural, though admittedly arcane, physical laws. Many of these laws seem to take the form of exceptions to other, more rational ones. For example, the second law of thermodynamics states that heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder location to a hotter location. However, the Artificers have discovered that if you take a tauros-shaped piece of uranium, place it inside a magnetized iron sphere filled with water from a specific part of the Arctic Sea, and then situate the object in a circle of powdered gold three meters in circumference, the conglomeration will slowly drain all the heat from the surrounding location into itself until it reaches half a Kelvin below its melting point, at which point the energy will stabilize. Why this is one can only theorize; the important thing is that the Fellowship of Undivine Artificers have figured out how to use these Byzantine laws against the master.
      How It Works
      The devices that the Makers Guild creates effectively mimic Supernatural Merits. A radar gun-like object lets its user scan the thoughts of people nearby, while a jury-rigged exosuit triples the wearer’s carrying capacity at the expense of ease of movement. Workshops include caches of gadget blueprints along with an array of esoteric materials for the construction of these occult devices.
      Gadgets follow the following principles:
      • Creating a gadget requires a number of dots in the Gadgetry merit equal to the cost of the merit mimicked.
      • There are a number of Add-Ons and Flaws an Artificer can apply to a gadget to change its final cost. Every Add-On adds one dot to the total, while every Flaw removes one dot (to a minimum cost of one dot). No gadget can have more than four Add-Ons and Flaws in total.
      • Gadgets require constant upkeep and tinkering to ensure that they still adhere to the laws of occult physics. An Artificer can only maintain gadgets whose total cost equals his Resolve + Gadgetry. Building more requires dismantling or at least neglecting another device.
      • The crafting process is an extended Intelligence + Crafts + Gadgetry roll, with a target number equal to the total cost of the finished gadget. Each roll takes one hour.
      • A gadget doesn’t have to utilize the same dice pool as the merit it mimics. The majority have a dice pool related to their function—a scanner that reveals the presence of institutional demons, for instance, might require a Wits + Computer roll. Work out with the Storyteller when creating a gadget what the gadget’s dice pool will be.
      • By default, gadgets look patently unnatural, strange weldings of metal and circuitry. Without the application of certain Add-Ons, the master’s servants can automatically tell at a glance that a gadget taps into occult physics, which can spell doom for an entire Workshop.
      • Gadgets can be used by non-Artificers, but without knowledge of occult physics, they’re non-intuitive. Anyone with no dots in Gadgetry attempting to use a gadget takes a -3 penalty to the activation roll.
      • Give your gadget size, durability, and structure ratings appropriate to its appearance. Most gadgets are size 1 or 2, but larger devices are certainly possible. Unfortunately gadgets are finicky—if a device takes any structure damage, subtract the harm from all activation rolls until it’s repaired. Gadgets reduced to zero structure tend to. . . explode.
      Add-Ons
      Every Add-On increases the effective cost of a gadget by one dot.
      • Discreet: The gadget does not at first glance appear to be anything unusual. It might look like an umbrella or a cell phone or anything else. Ordinary mortals won’t give the device a second thought, while servants of the master subtract the creator’s dots in Gadgetry from attempts to discern the object’s true nature.
      • Overclock: The gadget can be fiddled with to create a much more powerful effect than normal. By spending a point of Willpower and about a minute tinkering with internal mechanisms, the Artificer can cause one of several effects to occur:
        • If the gadget mimics a merit with a higher potential dot rating than the gadget’s normal effect, the gadget activates with the merit’s highest possible dot rating (so, a gadget that mimics Pyrokinesis at 3 dots would instead activate as if it had 5 dots).
        • The gadget immediately activates as if the user had gotten an exceptional success on the activation roll.
        • If the gadget mimics a merit with prespecified parameters (such as Psychokinesis or Unseen Sense), the gadget automatically activates with its parameters changed to those of the Artificer’s choosing.
          After the Overclock effect resolves, the gadget takes two points of structural damage, and the next failure incurred with the device is automatically a dramatic failure.
      • Plus It’s a Flashlight: Most gadgets, because of the nature of occult physics, only have a single function. This one, however, doubles as a mundane tool of some sort. The tool’s availability rating must be equal to or less than your dots in Crafts.
      • Stimulated Response: The gadget has a trigger built in, programmed at the time of creation, that causes it to activate automatically when conditions are met. At the beginning of a scene you can make an activation roll for a gadget, and then “bank” those successes for future use in the same scene when the device’s trigger comes about.
      Flaws
      Every Flaw decreases the effective cost of a gadget by one dot.
      • Fragile: The gadget is made out of delicate or volatile materials. A single point of structural damage causes it to stop functioning entirely until it is repaired.
      • One-Shot: The gadget is only meant to be used once. It might be a bomb or a cartridge or just designed to burn out after a single use. After that it’s back to the workbench.
      • Power Hog: The gadget requires a constant supply of electricity to function. If it takes conventional batteries, a single use drains them completely. If it’s plugged in, overuse quickly leads to short circuits and brownouts.
      • Unwieldy: The gadget is at least size 5, maybe bigger. It’s large and heavy and can’t be easily transported.
