    Sell me on the God-Machine

    11-20-2013, 11:31 PM
    Or at least help me grok it. As I said in another thread, I'm so far from grokking it, I think I actively korg it. And that's not even a good neologism like grok.

    I don't mean the mechanisms of Infrastructure, which is pretty well-explained in terms of Occult Matrices in chapter 1 of the Demon text and can be mapped to a lot of gnostic demiurge/reality-cancer concepts that aren't necessarily The God Machine.

    I mean the what and why of the GM itself, this thing that can warp space and time but
    can't breach a thin layer of rock to provide oil to an array of machines that's somehow been waiting for that to happen for millions of years
    in one of the God-Machine Chronicle story seeds

    I don't find that kind of mysterious inconsistency mysterious, just irksome. It's like the GM is at once too big and powerful to fight, and yet too weak and dependent on nigh-coincidence to take basic care of itself - so how does it exist as a threat in the first place?

      He was content to continue with the rig’s original function for a time, but then the God-Machine found it had need of the platform. It moved the drill slightly and pushed down into the sea bed, seeking to uncover something left buried for millions of years (see Blueprints).
      If the drill continues unabated, it eventually opens a cavern below the sea floor. Therein lies an exposed section of the God-Machine that hasn’t been in operation for millennia. It requires the oil above it to work, meaning that if the drill reaches this cavern, the God-Machine pulls the oil from the deposit and then comes to life, absorbing the drill and the cables and assimilating the rig itself.
      I have toys in my basement that I haven't played with in years. Some of them require batteries to operate that have long since run down. I am fully capable of going down there and digging those toys out and putting fresh batteries in them, but:

      a) I'm lazy and haven't been in the relevant part of the basement for years, so I'll probably ask a family member who's more familiar with the area to get them out of storage, and
      ​b) I'm lazy and the moment I put batteries into those toys I am kind of obligated to play with them before the batteries run out again, which I probably won't do unless I have to entertain my baby cousins over Thanksgiving, so I'll probably put off digging the toys out for as long as possible until Thanksgiving is around the corner and I know my baby cousins are coming with my aunt to visit and I will have to keep the little squirts occupied while my parents catch up with their sibling/in-law.

              The way it comes across to me is that the conditions are so incredibly contingent that it seems that the God-Machine's only real power is to eff the supposedly ineffable and riff on strange power confluences that nothing else can understand - and then it fails to eff not even the supernatural forces but the plain old mortals who come in to eff things up, sometimes managing to catch an eff of the ineffable themselves in the process.

              The more I think on it, the more I end up with a disappointed view of the God-machine. It seems to know just enough and have just enough agency that its failures can break the world but it can barely keep itself running, much less stay in good working order. From a perspective outside the setting, I don't feel a sense of victory at the thought of defeating the God-Machine, I feel like I'd be kicking the universe's biggest puppy for messing up the carpet when it didn't know better. Except the carpet is usually human (and sort-of-human) lives, so better do it anyway. It's not like the character knows anything other than to be scared of what happens if the seemingly-godlike machine succeeds (or worse, fails spectacularly).

              Maybe that's actually the way it's supposed to be? I guess it would fit the overall themes of the World of Darkness. Everything is broken.

                      I am not sure I get your question, but remember that the GMC does not work on a human time scale, and works mostly through "objects" already in motion. So when it needed fossil fuel, it ensured humans started extracting that from the ground. The amount of energy needed to reshape space and time is huge, the amount needed to nudge a human to go drill for oil is very little. The GMC would rather wait a few thousand extra years before using the fuel than waste energy to do it now, unless the need is huge. And here the unknowable comes in:

                      The GMC is likely doing a cost/benefit analysis on the concept of just using angels or other parts under it's direct control to get fuel for Infrastructure vs. using "incidental" human activity. Obviously, the energy needed to do the former was to high compared to the benefit In other words, if time is not an issue, the GMC will always do the most energy economical action possible.

                          The God-Machine has two major goals: survival and growth. I'm going to focus on survival, since it'll be basically be the same.

                          It's already surviving, it's just needs to maintain that. Maintaining survival means building up redundant infrastructure, and keeping enough delicate processes on hand to make timely adjustments. That's the status quo it strives for. Things working as intended. All lights green. All systems go. Optimal operating procedure. This means the world and its physics continue to function as well know it. Including some of the supernatural elements we've come to know and love.

                          Which might be good, but maybe not. Maybe the world would be a lot cooler, a lot less of Darkness, if we should shed this pseudospiritual parasite. Since there are entities (demons, stigmatics, hunters, player characters) possibly working toward that goal, maintenance is the primary goal of the GM. It knows what it needs to do to accomplish this goal: infrastructure, infrastructure, infrastructure. And so it puts its existing infrastructure to work creating additional infrastructure (yo dawg, etc etc etc). To do that, it might need to repurpose things. Or use components its been sitting on.

                          Example: The GM needs an angel that isn't a hunter angel to make some small component in, say, a factory that's infrastructure. Let's say, some kind of builder angel. A maker angel. The only angel in the area? Of course its a hunter angel. So it calculates the most viable options:
                          1. It can stop maintaining the hunter angel's cover, and use it for the new angel. That might later require additional infrastructure to adjust to fit. Then there's a hunter angel wandering around without a cover, which is not efficient.
                          2. It could try to use the inappropriate angel to make this thing, but that might require additional support. And the potential for disaster is very high.
                          3. Dispose of that angel and use the aether budgeted for the hunter angel's purposes to bring a new, appropriate angel and cover into being.
                          For the sake of argument, it finds 3 is most efficient as far as resources. The infrastructure to do this exists, and the aether is already budgeted. It just needs to get rid of the angel. Fortunately, that hunter angel had been trailing a whole posse of demons, and requesting backup. There's no way it can challenge the whole ring on its own and survive. So the GM tells it to proceed with the plan. There are three outcomes, all acceptable.
                          1. The angel somehow wins. A different kind of victory, and definitely the worst outcome, but it's still furthering the GM's goals.
                          2. The angel totally fails. It is obliterated without harming one demon in any way. Furthering the GM's goals! Now it can create its builder angel.
                          3. The angel partially succeeds, destroying or damaging some of the ring, and perishing. GM's goals met plus bonus.
                          From the demon (player character) angle, it looks like a single, unprepared angel has attempt to catch them off guard. This doesn't make any sense! The Destroyer took demonic form, and pounded the hunter angel's face off. It cost him his cover, but they all survived! What a stuuuupid angel, goddamn.

                          Elsewhere, that unused aether (and a bunch of subtly manipulated infrastructure) goes into the newly freed-up RAM slot, and a new, purpose-filled angel steps out. It makes a thing, implants it, and the GM's new redundant back-up comes online, which is far more important than a single hunter angel. A special bonus, this ring of Unchained is weary now, and they aren't going after infrastructure for a while. Or they are, but they're way cocky. A win for the GM in either case.

                                  I guess being a programmer gives me basically automatic understanding of this unknowable thing, haha. Well consider it an operating system, one that responds to various input and output capabilities similar to any other operating system. It has scheduled tasks that it performs, automatic updates, things of that nature. I'm sure everyone knows how that goes. Now take it a step further and think that not only does it do software updates, but tells when hardware updates are necessary and when to unplug unnecessary systems.

                                  That is basically what the God Machine is, but with a little more sentience than that... or just the same amount (It could be very well simply running a program that was long before executed that may have run amok).

                                  The God Machine isn't GOD, it simply has similar capabilities when running the appropriate programs due to precise infrastructure (You try running high quality games with a bad graphics card). It could have been Made by God, Aliens, Spirits, King Kong, Godzilla or Cthulhu, but it is still a Machine. Its simply a machine with major capabilities that impact the entire world depending on what function call has been made.

                                              It's an unknowable and ineffable God that is decidedly not loving and sinister, though not necessarily outright malevolent (and protects itself and therefore earth from some apocalypses, like Y2K f'r instance), it's the secret behind the world in a conspiracy-theory kinda way that lets you play Call of Cthulhu-style adventures or Matrix-style adventures.

                                              It can link in to any of the gamelines and their cosmologies (some more easily than others - Promethean and changeling being easier, vampire and Mummy being harder IMO). It can catapult you off into sci-fi - it's an alien computer! A machine built by future-humans! The result of a transhumanist Singularity that all of humankind coalesced into! You can file off the machine parts, too, which is a turn off for some people.

                                              It's a big enemy that you can't defeat, like the Cthulhu Mythos, but can delay, harry and win tiny victories over which gives your players the rewards they need and keeps the horror angle intact (well, you can defeat it and one of the GMC book's hooks gives you the option, but generally it's not presented that way). It can be used to justify very, very weird goings on such as time travel, cryptids, multiple dimensions...

                                              What is the God-Machine? A toy. A framing device. A deus ex machina in every single way. And it's yours to customise (or ignore) into something you can work with.
