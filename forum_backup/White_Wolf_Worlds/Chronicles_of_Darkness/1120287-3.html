                    Alright, so, here is where things get really blurry. Because of course that isn't the end of it.

                    Fifth Century Britain Part Three: Arthur Roulette

                    So Ambrosius makes it back to Britain just in time for the Saxon kingdoms to start pushing back into British territory. More Saxons are flooding into Britain because Clovis I of the Franks, one of the first kings of the Merovingians (oh, hey, look, a canonical Proximus dynasty) is uniting the Franks and the Gauls and doing a spectacular job of it. Europe isn't looking like such a great place to colonize and/or adventure in at the moment. In the late 470s a new Saxon leader emerges, a fellow by the name of Aelle who doesn't go for the traditional overland approach from established Saxon territory, instead overwhelming the southernmost Saxon Shore forts from the sea. Aelle brings three ships of men (as many as Hengest and Horsa before him) but unlike Hengest and Horsa, he doesn't try to take on all of Britain with them, just Sussex, and he has his three adult sons with him to back him up. He entrenches himself so well into Sussex that the British have to write the territory off as a lost cause. Aelle later becomes the first Saxon High King.

                    With fewer soldiers on his side, more soldiers on the enemy side, and enemy warriors who have gotten used to his tactics, Ambrosius' second campaign is significantly less successful. There are still victories, but they are significantly less enduring. Aelle is constantly testing at his borders, and in 485 he breaks whatever peace he had with the surrounding British, having gained the backing of the other Saxon leaders on the south side of Britain, and communicating with the Angles (possibly Jutes, if that's where Hengest and Horsa's original land grant was, before Kent) in the north around Norfolk. The Anglo-Saxon groups, wherever they may be found (except for a group settled around Gloucester who may have been the ones that Vortigern initially settled in the 420s, and who revolted with Hengest and Horsa in the 440s) go on the warpath. This single allied group, the Gewisse, may have eventually formed the kernel around which Wessex later formed. This Saxon offensive all along the Southern and Eastern fronts was potentially met by two sustained campaigns from the British. Ambrosius, supported by the sons and grandsons of Vortigern, in the south, and an unknown leader based in the north who went all along the eastern front, potentially even fighting off resurgent Pict and Gaelic invasions from the north and the west.

                    Ambrosius' lines started crumbling with a massive Saxon push in 491, starting with Aelle besieging the Saxon Shore fort on his northwestern border. The siege is a massacre where the Saxon Shore fort falls with "not even one Briton left there". Saxons and Jutes moved northwest from their territory, Angles moved southwest from their lands, marching across central Britain and moving to break the unified ring of British kingdoms. Traditionally in 495, the first king of Wessex, Cerdic, arrives in Britain with his warriors, hemming in the British of the southern kingdoms. The Anglo-Saxons know they have to divide the British kingdoms to end the war, so they put everything they have into one assault. The unified British armies make a stand at an unknown location known only as Badon Hill. The Saxons lose, incredibly badly. So badly, in fact, that the majority of the Saxon kingdoms cannot raise an organized campaign for almost another forty or fifty years.

                    Gildas, the only near-contemporary writer, ascribes the victory at Badon Hill to Ambrosius Aurelianus. Aelle's position and his aggressive action in the 480s and 490s fits with the traditional timeline given for the "Arthur" campaign, and the archaeology best supports a concentrated, unified Southern Britain, but doesn't account for more northern Anglo-Saxon/British conflict. The archaeology also supports tense Anglo-Saxon/British interactions around the Humber and in the area that would eventually become Bernicia.

                    So, who the hell was Arthur, the man fighting this campaign leading up to the confrontation at Badon Hill? Let's look at out options:
                    • Ambrosius Aurelianus (~430s-500): Ambrosius definitely lead the second campaign against Hengest and Horsa in the late 450s into the 460s, considering Vortimer was dead and so was Vortigern is he wasn't so disgraced that he wouldn't even consider making a play for the High Kingship again. However, Ambrosius would be in his late fifties or early sixties by the time of Badon, which makes him unlikely to be the man leading the charge, and is even less likely to have lived to fight at Camlann. He must have delegated to someone.
                    • Riothamus (unknown-468): If he is a separate person from Ambrosius, there's still the problem of time period, the fact that we never hear from him again after Euric and the Visigoths slaughter his army (even though records say he survives) and there's no mention of him in Britain. He would also have needed to be able to lead at Badon, and live to die in combat around 515 CE at the legendary battle of Camlann.
                    • Pascent (~430s-500): The last surviving son of Vortigern, King of Powys, being the legendary Arthur? The timeline issue still crops up, but if Ambrosius was leading the military campaign against the Saxons he would need someone handling governance, someone he could trust. Nennius says that Ambrosius made Pascent governor of the "province" his father had ruled. While he almost certainly wasn't Arthur, he could probably be called Governor of Britain under High King (or Vicarius) Ambrosius.
                    • Cadell "Bright-Hilt" and Riocatus (~460s-530): Cadell was the son of Pascent, who might have been a penniless lord due to the disgrace of Vortiger, barring one exception. Virconium, formerly Vortigern's capital and the last thriving Roman city in Britain (the others were inhabited, but had nowhere near the same level of inhabitation or industry). A king known as "Bright-Hilt" ruling from the last thriving Romano-British city near several potential sites for Badon is quite compelling. His cousin Riocatus (King of Battle) appears to have been a cleric who eventually went to visit their uncle Faustus in Gaul, but returned around the time of Badon and may have even inherited the throne from Cadell.
                    • Owain White-Tooth and Cungelasus (450-530 and 480-560): There is literally no evidence that Owain was Arthur other than that he lived at the right time, ruled in Rhos, a sub-kingdom of Gwynedd, and was associated with a place known as "Fortress of the Bear". His son, Cuneglasus/Cynlas the Red, was a brutal despot, who was famously known for divorcing his wife to marry her sister, a nun, after abducting his sister-in-law from the convent where she was living, but is outright called "Bear" (Arth) by Gildas. Gildas also ascribes to him "arms unique to himself." He was also related to Maelgwn, King of Gwynedd, first attempted High King after Ambrosius and given the title "Dragon of the Island" by Gildas.
                    • Brychan and Dyfnwal (~430-500 and ~455-525): Two northern British kings (father and son) who were actually given the title Pendragon, but existing legends distinguishes between Arthur and Brychan, who in legend had to be stopped by Arthur and his warriors from rampaging across Britain and slaughtering a prince of Gwent who kidnapped his daughter. Most of his children entered the church as priests, monks, and nuns, including a son named Arthen.
                    • Agricola and Vortipor (~440-510 and ~470-540): Agricola is praised by Gildas as a good king, but his son Vortipor, called "Protector", was one of the despots criticized by Gildas. Vortipor, a title like Vortigern or Vortimer, is written as "Garthuir" in Gaelic and he could have helped his father, who was an important Welsh king in the defence of Southern Britain, win at Badon Hill. He'd fit the classification of a high-ranking general who was not, himself, a king.
                    • Urien of Rheged (535-591): Too late to be Arthur, and a separate Arthurian character in his own right as Uriens of Gore, Urien king of Rheged was known for being one of the most fearsome warrior kings of the north with a famed band of cavalry. His brother, Lleuddun (Loth), his son Owain, and his nephew Gwrfan (possibly Gawain) all contributed to Arthurian lore. Urien's distant relative Morcant (Mordred?) was part of an alliance of five kings successfully keeping the Angles contained, but Morcant had Urien assassinated, and the alliance collapsed in the wake of his death, leaving the Angles free to act. This was also the period of activity for the bards Myrddin and Taliesin.
                    • Arthwys ap Mor (~450-520): The only man on this list whose name has the "Art" prefix, and King of Elmet in the north. There's a long literary tradition of a Northern Arthur, starting with his very first appearances in the written record. He is a descendent of Coel Hen (Old King Cole) who if he wasn't the last official Dux Britannium he almost definitely took over the position and kept passing that duty off to his relatives or allies, the warrior-kings known as the Men of the North. He was based out of York (Ebrauc) a Roman fortress-city, and his descendent's kingdoms were mostly situated around there. Arthwys may have given his name to the forest of Arthuret, where Myrddin would run mad almost half a century after his death. Arthwys' brother or son was Elutherius of the Great Host, whose sons were Peredur and Gwirgi (Percival for sure), known for possessing a warband of foot soldiers considered invincible when paired with the warriors (especially horsemen) of the other Men of the North. Elutherius is inaccurately attributed a son, Arthur "Wing-Headed" or "Highest-Head", when he fact he had a daughter whose name was superficially similar to Arthur.
                    And that's the last of the fifth century. It's been a wild ride! Hope you enjoyed it. My personal pick for the "core" Arthur is Arthwys ap Mor, but a lot of men on this list, most notably Ambrosius Aurelianus, Cadell "Bright-Hilt", and Urien of Rheged, and their relatives and descendants, all contributed to the legends that eventually became the Arthurian Romances. I'm going to be tinkering with my resources, seeing if I can update what I know and hammer out a reasonable, legible timeline. You know, with dates and stuff.
