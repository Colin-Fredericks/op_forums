    Well I can certainly sympathize JulianMoon, although more because we're sort of photo negatives of each other. For years I'd tried to either change or remove elements of the metaplot from my Masquerade projects, but to little avail as 90% of the players were frustratingly versed in it. (That is, of course, until Requiem 2nd Edition descended from evil heaven and I had no reason to play Masquerade ever again.)

    But as I said I sympathize with your plight, and beyond the recommendations already made, Danse Macabre is an excellent choice although I'd also add Damnation City for the city examples and Prince archetypes, one series of books I highly recommend you read if you want to craft a Requiem metaplot is Mummy the Curse. And I know, it's a strange suggestion, but here me out:

    Mummy the Curse is a game about truly immortal and impossibly old monsters who were created by Gods(ish?) and were once the blessed and favored servants in the first true empire the world had ever seen, so old now that no trace or evidence of it's existence remains. They survived their Empire's collapse and to these very nights manipulate fanatical cults and shape society in accordance to the old Gods and possess near god-like powers themselves, but so much of their memories have eroded away that some only have the cause left to fight for without even knowing why. This would be a great template for the Methuselahs of your metaplot, and Irem can serve as a great template for how your own First City could have operated. And most of all, it showcases the dangers of fading memory and lies. As the truth behind the Nameless Empire is only known to the ST and not the players, so the Arisen discover their horrible past alongside their players who learn just how terrible the cause they fight for is. Not unlike the Jyhad itself, where ancient grudges have played out for so long that the reason behind them would probably spark another Anarch Revolt.

    And that all I've got. Good luck.

        Though I'm as against the metaplot as a thing in the books as most here, I'm not against chronicles based in complex, world-and-beyond-spanning plots.

                    I think oftentimes people who say they want "more metaplot" in Requiem aren't necessarily looking for setting and plot details to change from one release to the next. More, they are often looking for setting and plot elements that operate on a grander scale, or that interact more directly with the real world, or more clearly defined setting and plot details. Most of them probably want some combination of all of the above. But I think that gets lost when it's all lumped into "metaplot" and the folks who don't want Clans suddenly being allied to different factions or swapping out Disciplines, or suffering mass hysteria because their Antediluvian was killed when a new book comes out say to get that shit away from our Requiem. A lot of what folks want out of Masquerade's plot can be adapted to Requiem without the Meta part.

                        Internally, I divide this kind of detail into three categories:

                        Backstory and sample settings: Although 1e started with a light touch on these, we started going backstory-and-example-heavy in 2007 with Requiem for Rome and then the clanbooks. At the time, this "metaplot" was controversial. But now, we've got Dark Eras and everything!

                        Fiction arcs: See the Chicago story arcs, and the glass armonium/patron storyline in the clanbooks. This stuff moves forward linearly, but does not impact the setting. Chicago tends to update when I work with Greg Stolze.

                        Change over time (what I usually call metaplot): This is New Orleans-only, for various historical development reasons. The Katrina/Week of Nightmares story in the clanbooks moved New Orleans materially forward along with current events. Secrets of the Covenants revisits Vidal in the present day, visibly changed by those stories.

                                A "metaplot" is a combination of things.

                                In those cases, I dare to say that people aren't necessarily wanting a grander scale (though it can be a part of it), nor a change over time (though it adds a sense of density). I doubt it have to do with relating to real life in any way, though it fits the setting of CoD, anyway.

                                What I think they want is self-reference. Is to new material complement older material. Is to tidbits in Book B highlight elements that cross over back to Book A, so you get a sense of connection, of a greater background and maybe some major stories forming through the minor ones told.

                                Maybe it could be a good idea to offer a book of ideas on how to link elements to create a "metaplot", with some examples. It wouldn't substitute a true metaplot for whomever want it, but would certainly be a start, and a good addition by itself (since we can still make use of it on our games. We can have a metaplot on our games).

                                The last element, the sensation of an author creating a big material for you to learn and explore, that won't be done, period. But maybe some lines of Novels and such could do that?

Why because it's YOUR STORY not some level30 npc. If the world's problems could easily be solved we wouldn't need brave adventures to save the day. With the players writing the setting but its the St who has to run it.

                                    I'm somewhere in the middle. I know that having a background setting is good backdrop to work with and know where it's going. The War happens but it isn't the central conflict of the story. But you know how many stories have dissolved in front of a larger metaplot?

                                        The way I see it, there's nothing stopping you from having your own huge figures controlling everything, with plots that change over time, and such-like. But such things shouldn't become so all-encompassing that it becomes difficult to run the game without them. You can run a game in New Orleans without having Vidal around, or in Montreal without the Nameless, without players going "But they Vidal is Prince!" or "But the Nameless keeps that from happening!". Those are optional settings, and other fluff might reference them, but the game doesn't RELY on them.

                                            Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post

                                            I think a big part of why that particular bit of fanon is so popular is due to the fact that it kind of redeems TFV as hunters. It's hard to feel like your character is a candle against the darkness when you know your character works for an organization that's canonically the puppets of a vampire conspiracy. Or, you know, ignore the fluff for a setting. Onyx Path isn't going to issue a cease-and-desist order on you for saying that the Nameless never showed up in Montreal.
