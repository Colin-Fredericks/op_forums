                    Originally posted by Teatime View Post
                    As I read your essay, several questions occured to me. You may have already addressed these matters in writing, but if you put them in different words, it may help your ideas really "click" for me. I'd appreciate if you devoted some time to answering them:

                    I'm a big proponent of concrete examples. I believe they can communicate ideas in ways that more abstract writing can't. This is why I value the fragment from Beast Player's Guide where a Begotten interferes with a lovers' spat, only to be forced to do some soul-searching in a trunk of a car. What do you think could be archetypical character arcs for Beast characters - from before Devouring to after their death or transformation? I know it's a demanding question, since it requires no less than an outline of a game campaign. However, if you describe a story that engages with your endorsed themes properly, it would be really helpful in getting your point across - for me, and possibly others.

                    You praise Beast and Hunter for approaching the CofD franchise from new angles, prompting a critical look at previously established concepts. However that only seems possible for readers well versed in other CofD books. Am I wrong, and these themes are clear in those two corebooks? If not, is this knowledge necessary to make Beast and Hunter thematically complete, allowing a new reader satisfying comprehension of their themes and moods?

                    Next, there was a thread I wasn't part of, where you promised to address a certain matter, once you weren't tired. Does Devouring, as you understand it, constitute self-actualization in its entirety, or is it merely a breakthrough that begins a path to self-actualization, with its struggles and backslides? If it's the former, doesn't it limit the amount of conflict one can put in the game, to the game's detriment?
                    Coming back around to this:

                    1) I don't have any off hand (Beyond my own personal desire to play a Beast who works to create a unified monstrous state with herself as the High Priestess of that), and it's kind of hard, because of the actually challenging things about Beast is that it's driven by internal motivations and personal goals. Beyond that, Beast starts in a place that's a-typical of a lot of stories, effectively starting where a lot of other stories would typically end in the same way that Changeling does.

                    But I'll definitely get back around to it.

                    2) This is another one of those actual catches that needs some critical thinking and playing around with-Beast makes sense in it's context as a crossover game from the perspective of contrast with the rest of the Chronicles world, but that does mean that there's some trouble dealing with Beast independently, and Beast needs to understood as it's own entity. Hunter got to have it's cake and eat it too through the perspective of it's characters, who frequently didn't have the whole picture and could operate under that decontextualization and ignorance-it was a good game on it's own and better with understanding the game-the themes are present in Hunter, but don't get in the way if you don't have the context. Beast wears those themes openly, but it's hard to appreciate it without the context. These are the things where a change in presentation will strongly go a long way.

                    3) It is a core actualization-psychological theories that deal with ideas of actualization frequently describe that a person can actualize on certain matters while still being unresolved on others, or that the actualization of self leads to the next coalition of various psychological aspects to then be actualized into a new layer.

                    In the case of Beast, I consider the Devouring to be a strong enough point of actualization that it makes transcendence a major thrust of the game (again differentiating it from it's peers, except perhaps certain readings of the Mage: the Awakening First Edition corebook [back before the occult detectivery of Second Edition was added in, Mage had it's own problem of "Well now what do I do? with the general idea being "Whatever you decide to set yourself to"]), and I think that transcendental element works well for the Hunger vs Kinship theme of Beast in the sense of understanding it as Individual vs Community, since the pursuit of transcendence both aggravates that fight while also being the key answer of actualizing that conflict.

                    As for limiting the amount of conflict, no, I don't think so. I think it changes the nature of conflict from a very familiar ground onto a more unfamiliar territory, which is challenging but satisfying. There will always be stories and games where in the characters grow through the hero's and heroine's journey (and other models) to become masters of two worlds (and Chronicles has ten games that wrestle with becoming masters of humanity and monstrosity in thoroughly unique and interesting ways), but Beast essentially asks "What now?" from there as it's starting point, and that is a thoroughly thrilling question to tackle.

And before you wonder why I treat you as if you were a developer - along with Cinder you're someone who has most to say on this subject. If we're lucky, me pushing you may result in new ideas on your part and who knows, maybe future writers are on this thread right now. (Hint, hint... nudge, nudge...)

                                                            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                                            It is a core actualization-psychological theories that deal with ideas of actualization frequently describe that a person can actualize on certain matters while still being unresolved on others, or that the actualization of self leads to the next coalition of various psychological aspects to then be actualized into a new layer.
                                                            I'm not fluent in psychological terms, but the approach you're describing seems to allow character development throughout the game - which is exactly what I endorse. This would make my worries about limiting the amount of conflict moot. Still, it's difficult for me to truly understand what you mean by self-actualization without practical examples.

                                                            I've had some thoughts on how to present the Devouring if I ever ran a game. They're probably at an off angle towards yours, but I guess I'll find out. The book already states that future Begotten may experience Primordial narratives before the Devouring, but it doesn't really dwell on that point. I think future books should hone in on this. Here's an example taken from a different work:

                                                            *Spoilers for 1922 by Stephen King*
                                                            The short of the story is - A farmer murders his wife while manipulating his son to become an accomplice. He convinces himself it's necessary to save their farm and to ensure his son's future with his sweetheart. In reality, he hates his wife too much to look for an alternate solution. The murder sets off a chain of events that leads to him losing his farm, friends, and the death of both the son and the sweetheart. All that with the protagonist still technically getting away with murder. The movie and novel both end with him, having understood his true motivation, making a written confession and dying in ambiguously supernatural circumstances. What occured to me was, if at the end of the story he transformed into a Beast, that would make an amazing background for a character. His visions of ghosts and rats, accompanied by musings on darker half of his nature don't hurt the idea either. The aspects of the story that I believe would benefit Beast, include:
                                                            • The events surrounding the Devouring stem from the protagonist's actions and character. That would make becoming a Beast feel more personal and earned, rather than being inflicted from without.
                                                            • Such a Beast has a major story behind their becoming. This is more thematically resonant and richer in plot hooks than a psychodrama taking place mostly in the dreams.
                                                            • The character begins play with hard earned self-knowledge, having to come to terms with himself and the world.
                                                            • It leads to a situation where the Devouring can be promoted or prevented by interfering in a nascent Beast's life - enabling more plot hooks.
                                                            • It might (possibly) fit your approach where Beast starts where other stories would end.
                                                            Now, this was a really bleak example. Some players are ready to engage in such characters, but it wouldn't hurt to broaden the available options. I would let the Devouring also accompany self-destructive actions and major acts of self-sacrifice, as long as hurt and revelation are involved. This plays well with my preferred approach to the Dark Mother, where she loves not only those who hurt but also those hurting.
                                                            *Spoilers end*

                                                            I've had a lot of thoughts, but I had to edit them down to keep my post coherent.
