    How to make/keep Werewolf combat dangerous and interesting?

    06-01-2021, 04:15 AM
    Disclaimer: I don't have much experience with Werewolf combat, or Chronicles combat in general (my chronicles are more focused on investigations and mysteries, rather than on violence). Reading Werewolf mechanics makes me think that basically there are three "tiers" of werewolf combat:
    1) Not using Gauru;
    2) Using Gauru without falling in Kuruth;
    3) Falling in Kuruth.

    By "tiers" I mean that those combat situations are pretty distinct. To kill werewolf in Gauru (and I state as an axiom that for combat to be interesting for players, there should be real possibility of being killed in combat) you have to be able to deal at least Health in lethal damage per turn while overcoming Defence and possible armor, or have source of aggravated damage. My problem is that this amount of damage being dealt practically forbids usage of non-Gauru forms in this particular combat. For example, let's imagine our theoretical pack fighting the umia of fire spirits with potent Blast numina. If they deal ±10 level of lethal damage, that's basically acceptable for pack Rahu with 10 levels of health, going to Gauru and then straight to Kuruth. But everyone else dies on spot if fire spirits concentrate on him, so they need to use Gauru as well.

    This divides combat in those three tiers I explained above. Which is manageable if you try to pit combat in places where you can't go into Kuruth as often as possible. My problem is that, for example, in Shadow you don't have strong incentive not to go into Kuruth, ripping and tearing your way through the enemies (or I don't see it). So certain locations actively prohibit battling in normal human/wolf form. Other problem is that tiers basically enforce certain way to "solve" the combat. If you have a brood of Azlu, you either make them strong enough so pack has to go into Kuruth, or you don't (and they just die anyways if your pack choose to do this). You don't have any middle ground - or again, I don't see it from my lack of experience with this line.

    Do you encounter the same issues? How do you solve them? Is there something I don't see about this? I actively want to do interesting combat scenes in Werewolf, but solving them through flipping the switch and going into Gauru is not interesting - and never allowing Gauru and Kuruth by always forcing PCs to fight in unfavorable conditions is not very viable - and again, not very interesting.

      I am so glad you started this thread. I find that Werewolf Second edition has a great deal of challenges in combat from a purely mathematical point of view. Here are my thoughts on the matter.

      1) Not using Gauru; This one is the simplest. Out of Garou a werewolf is simply a human, plus regeneration plus gifts. However, they would be relatively flimsy. At this point they do not have crazy regeneration so there is no need to "roll over damage". One option is to ambush a werewolf with firearms while they are in human form and fill the whole healthtrack with lethal or aggravated damage (not too difficult using and ambush and firearms)


      2) Using Gauru without falling in Kuruth; This is probably the default. A werewolf in Garou can probably destroy one of the enemies in the core book, I use the Claimed hound as a challenging foe, but still kind of Cannon Fodder. Using the Garou form can probably demolish one, but after using the Garou form and probably expending Willpower and Essence, if there are any other enemies that might be a challenge. Alternatively, the foe can dodge a werewolf in garou which would be a headache, who would be crazy enough to stand and fight?

      3) Falling in Kuruth. This is the big Elephant in the room. One could dogpile the Raging Werewolf and roll over damage, but you probably need lots of guys to do that. A swarm of Hosts, a bunch of spirits, a pack of claimed, a squad of humans with guns are probably needed to bring it down (especially since humans are probably affected by lunacy).

      ..or, you play is smart and use Silver. That is the great equalizer. Get some claimed with improvised pieces of silver, load up shotguns with silver buckshot (look online, they actually sell silver shot for industrial purposes, really), forge a silver knife and the fight becomes much easier.

      Alternatively, you can run away. I think that since the Garou will collapse after the Garou is finished, one can move in for the kill afterwards. Climb walls, run away if your speed is higher, flee to twilight. I figure that a claimed might have his host body slaughtered, but the spirit can possess a corpse and move in for the kill after the Kuruth ends (and what Kuruth does not have an abundance of corpses).

      or...you can look at the material damage of Kuruth. Did the Werewolf kill a packmate, or destroy some property, or engage in cannibalism (a big no-no according to the oath of the moon). Is the territory filled with fear? The werewolf might be alive but the damage done might be irrepairable.

      There are lots of rich possibilities!

          Having been playing Werewolf second edition for some time I don't find this to be a big problem. The question you have to ask yourself is this: what role do you want combat to play in your game?

          In most RPGs combat is a challenge - a difficult task that the characters have to work together to overcome. My contention is that, except for a few set pieces, combat itself should rarely be the challenge in Werewolf.

          Werewolves are, to be frank, supposed to be ridiculously overpowered in comparison to most of the threats they will encounter from one night to the next. The other important thing to note is that most of the foes Werewolves face know this. The Azlu or Spirit umia you want to set up as antagonists for the pack aren't going to be stupid enough to take them on in a head-on fight. That's a good way to get yourself Gauru'd.

          For Werewolves then, the main challenge of the game is in finding the prey that's causing them issues and dealing with problems you can't solve by simply going Gauru. To take your example, perhaps the fire spirits might be hiding out in the local incinerator that's responsible for disposing of municipal waste. It's operated by the government and guarded 24/7 so the Werewolves can't just waltz in and go Gauru. In this case, the challenge the pack faces is first in locating the fire spirits, and second in setting the stage such that they can gain access to the building and take the spirits out without too many repercussions.

          Which brings me to the second major point. If you think about the themes of Werewolf, one that it focuses on is the nature of wolves (and to a certain extent humans) as hunters. If you think about hunting animals in nature, it's typically identifying, stalking, and chasing prey that's the hard part of the hunt. Actually killing the prey usually isn't the difficult part, and in almost all cases the failure state for a hunt is that the prey escapes, not that the prey turns around and murders you. Actually making the kill (and getting to eat) is the reward of the hunt, not the challenge.

          For players then, getting the chance to use all their cool Gauru powers and feel like a badass Werewolf is the reward for a well-executed hunt. All the other parts of the hunt can and often should be difficult - just finding the prey, chasing it down, etc. Most prey will only fight Werewolves if it has no other options, and the job of Werewolves is to arrange things so that that is the case.

          So, with that all out of the way, let's now consider big set piece fights, and how to make combat interesting for Werewolves.

          Fights in CofD are nearly always short - from experience even two full packs of Uratha in Gauru will kill each other in 4-5 turns. As a result, you can consider death rage as lasting "forever" in a combat, since even the lowest Primal Urge Uratha get 10 minutes of it (100 turns if you take each round as being 6 seconds).

          So if the Werewolves death rage their health regeneration becomes essentially infinite. However, the players should not see death rage as a positive state for their characters. The usual way to counteract this is to make death rage result in the Werewolves destroying something and killing innocent bystanders. But let’s be frank for a moment, most players even if they're committed to roleplaying, don't actually give two hoots that they smashed up the local library and ate the librarian. In fact, they probably got all their essence back from eating a human and so on balance they might see it as a good thing.

          A better way to balance death rage is to make it important that the player keeps control of their character. There are lots of ways you can do this, but the easiest is to set things up so that the fire they're putting out right now isn't the only fire they have to put out tonight. Ten plus minutes of death rage, the resulting destruction, and then calls to emergency services, police, roadblocks etc. could be catastrophic if the characters are on a tight schedule, even if they don't get caught.

          If the players can't (and don't want to) Death Rage, their relationship with Gauru also changes. Gauru can only be taken once per scene, and only maintained for so many turns. It also usually takes either an action or an essence to shift. With these restriction in mind, Gauru then becomes a resource that they can deploy tactically for a few turns - probably after they've taken a fair amount of lethal and need to heal themselves back up. And for players who didn't build combat focused characters, Gauru may be their only way to contribute meaningfully to the fight. For those players, taking Gauru means choosing the moment when they can have the greatest impact and deploying the form then.

          Having the pack need to run from one fire to another also lets you wear the characters down without needing to touch their health boxes. A Werewolf without essence or willpower is actually a lot less threatening than you might think, and barring oath violations, these resources come back slowly. If you have the pack face off against multiple sets of foes in a single night, even if those foes aren't a threat in and of themselves, the repeated drain on their resources might turn them into a more substantial threat.

          Putting these things together we can imagine a situation where the pack has tracked a claimed to the place where it's hiding out with the cult it has been building up. Since the pack failed a subterfuge roll last session, the claimed knows that the pack is on to it, and has planned for this moment. When the pack kick down the door, the claimed is already getting into a car in the basement garage. Meanwhile, its cult closes in around the pack and opens fire with handguns plus a couple of rifles and one submachine gun.

          In this situation the pack needs to chase after the claimed before it gets too far away. Killing these humans is not a problem - the challenge for the pack here is how to deal with these humans quickly and without using too many resources. They're going to need those resources later when they are chasing down and eventually fighting the claimed. Death raging here is not an option either - they can't afford to waste ten minutes or more killing these humans (not to mention the fact that they would almost certainly break the Oath of the Moon and eat one of these brainwashed people) because by then the claimed will be in another part of the city or in some other bolthole.

          Resolving this problem could be a great chance for one of the less frontline characters to shine. Perhaps the Elodoth has a gift that can pacify the cultists, or the Cahalith can impose a specific lunacy condition on them all that will allow the pack to escape quickly. How long it takes to get away will determine how far behind they are in the chase scene that comes immediately afterwards, and whether they win or lose that chase will determine if the claimed has the opportunity to arm a homemade explosive that he's got on the door of his safehouse.

                      Gauru is meant to be the killing form, specifically there for killing blows. So you don't generally just switch to it because. That said, it's super useful with the healing so if another form isn't cutting it and retreat isn't on the table, it's likely an option werewolves will fall back on.

                      I think you're not really disassociating the last two tiers much. Gauru without Kuruth is a thumbs up, you're powerful and smashing, you basically have to attack but you can still shift down. Gauru with Kuruth is going to be raging for at least 10 minutes. Consider that a turn is about 3 seconds, that's a lot of turns. They recommend the blackout experience for this. While you can say "Well there's no humans here so no problem" in werewolf that's not as much of a given in the Hisil, where suddenly turning on spirit allies and wrecking loci can lead to similar long-term ramifications as turning on random humans and wrecking buildings.

                      So while you can be on turn 7 of Gauru, and realizing you have to shift down, there's a difference between 'It will end this fight' and 'I'm going to be killing everything I come across for 10 minutes to 12 hours.' Especially since if you haven't beaten whatever you pumped yourself up against in Stamina+Primal Urge turns, it's entirely possible being in Gauru isn't making enough of a difference. Going Kuruth at that point can be as much of a 'I guess I'll just die' button as an 'I win' one.

                      Kuruth can cause more problems than it seems to solve from a reader's standpoint. It's designed this way, part of the drawback of being a werewolf and a reluctance to just let you use the form (with all its powers) constantly. A simple issue that comes up is once you have PCs of different Primal Urge. PC A is in Kuruth for 10 minutes, PC B is in Kuruth for 15. Once PC A falls out of it, they're another tasty morsel for their packmate.

                      This isn't that hard to get across in play, you generally won't see people going gauru, or pushing into Kuruth, all the time. It may take them a bit to get used to their powers as is, and a demonstration via an experienced werewolf NPC can help, but if not there's always the other side of the table, where now the spirits won't deal with you, or the humans have an APB out for your human form.

                      This paragraph kinda meanders. All that said, I do think Chronicles would benefit from offering a lot of handholding advice for combat, and how to make it interesting (and dangerous). But part of the issue is that it can be so contextual. Every gameline is going to come at combat differently, even Hunter and Werewolf, which are very similar to the point of having similar Tactics mechanics in both editions, are drastically different in that one you play a relatively fragile human and the other you play a regenerating shapeshifter. I've found that starting PCs off as mortals, then getting them through the change, can help players get a grasp of their capability a lot. It's easy to look at the books and not really feel how different it is when your PC has taken 1 Lethal and is now like more than 10% closer to death for two in-game days. And as a player, finally having your character change and realize you can just heal that in less than a scene, or with a point of energy, it's a big change. But man, the whole transformation from template to template thing isn't something you want to run every game with, it can be such a slog at times.

                      To sum up.

                      Spicing up combat is a subject of a lot of RPG advice columns, blogs, and youtube videos. It's been further explored in different forms of media, combat sequences in films, books, video games. There's a lot out there that can suggest ways to give satisfying story arcs with the right amount of peaks and valleys for action. Sticking with the topic of the thread I would say one thing to remember is that Kuruth is not an 'I Win' button, even if it looks like it. Character choices have consequences and while you don't always have to flip the tragedy switch, think about what would happen in other parallel settings if someone decided to just kill a whole bunch of people. But this is a lesson I think the players who've read the book and are in the pilot seat of their character will understand early on. Combat with werewolves can be over and done with fast, especially given the D&D effect. 5 rounds is a long time, and 10 minutes of that is a lot of killing.

                      There is more specific advice, I think Aurumae got into it a bit but aside from 'You don't want to be out of control with an enemy that can withstand a lot of gauru as is,' you have a variety of ways to spice things up. Complimenting Aurumae's idea, adding layers between the PCs and the BBEG is an easy and obvious one, spirit nobles have spirit minions, idigam have heralds and others borne from Essence shaping, werewolves have packs, humans have other humans (and occasionally ghouls or proximi or charmed minions), even hosts have lesser hosts and spirits or people they've suckered into working for them. Just like in fiction, having an antagonist you can sacrifice to the protagonists is a valuable asset when it comes to keeping a more plot-relevant antagonist alive. The leader has time to escape, or can lob Blasts or use Influence or other powers to affect the terrain of the battle. Tilts like Ice and Blizzard can be used to the werewolf's detriment, as well as making what was the standard white room into a white room with impact. One doesn't even have to stop with the books, obviously, and can customize Tilts so that the snow drifts piling up at your feet are necrotic somehow, sapping you of Essence or even blood (in the form of Lethal) every turn, making rollover that much more likely. Another thing that's been popping up in more combat-oriented RPGs is the staged battles, meaning that a fight with a BBEG is divided up into stages and, for example, when you've done a certain amount of damage to the priestess with spider-trappings she turns into a more drider-like creature, and upon dealing a certain amount of damage to that she goes all out, revealing her true form as a house-sized spider dripping with demonic ichor. In each of these stages the antagonist has different tactics, powers available, and presents itself as a different threat. Using the example the first form might use a gun and vastly increased Dexterity and Speed, plinking the PCs as they deal with her thugs. In the second form she can spew webs all over the place, making movement and combat difficult, maybe giving her a chance to wrap up certain opponents to get them out of the fight until they can claw their way out (or be clawed out by others). In the final form you have the biggest and baddest of forms, where bites do damage that lingers over turns, and a tarsus that can stab through concrete, potentially leading to the entire battlefield collapsing. There's ways to spice this up with things like having minions, with more obvious servants in the first stage, none in the second, then lesser azlu formed by shed shards in the third. The big thing is, this is not the kind of fight you want to be blacked out and out of control for. If your PCs haven't gotten the message (again, Kuruth not being an 'I Win' button is something I think players will understand), then having the antagonist make a very clear 'I'm going to outlast your gauru limit' sign through some delay tactic or even outright saying something like 'I can outlast you, and kill you while you're exhausted and unaware' should manage it.

                      Obviously your goal isn't to kill the players, but if your PCs are just going Kuruth against antagonists that are meant to be powerful and smart, then you might just have them make the smart move and leave while the PCs do exhaust themselves. They don't even have to know that the antagonist isn't dead. Without examining the bodies of all the others slain during their frenzy it's possible for them to believe they killed the antagonist who is just now regathering their strength.

                                  It feels like there's a lot of exclusion of the rest of the Uratha's forms in all this. And of course the plethora of Merits and other mechanics werewolves get (like Flaring Renown, Auspice Aspects, and Pack Tactics from The Pack).

                                  Compressing everything not Gauru into one "tier" is kind of ridiculous. A pack of Iron Masters kitted out in SWAT gear meant for their Dalu forms is not the same as a random group of Uratha stuck naked and without gear in Hishu. Getting piled on by Urshul form Uratha isn't some picnic.

                                  Gauru is the most potent form, sure, but in raw offensive power it's not actually that far ahead of Urshul, and Urshul doesn't have Rage to deal with. That +3 Health also means they can take a solid hit too. The three non-human non-Gauru forms give the Uratha a huge amount of tactical flexibility.

                                  Depending on the make up of the pack, some threats might really mean Gauru or bust, but the same threat might be easily something they can handle in other forms. Like any game with flexible character design, encounter design is complicated because there's no shorthand for what kind of threats are going to fall where.

                                  ------------------

                                  I'm also not really sure how much you can do about having a different of preference around the point of combat, and how WtF 2e incentivizes things. Forsaken is about hunting. The Wolf Must Hunt is the tagline of the game. The Uratha are hunters, and they're built around hunting. Violence is part of their lives, but it takes lots of forms, and not just combat. The role of combat in Forsaken is aiming for just might not be what the OP wants? Either you adjust to the game, or you figure out how to adjust the game, or you find something that suits you better.

                                  CofD, and Forsaken in particular, is not built on the idea that combat is only for when "serious" harm is a risk. That's why CofD stresses establishing stakes for the first to figure out things like Beat Down thresholds. If you add an extra bar of threat before you use combat, you deny yourself a tool to make the overall story interesting by going after one of the Uratha's weakness: endurance. As hunters, they are designed to end fights fast, or not win them. Making them use up resources on "easy" low risk fights makes the bigger fights more interesting.

                                  To an extent, it's kind of like designing a dungeon crawl. If you make every fight in the dungeon something that leaves the party drained and barely capable of moving on, you never get to the "crawl" part of the game. A good dungeon crawl is a series of encounters that gets harder somewhat because of increasing danger, but also because of dwindling resources. Forsaken just gives you a lot more options than combat encounters and traps to challenge the character's ability to handle their resources.
