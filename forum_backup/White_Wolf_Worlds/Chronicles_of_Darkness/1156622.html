    How to prepare/write a Story?

    11-07-2017, 05:48 AM
    Hey folks,

    I'm planning on running a Demon: The Descent chronicle in the near future (a few months) and I'm was contemplating how to prepare an actual story in the storytelling system. I've run Changeling: The Lost in the past, but when I did I ran it more like a D&D game with no clearly defined scenes or stories, but more as one continuing campaign going from day to day.

    Now, for Demon I want to make better use of the actual storytelling system. I looked at the example story presented in Demon, called "How an Angel Dies." It has this nice Scene Flowchart and a lot of information on different scenes and how to run them. Since that's a lot more work than what I'm used to preparing for any given game I've run (my prep usually is less than a single A4 page for each session), I was wondering how others prepare their stories and chronicles.

    Do you write the entire story in advance, preparing vital scenes with detail and make a scene flowchart like the on in "How an Angel Dies"? Or do you go chapter to chapter (session to session) and just prepare whats immediately ahead. Also, how do you transititon between scenes in play? That's something I've been wondering about. In previous CofD games I've played in we'd often have someone asking "is this a new scene yet?" for the purpose of durations, etc. and I'd like to make that transititon more direct and clear.

    I guess, what I'm asking is: How do you prepare for a storytelling system game in contrast to how many people prepare, say, their D&D or Pathfinder games?

    Thanks in advance

          I'm currently Gearing up to run a mortal game with a pair of police detectives as the player character, and although my ideas for the campaign are somewhat nebulous, I've written scenes for the first case, basically i looked at it like a police procedural, start with a premise so in this case its a missing person, i then worked out the resolution to the case or at least possible resolutions.
          that's a beginning and an end.

          i then detail the opening scene, although leaving it fairly open, so the players can work out how things go. introduce the plot and all that, i then write although not in massive detail a number of avenues of investigation basically working out what bits of information can be found where. but giving the players plenty of opportunity to pick the order of scenes and how the different scenes resolve in such a way that it will bring the story to my planned plot resolution

          then i try to work out what different things my players could do in those scenes and how they will effect that final scene, and i write that scene allowing for different variations.

          with a more standard dnd/pathfinder type game, there are similar steps of premise, clues then resolution, but also options for dungeon crawls, which is just maps monsters and loot, with a sprinkle of lore every now and then.

          we will see how this turns out when i actually run it, so ask me again, in i don't know, February - my planned start is early January. and this is my first time as storyteller in a long time

              A day-to-day approach can work well, particularly if the players are proactive about what their characters do. In that case, the Storyteller has to provide opposition, obstacles and the like. For example, a Demon: The Descent game about a ring of Saboteurs could focus on their plans and the God-Machine's response to them.

              For a more reactive plot like an investigation, I generally start with an opening scene and some clue to draw from it, and a rough idea of other scenes it might involve. I'll make notes, and might rough out something like the flowchart although I usually just keep it in my head. These notes are often pretty short bullet-points, mostly to keep me straight about what's happened and what the players have found.

              There's a thread about different approaches to investigative games on RPG.net that might give some advice. Likewise, Run A Game has an interesting article on scene framing. I tend to let scenes develop naturally, but when a game has scene-based effects and powers it's a good rule of thumb that a situation where you say "what do you do next?" or "where do you go next?" is a scene change. When one situation moves immediately into another - someone they're questioning makes a run for it - I generally let it count as one scene for the purposes of scene effects.

                  The way I've ST'd games often started by creating the setting. Think of the town you're using, and what prominent power players and sects look like. You could even create characters both mundane and supernatural that are parts of or even leaders of those sects. After that, of prominent powers there (both mortal and supernatural). Since you're using Demon the Descent, this could mean both active Demons in the setting and agents of the God Machine, and any pactees and cults if you wish to include any.

                  Feel free to let your inspiration run wild. If you have fun or interesting ideas for characters, let them fly here and make them so. The same goes for scenarios too.

                  After all of this, with the setting thought out and the power players, it may be a good time to come up with potential scenarios. What will be the other characters agendas and how can the setting or enemies play into that? How can the player characters stumble into potential scenes?

                  I do want to note that you can play out any scenarios you made with other characters and that the player characters don't have to complete them all themselves. Advice I got from a friend I ST for says this makes the difference between the game feeling like a stage and feeling like a living world.

                  If you're down on inspiration for ideas on any of the above, you can stop and rest for the day or until you feel inspired once more. And after you feel you have a good amount of material for a game, I'd say the game is ready to be played.

                  I should note that this point of view is namely from my own experience and that everyone has their own way of doing games. If this doesn't work for you, then I'm sure that other posters may have a style of storytelling that's more appealing. ^^
