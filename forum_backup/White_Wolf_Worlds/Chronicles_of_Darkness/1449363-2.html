                    Happy Monday everyone! Time for an excerpt.

                    So last time I said I'd do the Warumono, but that's because I'm a dope and forgot the Typhos. So, we're talking about them today, because they have a much more interesting development story to riff on.

                    The Typhos are the bloodline who had the most ink spilled on them over the course of the project. In first drafts, they still had the medical aesthetic and "fake pharma company" front, but otherwise their goals were very different. Initially, they were more trans-vampirist; instead of trying to achieve more metaphysical changes like the Dragons, they focused on physically perfecting bodies. It was a little old school Tzimisce, though without the spiritual aspects.

                    Ultimately, this concept didn't really work. While the aesthetics were different, they were just Ordo Dracul with a twist. That said, I liked the bloodline of Ventrue surgeons concept; power takes many forms, and a physician has immense power over his patients. With that in mind, we retooled the idea to be more external. Instead of themselves, the Typhos would work on perfecting their victims — perfect, docile prey who can never fight back. This concept lent itself better to a horror theme, and made the bloodline more useful as antagonists. We more or less write these bloodlines from the perspective that players will use them as characters, but we always make sure they have utility for Storytellers.

                    The other thing thing that needed reworking was their bloodline gift. Initially this was a form of Blood Sorcery with a fleshly and medical overtones. While it wasn't a bad idea in the abstract, it ultimately ended up being a fancy way of doing Protean. I also felt like we'd done a fair number of blood sorcery variants in these books, and I wanted to break out of that format. But if this wasn't going to be blood sorcery, how was it going to work? Devotions didn't really interest either of us, as that seemed a bit boring, and not fitting to the theme. We also considered Scales of the Dragon, but A) we didn't really want to give them a Coil (them being too much like the Ordo being the problem in the first place) and B) we already basically did that with the Adrestoi. I had initially suggested the procedures be Conditions you could put on other characters, but the author pointed out that this meant that the player character doesn't really get much out of that, particularly considering the bloodline's themes. That got us thinking, though: what if these are asymmetrical powers? You benefit from one effect, the other character benefits from another. And which system in the core book already does that? Invictus Oaths.

                    Using Oaths as the basis for this system (Arthmoic Surgery) is one of my favourite little mechanical flourishes in the book. It makes what the Typhos do just as much a social as a medical thing, and it does away with needing to do a lot of rolling. I figure, if they've let you be a Typhos, you probably don't have much trouble performing surgery. That, and using the Oaths system suggests there's more to what the bloodline is doing than just surgery.

                    So, enjoy the icky machinations of the Typhos Institute. Tomorrow I'll be back with the Warumono, for real this time.

                    Typhos: The ones who make you better

                    “Side effects? Those were outlined in the waiver. You did read it, didn’t you?”

                    The clinic is spotless, all shiny glass and gleaming tile. The magazines in their little stand are immaculate, like they’ve never even been read. Is this your second time here or your third? Has it been more than three? You know that answer should be important, but it’s not — not now, anyway. The only important thing is getting more. More of those little red pills, more of the feeling they gave you. Every time you’re here (and it’s definitely been more than three) you come away a little… less, but the doctor says he can help with that, too. There’s a buzz from the receptionist’s desk, and her gleaming smile mirrors your own desperate one. The doctor is in.

                    Kindred are parasites. For all their pretension and personal power, they’re utterly dependent on the teeming host of humanity, and should that willfully ignorant mass ever decide vampires are real, they would be consumed in the resulting conflagration. Herbert Spencer Cartwright has spent his Requiem grappling with this problem, and in the Typhos Institute, he believes he’s found a solution: The most successful parasites are the ones that alter their host to suit their needs.

                    The Typhos Institute is an international biomedical research group, privately owned and controlled by Cartwright’s childer and grandchilder, and employing hundreds of ghouls and mortals. Never publicly traded, the Institute is insular and secretive, its human experiments unknown to all but the Surgeons themselves, and perhaps a few trusted (and blood-bound) employees. The Institute runs several for-profit clinics in the UK, the Americas, and parts of Asia, where it cultivates a list of loyal clients who double as the bloodline’s test subjects. Cartwright himself is still active, and still working toward the goal that inspired him to found his bloodline in the first place: — creating a better human… to rule.

                    The Typhos work to engineer a new breed of mortal, docile, unquestioning, and content in their role as the Kindred’s food source. These efforts have yet to produce satisfactory results, but the techniques they’ve pioneered over the centuries have been refined into Arthmoic Surgery, a collection of insidious procedures capable of enhancing their subjects physically, but also of making them more susceptible the predations of vampires.

                    Typhos exploit their patients by preying on their fear of mortality, claiming their procedures can extend lifespans, improve health, and cure illnesses… for a nominal fee. Unlike many other luxury clinics looking to make money off the insecure rich, the Surgeons’ methods actually work, but they also extract a higher price. Arthmoic Surgery makes you better, but what it mostly makes you better at is being a victim. Patients find themselves losing time, having difficulty thinking critically, and being very willing to sign up for whatever new procedure their predatory clinician prescribes. All of which would be alarming for the patient, if they still had the capacity or will to question their own actions.

                    It’s all relatively small-scale for now. The Typhos only have so many members capable of performing Arthmoic Surgery, and the exclusivity of their clinics means their victims/patients’ number in the high hundreds, at best. Cartwright is always looking to the future, however, and with technology like cloning and gene therapy on the horizon, his ambitions to “improve” humanity are global.

                    Other Kindred look upon the Sawbones with suspicion, saying they operate too openly and that their ambitions threaten the Masquerade. However, few can deny their effectiveness, as their methods for creating obedient blood dolls are more than proven. The fact that they take commissions, and will apply their techniques to any mortal of a vampire’s choosing (no questions asked) also has a lot to do with the All Night Society’s continued tolerance of their work. They’re even willing to give a down-on-their-luck Lick a break on their usual prices, so long as the buyer doesn’t mind being a test subject himself, or making a few bodily donations. All in the name of science, of course.

                    Why you want to be us

                    You believe in a better future for all Kindred, one in which humans no longer fight their place in the natural order. You tire of the nightly hunt, and wish to learn how to make your food come to you. You enjoy opening people up to see what makes them tick.

                    Why you should fear us

                    It’s not us you should fear. Our patients are many, powerful, and ever so loyal. Our work is for the good of all, but we won’t hesitate to remove any who threaten it. Do not stand in the way of progress, lest you be trampled by it.

                    Why we should fear ourselves

                    What if, in trying to adapt the host to our needs, it instead notices us and crushes us before we’re ready? It’s too late to stop now; we’re as addicted to control as our patients are to being controlled, and we won’t stop until we’ve perfected it — or it kills us.

                    Twists of the Blood: Conspiracy Theory

                    Readers with access to Deviant: The Renegades may note the similarities between the Typhos Institute and the grasping, shadowy conspiracies that hunt the Remade. While this isn’t their primary purpose, the Institute’s goal of creating a “better” human could easily result in Divergence. Alternatively, if the Typhos discovered the existence of Deviants, they might try and capture one for study and dissection. After all, what could be more terrifying than discovering that the corrupt pharmaceutical company hunting you down is run by literal vampires.

                    Used as a conspiracy in the Web of Pain, the Typhos Institute has a Standing of 3–5; Nodes are primarily found throughout the United Kingdom, India, and North America; Icons relate to extreme medical practices; and any individual Surgeon can be a Linchpin character. See p. 217 of Deviant: The Renegades for more on conspiracies and their plots.

                                            Alright, time to take a long look at the Warumono (not a difficult task, really).

                                            The Wild Ones are the fourth of our new bloodlines, and one of the things I like best about them is that they capture a bit of the gonzo charm of a "First Edition–style" bloodline while still being a sleek Second Edition design. By which I mean, their niche powers (i.e., mysterious tattoos) are the kind of thing you could probably imagine finding in some lost Bloodlines: The Whoever book. They're also Japanese, a frequent White Wolf subject. It was also important to us to offer more non-North American/European character choices, but without falling into some of the stereotypes and insensitivities that make certain older bloodlines a little hard to read in 2021.

                                            The Warumono didn't change too much in development. I asked for a bit more high crime drama in second drafts, but otherwise the idea is unaltered. Most changes were mechanical. Originally, the tattoos were several separate Merits, but that was unwieldy for a number of reasons, so we condensed them into four Style Merits. That was a bit more thematic and aesthetically interesting, and not a nightmare for me to squash together in layout, I might add. And whereas they originally just gained Vigor, we also swapped out Animalism for Celerity — and thus the full suite of physical Disciplines — because why the hell not? Wild Ones gotta be wild. Their bane is pretty much the same conceptually, though I tweaked it a bit to refer explicitly to the lost visage, and made it more player-facing; originally it was a bonus to NPC actions to spot them, but I find banes work best when the player has a more immediate sense of when they go off. I'm also a fan of reducing Storyteller rolling.

                                            So, without further ado, here's the Warumono. Tomorrow I'll be including some dev notes on the Rotgrafen and Malocusians. After that, I think the Architects of the Monolith will be Thursday's topic; they've also been previewed on the forums, but their recreation as a covenant was kind of a story in itself.

                                            Warumono: The ones who give you trouble

                                            “You don’t want a problem? Too bad. I’m already here.”

                                            You know they’ve arrived before you see them. When you do, it’s unmistakable. Loud suits, slicked hair, and an aura of menace rolling off them thicker than the scent of cigarettes and old cologne. Something inside you squirms and makes you search for an exit. The Warumono are here, and only danger follows on their snakeskin heels.

                                            The world is a machine that grinds down the weak and feeds on the suffering of the vulnerable. The poor, the outcasts, and the ostracized — for those on the bottom, the only way out is up. The Warumono bloodline epitomizes strength through perseverance, and the will to pull yourself up by blood-soaked bootstraps. The Wild Ones originated on the bottom rung of the All Night Society, but have grown into one of Japan’s most powerful bloodlines. Their traditions of fraternity and courage have united them against their social betters, and as their influence grows, domains all across the world will soon tremble beneath the pristine boots of these immortal gangsters.

                                            The Warumono are a troublesome, but necessary counterbalance to the rigid hierarchies and social stratification of the Danse Macabre. The services they provide may exist outside the realms of haut monde, but they’re always in demand. Their domain is the shadowy underbelly cast by the light of society, and within this darkness they thrive. The Warumono have no qualms about working with anyone, though they do have a soft spot for the underdog. And with is the key word. The Wild Ones work for themselves first and others second. Consider the leather jacket-clad knight in San Francisco as he runs protection for Invictus businesses; consider the shock troop as he fights alongside a pack of werewolves in Myanmar; consider the firebug as she burns down a prince’s blood doll brothel. Each of them mercenary in their intentions, and each as untamable as a storm.

                                            As befits their sobriquet, the Wild Ones are anything but subtle. Each member is cursed to wear their power like a mantle, radiating outward and announcing their presence like a trumpet. They refer to it as the mark of the dragon, a sign of their rise against the natural order. Where they go, chaos follows. To help contain their overwhelming power, each member of the bloodline follows the philosophy of gokudo, the Extreme Path, a set of principles that governs their behavior and guides their unorthodox existences. Their devotion to this philosophy is so ardent that it’s written into their flesh, through ornate tattoos that grant their owners supernatural powers beyond the ken of most other Kindred.

                                            The Warumono have a reputation as being aggressive, brash, potent defenders of the old and dark customs of their nation’s criminal empires. Their progenitors were yakuza gangsters, gifted the Embrace in order to bolster Japanese Kindred against the influx of foreign vampires, and they soon grew to be an institution. The Wild Ones thrive even as the mortal yakuza dwindles in influence; the world always has room for the kind of violence and control they bring to bear. Whether roaming the countryside in biker gangs or stalking the back alleys of urban sprawls, the Warumono are animate typhoons whose passing brings peace.

                                            One way or another.

                                            Why you want to be us

                                            You know there’s a right way and a wrong way to do things — and then there’s the way that actually gets it done. You honor what’s come before because the past has value, but the path stretches out before you too, and you have to be a bad enough motherfucker to walk it. And who cares what anyone else says? If you’re tough enough, who’s going to argue with you? Or maybe you just want all the cool tattoos. Can’t blame you there.

                                            Why you should fear us

                                            Where we go, trouble follows. We wear the storm like a fresh-pressed suit, and damn if it doesn’t look good on us. The Extreme Path is a dangerous one, and our only companions on it are death and despair. Whether we want to be or not, we’re heralds of destruction with the guts to send the message. No reason to be afraid if you shut up and fall in line.

                                            Why we should fear ourselves

                                            We’re the eye of a storm, but look what happens to the rest of the world when we get together. It’s hard to build a house when you’re constantly repairing the foundation, so we keep building empires out of powder. We follow the path to keep ourselves in check, but maybe it’s just a way to put a leash around our necks. What would happen if we left the path and made our own way? Would we be stronger — or would we implode?

                                            Parent Clan: Ventrue

                                            Nicknames: Wild Ones, Gangsters

                                            Bloodline Bane (The Turbulent Curse): A Warumono’s adherence to extreme action makes it difficult to fly under the radar. Whenever a Wild One engages in violent action or draws blood, including feeding, she exudes an aura that makes her presence almost impossible to forget. For the rest of the scene, the Lost Visage fails. Mortal eyes are drawn to her, and any mundane rolls to act subtly, stealthily, or to escape notice are capped by her Humanity.

                                            Disciplines: Celerity, Dominate, Resilience, Vigor

                                                Alright, slightly late start today, so let's get straight in to the Malocusians and Rotgrafen.

                                                Malocusians: http://forum.theonyxpath.com/forum/m...entrue-preview

                                                Rotgrafen: http://forum.theonyxpath.com/forum/m...entrue-preview

                                                (there were some dev changes since those excerpts went up, but mostly just a bit of editing, nothing big)

                                                So, the Malcusians. The goddamn Malocusians. This bloodline has been on my plate since Bloodlines: The Devoted, and they've utterly confounded me every time I revisit them. They were originally to be one of the big updates in Devoted, but I could never find good a hook for them. Part of the problem is that they're one of two territory-oriented bloodlines in Invictus: the Annunaku (Bloodlines: The Resurrected) share a lot of themes with the Malcusians, and, frankly, the Landlords do them better. The Annunaku have a fun backstory and a pretty cool Discipline (even for it's time), whereas the Malocusians have no backstory at all, and a really disjointed Discipline. They even have a similar bane to the Annunaku. The other part of the problem is that, for some reason, the Spiders' writeup only covers about a page and a half. Their Domus Discipline is far longer in terms of word and page count. I almost feel as though something was lost between final drafts and publication, or there was a need for big cuts (there are six bloodlines in Invictus!), but that's just speculation. There are some rumors in the book about a Ventrue branch of the Annunaku bloodline, which I...think is meant to be a hint about the Malocusians, but if so, it's rather opaque, and never really comes into play.

                                                Ultimately, the Malocusians ended up being one of the short updates in the Appendix of BTD, but I wasn't all that satisfied with that. Their power covered a lot of niches (Auspex, Nightmare, Protean, even Animalism), and I had trouble figuring out what to focus on even in a short update.

                                                Long story short, when we finally got to our Ventrue clan book, I wanted to take another crack at them. However, because I already had way too much on my plate, and because delegating your problems is one of the perks of being the boss, I assigned them to one of the other authors. However, he ran up against the same problems I did; i.e., there just wasn't much there. We had a long conversation about what to do with them, and we almost ended up turning them into a Horror, but that would've meant adding a new bloodline, and ultimately, I didn't want to have more new ones than old. I struggled a bit to find old bloodlines to update for this book, since most of the Ventrue with unique Disciplines had already been done in Devoted, and I wanted a good reason to do an update rather than just grabbing one for filler.

                                                Anyway, I'm glad we kept them as a bloodline, because I think they might be the best update in the book. Simplifying their Discipline spread to just adding Obfuscate covers a lot of the "haunted house" aesthetic they have going on, and it gives them access to both of Vampire's area-of-effect powers (Lord of the Land and Oubliette). We also took their original bane and tweaked it a bit, making it not as big a deal for neonates, but a real issue for elders. One of our biggest concerns was making them more playable. Vampires who are bound to one place are very thematic, but they're hard for people to use as practical player characters.

                                                Here's the new Malocusian bane, as a little bonus excerpt:

                                                Originally posted by False Gods: Ventrue
                                                Bloodline Bane (The Sedentary Curse): A Hermit’s home is the most important place in the world, and to leave its bounds is akin to discarding a limb. If a Malocusian does not spend a point of Willpower upon exiting his domicile, he can only remain outside for (Humanity – Blood Potency) hours before the Beast starts clawing at his mind to return. After this time limit expires, and for every additional hour the Malocusian remains separated from his haven, he suffers a cumulative –1 to all rolls to resist frenzy, to a limit of –5 (though this still compounds with Conditions like Tempted). This penalty remains until the Spider has rested a full day within his home. If Blood Potency exceeds Humanity, Malocusians have a maximum of one hour before penalties accumulate.
                                                Anyway, that's the Malocusians. As for the Rotgrafen, I don't have much to say! Most of their update was quality of life stuff. Generally, that was making their backstory a little less definite and giving them a stronger mechanical edge. The biggest alteration is their bane. I never really cared for their aversion to fire; it was a little too Mekhet in 1E, and and ultimately it didn't work in Second Edition, because any Ordo Rotgrafen can just go buy Coil of the Ascendant and never have it matter again. Coils often present issues like that, and I didn't feel like writing around that one. Instead, I gave them something more active, a bane that maintained a frenzy-aspect while making it more roleplay-oriented.

                                                Their shapeshifting power was also a factor I thought the original lacked; if you're going to worship Loki and have Protean as a favoured Discipline, you should be able to shapeshift. That's the rule!

                                                Originally posted by False Gods: Ventrue
                                                Form of the Trickster
                                                (Protean •••)

                                                If Sigrún is to be believed, Loki granted her a fraction of his mutable nature when he saved her miserable life. With his Devotion, Rotgrafen can call upon his infamous ability to shapeshift, whether to infiltrate an enemy’s stronghold or just discard the tyranny of fixed forms. Unlike many bloodline Devotions, this one isn’t unique to the Rotgrafen, though their blood takes to it well. A multi-formed god wouldn’t want just one group to enjoy his gifts, would he?

                                                This Devotion costs 2 Experiences to learn, or 1 if the character is a Rotgrafen.

                                                Cost: 2 Vitae

                                                Dice Pool: None

                                                Action: Instant

                                                Duration: Indefinite

                                                Once a night, the vampire can transform into another person. They can be real or imaginary and of any gender, but they can’t have any qualities beyond the human limit. To create a flawless imitation of a real individual, the Rotgrafen must consume their blood within the same night. Otherwise, she takes circumstantial penalties on any Social rolls to fool those familiar with the original, as defined by the Storyteller. This Devotion can bestow Giant and Small-Framed (Chronicles of Darkness, pp. 47 and 49), and either level of Striking Looks, but Sanctity of Merits does not apply. However, it can also temporarily remove these Merits, in which case it does apply if the vampire permanently redefines her form. Transformation lasts until the vampire chooses to revert to her default, but she can only take on one new face per night. She can also spend a Willpower while resting in her Unmarked Grave to redefine her default body.

                                                Note that the vampire’s Physical Attributes and Skills remain the same, so she should be careful to play into her altered physique if she’s undercover.

                                                        The Annunaku have always sparked more interest in me too. I like that they're Gangrel who aren't wild and bestial, but who still fit the clan's themes really well. As an aside, since they gain Auspex in our updated version, and the Malocusians gain Obfuscate, there's a neat bit of counter-synergy there.

                                                        Figuring out that (maybe) there's supposed to be a link between the two bloodlines helped me put the Spiders into perspective, but it really only raises more questions about why their write-up is so brief. There truly isn't much there in terms of backstory, which is surprising, because I'd say a characteristic of many 1E bloodlines is an overemphasis on histories.

                                                        Anyway, let's leave the Malocusians in their rotting manses. Let's mistake the map for the territory, and talk Architects of the Monolith.

                                                        Original preview: http://forum.theonyxpath.com/forum/m...entrue-preview

                                                        (As above, the text is a little different in the final book, but nothing huge.)

                                                        So, anyone familiar with First Edition will know that the Masons were a bloodline. Our version broadens them into a covenant, albeit one that's comprised of a Ventrue majority (or at least a plurality), and which is led by a powerful Lord elder. On the surface, this change felt like an obvious choice, and I've received a lot of positive feedback about it. However, it wasn't so obvious to me at first! Here's how I came to it:

                                                        It all started with a Discipline spread. I'm a bit fussy about bloodline Discipline combinations, and I try to adhere to certain unwritten rules that the game follows. For one, I don't like bloodlines to have more than two unique powers. You can't be good at everything, right? Plus, I think there's a point where if you pile on all the specialized powers, it muddies the thematic waters too much. You also lose some of the fun things available in the common Disciplines.

                                                        For two, I don't like to give bloodlines inherent access to blood sorcery. I've broken this rule myself (i.e., the Csalad in Strange Shades), but even then I felt a bit conflicted about the choice. In my (admittedly pedantic) view, blood sorcery isn't something that should be inherent to a vampire. It's an act of deliberate, dark creation and alien intellect, not a side effect of being a dead thing that hunts the living. Maybe Crúac can be a bloodline Discipline sometimes (occasionally, rarely), but definitely not Theban Sorcery.

                                                        Which brings me to the Architects of the Monolith. Not only did they have three unique Disciplines (Auspex, Dominate, and Gilded Cage), but one of them was a Theban Sorcery offshoot. So I started wracking my brain about how to solve this problem of my own creation. Do I just let that spread stand? It's an unwritten rule, not a hard one. Auspex could go, but it fits their archetype. Could I drop Gilded Cage and just make it something they have access to? But does that then mean other Kindred can access it? Do I just drop Gilded Cage entirely and give them some special Miracles?

                                                        Decisions, decisions.

                                                        This led to other issues, like their bane. If you've read other NMD books, you know we aren't precious about preserving old bloodline weaknesses, though we do try to at least thematically touch on the 1E versions. But the Architects just weren't clicking for me. Not only did they have a Derangement, they had a very setting-specific one, in that they're obsessed with their founder's plans. I kept having trouble making that interesting mechanically and narratively, this obsession with a character you'll likely never meet. I've actually translated the Masons' bane before as a snapshot update in Bloodlines: The Devoted, but I wasn't super happy with what I came up with. Astute readers may note that me being unsatisfied with the short translations is a running theme.

                                                        Here's where I started to take a closer look at the old write-up, which formed a new question: why are they a bloodline? Why isn't this a minor covenant with a specialized blood sorcery? They have customs, organization, and an ideology. Other lineages have these things too, but the Architects, as written, felt much more like a political faction than a family. Now, bloodlines are probably the loosest of the so-called Z-splats, in that they can be just about anything politically and socially. I tend to find that's a strength of their design (YMMV), and one that's a lot easier to write for than, for example, Lodges and Legacies. But, I felt like the bloodline format wasn't doing the Architects any favors. It was constraining them in a way that it doesn't for other more politically organized bloodlines. Plus, I didn't want to just retread the Csalad, which was probably my biggest concern beyond the writer's block.

                                                        Turning them into a covenant would make their Derangement into a choice, not an involuntary mental condition. That got me thinking about some real life ideologies; a few of you will probably notice that my write-up is kind of a parody/horror version of (among other things) Objectivism, and the Carpenter of Ayn Rand. Rather than have the Masons be "crazy", they just believe in their cause to the Nth degree. The kind of vampire who's attracted to their movement is going to fall for it hard.

                                                        Anyway, that's a long-winded way of saying their Discipline spread annoyed me so I made them a covenant.

                                                        If you prefer the Masons as a bloodline, and if I might insert a non-self-serving plug for once, the folks over at Darker Days Radio did an update of the Masons in The Secret Frequency Files. I ultimately prefer the Architects as a covenant, but I quite like the bloodline's presentation there, as it fits the original version's "hidden masters of the world" themes. That version is closer mechanically to the original, but their take on the backstory is quite different. There's also my short update in Bloodlines: The Devoted, so you've got lots of choices for bringing the Architects of the Monolith into Second Edition.

                                                        Alright, that's it for today. Tomorrow, we'll be going into the other covenant, the insidious Legio Noctis...

                                                            Happy Friday all! Time for our last excerpt of the week.

                                                            The Legio Noctis are a brand new covenant of soldiers and secret agents, though they aren't entirely without precedent. They claim to have roots in the Camarilla's Legio Mortuum (sic) and the medieval Grim Battalion, though those connections are implied to be complicated at best and tenuous at worst.

                                                            Like the Typhos Institute, the Legio went through a fair few changes over the course of development. Originally the concept was a lot more secretive and conspiratorial, and few vampires were meant to know they even existed. They were also implied to be EVERYWHERE, planting agents in countless domains and having a top down structure that worked with laser-like focus to take over the All Night Society.

                                                            That wasn't a bad start, but I'm wary of including antagonists of that scale in Requiem; an all-powerful enemy is hard to make interesting. It also makes it difficult to justify their secret nature if they're actually out there conquering things. So, we toned them down a bit to be more ambiguous in their ultimate structure, and a little less unified than they tell their grunts. That, and I wanted to distinguish them more from the Architects of the Monolith, who also have a bit of that "secretly taking over the world while you aren't looking" theme. The author and I also felt the first version ultimately ended up being too fascistic, which was a theme neither of us were comfortable with exploring in any overt fashion. That's the danger of doing these covenant write-ups partly in the first person, as sometimes the dramatic irony/perspective isn't clear without going really over the top. And even though this covenant was meant mostly for Storytellers to use as an antagonist, I did want to leave the door open for player characters — if the troupe is down with it — so that meant softening them.

                                                            In the end, the Legio are more like a vampire Blackwater (or the CIA, really). Their ultimate goal has more to do with Kindred globalism than militarism. They still want to reforge the Camarilla, but they do so through shadowy imperialism and false flags rather than having the goal ruling over domains. They place pawns where they need them and create ever larger convocations. How successful they've been is up to you, as is just how sincere their praetors really are about this whole New Camarilla thing.

                                                            The one thing that didn't really change in drafts was CLOTs, apart from some mechanical tweaking. I really liked the idea of pseudo-Devotion tactics, and I think they're a really fun toolkit.

                                                            Alright, that's it for now. The following is slightly abridged, as the Legio have a fairly long entry, so check out the book if you're interested in getting all details. Next week I think we'll start looking at the ghoul families, starting with the Crassus!

                                                            The Legio Noctis: The Last Legion

                                                            "No terms. No quarter."

                                                            The big picture

                                                            The Legio Noctis is an organization of Kindred mercenaries and black ops specialists. They would say they’re merely Soldiers, but they work more like surgeons to remove threats both external and internal. It’s their duty to protect, whether that means icing werewolves or committing regicide. The Army of Night deploys globally, funded by their work as operators and professional ratfuckers to the All Night Society. The CIA can only dream of their regime-change tally. If you know where to look, and you need to be rid of the upper echelons (or just the weird shit), the Legion is ready, armed with centuries of war profiteering. They’ve invested in weapons technology, purchased politicians, brokered arms deals, and made a buck off conflicts ranging from the American Revolution to the Cold War. When in doubt, always bet on red.

                                                            Officially, they’re apolitical, and they’ll lend their might to any Kindred who can pay their price. Their forces have backed militant collectivists, suppressed revolutions for absolute monarchs, spread vampiric perversions of neo-liberal democracy, and propped up pagan theocrats whose sole ethos is watching their enemies burn in pleasing ways. Ideology has little to do with their work. Whatever else a Soldier might believe, the Legion demands dedication to its own philosophy above all others: Victory and Death. The one should sacrifice for all; the war never ends.

                                                            But that’s the thing, isn’t it? It’s all well and good to be perpetually at war, but what’s the victory condition? The Legio Noctis has an answer — change. And once that change comes, the Legion can usher in utopia. Every puppet prince they place, every coalition government they build, every “new” political movement they usher into power is another tick on their timeline. The true goal is to become obsolete. The Last Legion.

                                                            Under the lens of a bellicose yet big tent philosophy, the All Night Society is fractured, narrow-minded, and impotent, forced to hide from humanity because they’re too selfish to work as one. The modern world is a miracle of connection and communication, yet most Kindred are afraid to ever leave their tiny domains. They’re given eternity and spend it trapped in city-shaped tombs. The Legio Noctis wants to fix that. Behind its secretive veil of blood money, it works to build a New Camarilla, a global Kindred empire forged by the might of the first’s self-appointed heirs.

                                                            Where we came from

                                                            We call him the Nameless Soldier. Oh, we have dozens of epithets and titles for him, but none have ever been confirmed. The most popular story says he was a Ventrue — perhaps the first — calling himself Lucius Romanicus Nox, a patrician who fought his way through the Julii’s doom and came out the other side different. But the truth isn’t important. He is the king who walks among the rank and file. He is the general on the front line. And he saved us from ourselves in our darkest hour.

                                                            For more than a thousand years, the Legion of the Dead was a shield against barbarism. For the Kindred of Rome, it enforced the law and stood tall against forces no one else was brave enough — or capable enough — to fight. Indeed, the humblest of the Camarilla’s Wings limped on after the rise of Christianity and the fall of Rome, but it wasn’t enough. After Rome fell, some of the Legion regrouped in Constantinople and restructured its ranks. It kept the peace between hundreds of Kindred refugees and the Lancea et Sanctum, who hoarded wealth and blood from the starving Roman masses. It was a cruel, grueling duty, to ignore the pleas of friends and kin, but a duty nonetheless.

                                                            Yet we were betrayed. Not by barbarians or spies, but by the Sanctified and our own leaders. They shattered our unity to create a “Legion of the Damned” made up of our most formidable and devout, then left the rest of us with little but a burnt offering. Divided, we could not save the Camarilla as it died a Final Death, gasping in the gutters through a slit throat.

                                                            We would have fallen too if the Nameless Soldier had not arrived.

                                                            As riots grew and oceans of Vitae flooded the streets, he appeared and took command of the shattered ranks. For the first time, the Legion defended not an empire, but itself, and because of that, we triumphed. This was the first lesson we learned from the Soldier, and we shall never forget.

                                                            From the ashes, a new Legion was born. They called us the Grim Battalion — assassins, killers, mercenaries, and worse. Perhaps we were, but we were free. Under the Soldier’s Laws, we reorganized and revitalized. We built our strength until our power grew enough that we posed a threat to the new covenants. They sought to enslave and destroy us, but that was the second lesson. The foolish fear the righteous; the liar hates the truth. So, we vanished into history. We were not gone, though. We merely adapted.

                                                            Our practices

                                                            Outwardly, we’re exactly what we seem to be, an elite band of professional mercenaries and secret agents that takes care of problems other vampires can’t deal with on their own. Is your prince’s mind infirm with the weight of ages, yet still all-too-powerful? Have the Acolytes gone too far with their unholy perversions? Are the Dragons doing anything? If you have the coin, you have our swords. Or rather, our Counter-Kindred Logistical Operation Tactics (CLOTs). These strategies endow our squads with the ability to overpower even the most terrifying powers of the Damned with our deadliest asset: Teamwork.

                                                            Inwardly, we have bigger fish to fry. The New Camarilla isn’t going to build itself, and the Kindred require a strong hand to guide its construction. Once the fighting’s done, the operation’s only just beginning. Sometimes we leave behind a force for “peacekeeping,” or to make sure elections run the right way. More often, we’ve been in a domain for decades, engineering whatever crisis required our services in the first place. We love a false flag.

                                                            Apart from our more overt work placing loyalists to fill in the political vacuums we create, our field agents position themselves in their domains so they can become not just useful, but linchpins to their courts. To a member, infiltrators are trained to hide in plain sight: The sheriff’s deputy who keeps a ledger of the edicts his boss ignores. The wide-eyed convert who asks earnest questions of the bishop to sow unease among the flock. The beautiful courtier who guides guileless hunters to her hit list. These are Soldiers, one and all, and they’re ready on the frontlines before our ground troops ever touch pavement.

                                                            Vae victis, as the Romans say.
