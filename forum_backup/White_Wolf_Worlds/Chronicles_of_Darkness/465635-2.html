    Originally posted by revanauditore92 View Post
    I really love the NWOD setting and the streamlined systems. But then Demon the Descent moved away from true demons and introduced the "God Machine". I absolutely hate this concept. I was wondering if anyone knew of a way to completely remove Demon the Descent and replace it with cWOD Demon the Fallen but still use the NWOD systems.


    Wasn't very enthusiastic about the idea of the World of Darkness being turned into The Matrix, either. That's pretty much what it is, reality is one big computer program...

    But I've been told several times in the past that often at times, the rule books for WoD only serve as guidelines, especially since the entire meta-plot was discarded in nWoD to give players a sand box element. You're free to homebrew (hope I'm using this term right) as much as you want. Can't you just take the Fallen setting and update it with nWoD mechanics?



    Demon: the Descent = Story of the Matrix. The world is a program, while Demons and Angels are Agents



    Demon: the Fallen = Story of Prometheus. God intended humans to be ignorant and powerless, Demons gave them power and knowledge, God struck them down.


    Personally, I like Fallen's story better, since it actually makes the demons seem relatable in a way. All the other games focus on the concept that the monsters aren't as monstrous as the public would believe them to be. Demons are Angels who took pity on humanity and were cast out because of it. Any evil that they exhibit came as the result of thousands of years of being tortured in hell, or whatever version of that exists in the WoD.


    What's the relatable aspect for Descent, though? They're fallen Angels who try to hide from the God Machine by taking human hosts and bartering for their souls. All the other games in the series give you a reason not to be a total ass to mortals, but it seems like that's encouraged when playing Descent. It's you or them, and you choose yourself just to hide from the GM. Not a very compelling story, to be honest.

                                ...Okay. I haven't been in this thread because I am a huge fan of both Descent and Fallen, so I wanted to remain neutral. Revan, do whatever you want, if you want help updating the setting to the nWoD 2.0 give me a PM, I'll be happy to offer the lore know-how.

                                This though? This is an incredibly shallow reading of both games. My inner lore hound is in pain.

                                Both are more infinitely more complex than that. Unchained Fall for many reasons, most of them relating to emotional connection to humans. One fell in love with a target, another came to care for his human family, a third was given the order to destroy the city she had helped build and ignored it. They can, do, and will "take pity on humans", they wouldn't be down here with us if they didn't, most of them. I can think of all of one Unchained character who's a true misanthrope, and he's explicitly insane. The reason they are so good at playing humans is that they feel human emotions, unlike their loyal brethren (and those aren't really the most alien creatures in the world to begin with, as many exiles show). There's other problems (for one, the world is explicitly not a program, the God-Machine needs subtlety and constant construction process for a reason-it can't be everywhere), but this is a discussion about motives.

                                For Fallen: *cough*Abaddon*cough*. Yes, Lucifer was motivated by kindness, but one of the core conceits of the game is that everyone on his side had their own motives. And how those motives interacted with the world often went horribly wrong-Asmodeus' "breeding programs" come to mind. There's even a story about a ruthless soldier who only rebelled against God because he'd be able to get away with atrocities under Lucifer more easily. In the core book. And of course. there's Dagon and his attempts to save humanity by killing its soul, off the top of my head (admittedly he's an Earthbound and not in his right mind, but that's a good example of how benign motives lead to monstrous behavior).

                                Less monstrous than they seem, certainly, but make no mistake; a sympathetic monster is still a monster.
