      It depends. I once started a campaign on a dare and came up with it in 15 minutes, but often, I spend whole days preparing and gathering materials, thoughts and just flat out processing possible outcomes.
      First, while I pretty much don't have any kind of physical notes, I will say that properly organized, categorized, and academically-prepared game might be little harder to start on, but it will be thousand times easier to work with than just a bunch of notes. I coached some GMs with this method, and it increased their efficiency, self confidence and ability to both prepare AND improvise (due to having better grasp on their material).           Not everyone is like me, so I don't want this to spook you. But I personally give myself three months of prep to create the worlds I use in my games. On a weekly basis, I also prepare about two hours per character for any specific things they've made clear they want to do, either through their aspirations or their behavior in-game. A lot of the stuff I prepare never gets used, but I find it helps me keep the campaign more cohesive to know details about the store-owner they might ask two questions of or never even speak to once. I also feel like that's my doing my part to provide my players with a game worthy of the time they dedicate to showing up on time every week.

                  The trick here is that you don't prepare "games" - you prepare a living, interconnected setting. This means that, once you grasp it, you don't need to prepare "games" per se, because anything that happens will organically grow from what you sow. Like, you don;t need to prepare in advance thinking "what is doing Mage X at monday?" if you have a good picture of who Mage X is, you can just look at the mental image of your world inside your head and answer will pop up naturally.
                  Ever heard how authors say that "their characters have a life of their own"? It's that kind of a deal.
                  It's also a hidden strength of Chronicles games, because they tend to be very local, and it makes preparing a setting a one-time deal that you can just perfect and improve over time, instead of preparing a new world each time your party arrives at town, like in typical adventuring games.

                      My campaigns usually last between six months and a year. I plan around a theme, and then I make every part of the game connect back to that theme. That way, if they finish one leg, I can logically think through what the results of their actions would be, and I continue the game through the aftermath, until they complete another leg, and then the aftermath of that, and so on, until I've told the story I want to tell (the theme is at its logical conclusion).

                              Originally posted by Seta143 View Post
                              how will i know if i am ever ready?
                              When you feel comfortable and confident in your ability to do it.

                              I run long stories with close friends. I've found an important way to keep them interested is to work off of their likes. Second edition offers an extra way to do this with Aspirations, including long-term Aspirations. These are things that your players want for their character, and so things you should work to bring into the game.

                              I've found judging how long certain things are going to take in-game is really difficult to do. At one point the players could spend half an hour debating which door to go into, at another they could zip through an area full of security and traps as if it was nothing. So I would just suggest you have a lot of back-up plans, and again the Aspirations can help with this. There's a quote that I liked once and decided to pull it off a couple times in my games. 'When you're stumped or the plot is flagging, have a man come in with a gun.' This often leads to a wild tangent but providing you know your game and players it's something you can likely wrangle into a good story.

                              Kinda working backwards through your posts, this is more about your first one. Sort of like another quote, 'no battle plan survives contact with the enemy.' The players are not your enemy, you're doing this together, but you are challenging them and their characters. Players will do something unexpected, you just want to be able to go with it. Don't worry about having to look some things up. You should go over your rules enough that you won't have to look them up, but you can have all the notes you need for plot details. Try to remember what you can so it will go over smoothly, but never be afraid to check back on those notes. Before a session I usually try to go over how 'the plan' goes in my head, so I'll know if I've got them crossing a river at some point I should know the rules for swimming, or at least have them readily accessible.

                              Designing a campaign can take huge variations in time, though. Some people are great at ad libbing and barely plan at all, others want to world build and write up character sheets for NPCs the players will never meet. Find a medium that you're comfortable with. Certain external sites have good advice, such as the Gentleman Gamer's YouTube channel. One of the best pieces of advice I ever got for a game I found on one of his videos, basically just having a list of names available so when or if characters decide to avoid your plothook and follow the interesting description of an NPC you have a name for them, or if the guy who comes in with a gun gets brought down and questioned. I've found names to be one of the hardest things to come up with on the fly. He has a few videos recapping game sessions where he talks about the planning he's done for them. There are a few other writers and even a developer who have detailed their games, including some of the work done to plan it. Dave Brookshaw has a few for Mage, here. As I hear it they were so well done they worked to get him hired as Mage developer. Rose Bailey had something similar among this thread, though there's other actual plays too. I'm not sure how much detail they offer in terms of campaign planning but you can see other peoples games which should help a bit. Chris Allen has a couple games where they've shared a bit of the setting building with us, including Eater of Names here, and their Paris game here.

                              There's a lot of advice you can get for general running of campaigns online, but most of it is going to be Dungeons and Dragons and similar games, the advice of which doesn't always translate well to storytelling, which isn't as involved with scaling combat.

                                  I like to make lite preparations to chronicles because I will check again those things in few weeks. I start with stating 'Big Problem' of the game - like 'half of PCs are candidates to become Messiah of Magic' or 'PCs are given Philosophers Stones from Nicholas Flamel'. Then I prepare (very light) outline of possible Chronicle Arcs - nothing more than 3-4 Arcs. Most of the time Arc can be one proper Story or 2-3 of those. If I have nice thematic reference, I can use it as basis of Arcs. Like in my Mage games basic Chronicles outlines looks like this:

                                  Sixth World / Discordia Concors - Mage Chronicle
                                  Problem: 'Half of PCs are candidates to become Messiah of Magic - Seers and Abyss is hunting on them'
                                  Solution: Visiting Seven Vasu, powerful Archmasters from Time Before that want to remake the Fallen World - and assessing who is Messiaha.

                                  1. Prolog - PCs are Sleepers that are in Bethlem Royal Hospital, where one Guardian is looking over their unusual Awakening. They stop Tremere that tries to soul feed on patients.
                                  2-8. Visiting next Vasu - Life and world changing decisions.
                                  9. Finale - Deciding who is Messiaha and final decisions on those.

                                  Magnum Opus - Mage Chronicle
                                  Problem: 'PCs are given Philosophers Stones from Nicholas Flamel - Stones are made from human lives.'
                                  Solution: ??? ( Open ended )

                                  1. Prolog - PCs get Philosophers Stones from Nicholas Flamel and he leaves them after this.
                                  2. Nigredo - PCs dwell deeper into mystery of Philosophers Stone as Sleepers, Awakening in process. They fight with heretic cult, ghosts and alien spirit.
                                  2. Albedo - PCs train under Orders and mages of Boston Consilium. They need/may protect their Stones or 'sell them' for favors. Enemies from previous Arc can return.
                                  3. Rubedo - Probable journey after Flamel when he shows up once again.
                                  4. Finale - Magnum Opus - Final transformations and secrets, possible Archmastery.

                                  Once having those basic Outlines, I hammer opening Story - the Prolog - that will set mood and tie characters. From then on I need only to revist my chronicle Outline once per few weeks and maybe add some cool elements or twists ( making chronicles longer by this very act ). In Sixth World chronicle we had 3 Vasus Arcs, 'Timeline Reset' Arc, we have now King in Yellow Mystery side Arc. After which we will return to main story with my devious ideas on it...

                                  Magnum Opus Outline I started to wrote after reading Uncrowned Kings Legacy from 1E Mage corebook. I 'stolen' Legacies structure of Attainments and remake them into chronicles Arcs. As game is about Alchemy, I think about adding one Promethean to the game for nice subplots in it as it works with Alchemy angel chronicles is about. One player was very eger to make Seer of Throne character, so I added NPC Seer that will try too recruit her. Maybe we will dwell in to Secret Concord and Red Word Cult of Boston Unveiled book.

                                  In the end, in my point of view - first take on chronicle should be light. You want just to take general direction of game. More of it will come with each PC that players will add to mix and story that will unfold at game table. Just remember to revisit basic Outline in time of few game sessions and listen to what you mad inspirations telling you or your players wants in the game.

                                      More on the subject, I usually start with a very simple description of my plot. Let's take one of my games as an example. It was a Promethean game.

                                      Splat: Promethean
                                      Location: Quebec Province
                                      Nemesis: Centuries-old centimani. He was unable to create an heir and went mad with isolation and grief. He's interested in the players because he wants some company even if it means capturing them.
                                      Weird stuff: The Throng will discover an 11-years old Scion

                                      This was basically my basis and I ran a 15-session game with it. It was awesome, one of the best games ever. As many have said before me, you need to write down as much as you need to be confortable. I improvise about 70% of my games, some prepare carefully for every detail of the next session, others create a world and go with it.

                                      As a player who's played with many different ST, I can say I hate hard rails, no matter how they're placed. Rails are acceptable in D&D (and even then, only in a crawl) but in ChroD they just grind my teeth a lot.
