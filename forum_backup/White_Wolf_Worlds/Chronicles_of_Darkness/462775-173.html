    Originally posted by Banu_Saulot View Post
    Has the question "What the hell are the Idigam and what do they do?" already been answered?
    Cuz I really can't wrap my head around it (I guess that's the purpose). I mean, their origin is unknowable mumbo-jumbo that nobody is certain in-universe or out. But I still don't get where they come from (like, they fall from space? appear in the dusty cellar of an old house? a super-computer? in the mind of a lunatic in an asylum?). And their purpose or activities aren't explained either, do they warp everything like Paradox bugs, do they try to fit in a world too alien for them, or try to make the alien world more natural according to their view? I wanted to understand how to use them in game by understanding what they are and do. (although not it might not be a simple question anymore)
    The core reason for their being is up to you, but some of this at least has been answered.

    Where they come from: Here, our world. Father Wolf caught as many as he could and threw them up to the moon to act as a prison. These are called the Moon-Banished. During strange astronomical events and things like the moon landing many have hitched their ways back. Others fled from Father Wolf, to the depths of the sea or Earth, and bided their time until they sensed no danger from him and resurfaced. These are called the Earthbound. There's no mechanical difference between them but the the ones who've stayed on Earth are probably more tapped into how the world works than those trapped on the moon.

    Their purpose seem to be to create new things. I've mentioned elsewhere I think they acted as a bridge between the Abyss and the real world, but discounting mage they can just be a foundation for things that don't exist, but should, or could eventually. Some of these things probably shouldn't exist, like a star in the middle of the Pacific Ocean that would wipe out all of Earth. Some of them are probably less inherently catastrophic, like a variant of the Underworld or the concept of Recollection. Whatever the end results, however, in gathering the Essence and setting up the Resonance to empower this creation and their own chrysalis, they can do a lot of the type of damage that attracts the attention of werewolves. And since they're not fans of Father Wolf or his children they're likely to not react well to that.

    They're couched as an out of context problem for werewolves because they don't offer a simple solution. They're spirits, but do a lot of things spirits can't. They don't claim people, but they can make all sorts of Claimed and Ridden. They're not hosts, but they can make hosts. They're not werewolves or humans, but they can co-opt werewolves or humans. They offer a Storyteller an opportunity for big story antagonists. Things in the vein of Sauron or Randal Flagg or Father from Fullmetal Alchemist or the Anti-God from Prince of Darkness. They're highly variable and hard to understand, but from an ST standpoint they're pretty easy, in that they're powerful NPCs that are interested in a concept enough to want to make it real, through themselves. How they do that can be complicated (setting up conspiracies and cults) or simple (brute-forcing their way through the understanding).

    The section on them in the second edition core offers a lot of options that could help guide a storyteller, but the obvious section to look at is the example idigam starting on page 222, which vary a lot, or the ones in Night Horrors: Shunned by the Moon. And while they've mechanically changed a lot across editions, the ones written up in first edition's Night Horrors: Wolfsbane would still fit thematically.

    Idigam can be a pretty big story aspect, and that alone can be the most daunting thing about them, especially given they're not likely to have a personal vendetta against an individual werewolf or pack. For that one might use the Geryo, introduced in Night Horrors: Shunned by the Moon and the Contagion Chronicle. They're old and powerful, also hate werewolves, but I think work better on a more personal level.

        Originally posted by Banu_Saulot View Post
        But I still don't get where they come from (like, they fall from space? appear in the dusty cellar of an old house? a super-computer? in the mind of a lunatic in an asylum?).
        All of the above. Idigam are natives to our world so they can show up basically anywhere. A subset of them were imprisoned on the moon but hitched a ride back after the moon landings. The rest have just gone about by being dormant, hidden, inaccessible and/or disguised. If a pack encounters an idigam, it's generally either because the pack just moved into the location, the idigam moved into the location, or the idigam has just gone under their radar until now.

        As for what idigam are, I think the most concise (but probably too simplistic to be fully accurate) explanation would be that they're spirits that somehow are free from having to conform to a specific nature. They can assume and abandon concepts that would be so innate for normal spirits that they could not exist without them. A spirit of fire could literally not stop being a spirit of fire because that's its core defining trait, but an idigam coalesced around the concept of fire can actually stop being a fire spirit and just become a spirit with the potential for a nature but no nature attached, if that makes sense.
        Essence Shaping could very well be a result of a spirit having such control over their own nature and Essence that they can apply similar principles on others.

        Other than that I think nofather has got you covered.

                Originally posted by Banu_Saulot View Post
                Is every Uratha born (First Changed) already sworn to the Oath of the Moon, and has the 'suffer less from silver' thing and Auspice?
                Auspice yes, Oath no. Barring Pure tampering or other aberrant circumstances, a First Change includes (culminates in, often) the acquisition of your Auspice and your first bit of Renown.

                Ghost wolves often adhere to the tenets of the Oath in practice because many of the things on it are breaking points anyway, but — most straightforwardly — no Ghost Wolf has a tribal ban to worry about breaking because those bans are part of a tribe's variant on the Oath, which is sworn at initiation and tends to accompany your first bit of tribal Renown.

                            I’m having some trouble grasping how Rites that target unwilling or hostile entities can be used practically. It may be something too important to kill, having vital knowledge or earning a reprieve from destruction in other ways, it might have a method of being too powerful to kill, the werewolves never being able to drain its Essence fully. Otherwise, fetishes can act as a 'prison' alternative to the death penalty of final discorporation, with the added benefit that werewolves can get some power out of it. Going around just slaughtering every spirit you don't like isn't really the Forsaken way.

                                                The frenzy equivalent in Forsaken is Death Rage. In 1e, one of the possible triggers for Death Rage was taking damage in your last Health boxes, and if you took damage in those boxes the goal of Death Rage changed from "slaughter everyone" to "get away, slaughtering anyone blocking you from doing so." 2e doesn't have that – and the amazing regenerative powers of 2e's Gauru form give a werewolf in Death Rage little to fear for his own life anyway.

                                                        As written, the table values are for healing outside of Gauru. As the section on Regeneration near the table notes, Uratha in Gauru are an exception and heal all bashing and lethal damage every turn.
