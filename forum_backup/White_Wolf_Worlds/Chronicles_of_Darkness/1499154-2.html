    Originally posted by Jester-PFG View Post
    The idea was to provide a means with which my players could expect to tie up a plot or an antagonist with absolute narrative finality and provide a sense of closure. In short, it's to close the book much like the way the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki not only ended an an empire, but also brought about a massive change in culture and marked the end of an era, so that my players can open a new and potentially more interesting chapter in their chronicle, rather than just have that same antagonist come back and come back and come back again. Finally, the construction of nuclear weapons doesn't just represent the splitting or fusing of atoms, but represent the culmination of efforts of a major national economy to research and to produce; the placing them on this scale is to recognize this.
    This is exactly the undue reverence for nuclear weapons I was having trouble with. They're very big, very destructive bombs, but bombs none the less. I think elevating them to something almost holy, something uniquely capable of ending a conflict, is a dangerous way of thinking. Why do they have to be something that ensures narrative finality?

    From what I understand, the role of the bombs in ending WWII is still the subject of much debate, with a lot of military voices saying Japan was already in the process of surrendering and the bombings were militarily unnecessary.

    As for requiring the culmination of efforts of an entire nation, couldn't you say the same about, say, a fighter jet? An unmanned attack drone? Any other piece of cutting edge military equipment?

    Originally posted by Jester-PFG View Post
    Trivializing the nukes takes a lot of that narrative gravity out and what's otherwise a very planned and very deliberate exercise that my players would be sinking a great deal of effort into given the sheer amount of time and planning that they would be spending in Playbook operations to position assets, write their playbooks, and do the research, and it very much is not fair to them to just say "...and the big bad evil dude simply shrugs it off" when they could have spent a very long time putting in the work themselves and also made the deliberate choice to accept the literal and political fallout of breaking the nuclear taboo. It very much isn't fair to them for me to as a GM simply refute what could have been a lot of real-world research, my players going the extra mile to show their work, and a considerable amount of narrative escalation during the use of Playbook Time with a simple "but it says here on the book that this antagonist is immune" after they've exhausted all other conventional options and were willing to make such a sacrifice.

    In short, it's there to give nukes a bit of narrative gravity and keep their use both taboo, but also very meaningful while also giving them a distinct purpose in game that sets them apart, and present a very literal "nuclear option" for when the Godzilla Threshold is crossed.
    I feel like the solution to this fear of a narrative anticlimax is much, much simpler than completely uprooting the setting's cosmology and overruling a vast swath of powers. If you, as the Storyteller, wish for your players to be able to use nuclear weapons to end a threat and have it be satisfying, simply don't introduce any threats that would be able to disregard being nuked to Kingdom Come. That is entirely within your power, as the final arbiter of what abilities and weaknesses the threats your players face have.

    Moreover, the Chronicles of Darkness is, largely, a setting for horror and occult mystery. If nukes are able to kill anything, this crowds out stories like…
    • The players have found the enemy's Bane. Can they convince someone in power to listen to them that a sprig of holly tied to an arrow will do much more to the monster than nukes before the bombs are in the air?
    • The players nuke the monster and it doesn't do anything. Can they live with the regret of the collateral damage, knowing it was for nothing? Where do they go from here?
    I see you've said you're tabling the strategic warfare for now, but these are some thoughts I've had rattling around.

                It's about a month behind schedule, but the Low Light Engagements and Nightvision optical devices update is finally here. While I was at it, I also did a bit of reorganising of the document and I've started restructuring some of the source code used to typeset it, along with cleaning up the version control history to make it slightly easier for me to edit going forward. Some of the different vehicles and tank guns are now segregated by era, since it felt a tad bit awkward to have something like the old QF-6 Hundredweight six-pounder gun, a weapon from the 18th century, in the same section as the Rheinmetall L/55A1 120mm Smoothbore Gun, which is being rolled out to the newer Leopard 2A7Vs serving with the Bundeswehr or Armed Forces of the Federal Republic of Germany in the (at the time of writing) modern era.

                This new update adds mechanics for what's called "Visibility States" that add six different levels of relative visibility to the scene and objects within it ranging from daylight to total darkness. Rather than rely on an objective scale based on illumination, I instead chose to make it relative to the viewer to allow the GM and players to be able to quickly describe how visible something is to the given player or NPC given the equipment they have.

                The Visibility State works on a scale of 0 to 5 and has different examples of how visible something is base on ambient lighting conditions, along with detailed examples for each one. For instance, 5 indicates effectively unlimited visibility, and 0 indicates complete lack of visible light entirely, with 1 representing something like the conditions during a New Moon on a cloudy night underneath heavy tree cover in an old growth forest, and 4 representing something like the lighting conditions on a busy main street with plenty of street lamps, along with different and detailed spotting mechanics for each state.

                As a result, lower visibility states denote decreased visibility, which can not only mean darkness, but under certain situations, can mean increased amounts of bloom. Anybody who's played a night game of something like paintball or airsoft will know, that at least until autogated Generation III Nightvision Optical Devices that featured intelligent autogating arrived on the scene, one way to prevent people from looking into a structure from the outside at night while still being able to see outside without the use of NODs is to place a glowstick on the windowsill and let the bloom take care of this. I've tried to allow the different mechanics representing night vision devices to model this somewhat and take into account the different generations of nightvision optical devices through the eras, as well as the different pros and cons of using different configurations of nightvision optical devices, such as monocle and binocular configurations, as well as single tube biocular configurations that were popular back in the earlier days of night vision goggles and are still in use with some militaries that can't afford or source better, as well as milsim players and airsofters.

                All of these mechanics are intended to allow for approximate modelling of the different generations of night vision optical devices from the primitive Starlight Scopes of Vietnam Era and Korean War era fame as well as the Infrared Spotting Scope that evened the odds at the Battle of Iwo Jima, to the modern Harris L3 Panoramic Night Vision Goggles that sometimes pop up in popular culture, and were showcased in Zero Dark Thirty. I've even added some mechanics for stuff that is at this time somewhat experimental, such as the use of Colour Night Vision Technology, which is starting to be retrofitted to and work with White Phosphor Technology.

                At a future date I do intend to add some more gear, such as Infrared Target Pointers which also present laser illumination (for tracking of muzzle direction, for instance) as well as infrared illumination that works in the infrared to near infrared spectrum and provide illumination that's only visible to night vision optical devices that can amplify light in the Near Infrared Spectrum, however I wanted to get the NODs update out faster and felt that that wouldn't require all that much work to put together.

                In terms of gameplay impact, it's been my hope that the new additions brought by the low-light mechanics will indeed help considerably with offering many more tactical possibilities with handling low-light engagements and very much change the game entirely when it comes to fighting at night. We ran an earlier encounter with provisional and somewhat more simplified mechanics for lighting and shadows, and it had a significant input on how the different encounters were fought, and the way that the players needed to use lighting to their advantage. While it doesn't as of yet cover things such as lighting discipline, it added an extra dimension to the tactics, so I thought I'd try to formalise this a little more and make it something that brings this into the Chronicles of Darkness itself.

                Onto some of the other updates that have been included with this update:

                Additions and Edits included with this Update
                • I rewrote the introduction to make it a little bit shorter and more to the point, and more in line with the other game lines.
                • The missing DDO values for the Rheinmetall 120mm L/44 and L/55 Tank Gun's kinetic energy penetrators such as the M829, M829A4, DM63, and DM73 APFSDS have been filled in. The lack of DDO values contributed to the rounds overperforming by as much as up to 7 AP.
                • The missing DDO values for the Russian 2A46 and 2A46M-5 125mm Tank Gun have also been filled in, similarly to the above.
                • The British Challenger 2 Main Battle Tank has been added, along with the L30A1 120mm Rifled Tank Gun with fully detailed tables, history, and mechanics. While the design itself is somewhat dated at this point and is being replaced by the Challenger 3, which is claimed to be a new take on the design, the Challenger 2 has detailed in-game stats and should be competitive on the modern battlefield with other modern main battle tanks including the Leopard 2A7V and the M1A2 Abrams SEPv4.
                • Much like the tables for the Royal Ordnance 84mm QF-20 Quick-Firing 20 Pounder Tank Gun's Mk. 1 High Explosive Shot, the Royal Ordnance L7/US M68 105mm Tank Gun's M456A2 HEAT, M393A2 HESH, and the Royal Ordnance L30A1 120mm Tank Gun's L31A7 HESH round all use the Next-Generation explosive mechanics, which use figures derived from the Kingery-Bulmash Engineering Calculations.
                • A section on the different mechanics inherent to different tank ammunition has been added, covering the rules for modern tank rounds such as APFSDS, HESH and HEAT, and historical tank round types such as APC/APCBC, APDS, APCR, APCNR.
                • Tank Guns have been split into different categories, and are arranged by era.
                • Tanks are now also split into different sections and are similarly arranged by era for easier navigation in the document.
                • The rules for HESH rounds have been tweaked slightly in anticipation of the use of the Next-Generation explosive rules. HESH rounds now have their own AP characteristics for the pat formed by the deformation of the round on the target. Although the appearance of spaced or composite armour have blunted the advantages that HESH rounds offer in tank-on-tank combat (this is taken into account under existing vehicle mechanics), HESH rounds nonetheless offer considerable capabilities when it comes to demolishing structures; this is one of the reasons why until now, the British Army continued to hold out on using a rifled tank gun and with the focus being on the use of HESH rounds for their main battle tanks, even long after the rest of the world had switched to the use of smoothbore guns for tanks.
                • Shotgun loads and damage tables have been tuned, after some research determined that the loads for the KS-23's 23x75mmR shells were loaded to significantly lower pressure than initially assumed. The shells are in fact, made from cardboard and the lethal loads are about on par with a 12 gauge shotgun, with the shotgun's true utility being in using less-than-lethal loads for riot suppression. I've added stats for 8 Gauge Black Powder loads, as well as suggestions for 8 Gauge Belted Industrial loads, which are used in boiler guns meant to dislodge clinker or carbon buildup from industrial kilns for comparison.
                • The Benelli M4 Super 90/M1014 has had its tables and rules corrected from an earlier typo. Additionally, the Benelli M4 Super 90/M1014 Joint Service Shotgun has had its fluff and special rules expanded upon to allow for "Ghost Loading" (alternately referred to as "Carrier Loading") which is described briefly in the United States Marine Corps Operator's Manual for the M1014 [See Footnote 1]. By allowing one round in the chamber, one round on the carrier, and seven rounds in the magazine tube, the M1014's magazine capacity effectively increases to 7+2. Ghost loading takes an extra turn.
                • As an easter egg by request from one of the other members that I spoke to online, the Ford Model T has been added under Passenger Cars in the vehicles section, with fully detailed tables, history, fluff and special rules to make it uniquely feel like an old horseless carriage that dates back to the eras before the First World War. Now your characters too can party like it's 1912.
                • Minor fixups and tuneups have been added and a few notes have been fixed and tuned.
                • As we have discussed, nukes have been changed to encourage players and GMs to come to a consensus on the different narrative possibilities prior to gameplay begins.

                Next update will probably be the largest one so far and that will be implementing the Next Generation Explosive Rules for the entire game and all existing entries that haven't been moved onto Next Generation. However, I'm very happy that I got the NODs update down.


                Further Proposed Additions - Tuning of Shotgun Damage Characteristics

                Some further discussion items I've wanted to table is a micro-update, specifically for shotguns. Earlier on, what I did was I added the "Low Velocity" rule for shotguns, which simply made them less effective against object Durability and especially against armour. However, recently, I've been considering with the "Low Velocity" rule and after some experimentation I've felt that this double-dips on the damage rating and sells them a tad bit short, especially given that a shotgun carries a Strength Requirement of 3, wheras a .30-06 Rifle carries a Damage of 4 and has a Strength Requirement of 2.

                Something to consider on this is that while a 147-grain .30-06 rifle with a muzzle velocity of 2,800 feet per second may have a muzzle energy of around 3,500 Joules, a 2-3/4" 12 gauge shotgun slug weighs about 1-1/8 ounce, and will be leaving the barrel of a shotgun at around 1600 feet per second, for a total muzzle energy of 3,700 Joules. While a 12 gauge shotgun slug also won't penetrate an NIJ 0101.6 Class II Kevlar Vest, which a .30-06 Full Metal Jacket will at 50 metres, a 12 gauge buck hammer shotgun slug at the same range definitely has the potential to leave a much bigger hole in ballistics gelatine protected by one layer of denim fabric.

                Similarly, at around 25 yards, five shells of Federal Premium FliteControl 00 Buckshot, each of which contains nine pieces of 00 buckshot, fired offhand through either a Cylinder bore or Improved Modified choke will land all forty-five pieces of 00 buckshot within a pattern of around maybe 10 inches. 00 buckshot will usually penetrate around eighteen of ballistic gelatin reliably at that distance, which is more than the twelve inches of ballistic gelatine the FBI considers to be "adequate." At the distance, a pattern of nine pellets of buckshot within an area less than 10 inches in diameter centered within the torso is enough to stand a good chance of landing at least one of the nine buckshot pellets in the heart and one or both lungs of an unprotected human and result in a first-shot fatality.

                Because of that I'm considering bumping the twelve gauge shotgun to "4L 9-ag Low Velocity", rather than "3L 9-ag", or where they are now at "3L 9-ag Low Velocity". The Low Velocity rule in Gods of Iron doubles the effect of Object Durability unless the object is specifically listed as Fragile, and increases the effect of ballistic armour by +0/2. This in turn brings them up above parity against lightly armoured targets, but severely reduces their effectiveness against both Object Durability and any armour that has a ballistic rating. In Armoury 1st Edition, Shotguns also were Damage 4L 9-again, and so were full-sized cartridges such as the 7.62x51mm. This presents something of a compromise by bringing a 12 gauge shotgun up to be slightly above the level of a full-sized cartridge, but only at short ranged and only on unprotected, unarmoured targets. In essence this mechanically represents that a shotgun, in certain niche situations can be dramatically more effective than a rifle and produce the same muzzle energy, but only when those conditions are met; past about 40 metres, while the buckshot pellets can still retain lethal velocity, the accuracy becomes extremely questionable, even when firing high-quality, buffered loads with controlled flight wads.

                Finally, to counterbalance the shotgun spread effect, I'm considering halving shotgun damage by range interval; thus, a shotgun firing buckshot at short range would inflict full damage, then at medium range inflict half damage, then at long range a quarter of its original value.
