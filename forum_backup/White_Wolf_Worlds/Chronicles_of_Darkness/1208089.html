    Hope in the Darkness?

    04-17-2018, 02:44 PM
    So in the first Onyx Path podcast, the concept of hope was discussed in the context with the Chronicles of Darkness, with two games being described as the "most hopeful"- Promethean and Changeling. Now, Promethean is kind of obvious- it is a game about hope, becoming human and achieving New Dawn. Elpis is an actual force in that game, granting visions that while you are stuck in an eternal night right now, the sun would rise beyond the horizon, and that those who search would find. Changeling was described as having hope in an whole other type of context- that while you have been abused and mutilated beyond anything the human mind could tolerate, with a mind shattered and soul broken, you could still achieve Clarity and regain you life- or perhaps weave a new one. Your story hasn't ended- it has just began, and you are its writer. You are not a slave to the stories of the Fae, but you have regained control over your life, and now cement your own fate upon the story. Little Red Riding Hood is not devoured by the Wolf, waiting desperately to be saved by the Huntsmen. She cuts the Wolf's belly from the inside with a knife, cuts the Huntsmen head and go fourth to decide her own way to take.

    So yeah, both gamelines are clearly hopeful ones- but what about the others? I mean, the way I see it, there is no good horror story without having hope in it. If you know that all characters are doomed to die and have no way out, then there is no point in growing attached to them, and a story without relatable characters is, IMO, a story which should not be written :P There is always hope, as small and flitting as it may be- and in a dark world such as this, hope is the only thing which stops the whole complex of monsters and madmen from collapsing over their own heads.

    Hunter, for example, is truly an hopeful game in my eyes- it is the kind of hope that while fighting against all chances, you could actually succeed. It may be hard, it may require sacrifices, it may even kill you- but the Vigil would keeps on going, keep on protecting, keeps on holding the monsters in bay and show them that humanity can take a stand against the darkness and win. Sure, each candle is small, and each victory is meaningless on its own- but together, one by one, marching across the world and through the ages, the Vigil keeps on going. It is likely that the Vigil will never actually win, of course- but it would never lose either. It can't lose. If it would, well, that would be the end of humanity. The Vigil's fuel is hope- and without it, there will be no candle to light the darkness.

    Other gamelines also have their own hope- Demons hope to create their own Hell, and be free from the God Machine and its angelic servants. Mummies are slaves, but they have hope in achieving Apotheosis and reach the starry lands of A'aru, away from the tyranny of petty Laws and the hunger of the Devourer. Geist have hope, that you could look at the dissolution and madness brought by the Underworld and say "no", helping ghosts to move on and, eventually, either free yourself from your burden or use them to change the very nature of the Great Bellow. Mage have hope that they could find escape from the Lie and reach the Truth once and for all. Beasts have hope that they could rise above the story they tell, freeing themselves from the terror of heroes and gather a family which they have chosen for themselves. Even Deviants may have hope, that they could get back at the organization which broken them and maintain their minds in the face of the Scars which were inflicted upon them.

    Among the gamelines, I think that Vampire and Werewolf are the least "hopeful"- Golconda is practically in the new edition, and as such there is no real "escape from being a vampire", and Forsaken is a game of hunt, violence and death. Still, I think that there is some hope in Requiem- not a way out as much as having the ability to stay human even in the face of becoming a monster, and learning how to combine your own song and dance to those around you. Among all gamelines (other than Hunter), Vampire is the one in which you must maintain your memory of what being human actually is. You wear masks upon masks, lie to yourself and others, and in that mess of lies there is the hope that while you are a creature of the night, you could still remember the sun's loving embrace. Werewolf is a bit more problematic, but I think that its hope is that you are not bound to the influences of spirits and whispers of the shadows. That when things go bad, you could actually fight head to head against the forces of pain and sorrow, death and misfortune, and force them to obey instead of being bound to their will. And as you rise to become a Bodhisattva of the hunt, your pack would have to fear nothing- for there is nothing which could withstand your fangs or survive your claws, and only the fools would dare to threaten those dear to you.

    So- what do you think is the place of hope in the CofD?

      I think hope is the engine of action.

      However, hope consists of the joining of three elements: Goal, Ability and Agency.

      Goal is either an end-state or an intermediate step if the end is not reasonably foreseeable. It's the promise of a dollar more tomorrow than today that encourages working through the misery. It's lasting through the night one more time and being able to smile at yourself in the mirror and lie convincingly past the fangs that you're a good guy--still human.

      Ability is simply that you have resources, education or instruction, materials, funding, friends--whatever you need, to get the job done. If you clearly lack resources, your goal gets dragged down to more and more realistic levels of attainability until it reaches meager levels such as "one foot forward... do it again" or "take a bite... chew." These can substitute for a time, but it takes indomitable will to avoid snapping after a while (see Holocaust and other total institutional survivors for examples, or medical suffering stories).

      Agency is whether others permit you to take action toward your goal. If you can reasonably expect your sandcastle to be stomped on as soon as you get a tower together, you will not bother to build it. Likewise, if you can only build it at the edge of the water, you may just wait until the tide is going out and not in.... As with resources, your goal gets dragged lower and lower until you arrive at a level that is attainable.

      Like any engine, momentum is important. If you have achieved a small hope, you can work toward a bigger one, and so on. Succeeding in tiny hopes build little momentum. Jumping immediately to goals that are too large--vastly exceeding your history of achievement, obviously out of line with resources or agency, or with serious consequences for failure--can undo momentum and destroy people in ways that even bullets don't.

      ---

      This framework can be helpful to think about while defining your characters as people, and conversely very handy to keep in mind as you consider how to defeat that semi-human monster who'd otherwise be unreachable through physical harm....

      --Khanwulf
