            I'm not really sure why it's more important for a player to commit to roleplaying a one-dot Attribute than, say, a three-dot Attribute. Or a two-dot. Or a four, which is perfectly feasible for a Vampire or Mage character who gets to distribute an extra starting dot. A one-dot Attribute has comparatively little actual in-game effect: it makes you worse at things, but no more worse than having three dots makes you better at it. If it were meant to represent a strong deficiency, it does a rather poor job of it. (It's not; Persistent Conditions represent that. That's why there are Conditions like Blind, Crippled, and Mute, not just to represent an edge case where your character might be driven Mute mid-game.)

            It also kind of messes up the distribution math. Starting dot spreads are tight in nWoD, probably with the choice of having to sink an Attribute to one dot to favor another baked in. Putting this kind of emphasis on one-dot Attributes effectively means every character's tertiary Attributes are always going to be 2/2/2, which while not broken is a rather bland boiling down of choice.

            Having one dot in an Attribute is its own drawback already; it doesn't need to be magnified.

                        For the record, I do require people to roleplay having a 3 in an attribute. I also require them to roleplay having a 4 in an attribute. And I certainly require them to roleplay having a 5 in an attribute. The only dot rating that I don't expect people to explictly roleplay having is 2, because 2 is average. I've never complained about those Attribute 1 people, just kind of grimaced and went on with my life.

                                        Ever since I started reading this book, I've been going on and on about how I don't know what the God-Machine is and I didn't want anyone to spoil it for me. Well, I have finally arrived.

                                        THE GOD MACHINE CHRONICLE

                                        The text begins with a question: “What is the God-Machine?” I feel my quest nearing its end. I feel like I'm about to unravel a truth that will forever change my life. I'm reading the next sentence: “That question doesn't have a single, easy answer ...”

                                        FUUUUUUUUCKK!

                                        Seriously, though.

                                        I'm trying to decipher the art piece. There's some kind of floating obelisk with jellyfish tendrils and a creepy eye. Maybe my previous idea that the God-Machine is like if “Cthulhu's home dimension had sex with a grandfather clock” is close to the truth after all. That picture feels Lovecraftian.


                                        VOICE OF AN ANGEL

                                        So the God-Machine created the world for the angels. These angels desired “humans” to serve them. First they took animals and tried to make them “humans”, but they ended up becoming violent demons instead. Having learned from that mistake, they managed to create their humans and spread them across the world. Humans didn't die, but instead went to sleep in the ground when they got old and were rejuvinated into youth. They created a city “where the orbit of the moon crossed the orbit of the sun.” I'm not going to try to learn what that means. Esoteric stuff.

                                        So after the humans in Eclipse Town got angry with their supernatural politicians, they whined to the God-Machine and got an answer in words. So the God-Machine can communicate with words? It is an actual sentient being then?

                                        The humans debated for seven years after they got their answer. That doesn't seem very human-like. Humans wouldn't debate for seven years. They would act on impulse and cause a bunch of chaos, and then try to debate first after they have ruined a bunch of things.

                                        Angels sleep apparently. And they have red blood (assuming the text refers to angel blood). But they are also ephemeral beings according to the Antagonist chapter. Can ephemeral beings sleep and have red blood? I guess. I just didn't expect it. Or maybe these “Ancient Ones” are different from the angels in the Antagonist chapter. Maybe these are old super-angels while the other ones are mini-angels pooped out by the God-Machine on occasion. Apparently the survivors were called Furies. I suppose the Furies are basically arch-angels then?

                                        Ah, this is cool. The Furies named themselves after concepts and shaped the world (and mankind) through curses. This then nerfed humans into stupid mortals. I guess the pre-cursed humans were the kinds to debate for seven years and it was the post-cursed humans that went ahead and acted irresponsibly and built huge walls and created Snapchat. Well, the Torment Fury who created “wyff-man” and “wo-man” wasn't very cool. That was extremely silly.

                                        So there are four Furies who haven't cursed humans yet, but who are waiting to do so?

                                        Right, I had forgot that all of this was said to a 12 year old by an angel. Are we sure this whole God-Machine Chronicle isn't just the figment of a child's imagination?

                                        We go on to learn about how demon cults guard hidden chambers under the Sphinx of Egypt. One Fury is Australian because he was sure that Australia wouldn't be discovered by man for a long time. Apparently he only considered “white man” because there is evidence of Aboriginal people in Australia dating back as far as 60 000 years. Seems like this Fury didn't consider that. Maybe he took the name Racist. Oh wait, the text continues and says that Aboriginals actually were there and gazed upon him and could read dreams. So maybe he took the name Stupid instead. He should have picked Antarctica.

                                        Jokes aside, I actually think this is a fun and interesting genesis story. It's definitely not what I expected it to be.

                                        The other Furies built civilizations elsewhere. One bitter Fury noticed that some of these civilizations were close to mastering Star Rites that apparently turn humans into gods.

                                        Electromagnetic waves are the shadows of the voice of the God-Machine. I like the part about Hughes and his discovery of electromagnetic patterns. That's the kind of mystery story I enjoy and would enjoy roleplaying.

                                        Apparently Oppenheimer was a demon cultist. That's kind of silly, but it fits the theme of the setting so I'm okay with it. I did enjoy the story about the Apollo missions being a cover and austronauts bringing back the skeleton of Death.

                                        Okay, the intro text is over. I actually enjoyed this. It's a bit more sci-fi than I expected it to be, but I enjoy stories like this.


                                        FICTION: DAVID NELSON

                                        Interesting.


                                        GEARS WITHIN GEARS: THE GOD-MACHINE CHRONICLE

                                        “People see something the God-Machine intends to keep secret---or they are singled out as the right shape of person for a particular task, the right size cog with the right number of teeth---and they disappear.”

                                        I picture some Junji Ito horror show in my mind---the spiral of humans at the end of Uzumaki. There's been nothing to suggest that the God-Machine is made up of anything other than “supernatural stuff” but wouldn't it be creepy if parts of the God-Machine are actual human cogs?

                                        Okay, so it is an actual interdimensional machine. Is it built by something or is it the cause of its own existence? If it's interdimensional, and controls spacetime, then it wouldn't need a beginning or an end because it makes time meaningless. I'm going with the idea that the God-Machine is its own cause unless something in the book says otherwise.

                                        Now the book outright states that the truths about the God-Machine are undetermined. I'm okay with that.

                                        The God-Machine's structure consists of both literal and figurative parts. This is good, because it lets me bring something physical and substantial into my games if I want to. And it lets me be more esoteric when I want to.

                                        Now it says that the God-Machine can't violate the laws of physics. But earlier it suggested that it was interdimensional with tendrils stretching all over the multiverse. Maybe it has to abide to the laws of physics of the dimension it taps into, but it cannot alter the laws of any of those dimensions. At least not willingly.

                                        But can it do so unwillingly?

                                        The notes about Infrastructure and Linchpins were very inspiring. I can think of a few ways I can integrate that into my stories.

                                        The optional rule about Fate doesn't really seem necessary to me. I think Aspirations are enough.

                                                This is a Clarke's Third Law thing. It can't violate the laws of physics, but its understanding of the laws of physics are sufficiently advanced to the point where it can do things that might look like violating those laws to us primitive humans.

                                                So yeah, now that you're here, I'm going to give the meta-game answer to what the God-Machine Chronicle is, because it's been driving me crazy waiting this long. I'll put it in spoiler tags for now, in case you want to finish the section before reading this, but I can't wait any longer.

                                                So, when Onyx Path decided to do a second edition of the game, which was called World of Darkness, or colloquially as "new" World of Darkness at the time (as opposed to Classic World of Darkness, which previously had the name World of Darkness), the owners of the IP (Crowd Control Publishing at the time) were not too keen on the idea of a new edition. D&D's identity crisis and ensuing edition war was a still a recent memory, and new editions were probably seen as bad publicity. So instead, Onyx Path pitched the idea of "Chronicle books", which would feature optional rules updates to 1st edition alongside a canned chronicle (which is CofD's term for a campaign, much as story is CofD's term for an adventure and chapter is it's term for a session). The God-Machine chronicle was the first of these chronicle books, and it is still available for sale. The next such book was Blood and Smoke: the Strix Chronicle, which was the equivalent of the God-Machine Chronicle for Vampire the Requiem. This book sold incredibly well, but caused a lot of confusion over these rules updates, and what they meant for "new" World of Darkness going forward. The financial success of Blood and Smoke was apparently enough to convince Crowd Control that it was safe to call the new edition what it was, and Blood and Smoke was re-released with a new title and cover, as Vampire the Requiem Second Edition. Since Onyx Path was finally allowed to officially call Second Edition what it was, they decided to publish a proper core rulebook for Second Edition, since the God-Machine Chronicle had been written as a set of patch notes and had to be used alongside the First Edition core book. Around the time that the book that you've been reading was in development under the name World of Darkness Second Edition, Paradox Interactive bought the World of Darkness and Classic World of Darkness intellectual properties from Crowd Control, and changed Classic World of Darkness' name back to just World of Darkness, and the "new" World of Darkness to Chronicles of Darkness. So, the book's name was changed accordingly and the Second Edition part was left off of the title.

                                                So TLDR, the God-Machine Chronicle is the name of the campaign setting that was used to sell Second Edition before it was called Second Edition.

                                                    Spoiled for discussion of the writing process of this section. I'm specifically talking about the history of the "Voice of an Angel" piece of fiction, because it's an important part of the meta-game answer of what the God-Machine Chronicle is that Charlaquin doesn't explain.
                                                    I would add that the God-Machine began as simply the Voice of an Angel, which was the first piece of fiction at the beginning of the first edition World of Darkness book. The piece became very popular amongst readers and writers both, so the writers began dropping references scattered throughout different books to something called the God-Machine. When the time came to do an updated rule-set, they wanted to give an example mystery, as an 'example chronicle' of sorts. The first edition base book really only had Ghosts if i remember correctly, but it had this fantastic piece of fluff. Additionally, the idea of the God-Machine offered the opportunity to do a novel take on Demons as a playable splat, so they began working on the Demon: the Descent and the God-Machine Chronicles side-by-side.

                                                    What this most notably means for you, though, is that the Voice of an Angel was written YEARS before anything else in the God-Machine Chronicle, and is included to show the inspiration for the rest of the chronicle, and as an in-world document, and as you've already noted in a few places, is contradicted in quite a few ways by other portions of the text, including the actual game mechanics, such as the ephemera bit with the blood you noted. (Though Angels CAN have blood in some circumstances, it's really quite complicated).


                                                    Which reminds me, also linking into the past discussion on what books you should read, you should very much consider reading the fiction anthologies. I only have the God-Machine Anthology, but it's fantastic writing, and I think you'd very much enjoy it.
