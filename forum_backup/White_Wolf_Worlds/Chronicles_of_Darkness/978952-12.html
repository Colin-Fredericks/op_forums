                            Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post
                            Sure, but your examples all have consistent settings shared between the individual shows/movies/games/whatever. Which you could do with Chronicles of Darkness, but not without sacrificing the setting modularity we were discussing. You can establish that Luke Cage is part of the same universe as the big green guy and the guy with the silver hammer through common setting elements, but if you say that in this show, Thor was born a human and gets his power from the hammer, but in that movie, he was born in Asgard and gets his power from being a literal God, saying they're the same universe doesn't really track. If you stick with one canon setting bible for Chronicles of Darkness, you can make a transmedia franchise with it. But you can't then say "in this show, only Demons exist and Vampires are made up" when you've got a video game in supposedly the same universe where Vampires and Demons both exist and interact with each other.

                            I don't know. I actually feel like ChroD is basically the ideal sort of model for a transmedia property. I think that with shared universes, like the one Marvel has developed with their film and TV stable, it's more about the illusion of connectivity than the explicitness of it. I look at the properties within of the MCU as a collection of concentric circles. The Netflix shows make mention of their partner series and the events of the movies, but they're always asides. Iron Man, Thor, and Captain America have their own adventures that can largely ignore one another. It's a collection of plot points that writers and directors can pull from but aren't required to.

                            I think the regional scale of ChroD makes it an ideal fit for that tightrope balance of a shared universe and creative license. You could have a Requiem show set in Mexico City and a Forsaken video game about a pack of werewolves embroiled in the drug wars of Oaxaca (and that's really an awesome game idea), and you never have to worry about the two bumping up against each other. And it won't affect an individual's tabletop game any more than, say, Shadows of Mexico does. If a vicious struggle between vampires and werewolves breaks out in ChroD Seattle, it's not going to greatly affect whatever stories are being told in Phoenix, or Vancouver, or Prague. But if there's a gang war between leeches and lupines in WoD Seattle, there's always that lingering question of why the Technocracy or Camarilla, or any number of other international supernatural organizations, don't get involved.

                            I don't think ChroD is less appropriate for transmedia consumption than WoD because of its modularity. I just think the WoD is more appropriate because of its tone and style. It's more visceral and swaggering, and that makes it more appropriate to big budget aesthetics.
