                            I did a response on the blog, but maybe it's better here, so I'll repeat myself if nobody minds...

                            The Otodo seem pretty interesting, and I'd look forward to using them. That said, what with "dark bloodline", "burning blood in their veins", and "unholy birthright" in a Japanese context, I can't help but think "Chuunibyou" and wonder if there will be a lot of eyepatches.

                            Anyway, someone on the blog side was mentioning that “Seitokuken” isn't really a common Japanese term. I just remember how "majutsu" "魔術" is usually used interchangeably with "magic", but actually contains the character for "demon" in the first half. Some fantasy games, especially in the line of Dragon Quest parodies, tend to make a distinction between "Holy Power" as a good, oppositional force to the always-demon-related "Magic". By that extension, you could go for the fairly literal, "鬼術" "Kijutsu", which is literally "Oni Techniques", but generally basically means sorcery. Alternately, there's "鬼技" "oni-wara", which means something similar, and if you want to get away from overtly saying it's an oni, "鬼血術" "Oni-chijutsu" is "Oni-blood technique", which I don't think exists as a term in Japanese, but would likely be something you could pass off if you explained it.

                            Ultimately, I also have to mention I'd be more than a bit bummed if there weren't any way to play Buddhist priest or Shinto Miko conspiracies. I linked this pdf in a previous thread, as an example, but basically, you could lay the groundwork for a state-sponsored exorcist division. It could have room for both exploring actual historical religious practices, but also it would let me get a few anime-loving friends into playing this game if I convince them that it's basically playing a game of Inuyasha or Touhou or Kekkaishi with more historic connotations.

                                    Well, in that PDF, it has the following:
                                    Tamura Hachidayū’s history and the control of miko
                                    According to a 1684 document penned by Tamura Hachidayū’s occupational precursor Kōwaka Kandayū 幸若勘太夫 (or Kōmatsu Kandayū
                                    幸松勘太夫; the name Kōwaka may have been merely an “artist’s name”), the latter’s ancestor had received an official “vermillion-seal certificate” during the sixth month 1618 from none other than Tokugawa Ieyasu (who had in fact died in 1616). This weighty license supposedly granted Kandayū control of the “dance masters” (mai-dayū) and female “catalpa-bow shamans” (azusa miko) of the eight Kantō provinces, over 600 people in all (Hayashi 1993, 3; for a 1790 listing of villages and number of houses see Hayashi 2003, 64–65). Records from 1800 identify the initial recipient of Ieyasu’s largesse as Kōmatsu Kan’emon 幸松勘右衛門 ; supposedly his son called himself Kandayū. Kan’emon had apparently arrived from Mikawa Province, the ancestral home of the Tokugawa family. Once in Edo, he was allegedly granted a plot of land at Tawara-chō and permitted to supervise the “dance masters” of Edo and the Kantō provinces (Matsudaira 1942, 614).
                                    Again, I think you could set up a secret state-sponsored (or possibly not really state-sponsored, and built upon a forged signature of a dead Shogun - OR WAS HE REALLY DEAD?!) conspiracy using a concept like that.

                                    (The rest of the text goes on to talk about how it was basically a lot of infighting over which favored factions got control over specific branches of the religion, and who got the right to sell specific religious charms at different festivals, but that's just the official history... Multiple rival political families vying for rights over religious factions, whose members were generally fairly fluid, and could shift allegiances to suit their own needs, itself, is something fairly suited to WoD, as well. One group, but the different sub-factions are controlled by rival political families, and are only officially allowed specific rituals.)

                                                Reading it again, one of the other interesting things that is in the "Female Shamans in Eastern Japan" excerpt is that they mention regulations encouraging/enforcing that they intermarry with onmyouji and yamabushi. Presumably in real life, this could be as part of an enforcement of caste that was a huge part of Edo, but since magical powers were supposed to be based upon bloodline, (especially through relations with some sort of divine or divinely-sponsored magical creature,) you could say a similar preservation of magical powers was in effect.

                                                Although it's lower on my personal wishlist than miko and bhikku hunter squads, one of the things about yamabushi is that tengu, in their not-always-evil mythological roles, are often associated with yamabushi. (One very prominent story has a tengu that takes the form of a human to learn their branch of ascetic Buddhism, and in general, they are portrayed as wearing a yamabushi's clothes from then on whenever they aren't the automatically-evil sorts...) Very broadly speaking, I prefer it when the non-humans aren't Always Chaotic Evil, as there's far more storytelling potential for them when one can't be excused for just shooting them first.

                                                ===

                                                Also, to go back to a previous topic that Aspel brought up again, but the backstory on how the oni created a bunch of oni-blooded children really does have the vibe of, "Boy meets monster. Five minutes later, freaky interspecies sex!" Considering how saturated Japanese folklore (and its modern anime interpretations) are with monsters that pretty much overtly are either false-face seductive women or brutes out to "steal our women", (and oni have been both,) it really does beg the question why this one village is the only time such a union ever occurred. Even if we take this as just straight-up a copy-paste of the Lucifuge into another culture, even in the Lucifuge, it reads as the devil manages to keep on getting around.
