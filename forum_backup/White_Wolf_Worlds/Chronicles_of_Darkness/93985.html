                                      Mm, yes, you're correct. My notes say it was the last week of January rather than the first week of February, as I had remembered. But that's seven weeks, not seven months. The editing process is four to six weeks for anything smaller than a 20th anniversary edition. Exact deadlines depend on editor workloads -- like with any other part of the process, we're usually working around day jobs.

                                      In this case, the original editor hit a problem and flipped the book back to Colin, who reallocated the work. We lost a few weeks, but it's moved to art direction now. (The cover was done way early because we were commissioning a lot of work from that artist at the same time.)

                                      Redlining's also different from correcting an academic paper, but the development process is a fairly long thing to explain. Maybe I should do a dev blog describing it from beginning to end.

                                                  Originally posted by Rose Bailey View Post
                                                  Redlining's also different from correcting an academic paper, but the development process is a fairly long thing to explain. Maybe I should do a dev blog describing it from beginning to end.
                                                  Honestly, I think that sort of thing would be a good idea in general. I think a lot of the complaints about development times come from people having an idea of how long the process should take, given their own experience with creating media or similar projects (I've been using Master Degree's for example). So if you explain exactly what goes on in each part and how long those things take on average then people can adjust their mental image of how long some things should take.

                                                  The other issue is that certain projects and lines seem to be much, much quicker through this process (without any comparative drop in quality) then others. For Example:

                                                  Mummy's been out a year in POD basically and 14 months for the Kickstarter backers. It's had a single splat book (Guildhalls) and a couple of Kickstater extra's (Wallpapers, Ready Made Characters, Character Sheets) released in that time.

                                                  Demon's been out to Kickstater backers for about 2 Months. I think the POD's out either end of March or beginning of April. However, it seems that in the next 14 months that its going to get a lot more line development e.g. Players Guide, Heirs to Hell, Demon Seeds, Ready Made Characters, Splintered City: Seattle, Storytellers Guide, Translation Guide, Demon Story Interface. Honestly I can see the majority of these delivered to backers and players in the next 12 months. Considering the majority are already in the redline stage or further at current.

                                                  I think shedding light on the development issue may waylay some peoples thoughts on this issue a bit. Because without any transparency it seems that one of two situations are occurring:

                                                  1) That Onyx Path is supporting certain lines over others. I don't truly believe this, though it is fair to say that White Wolf in the past has tried to give people what they want and some games are just more popular then others e.g. the extra books in Changelings release cycle, the fact that their are more Vampire and Mage books then Werewolf ones.

                                                  2) That one Dev Team/Line Developer is far more hard working/passionate/on the ball when it comes to getting the line he/she is shepherding out then the other one. Which is not a nice impression to give in general I think.

                                                  Obviously then there are the people that realize that maybe the writers on one line just have had better luck and had more time to develop their game and the comparative speeds are nothing that either group has any really control over.

As far as I know it MIGHT be in the writing stage at current, but I think its schedules for the last 3 months or so of 2014 so...yeah.

                                                      I'll add an explanation of the process to my list of dev blogs. Ethan Skemp wrote a fantastic walkthrough back when we were at White Wolf, and many parts of the process are the same as in those days. Unfortunately, I don't have a copy anymore.

                                                      As for how long it takes... truth is, deadlines for each step of an individual project are often set based on the team involved. For example, if I know I can get every member of a team on a book immediately, it might take only two thirds as long as a book where one or more writers has another contract to attend to. In a few cases, I've rescheduled books so that I can work with a writer I specifically need. Another example: the difference between a four week and six week editing job is often the editor's workload rather than the length of the book.

                                                      Add to that schedule slippage. We have only two or three writers or developers who aren't juggling a day job, and most of them have families, too. I'm one of relatively RPG workers I know who's both full time and has no kids. We have been tightening up the schedule and improving our planning -- that's one reason Demon is moving along well. It's still got slips, though -- Vampire, Kickstarters, and The Demon Seed Collection all pushed back the Translation Guide. Even within that, we lose time when somebody's child is sick, or development gets pushed back because another book needs attention, and so on. We don't ruthlessly drop contributors as soon as they ask for an extension -- there were times at other jobs when I've had to do that and I don't like it. All of that goes for artists as well as authors.

                                                      We do our best to be transparent about our workflow. It's easiest and most honest for us to talk about what's going on with a book at this moment (the Monday Meeting update) than anything else. We show our initial target dates and we try to keep up a flow of communication about where each book is between first draft and publication. Explaining in more detail what each of those steps mean is a good idea, though.

                                                      And just to be clear, we haven't moved any resources from Mummy to other lines. The only place Mummy books bump up against other books is in the art queue, and so far I don't think Mummy books have waited any longer for art direction and layout than anything else. (I'd have to double check that with Mike; he schedules and manages all of that after my people have handed off the book.)
