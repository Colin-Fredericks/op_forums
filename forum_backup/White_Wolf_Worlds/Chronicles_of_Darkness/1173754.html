    My Conundrum with Conditions

    01-06-2018, 10:53 AM
    So, I’ve been running a mage game for about a year and a half now. Our sessions are a bit chaotic. We have 5 players, our sessions run for about an hour and a half, and we play just about every other week. Adding to our complication is that we play over Skype and Roll20, so tech problems inevitably interrupt us.

    With all of that, and the complicated mechanics for Mage: the Awakening, I feel like I completely neglect conditions. Which, of course, is upsetting because they’re a major part of the system.

    Does anyone have any tips on using conditions effectively in crazy situations like this? Should I be setting rules for myself, like, at least one condition per player per session? Or should I lean on handing out conditions in narrative situations rather than mechanical ones?

    Also, I suck at improvised conditions, so tips there would be icing on the cake.

    Thanks in advanced for any tips and tricks

      On roll20 the simple solution is to use the note system and transcribe each condition via copy paste. Set them to be visible to the players so they can look at them as needed or just to familiarize themselves.

      As for how often, as often as is appropriate for the narrative. Let's go with the scared condition. They come across a big scarry monster and you call for a Resolve composure roll. Those that succeed maintain their composure but those that fail gain the condition with a dramatic failure giving another stronger condition. So in short, if an effect causes it in it's description or if it makes sense for a general situation to cause it. So, that answers your "narrative or mechanical situation" side of the question with both. Maybe plan for situations where a condition could come up or build a situation from a condition you'd like to show off.

          My advice on existing Conditions is to read the list during your prep time and chose a few to keep in mind. Make cards or notes, anything that makes it easier for you to remember them, and then get used to handling them. Take three to five to begin with, and give them to your PCs whenever you want. Narrative reasons, plot points, make a roll meaningful, anything. If you come across a situation where you think the characters should have a consequence, give a Condition.

          Frequency is a question of how much Conditions improve your game and, on a side note, how often you want your PCs to get XP. A single Condition for the entire group every two sessions is more than enough to keep the game going. But Conditions nudge the PCs into roleplaying consequences, so they can improve your game and be worth more frequency. Finally, they do this through XP, so take primarily into account how fast you want them to grow. If you want a fast pace and a game dense on personal impact over the PCs, you can go as far as a Condition for each PC every scene, unless the PC starts the scene already with a Condition (since it grants Beats only once per scene).

          Improvised Conditions are easier than it seems, just forget the examples given, they aren't meant for improvisation.

          An improvised Condition is made of just two necessary elements: a name and a resolution/beat. You don't need a cause, because you're making the Condition up precisely when the cause occurs, so you actually already came up with it. The Name just gives you a rough idea of what this Condition should represent, so it is also reasonably clear on the spot. The resolution/beat is whatever you want the PC to roleplay at a appropriate time.

          So, for common Conditions, just choose between Automatic Failure, Downgrade Success, Automatic Success or Upgrade Success. Let the rest for your players. When the player chooses to, and before rolling the dice for an appropriate Resolution, the Condition Resolves and the PC takes a Beat. Automatic results forfeit the roll entirely, while Down/Up grade changes as appropriate between Critical Failure - Failure - Success - Exceptional Success. And that's it, just let the player do the job of keeping track of Conditions. You can actually let the players come up with ideas for Conditions, and generally help you with this part. It isn't a problem to let the players have a great deal of say on Conditions.

          You shouldn't think too much about Persistent Conditions, either. Unless use then when you really, really wants to highlight how something lingers over a long time. Anything else, you can just handle the same Condition again if you want to represent that the consequence didn't really went away entirely.
