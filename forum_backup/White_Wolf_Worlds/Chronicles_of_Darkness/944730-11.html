        Agreed about Mage getting exponential growth: raising an Arcanum by one gives immediate access to multiple new spells and a stronger showing (i.e., more free Reach) with all existing spells in that Arcanum. If it's a Ruling Arcanum, the mage also gets a stronger showing (i.e., more free levels in the primary spell factor) with all spells in the Arcanum, both new and established. That's not counting Attainments and Praxes.

        By contrast, in Vampire you need to buy or improve each power you have individually; and you don't get supplemental experiences to speed things up, either, the way mages do. (Exception for when you use the Mage-inspired blood magic system in, I think, Blood and Treachery?, in place of the normal Cruac and/or Theban Sorcery rules.)

        Mage's exponential growth strikes me as the reverse of the issue that 1e Werewolf had, where the mechanics made it difficult for werewolves to advance power-wise. Fortunately, 2e has fixed that problem; but 2e's power development is still mostly linear, with each Facet being acquired individually.

        Promethean powers are weird: every Promethean gets eight powers (i.e., one Alembic from each of two Transmutations, each with an escalating* set of four powers) automatically, and those free powers shift around as the Created changes Refinements. Mastering Roles gives you an additional pair of Alembics (again, one from each Transmutation; eight more powers) whenever you're in that Refinement. Basically, it's a try-before-you-buy setup, with a potentially massive swing in how many and which powers you have access to at any given time. When it comes to actually buying the powers, you never buy them individually; you always buy them in sets of four (i.e., as Alembics) in the form of keeping one of the Alembics you had been trying out when you change things up. (There's also a “magic item” — an Athanor — you can try to make when you master a Refinement that lets you retain access to all of its powers and sundry benefits after you've moved on to your next Refinement.) When actual, irreversible growth occurs, it's linear; but it's a fast linear, four powers at a time. I can actually see a Promethean “keeping up with the mages” in a power escalation race by cycling through the Roles as quickly as possible and locking in new powers as soon as they're acquired. Not that I'm encouraging power escalation races, mind you.

        We've got spoilers on Changeling 2e's Contracts; but I'm not recalling specifics. I think 2e's Contracts would experience linear growth like Disciplines and Gifts, with power buildup occurring one power at a time.

        There are no specifics known about the 2e powers of Hunter or Geist, as those games are too early in the development cycle; and Mummy 2e hasn't even been announced (yet?). But barring radical reenvisioning of their power structures (such as what happened with Promethean), Hunter Endowments will mostly be one-power-at-a-time growth while Geist's Keys and Manifestations will provide multiplicative growth: each Key you acquire unlocks one power per Manifestation that you know, and each Manifestation that you attain grants one power per Key that you have. And Mummy's Descent mechanic means that they deal with an overall power drop over time.

        Demon, Beast, and Deviant: I think all three are one-power-at-a-time growth, with Deviant making it easy to escalate how much a power can do — but due to the link between powers and Scars, that's not a good thing: it's been suggested that such Blowouts tend to end in the Deviant's death.

        Summarizing
        Vampire, Werewolf, Changeling†, Hunter†, Demon, and Beast operate off of “power creep” paradigms, where it's assumed that how much and what your powers can do increases slowly but surely. Mage, Geist†, and Deviant† operate off of various “escalating power” paradigms, where your power level never decreases, and can increase very quickly. Promethean and Mummy† operate off of “fluctuating power” paradigms, where your power level sometimes increases and sometimes decreases.

        I think that tackling the “Mages can do everything others can do better” issue needs to be done, but in the context of these various power development paradigms. Sure, mages escalate quickly; but what does that mean in a Mage/Mummy game where one protagonist's powers are on the rise while another's are in decline? The solution may not be as straightforward as “nerf the Mages!” or “buff everyone else!”

        * That is, how many of these powers you have access to depends on how much Pyros you're willing to invest in the Alembic, from zero to three.
        † Speculative, as the ChroD-compatible editions aren't out yet.

            ​Edit: in light of Dataweaver's post, I'll expand on the Gnosis requirement thing a tiny bit, only looking at Vampires for simplicity: it costs a Mage that starts with a Ruling Arcanum at 3 and Gnosis at 1 48 experiences to take that Ruling Arcanum to 5. To contrast, it costs a Vampire with a starting in clan Discipline at 3, 6 experiences to get it to 5. Mages are also capped in terms of how high they can take their other Arcana without upping Gnosis, Vampires are not; it only costs 40 experiences to increase 2 out of clan disciplines from zero to 5, so our example Vampire can have 3 disciplines at 5 and still be 2 experiences shy of what it cost the Mage to become a Master in one Arcana (or 12 experiences shy if they're in clan instead), it will cost the Mage an additional 17 experiences to get their second ruling Arcana to 5, assuming they started it at 2. Additionally, Mages are the only group that uses a combination of 2 traits for their dice pools, rather than three. Vampires do not use Blood Potency as part of their dice pools to activate powers, so that starting Vampire that threw those first 6 experiences into a Discipline is not significantly impaired by the low BP. Yantras and Arcane Experiences are necessary to mitigate what would otherwise be crippling disadvantages. The effectiveness of increasing a dot of Arcana vs increasing a dot in a Discipline isn't just a matter of what you get, it also matters how much it costs. And Vampire powers are dramatically cheaper; you *should* be getting less for them. (Sidenote: the power increase for Arcana isn't exponential. That means something very specific dammit! It's more arguably logarithmic; going from 1-2 definitely feels like a bigger increase than 4-5, but putting aside the narrative effectiveness of the practices, the increase in mechanical 'stuff' is linear, you get one extra die, one extra reach, etc, every dot you go up. That you get more than you get from other power systems is baked into the higher costs and has nothing to do with the growth curve)

                                                    The problem with mages in a crossover troupe is the threat of overshadowing other PCs. Direct power levels don't really matter so long as every member of the game settles into their own unique niche. An Myst/Acanthus occult investigator, a Rahu/Blood Talon combat monkey, an Invictus/Ventrue social butterfly and a Winter Darkling spy/thief can all get along in the same group and have fun together, so long as we avoid personality clashes and everyone stays in their niche. The problem with mages is that the flexibility of their magic lets them easily enter into combat as an equal to the werewolf, engage in social manipulation on par with the vampire, and can engage in rogue operations equal to the changeling. The mage ends up dominating the group as a result in such a case. Our theoretical diviner, once they have enough experience, can effectively shut down any combat, manipulate people effectively with Blessings and Curses, and use magic to supplement any covert operations. With Eregore, our witch can even just rent magical items instead of learning the appropriate Arcana themselves!

                                                    When one PC has the answer to everything, it makes everyone else in the group feel redundant and overshadowed. And that's a huge problem here. All social groups, from ants to human workforces to wolves in a hunt, to animals. We are drawn to establish niches that can contribute to the group as a whole. Mages, by virtue of their flexible powers, tend to break from the standard niches, which is the heart of the problem in this case. Others have stated that, when the mage tend to step back and get less involved in the games, everyone has fun in the games. This isn't because mages are less involved, but rather that the mage player is helping to reinforce the niches instead of breaking them. And that's not an easy player skill to cultivate.

                                                    Now, crossover troupes aren't the only kind of kind of crossover. I tend to create four levels:
                                                    • Crossover troupes, where everyone is a different supernatural
                                                    • Antagonism between splats (ie Hunters versus anyone else, vamps v. wolves, etc)
                                                    • Casual interaction (ie Beasts making friends with everyone)
                                                    • Cameos (other supernaturals show up for a brief plot in some role, then vanish)
                                                    We already talked about the full crossover troupes. Now, lets look at the other three. In a cameo, its not really an issue, because the ST has enough control over the NPCs in either case that it won't affect anything long term. Casual interaction and antagonism is where the issues lie.

                                                    Mages in casual interaction can be problematic because they have the insatisfiable desire to know. For non-mage PCs, this can be upsetting, because they generally have no way of blocking a mage's abilities to learn stuff, even private matters, without some rather extreme responces (which ups the interaction to antagonism). This is why I agree with the earlier statement about mages being considered invasive. Because with a mage, your expectations of privacy are only as good as the mage's morals, or maybe a good Social Maneuvering build. Meanwhile, from the other side? Mage PCs get to be all invasive to their heart's content, which I personally think should be a Wisdom breaking point. Its actually a good source of Arcane beats, which encourages you to go deeper and deeper into the issue. The problem I have is that I think gathering the info is too easily done, and needs rather creative STs to come up with appropriate measures to engage the plot without devolving into an antagonistic relationship.

                                                    An antagnoistic relationship is where things go to hell in a handbasket. The Arcana is simply designed to ignore the basic defensive abilities of supernaturals. This is a design decision for how mages think about other mages as antagonists. Which is more important for a game, but it completely messes with any and all crossover assumptions and challenges. Other supernaturals need to try to attack mages from the holes in their considerable defenses with clever results, while a mage can use direct attacks if they so wish. Victory may not be a given, but there's definitely a major bias towards mages as a result of the game's design. I believe it was DaveB even said that mages are inappropriate enemies for the Vigil, and you should use Witches instead. I think the same logic applies to other supernaturals. If your specific table doesn't have a problem, great. That doesn't help that there are a notable number of tables that do, and that will need to be addressed in any crossover book.

Prometheans, with Disquiet and Wastelands, tend towards short lived interactions before they have to move on. Even Silver Refeinement isn't meant to be a long term crossover game, and that's if the Refinement comes up, and if the Refinement targets something other than spirits and ghosts as valid targets instead of something more crossover-y. And it should be noted that Silver's signature Transmutation tends towards magic to face off against other supernaturals antagonistically, or hide/run away from them, rather than encourage interaction. With Vampire? The issue here is daylight restrictions, which is actually a very significant hurdle back in 1e to the point of inplayability in many games. I haven't tried it in 2e yet, so I can't comment about the new daylight rules. Both of these are hurdles that need to be overcome for a crossover game to be successful.

                                                    With werewolves and chagnelings, a heavy issue is the presence of the Hisil and Hedge, respectively. The heavy influence of these other worlds practically requires any crossover to be able to deal with them as well to an extent if there's any significant interaction. There's nothing more anticlimatic than a werewolf dealing with a vampire infestation problem simply by making a deal with Helios spirits to do everything for them and the vamps having no recourse. Or having a demon exposed by asking spirits who happen to follow the spy.

                                                    The Hunter problem is, generally speaking, the fact that hunters tend to have other supernaturals as enemies. Being allies means you're a cancer cult. Demons risk exposure and basically dying out from angels just from having a troupe and acting out of character.

                                                    And so on. Mage is a complex game, and that means we get complex problems with it.
