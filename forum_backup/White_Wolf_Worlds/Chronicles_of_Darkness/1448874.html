    Modern World Building

    05-18-2021, 12:19 PM
    I've been running games for twenty years. I don't like to use much in the way of preexisting material.
    But the task of penning down the details of a modern-world setting from the ground up feels like a whole different animal compared to a homebrewed setting for D&D or the like.

    I've been sticking to small cities and towns for my stories, because they feel more manageable. I also avoid using locations that my players know well, personally (I am just terrible with maps, road systems and all that).

    How do you folks go about building a world for a modern setting? Do you know who the mayor and the chief of police are? Do you map out major highways so characters can say things like "it's just West of I-94"? If anyone has any example settings they'd be willing to share, that would be great.

          Yeah, due to Covid I've been play over Roll20, I generally take a screen shot of google maps over the city or location i want to use. and use major landmarks while fictionalizing may of the other details. It's nice to know the major roads and the general districts, failing that i often fall back on using The Docks, Downtown, Uptown and the Suburbs as broad and generic city locations.
          Even with a map on screen I don't track specific movement throughout the city, unless it's important. I keep my descriptions in second person and play from scene to scene.

          I have never had need of a cities mayor or police chief, Although in a mortal city level political game i could see the need for them. but I'll always detail a pair of detectives to investigate the players potential crimes. I also like to detail a few characters for each faction within whatever splat we're playing and maybe cross splat characters if the plot requires. typically the local head of the faction, The face of the faction and a specialist. I also choose a location for each factions base of operations within the city.

          I like to have a little list of people around town to inject random encounters or vignettes. These often are location specific; maybe a raving homeless man on the church steps rambling borderline incoherent prophecies, or teenage graffiti artist who could be tagging the players home base or is just out late and could spot or bump onto the PCs, maybe an aggressive patrol officer, or a serial arsonist who bumps into the PCs smelling of lighter fluid and than latter that night the players encounter a burning building.
          Just things it keep the city feeling alive, dangerous and mysterious.

          After all that I focus on plot relevant characters and things tied directly to the PCs backstories.

                      But with a modern day setting, I really struggle to get to that same level. I'd really like to replicate the way people in a town talk with each other about current events and places, but it feels so much harder to fake.
                      If you set your game in a small town where everyone knows everyone, then you can get a setting that's closer to the one you're used to. If you set your game in a big city, you'll likely have a crowd of strangers ignoring each other.
                      I'd say it depends a lot on what kind of themes and mood you are looking for. The small remote village may give a sense of isolation, while the big city may evoke some kind of claustrophobic mood, or a bit of paranoia (anyone in this crowd of strangers could be working for your enemies). In a way, the way you portray your town is a consequence of the theme and mood you wish to set.

                      Also, keep in mind that most supernatural creatures have their own communities which are MUCH smaller than the town they live in. Even in a big city you may have all the vampires/mages etc. know each other and fewer events and places to track, so it's easier to manage.
                      You may focus more on who's who and what's happening in the supernatural scene and less on the rest of the town/population. It's more important to flesh out the Vampire Prince of the city than its mayor. And it's easier to know what are the latest news every Kindred is talking about in the local Elysium.
                      Think about a couple of NPCs for each faction, for e.g. a low-Status and a high-Status one., and what are their current conflicts and goals to make the setting feel alive.

                      Even if you want to play a vanilla game with mortals only, the same concept applies. Think about it: you may know every gossip about your workplace or school, but nothing about what happens in the rest of your town.

                      Personally, I've been using existing cities as settings. Since I'm not familiar with them, I spend a lot of time on the web, looking at places through Street View to get a bit of a feel of it, searching its history, and stuff like "100 things to do in city X" or "100 weird things you can find in city X" to get interesting story hooks or inspiration (and regularly find out that reality is stranger than fantasy). Some places you can easily pick and paste from elsewhere as well.

                      Originally posted by 21C Hermit View Post
                      I hear Damnation City, a supplement for Vampire 1E, is good for this stuff, though I haven’t read it myself yet.
                      Yes, it's worth looking at. It gives advices on how to build your own city by putting together and describing its districts and quarters, and a little bit on how to "roleplay" the city.
                      To put it shortly: you think about a district or neighborhood, give it a general vibe like a theme or concept or Virtue/Vice, and perhaps a key NPC.
                      My issue with that book is that, despite being useful material for any kind of game, it's still too much Vampire-focused for my taste.
                      There's also Block by Bloody Block which is Hunter-themed and more focused on "this district is controlled by this monster, you need to defeat it/ally with it to gain access to the district's resources".
                      I wish there was a more general purpose CofD book for city-building, with advices for all splats.

                              However, what I meant to say is that it would be nice to have a book that not only gives you building blocks and advice to use and run them (which Damnation City and Block by Bloody Block already do) but also gives advices, story hooks etc. depending on the splat you're using, all in one place.

                              What I mean is something like this:

                              * Theater District / Location: the Old run-down Theater *
                              [some general description of the location ]

                              Location Example: The Old Theater was established a hundred years ago, but now it's run-down because of the competition of latest cinemas. Recently, it seems to have a rat problem.
                              Sample NPC: John, the creepy janitor
                              Story Hooks:
                              * General: the theater is haunted by a ghost
                              * Vampire: John is actually a Ghoul, who's breeding rats on purpose
                              * Werewolf: The rats are actually a swarm of Beshilu
                              * Mage: when projected, the movies seem to have a subliminal mesmerizing effect on the audience, resonant with the Mind Arcanum. It's a Mystery with Opacity 3
                              * Changeling: there's a dormant Hedge portal leading to a 3-dot Trod.
                              * Demon: John is actually a Stigmatic
                              etc.

                              The idea is to have several filters, which you can choose to turn on and off, depending on the splat you're running and how much crossover do you want your setting to be. Notice how some story hooks may be mutually exclusive, the idea being to use only a few of them at the same time. If all filters were "On", the location would be, like, the nexus of all things supernatural, which would shatter credibility and suspension of disbelief (but I guess it depends on taste).

                              Because some game lines have had less attention/development that others, I feel like there is a lack of world-building material for, say, Changeling, Geist, etc. I don't expect to have a whole dedicated sourcebook for each splat, so I guess it would be nice to put everything in an all-purpose book.
                              So, besides story hooks, also some lore and game mechanics could be "filtered".
                              For e.g. * Changeling: In the Theater District rolls to harvest Glamour have +1 dice

                                  Personally, I like to give things a "inspiration, not law" treatment: research as much as I feel I need to, make up the rest and outright ignore reality if it's in the way of a good game. If neither of you have been there, it doesn't really matter if you get all the street names right, or if it is a truly faithful representation (unless that's specifically what you are going for).
                                  Pick a place, take a map, follow the above advice given on googling useful facts, pick landmarks and stranger-than-fiction stuff which is excellent advice (did you know Cedar City has a landlocked lighthouse? Some say it's in preperation for when an earthquake sinks most of california, leaving it the last lighthouse standing on the new coast line. I couldn't make this up!) and go from there.
                                  Would it serve your story better if the landmark in question was older? Make it so. Maybe it should still be in the possession of the originators' families? Done. Wanna shift some districts? Go ahead. Increase or decrease crime rates, change the history, introduce new industry, change the climate, landscape and vegetation where needed... If you start with a strong feeling for the place, some background knowledge and a vision for the game, it should turn out alright (or maybe I just have low standards, that's quite possible).

                                  I also find movies to be a nice resource, because it often better transports a feeling for the places and gives some (albeit occasionally very stereotypical) examples for the people inhabiting it (" I'm walking here!").
                                  If you want to avoid big city names like New York or Las Vegas, why not talking a look at the closer area and neighbouring cities? For a cross-splat game I participated in, we picked Cedar City (among other reasons because it was close to Las Vegas) and turned it into a bigger and conflicted city with a highly active criminal underworld, having undergone a catastrophic event some years past that struck the supernatural communities very hard, but still very distinct from Las Vegas itself (it culture and it's people had an influence, but not an overpowering one). I doubt we portrayed Cedar City authentically, but that wasn't the point - we just wanted a semi-familiar setting with enough proximity to big cities to create plausible situations (in a handwaving, suspension of disbelief-way).

                                  From what you said, you're mostly simply very familiar with fantasy settings, and very used to building them to the point of it being natural. But when you started out, things presumably involved more work, and it sounds like this is what you are thrown back into to a certain degree.

                                  Although I am curious: did you have huge cities in your fantasy settings? How did they differ in your opinion from cities like New York, Atlanta, London, Berlin, Providence or Baltimore?

                                          Originally posted by Quixote View Post
                                          The more I think about it, the less my issues are with world building and the more they have to deal with my own personal difficulties in processing certain kinds of information.
                                          Specifically, things like road systems and bus schedules are an absolute nightmare for me. I don't know why, but it's always been that way. I just can't digest that sort of information as it's presented in a way that is useful to me.
                                          In that case, quick advice: keep it simple.
                                          There's a lot of information you don't need to deal with for storytelling. You're not playing at simulating the real world.
                                          When in doubt or having difficulties, don't be afraid to apply Movie Logic.

                                          Your PCs need to go somewhere on the other side of New York? They do. How? It doesn't matter. Just skip to the next scene when they arrive, because that's what a movie would do.
                                          Travel time can be roughly ignored, even if it takes several hours to go through all NY, unless your story has a time constraint (like dawn for vampires).
                                          Similarly, unless you want to play a car chase, it doesn't even matter whether your players travel by car or subway.

                                          Sometimes you don't even need to know exactly where someplace is. If the players ask Bob where he lives, you can simply answer "Bob gives you his address". In my experience, a lot of times that will suffice, because players will likely forget it's between "Main and 3rd". They'll remember it as Bob's house. They'll remember it by its most memorable qualities, like "the house with the all pink interiors".
                                          If you really need to set your location somewhere, just think in terms of districts and neighborhoods, like "it's in the slums" or "in the financial district", which is exactly what Damnation City and Block by Bloody Block do.

                                          As Wormwood said, if you're taking inspiration from real places, feel free to modify it as you like. If your city doesn't have a subway but you want to include it because it's cool, you don't have to design all the lines and stations. The subway will be wherever the Plot needs it to be.

                                          I think that, since you're coming from D&D, you have a lot of experience in making the game feel immersive and the world feel realistic, which is necessary for medieval fantasy settings. But in modern world rpgs, there is less need for immersion because the setting is one the players are already familiar with. Instead, because modern world can be very complex, it's better to ignore some details to keep the game going and not slow it down.

                                                  I think I get where you are coming from. I like to have things planned out in a way they make sense to me, so that the setting feels logical, coherent and alive to me (and hopefully, by extension, the players).
                                                  Even if some stuff is unlikely to ever come up in play, but gives me a better feel for the game, I'll go for it, and if the players ask about it, I can come up with something fitting on the fly, and very often one thing will lead to the entirely unrelated next (You start with the division of districts and most important landmarks, and end up with a human traffiking circle of grannies who abuse the fact that senior citizens living in the heavily priviliged gated communities enjoy reduced scrutiny due to everything made to keep unwanted attention out).

                                                  As for different kinds of approaching this, I too had trouble with keeping some stuff memorized, city mapping was often ridiculously hard to keep in my head, too. I like organisms, though, so I turn the city into analogues to keep stuff semi-logical and in my head. A beating heart with pulsating veins to represent traffic and transport of goods, organs representing important parts of the city (liver -> where most of the police force is, damaged if there's a lot of corruption, stomach-> all kinds of markets, intestines -> binmen and all things waste-disposal related etc), the actions of the characters resulting in damage, prevention of damage, perhaps even healing (unlikely though). The resulting shape doesn't always make biological sense, but it helps me keep track in a simple and quick way. The fact that the city is essentially some sort of visceral setting in my head is just a happy byproduct.
