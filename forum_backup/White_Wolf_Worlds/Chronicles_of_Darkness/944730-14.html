        The only issue of balance that is really important in games is between players and it isn't a matter of mechanics, it is a matter of focus. It is about how the Storyteller runs the game. For example, you have a group with two players. One has a character focused on social skills and powers and the other on combat skills and powers. The latter character can beat the crap out of the former one, but that ultimately doesn't matter in terms of game balance. What matters is that the game she runs has social stuff for the first character to do and combat stuff for the second character to do. This holds true whether all the characters are werewolves or if they are different sorts of creatures. The trick is running a campaign that makes sure that every character, regardless of the type or their abilities, has moments to do their thing and be in the limelight. If your two players are playing a vampire and a werewolf, you have to make sure that neither Machiavellian scheming nor spirit cop plots dominate (yes, I'm over-simplifying). Whether or not the vampire can beat up the werewolf or vice versa isn't really important.

        Power disparities will exist and these will inform conflicts and, hence, plots. Look at Hunter. Nobody thinks Hunters should be as powerful as vampires. The fact that they aren't is part of who they are and what makes the game interesting. Anyway, that's just my two cents but it is worth noting that I ran a World of Darkness MUSH back in the day with characters of pretty much every splat that existed at the time, so I know of what I speak. Two of my favorite plots during the life of the MUSH illustrate this. One involved a vampire hunter with no powers managing to assassinate several Kindred without getting caught and another involved a regular human stealing away with his Kinfolk daughter so she would be raised by the local Garou. In both cases, thorough and creative planning let them succeed. This is why the white room analysis that compares base powers isn't really useful, because it removes the role-playing element of the game and treats it like a war game or board game.

        Okay, going to stop talking now. Rambling.

            I don't like this approach, because it's based on idea that players are
            a) working as a team that delegates character roles (party face, party muscle, etc) [I'm biased here; we play a lot of games in a fantasy samurai setting where characters are often facing conflicting loyalties, and must handle discreet manners that you simply can't trust to someone outside of your family; you can't just go and ask your party face to negotiate a delicate deal that your father ordered you to oversee, for example; this means that characters can't "cover" for each other as much as in things like DnD; this type of play seems to be pretty popular in Chronicles, too)
            b) reduced to "social guy", "muscle guy", "mental guy"
            I dislike this design, because it basically means that when social encounter comes up, 3 out of 4 people are unable to engage with it competently; and so on. One of reasons why I really like CofD is that it's really easy and pretty encouraged to make characters that are competent in all 3 basic types of encounters (mental, social, physical) so unless you did so on purpose, you pretty much always can engage in given type of encounters. Which makes optimal use of our time.
            And while yeah, you "physically focused character" will get 11 points to spread around while someone else will only get 4, it doesn't mean that the last guy is drastically worse; usually it means that the first guy has more possible approaches, while second one has less possible roads to success; character with 11 in social skills can bribe, intimidate, persuade or trick their way out of an encounter; character with 4 points probably can only do one or two of these.

                Yeah I have to agree with WHW. Most of the time in Chronicles of Darkness, you make a character that fulfills a concept, not a party role like in other games. If a high society character's abilities also happen to make them an expert at maneuvering through street level issues, well...

                    Here is an example of invasive special abilities clashing with concepts of other characters:
                    In our current game, we have a person who decided that they want to be "know it all" type with massive scores of Occult and other mental knowledge skills. However, due to my character being a Fate Mage, I've discovered that I make him totally irrelevant and redundant - my magic can easily overcome the gap between our skills, and even without that, I'm simply better at "knowing stuff" because I can gain Informed Condition on demand using Exceptional Luck - which means that as long as I get 1 success on my Occult Roll, I actually get Exceptional Success guaranteed, which he - even with Occult of 5 - isn't producing in consistent manner. There is pretty much no point to his character theme, and choices he made at char gen are irrelevant, because my set of magic abilities overwrites it. In this case, it's a clear mechanical disbalance, not a Focus one. It's also pretty annoying to *me*, because I feel an obligation to self restrain.

                        I actually solved this same exact problem in one of my games:

                        "Oh, an exceptional success! You happen into book that happens to describe exactly what the phenomenon is...except then you start reading it, and it suddenly occurs to you it's invoking several obscure pieces of lore you have no freaking idea about. You simply don't have the expertise required to make sense of this, luck or no luck. Gained the Informed Condition in respect to indexing this particular library, but you're going to need help in decipher this thaumobabble."

                        Yes, it relies on bending the rules a bit, but someone who doesn't have that high a Skill score is going to find truly impossible feats...impossible, because they don't have the background. Suggest the houserule 'It is forbidden to roll to decipher something I can't think of a reason I'd know how to decipher'. Even beyond that, someone with Occult 5 is going to be regarded as a prodigy-give the poor guy some free Contacts symbolizing students and would-be mystics who respect him (he doesn't have the Fame Merit because he doesn't have popular recognition). If he can't be the roll-monkey, then give him fluff buffs that come from other parts of the character concept.

                        Yes, it's the Oberoni Fallacy (a broken rule you can houserule to fix is still broken), but the reason that fallacy exists is because it's what is meant to happen when the game group is adjusting a game to be more fun for the group.

                        (It's also what Onyx Path has in mind when they say that fluff comes before rules, I think-because rules are built around fluff).
