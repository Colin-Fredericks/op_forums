                            Given the descriptions, I'd say you have leeway either way, but I'd disagree with Hardwire.

                            1. I'd say that Ghost Gate, in its non-Reach form, can trap someone indefinitely. Its Reach form cannot. The reason for this is in the structure of the spells; Ghost Gate by itself is a spell which creates a transformative zone that rewrites a part of whatever passes through it, from "Material" to "Ephemeral". The Gate itself is the spell's creation but the effects of that Gate are not. The same is true for Co-Location, which temporarily rewrites a section of the world so that anything within it has its location rewritten from "Here" to "There". Once the spell lapses, the changes inflicted are not undone.

                            However, the +1 Reach version of Ghost Gate is different; there, the target is that thing which Ghost Gate's regular version rewrites and, like all such magic, its effects are temporary. There, once the Duration wears out, the subject plops back into meatspace.

                            2. Presumably, all mundane objects are destroyed, but bringing them back through the Ghost Gate seems like it'd reverse the process. Artifacts, I feel, are a special class of thing, which cannot be destroyed by this process. Forced into a Twilight state, yes, but... Still themselves. Retrieve it with a spell and it's still an Artifact, not merely an ephemeral copy.

                            3. You can make another Ghost Gate, walk in, walk out. Or you can cast a spell to pull it through as ephemera, in which case it's more prone to breakage. Either works.

                                                        Originally posted by Mrmdubois View Post
                                                        Fair enough, but I think that having the person themselves still ought to be able to fill in on the Temporal Sympathy for being a Symbol of their younger selves, right?
                                                        Not in a meaningful sense — again, step outside the confines of the example for a moment: try applying this logic to things that aren't people.

                                                        The main intersection of my hometown has remained the same intersection for as long as I've lived there, but if I were to show it as it is now to myself fifteen years ago, I would barely recognize it, because the pavement, the street signs, the local businesses, the buildings, and intersection across the bridge have all changed so dramatically that I'm actually having trouble remembering what parts of the walk-up looked like back then. Even if I did have a clear memory of the old layout, I certainly wouldn't consider the current one symbolically relevant to the old one.

                                                        This is to say nothing of how semiotic quandaries like Your Grandfather's Axe interact with treating something's present self as an unqualified symbol of all of its past selves — you may still be driving "the same" car that you got in college, but the muffler replacement you got last August isn't going to help you restore the vehicle to the way it was when it was hot off the lot.

                                                        Symbolism requires significance.
