                            I think for me, part of my current reservations about the WoD revival, and my irritation about Shane's comments (and I know, I know, it's probably a PR move and an appeal to the core fanbase) is that the WoD was so important and formative in my adolescence. Like a lot of things tinged with nostalgia, that leaves it sort of trapped in amber.

                            I found a copy of the Vampire: The Masquerade core book (1st edition, I believe) in my best friend's older brother's room. I was in fifth grade, right in that transitional phase between being a child and growing into a teenager. And frankly, the jagged drawings and devil-may-care demeanor seemed so transgressive to me. I was familiar and briefly intrigued with Dungeons and Dragons, and I loved the potential there, but my flirtation with traditional fantasy was brief, and the crunchiness of the rules was just too much for me to overcome. Elves and dwarves and superheroes seemed like kiddie stuff to me. The whole DnD Satanism scare was still bubbling in the background (and I grew up to a fairly progressive family in the South), but this was the first thing that seemed edgy to me. It seemed dangerous. It was my first exposure to fantasy-horror, and it went hand in hand with my discovery of punk music. It was something to spark my creative instincts without seeming too dorky. In retrospect, a lot of the world building was cheesy, but I gobbled up every piece of it, and I think for a lot of kids my age, it hit all the right notes in their teenage angst. Really looking at it, it's the same sort of world building you see in a high fantasy or sci-fi setting, but that fusion of the modern world with fantasy and the attitude of the writers (along with katanas and manwolves and trenchcoats) just seemed like the epitome of cool to me, and I think it served as a sort of anchoring element for a lot of kids figuring themselves out. I remember talking about these games with gutter punks and theater kids and bohemian kids. I remember playing a game of Werewolf with an older group of stoner skaters. It was the perfect place and the perfect time, and it intersected with everything that seemed cool to a 14/15/16 year old kid growing up in the early 90s. It seemed like it was saying something important, and my memories of friendships, first girlfriends, and music are all tangled up in the game. Eventually I moved on to other things, and my memory of the cWoD is still heavily shaded with those feelings of nostalgia. I can look back at it now and see the things that seem either dated or just outright cheesy, but it's something that always brings about a tingly feeling in my heart. I'm curious to know if people who grew up on Chronicles feel the same way. It doesn't seem like it's as firmly rooted in the zeitgeist of its time, but then, I wasn't a growing boy in that time, so I can't especially say.

                            So that's the perspective I come from when I hear these guys talk. It's just that nothing they've announced seems to reflect the desire to capture the die hard fans who have stayed with the game for decades (not that there's anything wrong with that), but those comments seem so insulting to the writers and editors who spent over a decade developing these games. I remember flipping through a few of these books a year or two after the CoD was launched. It didn't do much for me then. But now I'm in my early 30s now. ChroD doesn't grip me the way that WoD did then, but that's fine. Hell, you don't feel anything as strongly as you did when you're an angry, horny, impressionable teen. And ChroD is exactly what I want. I don't doubt it wouldn't exist without WoD blazing the trail, but in so many ways, it's developed on those core storytelling values in a natural and organic way. I like my stories leaner and with more player agency now, but I don't deride people who feel differently about it. And I just worry about how well, in the long term, ChroD will stay afloat. In the hypothetical situation where WoD becomes a worldwide phenomena, will the higher ups decide that OPP's games are diluting the brand? Or will they try to fold it back into their corporate umbrella?
