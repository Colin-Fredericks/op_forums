        Vampires harm people by their very existence. The LS and the Crones are the two Covenants that address this the most (the rest just sort of ignore it, or see it as a byproduct), albeit in very different ways.

        The Crones say you're a monster, and that it's not just OK- it's great! You need to embrace it, enjoy it, love it. You need to throw away the person you were and accept that you're something different, and much more horrifying. But not everyone is cool with being a monster. A lot of people, especially newer vampires, are going to be very reluctant to completely abandon the idea that they're...well, a person.

        That's where the Lances come in. They don't sugarcoat it: you're a monster. But, you're a monster with a purpose. You have to hurt people to live, yes, no getting around that. But you don't have to like it, and you can rest assured that it's all for a good cause. That's a pretty comforting message for someone who is coming to grips with being a walking corpse, but still strongly remembers their life as a human.

                Also, I think you're wrong about the Circle of the Crone vs the Ordo Dracul, OP. Almost all the source material for Vampire the Requiem makes it pretty clear the main rivalry is between the Circle of the Crone and the Lancea et Sanctum. The Circle of the Crone doesn't have an inherent issue with the Ordo Dracul, while the Lancea et Sanctum thinks the Ordo Dracul's doctrine is just insulting to God.

                In my opinion, if you want the Lancea et Sanctum to be cool, turn them into bad-ass crusaders who fight the pagan Acolytes (Circle of the Crone). The Invictus is all about political law and order. The Lancea et Sanctum is all about religious law and order. Together they oppose the Carthian Movement and the Circle of the Crone, who are more on the chaotic end of the spectrum (the Carthians do have their Carthian law, but they're vastly less strict and rigid than the Invictus, while the Circle of the Crone are often straight-up anarchists).

                The Lancea et Sanctum are also there to attend to the spiritual needs of the Kindred. They can act as a support network where Kindred can find shelter and where they can confess their sins without having to fear potential backlash (the Lancea et Sanctum takes confessions very seriously, perhaps even more so than the Catholic church!). Players who want to play the wise or altruistic vampire, might find the Lancea et Sanctum appealing for this reason.

                            Your problem with the Archbishop from Damnation City is that he was written up to be a source of conflict to drive a story, not an ally or role model, just like all of the other examples of potential Princes. This is a problem with World of Darkness and Chronicles, they are written up to be dark and often forget that you need points of light to emphasis the darkness.
                            If you want the Lance to play a role that isn't solely hostile, a threat to your players, then focus on them from a different angle.
                            The Lance wants you to join but they aren't going to force you, after all borrowed light is worthless when true night falls and they have more important things to do than try to rehab unwilling addicts. Instead play them up as the people who believed that Vampires are still people, in spite of being monsters, because they still have a place in God's world/plan. They are the ones who take the newly embraced and tell them that all is not lost, that they don't have to lose themselves to the beast and treat humanity as little more than cattle and play things.
                            Instead of turning them into the destroyers of innocence, instead play them as the hunters of the monsters who threaten God's innocent, not the creepy pedophile but the slayer of said abuser. Play them as the covenant that seeks a place in the light, instead of as hate mongers of all who are different. There role is to punish the wicked, giving the good an honest chance to prove themselves and overcome.
                            This is easiest if you provide an evil for them to focus on, something your players aren't going to empathize with and yes that can be any of the other covenants. They are all written up with the option of being inhuman monsters out to grind your players down and destroy whatever they love.

                            If that doesn't work, give them Katanas and dress them up as naughty nuns and Alucard from Hellsing. Throw in the occasional orgy (of violence that is) and they will be 'cool'.

                            I just wanted to add that if you and your players all decide that there is still no way you can ever like the Lance then that's okay to, every chronicle needs its bad guys.

                                            Originally posted by Master Aquatosic View Post
                                            I ask this to get an insight into your mind: given that they are also loosely based on a real religion, namely Neo-paganism, what makes the Circle of the Crone cool to you?
                                            It's similar to why the Gangrel are cool. There's a chthonic, primal flavor to the Circle - underneath all their superficial ceremony, there's something lurking that's ancient and alien and poorly understood even by the Kindred. (Note how vague the books are about the source of the power behind Cruac, for example, or the story hooks in 2e teasing a shared root with the Strix.) That juxtaposition between ancient, forgotten mysticism and a modern setting is thematically rich and has a lot of room for horror.

                                            The Circle is also easier to work with just because their religious connections are so tenuous. Yeah, they're generically neo-pagan, but there's not really a specific doctrine they espouse - as the book says, they're a highly varied bunch, a loose mish-mash of whatever traditions the local Kindred favor, and specific groups can have wildly different structures. There's more room to work with them without inadvertently making some real-world religious statement.
