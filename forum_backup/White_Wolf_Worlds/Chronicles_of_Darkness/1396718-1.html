                    As someone who has spent the past couple of years running relatively rules-light PbtA games (lots of Monsterhearts for instance!), coming back to my old NWoD favourites was like a punch in the face in terms of complexity.

                    The base die system is a bit hefty, but not too bad on its own. But all the subsystems really add up - and while some might be tagged as optional, many of the big ones (Combat, Integrity) are not. And once you get into splats... Damn, Vampire has Frenzy, Predatory Auras, Touchstones, Feeding, etc, etc even before you get into things specific to individual characters like powers or merits.

                    I think the first step in taking CoD from "rules medium" to "rules light" would be abandoning the general assumption that if something changes how things work in the fiction, it must also have some mechanical impact or a system for it. But that's a big part of the basic assumptions for simulationist games like CoD, and it'd really really change the feel if one did so.

                        Originally posted by Wade L View Post
                        But all the subsystems really add up - and while some might be tagged as optional, many of the big ones (Combat, Integrity) are not.
                        Integrity isn't optional, but it's hardly a hefty system. Roll Resolve + Composure +/- modifiers, apply the results based on the normal result options. It's slightly more than a page for the description and mechanics of it, and most of that is a table of suggested/sample modifiers. Though different gamelines have different Integrity equivalents which do range a bit in terms of complexity.

                        Combat is at least optional in the sense of there are two core combat systems (assuming you even get into combat) to pick from. You can always run a game where all combat is Down & Dirty and then skip over the big full combat rules: just tell your players so they don't invest in stuff that's not going to come up (incidentally also cutting down on Merit bloat a lot).

                        And once you get into splats... Damn, Vampire has Frenzy, Predatory Auras, Touchstones, Feeding, etc, etc even before you get into things specific to individual characters like powers or merits.
                        Even PbtA games suffer this though via playbooks. Yes, PbtA is a lighter system, but that doesn't stop Witches in Monsterhearts from having a unique game mechanic via Tokens to spend on Hexes; before you get to every character type's unique Moves. And Monsterhearts, while an awesome game, is also a much more narrow game in terms of scope. It can stay as light as it does because it doesn't try to do as much. If you expanded it out to as many different game play scenarios as the CofD covers, you'd see the same creeping up in complexity that adding more playbooks does.

                        Regardless of how light the core of the game engine is, and how much the designers want to keep it that way in practice, if you want to have a horde of different supernatural critters, and make them all feel unique in play, you're going to add complexity to the system. If you're going to add more viable playstyles and game scenarios, you're going to add complexity to the system.

                        I think the first step in taking CoD from "rules medium" to "rules light" would be abandoning the general assumption that if something changes how things work in the fiction, it must also have some mechanical impact or a system for it. But that's a big part of the basic assumptions for simulationist games like CoD, and it'd really really change the feel if one did so.
                        This seems... backwards to me. When things change how things work in the fiction is precisely where the focus of the mechanics should be. Again, this is the whole point of having playbooks with unique moves in PbtA games. On archetype does things differently than another, so they get different mechanics to represent that.

                        If you want to trim down the rules, it makes the most sense to start with all the rules that don't or redundantly represent state changes; hence things like reducing Merit and Condition bloat as a common refrain. Also things like standardizing mechanics more: if most supernatural shave an "aura" mechanic (Predatory Aura, Hunter Aspect, Flaring Nimbus, etc.) there should be a more standardized mechanic for it where the differences in accessing it are more like different Moves than distinctly different subsystems.

                        This really isn't about "narrative vs. simulation" or whatever. It's about how bespoke the design is supposed to be. The more bespoke the gameplay experience, the easier it is to keep the rules light. The more the group is expected to take a more general system and pick and choose what parts they're going to want, the heavier the rules get; esp. if the designers want you to be able to shift the tone of the game over time. If you just want to play Monsterhearts, Monsterhearts is better than CofD cut down to what you need to run Monsterhearts. If you want to start with Monsterhearts, and then transition to a mix of Monster of the Week and Urban Shadows.. well then CofD looks a lot more appealing as is.

                        It's easy to talk about how rules light a specific PbtA game is, because a designer has already trimmed things down to a specific and highly focused gameplay experience.

                        If you tried to make a "PbtA Core" with everything you need to play Apocalypse World, Monsterhearts, Masks, Urban Shadows, Ruma, the Sprawl, and so on with one book? It would get a lot bigger and a lot more full of "optional" rules as it would grow to at least look like Fate Core or Cortex Prime.

                        And idea of making the CofD lighter by making it more bespoke is a bad one. Bespoke games already exist, and are great, and there's no need to remove things some CofD fans like to become more like them to compete for the people already playing them. The CofD core appeal is it's toolkit approach that's in the middle between highly bespoke systems and more generic systems. Pitching more rules light approaches to the CofD need to maintain that part of the brand identity to really have value.

                            Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post

                            This really isn't about "narrative vs. simulation" or whatever. It's about how bespoke the design is supposed to be. The more bespoke the gameplay experience, the easier it is to keep the rules light. The more the group is expected to take a more general system and pick and choose what parts they're going to want, the heavier the rules get; esp. if the designers want you to be able to shift the tone of the game over time.
                            I guess this kind of leads towards what I meant about how CoD ties rules to fiction in a simulationist kind of way - the CoD stuff tends to be a pretty big tent that tries to describe, via rules, how a particular type of *world* works. Inside that world, you can run any kind of story you want, but the core rules of CoD aren't really about that - indeed, they can't be, because, again, you can run any kind of story you want.

                            PbtA games typically aren't about creating an engine to simulate how the world works, they're about capturing how a particular type of story works. They're all bespoke because a "PbtA Core" would be inherently nonsensical. They all have to be bespoke just because of the nature of the beast.

                            So if you want to slim a CoD game down to a similiar type of thing, you either have to a) cut it down to just one small type of story, which loses all the breadth, or b) go with a more generic narrative system where you lose all the cool detail.

                            Which, I guess, in the end is kind of just how it goes - if you want a smaller, lighter game you gotta choose what you're sacrificing. It doesn't come for free. There's a reason why, as much as I love my PbtA stuff, I'm coming back to CoD stuff now.

The possibility to go counter your Frenzy target for a turn by spending WP? The ability of Touchstones to talk you out of Frenzy?                                     Those are all rules that belong to the larger Frenzy system. (Though I might've gotten a few of the minor details wrong.)

                                    All subsystems for all the various splats are filled to the brim with different rules even if the resolution mechanism for all of them are based on the same types of rolls that are used in the CofD core book. That's a huge difference between CofD and the splat games. In CofD every subsystem (except Integrity) can be replaced with a single roll with modifiers determined at the time of the roll. Even combat can be resolved this way; it's called Down & Dirty.
                                    But once you dig into any of the other games you end up with many subsystems designed around aspects of that particular splat, each with their own collection or rules tailored to a particular experience. It's incredibly rare to find a system that is only "roll this dice pool; these are the results". Rarer still is finding a subsystem in one of those games that is actually designed to be optional. You are not expected to be able to just replace them wholesale with a single roll appropriate for the situation. That's what makes the splat games so very different from base CofD. And because they have their subsystems tailored for each individual game, they're all quite different from each other as well.

                                    Going from one game to another time and time again like I do, it's very clear that the Storytelling System framework (how to form dice pools, the different kinds of rolls, Conditions, Clash of Wills) is mostly the same across all games, thankfully, but all the games are still very different and very much their own separate games. That became painfully obvious when I started learning Deviant for one game, and now again when I'm delving into Changeling for a soon to be started game.

                                                        I like having a mix of approaches. For totally unexpected encounters, or throw-away ones, obviously you can just slap a dicepool on it and call it a day, without trouble. But I like statting out all NPCs that PCs have contact with on a regular basis in more detail than that. But not so much detail that I can't go through like 10 of them in an hour every so often, yet more detail so that a pattern of "3 dice +/- this" doesn't become so obvious. Inevitably, these will also often be members of the same splat the PCs are part of. (I tend not to mix splats, I find it dilutes the themes of all splats involved a lot.) And sometimes you want to stat out an elder vampire juuuuust in case, but you don't want to spend more than 5 minutes on it, but you want a bit more freedom than slapping 3 fixed dice pools on it.

                                                        I'd say that in terms of rising detail and lowering "speed" of use the steps I use/will use are:
                                                        - Just wing a dice pool
                                                        - Brief Horror rules with some tweaks (like tessie suggested)
                                                        - The peripheral NPC quick rules that I already use, as mentioned in this post
                                                        - Fully statting an NPC

                                                        It's that third one that I feel could have a place if an official "toolbox for speeding up your game/planning" if one was ever made. Perhaps not everyone would use that and many are fine with using the first two options on the list. But there also, consider that people with less experience in the system don't necessarily think of using Brief Horrors, and don't necessarily feel confident winging dice pools or tweaking the Brief Horror rules to suit them since they don't know what values make sense or make for good game feel/balance yet. There's this tendency on forums to assume because we--the ones ultra-involved and very knowledgeable of the rules--can cope, that there isn't a hole to be filled (the "Defense and attack dice pools are too closer together!" threads that keep popping up every few months like clockwork are another such thing that clearly bothers many prospective players but not forum veterans, but that's a discussion for another day). But since the only methods available rely on tweaking rules not explicitly made to purpose or require a robust feel for what size of dice pool make sense to begin with, I absolutely feel that there is a hole there.

                                                        It doesn't take more than a minute of googling to find that there are clearly people struggling with this, and not all of them will find their way to the forums before just shrugging and throwing the system in the bin for "too much prepwork":
                                                        - https://www.reddit.com/r/WhiteWolfRP..._npc_creation/
                                                        - https://www.reddit.com/r/WhiteWolfRP..._npc_creation/
                                                        - https://rpg.stackexchange.com/questi...ernatural-npcs (preCTL 2e question, but note the author's tags)

                                                        It's clearly something people who aren't steeped in the mechanics consider a problem, so I don't think it'd be bad if a hypothetical supplement would address it. ^^
