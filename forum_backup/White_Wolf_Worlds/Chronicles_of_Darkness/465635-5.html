    Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post
    And then, of course, there's the component of magic being fueled by willpower. The mage / wizard / sorcerer or whatever is disobeying science because they want to. Science isn't about getting what you want simply because you want it. I want the family pick up truck to turn into a laborghini when I honk the horn 5 times... doesn't mean the universe will grant my wish.
    Science is, very simply, a method of testing what does and does not accomplish an effect. Theoretical models are then designed to try to guess at why X works and Y doesn't, but these models ultimately bow to the science; a model that doesn't match experimental results is discarded, and the search for a new one begins. This happens all the time; a good scientist is excited about the plausible possibility that everything we know is wrong, because science is the incremental process of learning about the universe.

    Consequentially, if you've got a dude standing there who can wave his hands and wish really hard and shoot a fireball, a scientist isn't going to pop off his monocle and say "You're breaking the rules!" He's going to say, "Whoa! Either I'm missing something really cleverly hidden, or the rules aren't what I thought they were." He's then going to try to do what the wizard did and see if he can make the fireball, and if he can't, he's going to keep trying, and keep asking the wizard to do it over and over with slight differences (how about if you say it in Sanskrit instead of Latin? how about if we make up completely different hand motions but you still wish really hard? how about if we do it over here, over here, or over here? does the time of day matter?). Eventually, the goal is to identify the variables that do matter and the variables that don't matter in whether wishing really hard can make a fireball. After that the theorists start trying to come up with models to explain why this could be possible, but as long as the experiments can be reproduced by others under the same circumstances, science acknowledges the fireball spell.

    Science is a model to describe what happens in the world. If the fireball spell verifiably works, then it goes in the model.

        Exactly. You assume that the wizard is asking the rules of the universe to stop, but in reality all the wizard is doing is calling upon a set of rules of the universe that not everyone can use, and even that exclusivity is a rule. A scientist simply sees a new set of rules to uncover, a set of rules in which they really do need to start from scratch, and codify the "what" and the limitations before they can start getting to the "how" and the "why" of it.

        First they'd observe that some people can change the world according to their will. They'll codify exactly when this can happen, and what effects are possible, how this changes, and then once they have a large enough body of observations they'll begin to theorize, coming up with theories of separate dimensions, hidden laws, etc, trying to come up with the "how", what you think of as science; the science of how magic is made is no different from the science of how electricity is made, or how nuclear fission happens. First they observe that it happens, and what happens, and the permutations, then they create theories as to what causes it, and what causes that cause, as far back as they can go. It may seem very gnostic, but at it's core it is scientific.

                Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post
                I get what you're saying that science is constantly changed based on what we observe, but again, I still think that's pretty arrogant or doesn't hold up in some conditions. Take for example, the traditional wizard who casts spells with verbal incantations and gestures. How can that ever be science? You're basically saying the wizard is asking the universe (usually in Latin) if it will stop abiding the rules for a moment, and the universe says "Sure!"
                You're still thinking of science as a category of things. It's not, it's the method we use to categorize things. If a wizard casts spells by using verbal incantations and gestures, we would call it magic if we didn't understand how it worked. However, with proper study, a scientist could, in theory, figure out what words and gestures produce what spells, and how. In that process, they may have to re-define our current understanding of the laws of physics, but the process of figuring out what the wizard is doing and how it works is science. Once we have developed a working model that explains the phenomenon and can be verified by other scientist, the word we use for such a model is "theory", though since that word has a different colloquial meaning, we often qualify it as a "scientific theory". When someone calls a phenomenon "science" what they mean is that a scientific theory exists that explains it. Ergo, any phenomenon, including those we might call "magic", once understood is "science" in layman's terms, because the layman uses the word "science" to mean "something we understand."

                Science isn't about anything, except studying things and explaining them. If willpower is a necessary component in spell casting, a scientific theory of spellcasting will take that component into account when explaining how spellcasting works.
