    Cultural Appropriation in gaming

    06-14-2021, 11:58 AM
    This is something I've offered up for discussions with other systems, and it is rarely met with a warm reception.
    Still, I would like some input on the subject: how do you deal with the concept that many common tropes of horror and fantasy come from real-world cultures, and how do you navigate/mitigate the consequences?

    I think WWG and OPP have a brilliant model in the lists upon lists of sources of inspiration in their books. It basically says "this isn't our thing, originally. And here are some of the places where we heard about it and their different takes on it and how that has shaped our perception of it." I really like that a lot.

    To be clear, I'm not talking about like...golems and werewolves, the mythologies that have been well and truly relegated to the history books and to legend. Those guys are all ready and waiting to be popped into one Monster Manual or another. That seems fine to me.
    It's like...specifically cultural beliefs like the wendigo and the skin-walker. Stories that are very much a part of a living system, and ones that members of said culture have explicitly asked others not to appropriate/adapt/change/spread.

      I think a lot of people struggle with the concept of cultural appropriation vs. health cultural exchange. On one hand it is simple in theory, but on the other is quiet complicated in real world practice.

      There's a knee-jerk reaction to the concept because pretty much everyone benefits from and enjoys media and culture that is born of some amount of cultural appropriation; and thus people don't want to be "bad" so they reject the underlying concept rather than reevaluating things. Making it a hard topic to bring up in general.

      Threads on the forums here about it in the past haven't always gone well.

      how do you deal with the concept that many common tropes of horror and fantasy come from real-world cultures, and how do you navigate/mitigate the consequences?
      Do my best to keep myself educated; other people don't have a responsibility to teach me why something is disrespectful.
      Listen to my group to see what they need/want to enjoy the game; I'm not writing stuff for general consumption, what matters is my friends having fun.
      Do my best to speak up against problematic comments and behaviors; making my gaming spaces fun places to be, means making sure I take on my share of the emotional work of establishing what's acceptable.
      Acknowledge that no matter how much work I do, I will make mistakes; people that care don't begrudge honest mistakes, they just want you to do the best you can.

What makes golems different is that they weren't something that filtered into Christian theology and mythology, and thus became part of mainstream western culture with Christianity's rise to power. Golems remained a uniquely Jewish cultural expression until very recently, and with very little respect for Jewish culture in the process of it being a mainstream pop-culture element.

                              Quixote Don't overextend yourself to the point of a breakdown just to please everyone that takes issue with what you create. You cannot address potentially valid complaints if you are so stressed out that you can't think straight. Pay attention to those who come to you in earnest, and ignore all the rest. Because those who take offense in a manufactured and malicious way will never, ever be pleased with anything you do, or even who you are.

                              I wish you a lot of wisdom so you can reliably tell the difference between people who are worth listening to, and people who are just driven by spite. As long as you create without the desire to harm and with effort to be mindful, then your critics should likewise be patient and understanding. I'm obviously speaking from an idealistic perspective, such a balanced dynamic is something to strive for rather than something that can be established permanently. But don't let that discourage you from creating amazing art, of course. The world would be even more of a dreary and bleak place if all artists gave up in the face of adversity and error.

                                  Originally posted by Quixote View Post
                                  That is a very generous and amiable approach to what I'm sure most people write off as "difficult people".
                                  I get written off as a difficult person a lot... probably helps my ability to put myself in those shoes.

                                  I don't always side with the offended party; I do think that some people simply look for things to take offense to.
                                  I really don't feel this is a useful way to approach things. I've never seen, "you were looking for things to take offense to," ever help people understand each other.

                                  What I try to assume is a slightly different thing: The work in question set off red flags for the person, and they read it with all the preconceptions that entails. It's not an active process. Confirmation bias is something we all do, because we're wired to do so. When you see it as not people actively trying to be offended, but rather people falling back on mental defenses they've built up for reasons, you have a better path forward to discuss a potential way through that communication log-jam.

                                  For example: When I see modern Americans use the term"Judeo-Christian," esp. in a political context, it's a huge red flag for me. I automatically assume they really mean, "White evangelical Christian dressed up as either being the true form of all Abrahamic faith, or just trying to make one particularly view point sound far more inclusive than it is." Why? Because far, far too often that's exactly what I end up dealing with. It's offensive to me before I even get into the actual discussion, because there is a long - still active - history of Christians using their connection to Judaism as a shield or excuse, and then ignoring Jews and Jewish thought on the topics. Esp. in the social media era, I'm constantly aware of people that treat me, my people, and my faith as a prop for them to score points with, and disagree our actual feelings and beliefs.

                                  Intellectually, I know not everyone that's talking about Judeo-Christian whatever is actually falling into that behavior. But once something primes me to see that pattern, a pattern I've seen repeated more times than days I've been alive, it's a lot of emotional work on my end to not see the inklings of that pattern and assume that the full thing is going to emerge if I keep engaging.

                                  It's something like when someone says, "I don't want to sound racist, but..." and then whatever they say sounds more racist for it. Imagine someone saying, "I don't want to sound racist, but strawberry ice-cream is clearly the best flavor." It's silly, but you can't help but wonder, "wait, what to strawberries have to do with racism?" You start trying to figure out the connection, because it doesn't make sense and we want the world to make sense. That's what "looking for offense," really starts with.

                                  I've seen countless discussion break down because the person taking offense is, frequently unstated, primed to be offended at the base premise/introduction of the topic and is seeing justifications for it as thy continue, while those trying to defend the topic aren't seeing the initial issue, and they try to defend the work/discussion with counter-examples or in-depth nuanced analysis. It doesn't work because it never addresses that opening issue.

                                  Of course, there's always people that aren't actually offended, and are stating offense for other motivations. Ignoring those though, if you feel someone is genuinely offended, but seems like they're "looking" for it, and you want to try to get past that? Start with the red flags, not details. Figure out why they're in confirmation bias mode, because seeing that perspective is the only way you can state your side of things in a fashion they'll listen to. As they say, meet people where they are.

                                  The problem with that specific example is that there are people who hold those stories to be part of their cultural identity who have outright said they do not want people outside of that culture to use those stories, or even talk about them.
                                  It's important to remember that no community is a monolith. While there are cultures that have secret insider knowledge that's not supposed to be shared with outsiders, there's a lot more people that are just trying to reclaim agency over their own culture rather than keep things hidden from everyone else. Just because I ask people to not be OK with pop-culture golems doesn't mean every Jew feels that way, or it even represents a majority opinion of Jews. Maybe I'm just a stick in the mud and offending me isn't really that big of a deal for anything meant for general consumption instead of something just for a theoretical gaming group we're both in.

                                  Calls for outsiders to stop using those cultural elements is largely because it's so hard to reclaim those things after being appropriated. Getting outsiders to stop is the only way to get the breathing room for insiders to have a shot.

                                  I don't know if there's a way to keep everyone happy, or if a 100% hands-off approach is even a reasonable model.
                                  There's no way, and thus it is not reasonable, for anything meant for public consumption.

                                  Even after all the research I did, I still have a story about skin-walkers I'd really like to tell. And I *think* my approach would be respectful and accurate enough to avoid causing actual harm to the community that the story belongs to. But...yeah. Still apprehensive, of course.
                                  My advice in this particular regard is:

                                  1) Reach out to people like sensitivity readers that specialize in a given culture. Figure out what you can afford obviously (that's work they're doing, don't expect freebies), but do you best to get insider opinions on how respectful and non-harmful it is, rather than assuming. It might hurt to get negative responses, but you know. And if you get positive responses it says a lot about how much work you did.

                                  2) Some stories aren't ours to tell. It's good to accept that, and accepting that reduces a lot of apprehension. There's 8 billion people on Earth, you have lots of stories to tell, to lots of people that want to hear them. Letting some neat ideas get away because you're not the right people to tell them just means you're helping make room for the right person to tell them to do so. You still learned a lot of interesting things, expanded your perspective, and grew as a person in the process of ending up with a story that you decide you shouldn't' do.

                                  3) If you go through with it, you do your best, and you take your lumps from people that don't think its good enough in stride. All art has haters. Lots of haters have a point. It sucks, it's painful, but in the end you can find way to do better next time. There's no perfect art, there's just better art than your previous work, and we all need that critical eye on our work to get to better work.

                                          Yeah. Not to hijack the thread, but something I think can be indicative of these sorts of red flags rather than just harping on that to a receptive audience:

                                          The terms "Judeo-Christian" and "Abrahamic," have a history of switching with each other in which is seen as "respectful," vs. "problematic." Outside of the specific context of comparative religious studies though, there's no real good reason to try to generalize Christianity and Judaism, or Christianity, Islam, and Judaism like that. Jews complain about how whichever phrase is popular at that time being used to erase the Jewish part of things, and then what changes is the word, not the message. The motivations leading to appropriation never get addressed, the window dressing gets changed.

                                          This is the kind of things to avoid doing when you're thinking about, "how can I do better at using other culture's expressions respectfully." Getting caught up in the surface aspects, and not looking at what's really causing the upset is the kind of things that minorities esp. have long memories around.

                                          It's like all the companies that change their logos to rainbow colors for Pride, but then go back to giving money to anti-LGBTQ+ organizations and politicians. If you only put in the surface effort, and never address the underlying issues with what you're doing, you'll be seen, and the response isn't going to be good. When I talk about being accepted despite our mistakes, this is the contrast: when people don't see it as a mistake, just a play.
