    I can't say I'm much of a fan for advance actions either, but at least it means my group is far more likely to play the game, and thus that I get to play the game.

    While flavourful mechanics are certainly important to make a game stand out, the by far most important part is that it's fun when playing the game.
    X-Again and Rote Action increases the fun by making you reroll specific dice (turning failures into successes, or successes into more successes).
    Advance Action is less fun because you reroll your dice pool again (i.e. just feels like more rolling, even if it's in your advantage).
    Shifting difficulty means you need to recalibrate your brain to calculate it different from other rolls, and, in combination with how rare it is, is thus much more likely to make you mess up the result.

    Anything that increases the complexity of figuring out the result (which shifting difficulty does more than other dice tricks) without outweighing that drawback by increasing the enjoyment of the game is bad design.
    Now whether shifting difficulty is fun to use is subjective. My experience was that most people didn't think so, but this thread leans the other way. Make of that what you will.

        I mean, shifting the Target Number is such a small thing ("Hey, for this one roll which I specifically made the choice to use a power on, I should count the 7s as Successes too") that I really can't believe it's considered any harder than Rote Action's "Roll a set of the dice again". Neither of them are enormously disruptive, and both of them are active choices engaged in by the player. Arguably rerolling dice takes a bit longer to resolve. And honestly, Advance Action feels like a good bit more mental gymnastics, since you have to actually record your first result and then reroll and calculate the new result while holding the first result in your head to compare and contrast. Still not a large mental load, but surely larger than just remembering to count another number as a success?

        Personally, I think Blessed is the God King Tier 1 has been the biggest loser in the game transition. There was some grandiose about the way it worked in first edition. In the current iteration, if you roll poorly you can choose to reroll a bunch of your dice, and maybe you get lucky this time. In first edition, with it retroactively making 6s and 7s successes, you got to actually see in advance what your new result was going to be. "I've got two successes, but if I trigger this power now it'll make the four sixes and sevens into successes, bringing my total to 6 successes." That just felt right. It made the result seem inevitable, like Fate had decreed it thus. Compared to Advance Action or Rote, that turns Fate in the Mummy context back into being equivalent to the fortune and luck that Deinos equated it to. In First Edition, I never felt like Fate in Mummy had anything to do with Luck. Sometimes, the universe in Mummy simply decreed that something Would Be, and Arisen had the power to influence that decision.

        Of course, only Blessed is the God King of all Mummy's First Edition rules had that retroactive capacity, the rest of the rules applied the modifier beforehand. But I loved watching my players play with that Utterance, it always felt like asserting Fate in action.

        Still, at least it'll be consistent going forward. Target Number manipulation worked in Mummy 1e because there were a large number of powers using it, and any power involved with Fate tended to use it. Second edition has carved out whole Utterances and Affinities that used to manipulate Target Numbers in different ways as part of the whole halving the number of powers thing, reducing the number of Fate-related powers to a pretty small number, and then had the mechanics used amidst that number be inconsistent. At minimum, making the remaining Fate powers consistent will be an improvement.

                Honestly, that's irrelevant to me. Having a useful mechanic that is flavorful for it's purpose is what I'm concerned about.                                                     Edit: Unrelated, and the point I'm about to comment on was from pages back and therefore not relevant, (And also I'm still reading the first preview, my life has been busy y'all) BUT

                                                    Cosmic does not mean Really Fucking Big, it means This Power Changes The Shape of the Game. The one and only example was an ability that let you travel back and forth in time before 2nd Edition made that the default experience of reality, and thus was the only power in First Edition that let you go and change history as the present experienced it.

                                                    In concept, Cosmic Utterances are akin to Wish in DnD (or, actually, wishes in Chuubo's, that game's casualness with it be damned)-It's a power that allows the player to grab the reins of the setting from the Storyteller and change it to something else within it's confines.
