                    Here's what I'm thinking for the next update, which will be about explosives. Because explosives are such important things on the modern battlefield, I wanted to take some time to give them some special attention.

                    Because unlike GURPS, Chronicles of Darkness doesn't have its own explosives generation tables, I decided I would create some. There are some tables for first edition, but I decided I want to have a bit more detail.

                    I've received a number of questions asking me why I go through such lengths to take an approach like this, and I'll explain a bit more: it's because it saves work in the long run. It's far easier for me to develop algorithms and formulas that I can easily automate and plug into a spreadsheet, which I can then apply stuff to. There could be many different types of shells and bombs in a country's national arsenal, and it's not easy for me to take a holistic approach to each individual one. Instead, if I can punch the numbers into a spreadsheet and have it spit out the numbers autonomously, or simply enter numbers into a spreadsheet. I make heavy use of automation when I game, so the economy of system complexity may be a tad bit different than if I was doing everything by hand. This process allows me to keep the amount of special rules and exceptions to a minimum and be able to get game stats relatively quickly off real-world metrics like charge mass and mmRHAe.

                    First, there are a few thoughts I had in mind when I wanted to create these. The first thing was, I gained some newfound respect for the wounding system of Bashing, Lethal, and Aggravated Damage when I read a discussion on combat in Chronicles of Darkness by ArcaneArts, which discussed the different focus that Chronicles has that makes it different from games that use a traditional "Hit Points" based means of tracking damage, and how this is intended to allow for escalation of force to follow escalation of narrative; it makes sense that a game about narrative consequences would prioritize allowing players to do things like declare their intent before combat starts, and then vary the amount of force they plan to use to pursue their objectives in accordance with the stakes and the percieved consequences.

                    This makes completely logical sense for a game with the focus that Chronicles of Darkness is intended to follow. While I wanted to follow convention in order to keep the game within its comfort zone, I felt that by the time players are turning to artillery, cruise missiles, bunker buster bombs, and carpet bombing, and expending possibly tons of ordnance, my players have already likely escalated the conflict a wee bit beyond the level of loading shotgun shells with silver shot.

                    For this reason, I've decided to make a bit of a break from the usual, and I chose to take a multilevel approach to wounding that would be a tiny bit different. What I've chosen to do instead is to create a slightly different model of explosive damage for nonnuclear explosives, which would track blast overpressure and fragmentation damage separately.

                    First, I'd like to reiterate: this is being done for the purposes of gaming, not engineering. The values are heavily approximated, compounding errors accrue, and I had to do some massaging to get them to fit within the Storyteller system.

                    I decided to take a bit of a clean break from Chronicles of Darkness: Hurt Locker, and use different rules and mechanics for calculating explosive damage. These rules are no doubt going to be a wee bit more complicated, but are going to be easier to model things like the M830 HEAT-MP-T round fired by the M256 120mm Smoothbore Gun seen on the M1A2 Abrams, along with things like tomahawk cruise missiles carrying 1000-pound Joint Multi-Effect Warheads, GBU-24 Paveway IIIs, all the way up to the 1,900-pound Mark 13 High Capacity shell fire by the Mark 7 16" Guns on the Iowa class battleships.

                    I've tried to simplify things enough to keep this from becoming Chronicles of Darkness: Phoenix Command, but it's time to play with the big boys.

                    Establishing a Baseline

                    As usual with everything I do, the first thing I want to attempt to do is establish a baseline, to determine the candid and realistic effects of the different weapons.

                    First, what I decided to do was get some loose models, so I for input data I decided to base some of my estimates off the International Ammunition Technical Guidelines Formula for Ammunition Management.

                    You can find a copy of the formulas here, and there is an automated web-based calculation of the Kingery-Bulmash Blast Parameter Calculator you can experiment with that does everything auto-magically here:

                    Kingery-Bulmash Blast Parameter Calculator | International Ammunition Technical Guidelines
                    https://unsaferguard.org/un-saferguard/kingery-bulmash


                    https://data.unsaferguard.org/iatg/en/IATG-01.80-Formulae-ammunition-management-IATG-V.3.pdf


                    To condense the report, there are four primary ways that we can model people getting hurt from surface detonations. The first is blast overpressure caused by the explosive compound detonating into rapidly expanding gases which causes blast overpressure. The second is the mechanism of blast fragmentation, or fragments of material that are thrown about at high velocity by this action. The third, finally, are people being trapped within and crushed by collapsing structures blown down by the blast overpressure. Although it's quite plausible that a human being could survive a reflective overpressure of 5 pounds per square inch, the same pressure applied to the surface area of the side of a small residential house could quickly turn into many tons of force. The last is the thermal effects of the blast.

                    We'll take the first two mechanisms for now, and what I chose to do is to track them separately. The effects of a fireball and structural collapses I won't track.

                    Blast Overpressure

                    In terms of blast overpressure, I went with the following estimates for reflective overpressure. A blast overpressure of 1 psi (pound per square inch) is usually enough to break a window. A blast overpressure of 3 psi is enough to cause injury to a human being, and cause serious damage to homes and wooden structures. Turn that up to 5 psi, and you're looking at serious injury to a person, and widespread destruction. At 10 psi, most structures that aren't reinforced won't be left standing and fatalities are likely going to be widespread. Double it once again to bring that up to 20psi, and the results are going to look like what you get in the aftermath of a hurricane along with fatalities approaching 100%.

                    Blast overpressure drops off quickly in atmosphere, which means that damage inflicted due to overpressure should drop off quickly with range.

                    ~1 psi is comparatively little and I'm using it to establish an upper limit to the radius of a blast. While this may not on its own cause injuries to most people, it can cause window glass to shatter, which could turn into shards. If windows only are worth Durability 1 Structure 1, this should establish a lower limit of 2 bashing damage at most. Beyond this, no damage should be incurred.

                    <5 psi should be considered to be an upper limit for bashing before it becomes lethal. A good estimate for this would be 6 automatic damage, and 6 damage dice.

                    ~10 psi should be where Lethal Damage begins. 8 Lethal Damage is enough to fill the health tracks of the average person entirely and send them into bleedout, which we can take as "widespread fatalities." This would be served well by 6 automatic damage, and 6 damage dice.

                    >20+ psi is the level at which lungs collapse or get perforated, and should be taken as Aggravated Damage. 8 Aggravated Damage should be considered to be instantly fatal to the average person, and very nearly fatal to anyone that lacks four Stamina dots and is at least Size 5. For the purposes of determining narrative effects (for the purposes of forensics and recovery), I would consider someone getting enough Aggravated Damage to fill their health track twice over to be on the level of total bodily disincorporation, but as a lower limit to this level going with 6 automatic damage and 6 damage dice which averages to 8.

                    Looking at the math for blast overpressure using the Kingery-Bulmash equations and plotting them (rounded to the nearest metre) to the different reflective pressure thresholds, we get the following chart. We'll use the Kingery-Bulmash equations as a guide. Although these equations are intended to be used for a ground burst, given the low levels of accuracy involved with the system, it doesn't make much sense to redo this work for an airburst at optimal blast height.

                    From here we can easily predict the following characteristics of the bursting charge of an M67 Hand Grenade without its fragmentation casing, which is 180g of Composition B explosive. Using the Kingery-Bulmash calculator, we obtain the following values for Reflected Pressure in kiloPascals, or 138kPa, 69kPa, and 38kPa coming in to play at 2.5m, 3.5m, and 5.5m respectively. This rounds to 3m, 4m, and 6m, and then drops off below 7kPa at 61m.

                    This gives us an approximate baseline for the ranges at which the blast overpressure component of 180g of Composition B should inflict approximately 8 Aggravated, 8 Lethal, and 8 Bashing damage respectively for a ground burst, in the absence of its fragmentation casing. Rounding to the nearest whole number gives us 3, 4, and 6 metres respectively for each threshold.

                    At one metre, the same explosive will give us a blast overpressure of close to 1700kPa, or approximately 250psi.

                    For comparison, 1kg of TNT will produce the same pressures for the thresholds we're using as benchmarks at 4m, 6m, and 9m respectively, but also produce 8100kPa (1200psi) at a distance of 1m. Completing the chart below for TNT will provide us with some damage thresholds to work off of. TNT is ideal for establishing a baseline, as TNT is used as a benchmark for explosive equivalency, whether it be in kilograms or kilotonnes. If we keep on iterating, we can find that the blast overpressure of TNT reaches about 7kPa, or 1psi, at approximately 31m.

                    We'll figure out damage in a bit, for now, let's got onto fragmentation.

                    Blast Fragmentation

                    Shortly before I wrote this, I tried using the Gurney Equations to calculate the initial velocity of fragments for different ratios of explosive to uniform casing, then attempted to use that to calculate trajectories to plot the course that the fragments would take so I could develop an estimate of what fragments would land where at what velocity, and then remembered my mantra of this is a game, not engineering.

                    Perhaps that's not such a great approach.

                    Fortunately for us, the IATG also has a relatively simple Explosion Danger Area Calculator that delivers an extremely conservative estimate for an approximate distance outside of which not more than one fragment could be expected to fly when destroying ammunition by simply detonating it.

                    Explosion Danger Area Calculator | International Ammunition Technical Guidelines
                    https://unsaferguard.org/un-saferguard/explosion-danger-area


                    As luck would have it the math is pretty basic, however there are three different formulas for three different situations: for fragmenting munitions when the public is or isn't allowed access to the demolition area, and for the bare exposed explosive only. Although this is misusing the formula, I decided that using the formula for the bare explosive actuallay gave results closer to what I was hoping for for the lower end of things, especially for things like grenades and mortar shells.

                    Putting it all together: Damage

                    Here's the fun part. Now that we've figured out the area of effect and the radius of the blasts of the bombs, now let's take a few minutes to figure out what kind of damage they can do.

                    Unfortunately, I don't have any scientific way of translating real world data to game units, so what I've gone with is a slightly holistic approach that I determined based on the time honoured process of adjusting the numbers using coefficients and adjustment factors until I come up with something that looks like it would fit into the game's existing stats until something comes out looking like the stats that I wanted it to.

                    A short sidenote I'd like to take is that New World of Darkness: Armory, 1st Edition had an interesting rule that allowed explosive damage to scale with the size of the objects, to represent objects of greater size taking more damage from reflected blast pressure and increased surface area that fragmentation could affect. Hunter: The Vigil, 1st Edition also included mechanics for fragmenting objects. We're going to be bringing both of those back in a slighty modified way to account for hardened armoured vehicles which can withstand a near miss from even 1000-pound bombs, but for now we'll focus simply on the raw damage output.

                    Damage Due To Blast Pressure

                    What I've done so far is apply the function of log(Peak Pressure/4200,1.125)+6 to the Peak Pressure of the explosion determined using the Kingery-Bulmash equations, and used that to calculate the maximum damage that the explosion would cause at the point at which its blast pressure is the greatest. This establishes a fourth bracket for the explosive damage, which I figure should be taken as the maximum damage that the explosive should do at its origin. I then split up the ranges using a simple linear rule for simplicity.

                    Let's take two worked examples of play and derive the stats for approximately one pound of TNT which is being set off with no fragmentation. First, we round to the closest number on the chart which gives us 500g of TNT. This gives us a base of 6 Force, Aggravated.

                    At 2m, and all ranges underneath, it will do Force 6A: six automatic Aggravated Damage, and six damage dice rolled. Between 2-3m, it will do Force 6L: six automatic Lethal Damage, and six damage dice rolled. At 3-4m, it will do Force 6B: six automatic Bashing Damage, and six damage dice rolled. Between 4m and 24m, this will gradually taper off to Force 1B at 24m. Beyond 24m, the blast itself in the absence of any fragmentation does no damage at all. A simple linear function can take care of this part, so I won't go into detail there.

                    Our second worked example will be for a Mark 84 General Purpose Bomb, a freefall bomb with approximately 1000 pounds of Tritonal filling. We could go through the exercise of multiplying all the distance values by a conversion factor to re-run the numbers for Tritonal 80-20 to TNT, but in the interest of expediency, we'll simply use the numbers for 512kg of TNT and call it close enough.

                    We look at the numbers and see that when the Mark 84 detonates, the blast pressure will deal Force 44A on everything within a 9m radius of where it detonated, in the form of 44 Automatic Damage and 44 Damage Dice. This drops to Force 25A between 9-17m, Force 14A between 17-26m, and then finally drops off to Force 6A between 26-34m. Between 34-48m, this becomes Force 6L, 48-76m becomes Force 6B, and then tapers off to Force 1B over the course of 76-245m. Again, this is in the absence of any metal casing, so we'll apply fragmentation damage later.

                    Because the order of Bashing damage and Lethal damage counts and Bashing damage applied prior to Lethal damage is "pushed off" the health track, rather than upgraded, when dealing with explosives order of precedence applies and blast pressure damage is applied first, so it can be pushed off, rather than upgrade once fragmentation enters the fray.

                    Damage due to Blast Fragmentation

                    What I've decided to do with blast fragmentation is a bit simpler. The full fragmentation damage is done, in Force, at a quarter of the range, half fragmentation damage, again in Force, is done at half, and a quarter of the fragmentation damage is done between, rounding to the nearest whole number.

                    The All-Up Weight is used to calculate damage due to blast fragmentation, up to a limit of twice the mass of the explosive.

                    This is thankfully, a little simpler. Let's do another worked example again.

                    Let's say that Scratch the Changeling wants to make a pipebomb out of a pound of TNT. He goes and gets the TNT, and wraps it all manner of nasty metal stuff that he could expect to turn into a lethal cloud of shards when the TNT detonates. By the end of it, Scratch's IED consists of 500g of TNT, and 1kg of fragmentation casing and weighs in at 1.5kg. We round this to 2kg for determining the All-Up-Weight. We consult the chart and see that that gives us that it's a Force 7 Weapon, with a primary blast radius of 0-41m, a secondary blast radius of 41-82m, and a tertiary blast radius of 82-164m. If Scratch were to use Cold Iron to produce the bomb casing, this becomes Aggravated when used against Fey creatures. This is partially why I preferred to scale the number, rather than scale the damage type.

                    In terms of fragmentation damage, this gives us damage of Force 7L at 0-41m, Force 3L at 41-82m, and Force 1L at 82m. This is applied on the same turn as the detonation, but after the explosive overpressure damage is applied.

                    Now, for our other worked example, the Mark 84 Bomb. The Mark 84 bomb is only fifty percent explosive by mass, and the entire bomb weighs close to two thousand pounds. Rounded to the nearest value on the charts, we can take the value for AUW as 1024kg. The base value for its fragmentation damage will be 16, and have a range of 1310. So this means that everything within a 0-328m radius of the detonation will take a Force 16L attack, everything within a 328-655m radius of the detonation will take a Force 8L attack, and everything within a 655-1310m radius will take a Force 4L attack. This again stacks with the damage done by the air blast overpressure, Order of Precedence applying.

                    Other Types of Explosives

                    The wonderful world of things that go bang aren't limited to TNT. Explosives are just as varied as the people who deploy them, and there are explosives like RDX, HMX, and other blends like Amatol, Tritonite, and more.

                    We could simply recalculate all the values, but it's probably just easier to multiply them by a conversion factor and then get an idea of TNT equivalency instead. For instance, the brisance of Black Powder is very low and the relative effectiveness factor could be used to count it as only half the effectiveness of TNT by weight, and primary explosives like Mercury (II) Fulminate might only be a third. Something like RDX (Hexogen/Trinitrotriazinane) might count for one and a half. This way, we don't need to worry about doing a lot of complicated math in-game but still can capture the nuance to a certain extent.

                    To close out...

                    This was a big one, so thanks for staying with me on this. I'll be gradually phasing these in across Gods of Iron, and in the near future I'll be implementing some other mechanics for other effects such as HEAT, HESH, Shaped Charges, and the like. This changes gameplay in a great big way because it makes explosions a whole lot deadlier and more realistic, at the cost of some complexity.

                    There are a few hacks that you can use with these rules, such as not allowing blast overpressure and fragmentation damage to stack, which brings the performance of hand grenades and sticks of dynamite more in line with the values in Hurt Locker.

                    For now though, these guidelines are going to be powering some of the estimates I'll be using for things such as 155mm Howitzer shells, bombs, vehicle-mounted weaponry, artillery, and missiles and paving the way for the larger scale weaponry in Angels and Aces and Seas of Steel for use not just on the tactical scale, but also on the operational and strategic level.

                    As always, ideas that may assist with improving this are certainly welcome.

The stats given for 4kg of TNT or equivalent place the base damage at Force 21, which will roll to an average of 27 damage, which is almost exactly on the tick of 140mm, or just 10mm off the mark. Not bad for my estimates!

                        Also with the proposed rules, the new tables for the GBU-28 Laser Guided Bunker Busting Bomb would have the following stats. This is a proposal for the stats for the GBU-28, which I'm going to put into the main branch later.

                        Circular Error Probable: <2m, limited by the accuracy of the targeting laser. It's possible for laser guided bombs to be sent down ventilation shafts, chimneys, and even sent through windows, so 2m is quite a lot.
                        Kinetic Damage: 3d10+79 AP 575, when travelling at terminal velocity
                        Air Blast Force, Aggravated: Force 41 at 0-7m, Force 31 at 8-14m, Force 15 at 15-20m, Force 6 at 21-27m
                        Air Blast Force, Lethal: Force 6 at 28-38m
                        Air Blast Force, Bashing: Force 6 at 39-60m, tapering off to Force 1B at 195m at a rate of 1 Force lost, per every 22m beyond 60m.
                        Fragmentation Force: Force 15 Lethal at 0-260m, Force 7 Lethal at 261-520m, and Force 3 Lethal at 521-1040m.
                        By the tables, this means that the GBU-28 has a 50% chance of successfully penetrating cover rated Durability 679, or the in-game equivalent of 28 feet of high-strength, reinforced concrete[1]. Once it does, on average it should cause a total of 54 damage in explosive damage once the dice have been rolled, cumulative with the damage done via my rules for explosive order of precedence on the structure. Because bunkers and hardened underground bases aren't big open spaces, one way to simulate a contained explosion would be to raise the primary blast radius to the extents of the structure unless the structure is big enough that it can contain several times the entirety of the blast volume, which would also raise the damage done to the structure accordingly to scale alongside it.

                        It's reasonable to assume that this would cause the Earthquake tilt for a couple turns however I don't have mechanics for that quite yet.

                        This is a very quick and dirty rule, but I think if I was to find a quick and dirty "rule of thumb" to determine damage done by collapsing structures, in the absence of the GM's fiat, I would simply just go with the rule of "apply the greater of either half the structure's size, or its durability as automatic bashing damage to the occupants."

                                        I wanted to follow up a little bit to explain some of the differences between Gods of Iron and Dogs of War in a few different areas.

                                        I want to stress, I don't want this to be taken as the idea that one is better than the other, I just want to establish where the differences between them are architecturally at a high level and what my intentions are. Dogs of War, Armory, and what I do seem to be adjacent, but very different, and I wanted to compare-and-contrast what they do and how they might be able to contribute value in different areas to a game.

                                        So I thought I'd break down my thoughts on both:

                                        Backgrounds, Settings, and Contexts

                                        Dogs of War includes a lot of modern day events and modern conflicts, and casts them in a supernatural/occult light. It provides suggestions, NPCs, and dramatic figures and both conventional and unconventional militaries, provides supporting characters, reference examples of stock characters and antagonists the GM could insert into any campaign or adjust to put into an existing one, tools and suggestions for building military characters and scope for backstories. It provides descriptions of conflict zones and goes into detail about the skills that soldiers tend to have and how they tend to get them, and even suggests events that a character could have gone through during basic training. Dogs of War describes some general situations in military life, such as a character getting hazed by a bully during basic training, all the way to having to deal with the stress and horror of active war zones.

                                        Gods of Iron focuses on mostly hard factors, and assumes that your GM and players will already know what would be the correct grades of ammunition in service at the appropriate time period and location; for instance, the game will draw the distinction between a T-72A Ural, a T-72B, and a T-72B3 obr.2016, which despite being the same tank chassis, all use different amendments of the 2A46 Tank Gun, which in turn enables different ammunition types to be used, and it is the GM's responsibility to set the technology capabilities accordingly. As a result, it offers no historical context into the setting as to do so would be redundant given the target audience. No NPCs or dramatis personae are provided, supernatural or otherwise.

                                        Vehicles

                                        Armoury assumes that you are only ever going to use the armoured fighting vehicles in it for the purposes of a military response to a large and/or powerful monster, and are more done from the standpoint that armoured fighting vehicles are just there to serve as targets or as environmental hazards for your player characters. This approach is intended to be simple and fast to avoid bogging down the game in superfluous detail. Armoury takes a "less is more" approach, to avoid burdening the GM with too many choices.

                                        Gods of Iron provides a brief amount of history on specific units detailing their countries of origin, a bit of their history, and rules specific to each one. Vehicles are created using reference data to translate performance metrics and hard factors obtained from wartime testing or open source intelligence translated into the game via a system of algorithms. Gods of Iron assumes that game balance will be achieved by using vehicles of comparable era and capability with other vehicles of comparable era and capability. Currently the game has more than seven different main battle tanks which are still in service today, most of which have different sub variants and configurations. The depictions are intended to be candid; technological disparities are expected to play out similarly as to they do in the real world, without regard to game balance.

                                        Large Engagements

                                        Dogs of War attempts to avoid large engagements, by allowing units to boil down encounters between large units to a series of dice rolls without needing a map. Assets such as firepower and unit training and competency are boiled down to a series of attribute scores ranked on a scale of 1 to 10, and advantages such as equipment and assets and defence to a series of attribute scores ranked on a scale of 1 to 5 to allow encounters between big groups to be represented through the use of dice rolls. This can then be used to drive framing for what the player characters are doing.

                                        Gods of Iron's vehicle mechanics assumes your troupe will be playing out full scale engagements with combined arms warfare at true-to-scale or near-true-to-scale tactical maps, taking into account not only positioning but also facing and angle-of-impact, including simplified mechanics for armour obliquity. Vehicles have different armour values in accordance to their facing, such as tank armour being thickest on the front, thinner on the sides, and thinnest on the back and on the roof. Mechanics for translating structures, fortifications, and buildings are written with the expectation that players and GMs will be able to either re-create historical structures in game by using open source intelligence and real-world hard factors to faithfully transcribe existant or even theorized defences into the game and play them out in the foreground, possibly using automation or macros to expedite the crunch.

                                        Crunch

                                        Dogs of War and Armoury add very little crunch. Dogs of War includes a number of merits specific to military characters, and Armoury, Armoury Reloaded and especially Hurt Locker include a large number of merits for character options and fighting styles for personalizing your characters and NPCs and changing the way that they perform and play. Armoury introduces the concept of "Force" as a weapon characteristic for large weapons or explosives. This characteristic was brought back and expanded upon in Hurt Locker.

                                        Gods of Iron greatly builds on and expands on the amount of options for weaponry, which includes mechanics for range brackets beyond the usual short, medium and long intervals, has expanded mechanics for combat, including new combat actions such as the tactical advance to move between cover and gain the benefits throughout, new melee additions, gunnery mechanics such as grazing fire, traversing and searching fire, as well as time-on-target, circular error probable and how kinetic rounds experience performance loss over distances due to air resistance, spalling, and how different shell types such as HEAT, HESH, and APFSDS rounds perform, as well as some historical rounds such as APCBC-HE and Common Shell. Explosives are set to be overhauled to follow guidelines according to rules derived from the Kingery-Bulmash engineering equations and fragmentation calculations set by the United Nations International Ammunition Technical Guidelines. A small amount of extra crunch is a consequence. Only a few merits topical to some of the advanced mechanics are included.

                                        Supernatural Elements

                                        Dogs of War is first and foremost, a supernatural game at every step of the way and provides scope and suggestions for placing a supernatural twist on virtually every portion of the game. All of the influential figures are explicitly spelled out to be or influenced by the supernatural. Dogs of War includes a section specifically on the use of different supernatural character types in warfare and how they could fare.

                                        Gods of Iron is at this time with few exceptions largely agnostic of supernatural elements, leaving how the supernatural interacts with modern technologies as something to be determined from the consensus between players and GM. This is to help allow the players and GM to determine the genre of the game that they want to play. Very few weapons are spelled out with regard to how they impact the supernatural or other supernatural effects.

                                        Air Operations

                                        Armoury has simplified rules for aircraft and missiles, which are treated the same way armoured fighting vehicles are: intended mostly as targets or environmental hazards for the contingency of modern military equipment meeting a large and/or powerful monster.

                                        Angels and Aces, which is currently planned, is intended to take into account facets of modern air combat such as missile no-escape zones, energy maneuvering, radar interception, and rates of climb or descent. A preview of some of the content planned for Angels and Aces is available, including a simplified version of the AIM-54C Mk. 60 Phoenix, which emulates time on target and several of the different modes that its seeker head is capable of operating in, including Track-While-Scan Active and Semi-Active Radar Homing, Pulse-Doppler Single Target Tracking, and ACM Active Radar Homing, each with their own special rules. Angels and Aces is currently in preliminary planning.

                                        Naval Operations

                                        Armoury suggests treating a category of "Very Large Vehicles" as their own setting and location and simply uses them as framing for the action, suggesting that it would be pointless to track all the bombs, cannon fire, and missiles and to simply use the large vehicles to provide additional value to the focus on the player characters.

                                        Seas of Steel is also planned to follow either Gods of Iron or Angels and Aces. A preview of some of the content that could appear in Seas of Steel is available, with the inclusion of the US Navy 5"/62 calibre Mark 45 Mod 4 Lightweight Naval Gun and the US Navy 16"/50 calibre Mark 7 Naval Gun, which includes tables for the Mark 8 Armour-Piercing Capped shell, the Mark 13 High-Capacity shell, and the Mark 23 Nuclear shell. Current features include circular error probable mechanics as well as time on target, planned features currently not implemented include Angle of Fall to determine shell effect on both side and deck armour. The use of commercial off the shelf military simulators such as Command: Modern Operations to help make large scale military orchestrations is a planned feature for Zero Hour.

                                        Guns and Other Weapons

                                        Armoury has a number of both ancient and modern firearms and ancient weapons in its table, with rules specific to each. In addition to providing several famous examples,Armoury also includes entries for "Generics" which also can be reskinned to comparable analogues of similar role, effect, and era, allowing the GM to be able to pick generic examples to arm their combatants if they aren't certain what to use, and then tailor them to be narratively appropriate.

                                        Gods of Iron has a number of iconic examples of some specific firearms but lacks generics. Ancient firearms in particular have detailed mechanics on the processes to load, fire, and ready them, breaking down the step of reloading a cap and ball musket from a matchlock and providing differentiation between the different combinations. Unlike Armoury, Gods of Iron doesn't have as much focus on melee weapons but includes a few specific historical examples.

                                        Nuclear Weapons

                                        Armoury dedicates a paragraph to nuclear weapons, but except for a brief mention of "assume everyone in ten kilometres is dead" they are statless and mostly managed via GM fiat.

                                        Gods of Iron has simplified mechanics for nuclear weapon fireball radius, thermal radiation radius, fireball duration, ionizing radiation radius, and air blast radii for both total casualties and widespread destruction, which have been derived from real-world engineering calculations, as well as rules for deployment of Intercontinental Ballistic Missiles and Submarine Launched Ballistic Missiles via a special system called "Playbook Time" designed to emulate the fog of war, operational preplanning, and both launch on warning and survivable second strike capabilities such as road-mobile TELs and ballistic missile submarines. A number of nuclear weapons, including variable-yield nuclear warheads in service with the USAF and USN, and two reference examples: the LGM-30G Minuteman III and UGM-33A Trident II, are fully statted and able to be deployed via Playbook Time. This is done with the goal of capturing the goals, nuances and complexities of declarative nuclear doctrine, deterrance, and nuclear game theory. Nuclear fallout mechanics are currently in progress.

                                        To close out...

                                        Gods of Iron was written assuming that the players and GM are going to be wanting to apply real-world tactics, techniques and procedures to their games at multiple levels, and attempts to capture these nuances in order to turn the game into a tactical simulation, so it doesn't add the same value that Dogs of War or Armoury 1st Edition does.

                                        I hope that I would be able to provide some value to other conventional Chronicles of Darkness players, although now that you mention it, that might be difficult to avoid treading ground that the other books already have.

I'll briefly sum it up as well as my rationale for it below.

                                                        Because strategic warfare has so much disconnect from the actual impact to the people making the decisions, I decided to take a special approach to it. In a nutshell, when players wish to use strategic warfare, I treat it by putting the game into a kind of "Strategic Warfare Time" meant to emulate the structures of nuclear contingencies, and the capabilities provided by early-warning radar (for example, the United States' Ballistic Missile Early Warning System and the newer Upgraded Early Warning Radar that's replacing the older systems), launch-on-warning and survivable second strike capabilities. This "Strategic Warfare Time" is similar to "Initiative Time" but in reverse.

                                                        During this time I would be asking my players to place their all of assets (military facilities, nuclear launch facilities, cities, etc) on the map, then write down and submit the lists of targets they'll be attacking and place them face-down on the table. On our first playtest, I used to ask my players to specify which facilities they are sortieing from to which targets but that got too complex, so now I just ask them to tell me if they're using land based facilities or submarines. Once they're all in, we turn them face-up and then read off what sites, facilities, and cities were targeted and resolve them all one by one.

                                                        It's easiest to use a Commercial Off The Shelf military simulator to help expedite the step of resolving the missiles. Although there's a game called ICBM which is essentially all this and much more, the one I've been moving toward using for doing orchestration of large forces in RPGs these days is Command: Modern Operations, which is a cost-reduced version of Command: Professional Edition. The consumer version sold to the public doesn't allow us to edit the unit database like the professional edition does so this means we can only use units that exist already in it, but for the most part that's mostly fine for what I tend to do. Because translating the results the simulator gives me into the game is often just as important as what it's trying to tell me, I'm including some basic tables for manually determining the results, including for circular error probable in the case that someone uses one or more of them in a tactical capacity where the accuracy becomes relevant within a certain number of metres. This process is repeated for each round of nuclear strikes until all the players have elected to "pass" when we turn their target lists over, or they've run out of weapons to exchange.

                                                        At the present time, the use of these written notes meant to emulate an operations playbook (hence the name "Playbook Time") is very simple and I haven't allowed my players to write any sort of cascading orders (ie: "if that happens, then do this") to be created the way that a simulator like Command: Modern Operations does. However, that in some ways is something of a feature, to emulate the feeling of uncertainty and the fog of war. In real life, the time-on-target for an intercontinental ballistic missile can be as little as fifteen minutes, sometimes even less for a submarine launched ballistic missile if the submarine is willing to launch on a flattened trajectory at a coastal target, so as a result there's not that much time to make decisions on a case-by-case basis. From what I understand in the United States at least, nuclear warfare operations are done through the use of predetermined plans that are put into place and there's a complex system of designated survivorship and redundant communication systems put in place. Many analysts during the Cold War feared a decapitation strike aimed at taking out the leadership or command and control systems for the missiles and planned for it accordingly. The Soviet equivalent (which was inherited by the modern Russian Federation) was the "Периметр" or "Perimeter" system, which we sometimes called the "Dead Hand" as an equivalent which could autonomously launch the missiles in the event of a nuclear strike that decapitated the state. Great Britain has the "Letters of Last Resort". This complicated system of using written notes and doing things play-by-play is meant to emulate all of this.
