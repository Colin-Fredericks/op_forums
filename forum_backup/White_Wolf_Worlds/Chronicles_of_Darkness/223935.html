    My Group is letting me scare them...but im not exactly sure how.

    08-10-2014, 03:48 AM
    So the group I play with has been together since about 04-05, and we alternate every 4 sessions from one game to another. So its vampire four fridays and something else on sundays (we play twice a week obviously) then say aberrant on fridays and DnD sundays. Ive been running adventure! As thats my bread and butter to run, but Hunter: The Vigil is my favorite to play...and thats all the backstory intro you guys need from me I hope. Well the vamp gm and the hunter gm decided to pull a marvel and now their games take place in the same universe. This got me interested in running a hunter game that could possibly fill in the needed gap that niether game fully covers IE- real terror. The vamp game is political, and the hunter game is a rag tag group of mixed compact-conspiracies. But the element of fear isnt there. So, after all this babbling, I toss out to you, fellow gamers-what really would terrify you in a game. Im trying to keep the actual supernatural out of it at the start-they wont be running into vampires or werewolves at all for at least 4-8 sessions. Ive been "studying" criminal minds, dexter, the x-files, slasher movies as well as the books-slasher, inferno, heirs to hell, mirrors, and the rest of the whole hunter line. Ive got ideas...but what say you. Really...what terrifies your characters, or even you?

              You have a lot of options, but the best one depends on your group and how seriously they can take things. If everyone sits down and agrees to play the game straight, taking out the meta and cutting the side chat to a minimum then you can do a lot.

              Horror requires patience and subtlety. You need to slowly, and carefully add in the unnatural, unusual or scary to the story. The build up of the wrong/troubling will create tension, which will get worse the further along the story they get, until the end of the cycle where they'll be frantically trying to end it. The "scare" comes from the contrast between "normal" and where they end up, and how their choices made perfect sense to that point.

              An example: "The house that a player owns is perfectly normal, but mentioning doors open late in a session when the player shut it earlier. Second session make them roll a perception check while sleeping, and no matter how they roll, tell them they saw nothing." Put the players and their characters on edge while slowly increasing the strange and they'll get scared eventually.

              "Scare" games involve shock, twists and keeping your players from getting comfortable. A scare game is easier to do in a short time frame, because it requires less set up and relies less on exacting detail. Things escalate quickly, but unpredictably, which will cause mistakes and action without forethought on the part of your players.

              For example: "A player owns a perfectly normal house, but while they're in another room they hear laughter in the kitchen. When they run into the kitchen, have the TV in an other room blare at full volume and then shut off when they walk in to that room. When they invite the others over to check it out, have the first person to leave the room alone make a grapple check, as a thing launches itself at them from the corner. When the others rush in, they see nothing. Then the TV turns on again."

                  Coming back to expand on the unknown:

                  Involving the supernatural doesn't necessarily mean vampires and werewolves and gribblies, and in fact, is often more effective if it doesn't. Nyx's TV example could pay off even more if it has a long leadup — don't just right away give them the thing as a convenient explanation (however strange and unfamiliar) to blame it on, but rather, as they deal with other problems, seed the strange TV behavior in the background and leave them wondering what it means.

                  Remember that vampires are not scary. What is scary is the things vampires can inflict — injury, death, loss of control. The vampire itself is not a source of horror; we're all familiar with the broad concept of the vampire, so familiar that we even tell jokes about them. There's a muppet on Sesame Street designed to look like one. If you want to bring in crossover elements and have vampires and werewolves going on, use them as political players and free agents, the same as any other non-monster character. They can be threatening, certainly, but they're not intrinsically horrific. Rather, let horror emerge from what they are capable of as people, not what magic they have as monsters.

                  The intrinsically horrific is that which we don't have a familiar concept of. Mysterious, angry force projected from nowhere; things only seen out of the corner of your eye; impossibly free-floating infections. Being able to put a word to something or draw a picture of it makes us more comfortable, and less afraid.

                      Exactly. As a story teller in a massive crossover game I know first hand how tempting it is to just label things as "Vampire did it" and move along. The world of darkness is made of the undefinable and unknowable lurking in the cracks and behind the dumpsters. As stated, a Vampire isn't scary because he's a a bloodsucking immortal (Its that thinking which has lead to sex symbol Vampires), vampire are scary because they are inhuman things, with human minds, that consume the lives of others to keep their dead body moving.

                      Its the little details that get over looked (precisely because they're little and easily assumed) that build something scary. How often has a butcher shop been described as raw, bloody meat sitting on display, while a porcine man in a gore slick smock licks his lips after greeting every customer? The scene is creepy, players will immediately latch on to the butcher as the weird and the game progresses just fine with no serious scare.

                      A scarier butcher shop could be written as: The doorbell to the shop jingles pleasantly as the door swings open in to the butcher shop. Florescent lighting casts a sickly pallor over rows of still bloody meat. The smell of rot worsens the closer you get to the grinning man behind the counter, his flat nose and cold eyes offset by his wide, eager smile. In that example the butcher is weird and creepy, but the scene itself sets people on edge too, increasing that tension between normal and abnormal.

                      Add in a little bit more weird, note how few people are there, comment on how there's a drain in the middle of the shop, or say the doorbell doesn't ring, it groans with the last effort of a dying battery.

                          If you're okay with not sharing the world with the vampire gm's and other hunter gm's world, then really mix it up. Make their metaknowledge unusable. Really play up the unknowable aspect, while hinting that a stand-up fight will more closely resemble a curb-stomp. Have the problem use familiar objects in unfamiliar ways.

                          Another source for horror stemming from powerlessness would be the Cthulhu Mythos. The Shadow over Innsmouth is the seminal piece for many tropes, as well as The Case of Charles Dexter Ward would also be a good read for a human investigating the supernatural.

                          Once is happenstance. Twice is coincidence. Three times is enemy action. Four times is Infrastructure.

                          Rule #2: Never let the game-mechanics get in the way of a good story.

                                      You said... Miami? Captured on South Beach, Satan later escaped. His demons and the horrible Bloody Mary are now killing people. God has fled. Avenging angels hide out in the Everglades. And other tales from children in Dade's homeless shelters.

                                      Other than that, one piece of advice I'll give you.

                                      Be an unreliable narrator.

                                      Oh, don't be obvious about it; the best creep-out I ever did involved the players investigating a subway crash that happened in front of them. They went to a hospital (because one of their friend's wife and daughter were on the train) because they were going to visit their friend Mac and his little girl. They passed a few of the other victims from the train crash; however, coming back the other way, the rooms were empty. The patients there? Had never checked in.

                                      The subway station was awfully clean for a place that just had an accident...

                                      Oh, and the final moments of that session were walking into the girl's room and seeing the place plastered with 4 simple, childish figures, crudely drawn on butcher paper all over the room. The other place they'd seen those symbols? In a notebook they found at the crash, written in some unknown language; the figures were nearly identical.

                                      Play on calm as a way to build threat. Have a big build-up, and then have nothing happen. I don't mean have an anticlimax - I mean have nothing happen. That night where the big turf-war was about to start? Nice and balmy, with nary a peep out of the power players.

                                      EDIT: If you want a really creepy butcher shop, describe one with nothing wrong with it (clean, friendly guy behind the counter, fresh, succulent cuts of meat hanging behind the counter...) in excrutiating detail. Emphasize one thing in particular to an absurd degree (I dunno, go into extensive detail about the man's hands, or about how there's this one speck of dirt on the floor.)
