        The premise of hubris as a theme allows for a capacity of unintended consequences, but within reason (and largely deriving from things done on purpose).             Spending three hours traveling in the Hedge or a temporally-weird patch of the Shadow and coming out a hour later from the material frame of reference is different from speeding up a character's healing process to that of a werewolf's regeneration. An environmental effect or other functional and narrative movement of a character into a frame of reference that can be followed at the same subjective rate a la "year inside, hour outside" is mechanically and thematically different from "the character heals faster" — if you were to give a character with the Arcadian Metabolism Merit some amount of resistant damage, they would heal that damage slower than usual in the Hedge because the Merit's effects specifically reduce their healing time, but if that same character spent two days in the Hedge healing a box of resistant lethal damage and they come out of the Hedge an hour after they went in because of the place's metaphysical quirks, they haven't healed that damage in an hour, they were benefiting from the fact that they could spend two days in the Hedge and return to find only an hour had passed outside.

                        In addition to what Satchel said, I was not referring to the mechanic of Paradox. I was referring to the idea of temporal paradoxes or similar consequences as a result of time travel, or even lesser applications of the Time Arcanum.

                        It's actually funny to me that the First Edition book Imperial Mysteries introduced an idea of outright timeline editing as an example of the Practice of Dynamics, because I think per Second Edition it would actually be the opposite. First Edition said "you need Dynamics because the spell has to be capable of making all of the micro-adjustments that you wouldn't have known about to bring about the desired change", whereas I think that in Second Edition it would more go "you need Dynamics to ensure that a single change has a ripple effect of unintended and far-reaching consequences".

                        This is because my read on what the opening premises of the Time Arcanum are mean that, with the fact that you're always travelling into a past from the frame of reference of an established present, most changes you make will only have the ripples of what is directly affected by that change. It's like, if using Divination while in the past gives you a certainty of outcomes up until the point of the present that is not shared by looking at the real future, then I'd say those things mostly hold fast. If you make a surgeon woefully incompetent the day before a specific surgery such that the most obvious consequences are that the patient died and the surgeon was fired, then those changes will crystallize. But every other person the surgeon saved thereafter? Those survivals have been established as a matter of fact. The timeline will adjust to provide somebody else to have saved them (probably with the greatest subtlety possible).

                        Hence, you can go into the past to destroy some enemy and not return to the present with shenanigans of "oh no, your removal of them from a complex chain of events means that nobody ever developed a certain cancer drug, and huge numbers of other people are also dead as a result".

                        It's also something I would have intertwine with the necessary complexity of major world events that makes it generally impossible to stop them with single discrete actions. The usual time travel resolution of "World War II happened for way more reasons than the decisions of Hitler" combined with a cosmology of causality and time travel that is going to bias towards retaining World War II as an established feature of the past.

                            Anyway, for my own contribution to the thread's premise that is more sophisticated than "Supernal magic is limited by the technical parameters of spell factors":

                            I think one thing that is consistent with spells is that the definition of the Practices and the general premise of the Supernal mean that its spells tend towards specific, discrete and direct effects, rather than stuff that is more fanciful or multi-layered. There are Supernal spells that can effect esoteric and metaphysical things, like the soul, but even then the resulting outcomes tend to be singular, rather than things with more ritual qualities.

                            I think this contrasts with the base powers, and especially the alternate forms of ritual magic, used by other supernatural beings. I often make the mental contrast between a work of Theban Sorcery to cause beetles to emit from the mouths of liars. It's a pretty elementary piece of their magic in the interests of making a statement, but for Awakened power combines a number of effects of varying power levels. It's not just that it's not a straightforward thing to replicate, it's that the act of combining the multiple necessary actions to do so illustrates how the sum of those parts is not really an expression of Supernal power.

                            I see this not just in terms of a mechanical necessity and means to facilitate improvised casting and creative thaumaturgy, but something that lines up with the premise of the Supernal as a state of essential forms. If the Supernal is the state where the true triangle exists, spells are the work of making real, imperfect triangles more like it. That essentialism doesn't lend itself so well to giving things the properties of a complex representative image.

                                    <Snip>
                                    The usual time travel resolution of "World War II happened for way more reasons than the decisions of Hitler" combined with a cosmology of causality and time travel that is going to bias towards retaining World War II as an established feature of the past.
                                    I think I'm more agnostic about it.

                                    Re-reading the intro to the Time Arcanum, and I can't see 2e explicitly supporting this interpretation. It does describe the Past as a spins thread which is hard to alter, while the future is far more unpredictable.

                                    But when you actually go back in time it is unclear whether the threads will be weaving back in the same way. I think I would let a game dictate how much things change. But generally, having historic events remain similar (like the Great Depression still happens even if you change who became President in the US, and the resulting rise of fascism in Europe still happens - even if the details are different... Maybe the Spanish civil war goes differently and Franco rather than Hitler starts World War 2... ).

                                    But the more minute things seem like they should be easy to change. A surgeon doing/losing their job isn't going to have no impact on their patients. It may have hundreds of little changes.... I'm just not sure what a game would want to do with them in a narrative sense. You want your players to have some control over the narrative, some idea what the consequences of their actions will be. So having wild unpredictable changes may be less desirable from a gameplay perspective (never mind the difficulty for the ST of working out how those changes should impact the present).

                                    Games should be fun, and interactions meaningful. If you intend small shifts in the past to result in massive shifts in the present, then you should inform your players of the fact up front. So at least they know what to expect. But (while I don't know this is the most fun option) I can't see the text explicitly saying this chaotic result isn't how time travel should be viewed - I don't think the text is explicit either way.

                                                    Originally posted by orathaic View Post
                                                    I would ask whether Demon exploits are the same as magic (from a supernal perspective) - like can Mages actually *not* study them, given the sort of metaphysics described (the metaphor I'm thinking of it how Demons alter the hardware while Mages are studying the source code... You can't even see what a Demon is doing if you just look at the code...).
                                                    The analogy I usually make is that if reality is a video game, demons are speedrunners, the God-Machine is a tool-assisted speedrun, and mages are Cheat Engine. Demons have the ingenuity, expertise, and reflexes necessary to recognize and utilize glitches, sometimes very complicated ones: things anyone could hypothetically do to yield unexpected results, but most people don't know how to, and which require practiced reflexes to execute successfully. The God-Machine is doing the same thing on a greater scale. Its vast intelligence and calculation network is able to prepare very complicated, far-thought-out interactions, keeping all of the complicated glitch inputs in a very long input sequence memorized and frame-perfect. Mages do not need these complicated occult interactions. They are instead able to see into the "code" of supernal associations and directly alter select values in real time.

                                                    The analogy is not perfect. What makes demons able to execute Embeds is not just ingenuity and reflexes, but the subtle perceptions and workings of the multifoliate demonic mind, which is both physical and metaphysical at once. (This is a trait certain humans who express demon-blooded parentage share enough that they can learn and use Embeds.) Mages have insight into the supernal girding under the universe, but they do not have multifoliate metabrains which can engage in extradimensional thinking. Whether a mage could reproduce a multifoliate metabrain through supernal sorcery (and what kind of awful wreckage they would leave behind in their failed experiments before perfecting the process) is an open question for a chronicle to explore.

                                                    Exploits don't just employ this fluid extradimensional finesse, but use it to direct the flow of Aether in patterns to accomplish their causality-distorting results. If you want to strain the analogy to its breaking point, there is a speedrun technique for Dragon Quest that literally involves temporarily cooking the cartridge on a hot plate, making the game's memory more volatile in order to make a particular glitch more likely to execute. Call it controlled memory damage. A mage trying to reproduce an Exploit (as opposed to reproducing its results through other means) would definitely have to access a source of Aether, successfully manipulate it, and quite likely still need that extradimensional finesse to control that burst of Aether correctly.

                                                        Embeds are variously naturally-occurring or deliberately-engineered and maintained exceptions to common natural law that the God-Machine makes use of, which, taken as part of the occult physics that make Infrastructure work, are functionally in the same vein as Ceremonies and sorcerous Rites; as the God-Machine is the sum total of all its work and the Unchained are largeish pieces broken off of that structure, they have certain advantages in making use of smaller subroutines they manage to remember or get the hang of, while their demon-blooded offspring develop facility with more Embeds and Interlocks as they draw the attention of the God-Machine (in the reverse of order their parents do those things, and getting Embeds that aren't given to them for free in this fashion is more difficult than it is for demons).

                                                        Derived as they are from the toolkit of an architect dwelling in the structure of structures, demons are essentially self-directed complex ritual actors unmoored from their original purposes, capable of incorporating the smaller ritual effects of Embeds into their metaphorical bodies and moving around metaphysical loose ends to make greater deviations from the laws of common reality; demon-blooded instead approach this aggregation of lore at their peril and have less ability to work outside the system's margin of error due to never having broken the leash to begin with; to bring it back to Mage, becoming stigmatic like all active demon-blooded are prevents you from Awakening at the same time as it permanently enmeshes you with the metaphysical framework of the Machine (and Latents becoming mechanically identical to Offspring in any circumstance that would normally cause them to become stigmatics blurs the line with regular stigmatics further).

                                                        Meanwhile, Awakening clears external supernatural conditions of the sort that might steer a character toward integration with the Machine on terms not their own and puts you in a state where magical effects at your disposal are mostly created from first principles instead of tapping into an existing framework, but studying such frameworks gives you more insight into the fundamentals your own magic is drawn from. Mages can absolutely study Exploits — they can be the source of a Mystery like any other supernatural phenomenon — but spells don't work like Embeds do and making effects that operate in such a fashion instead of stringing together combined spells that emulate the end result is the work of archmagic, and the principle of using Aether to turbocharge Embeds into Exploits is adjacent to Enraptured magical savant abilities at best and Paradox at worst; dipping into a volatile well of power from the strains in reality around great magical workings is hard to thematically emulate in a splat whose core ability is already yanking localized ad-hoc rules for reality out of the raw chaos of creation.
