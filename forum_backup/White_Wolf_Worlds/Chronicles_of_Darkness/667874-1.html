                            Was literacy common amongst those cultures? Because the publications by Grim (and their colleagues in other countries who published their works around the same time) did coexist with a marked increase in literacy in Europe. Simply writing down a story does not mean a wider audience actually gets to know it... (My knowledge on Indian, Chinese and Japanese history beyond the broad strokes is not very good though.)

                            As for Grimm, they themselves actually changed their own stories to adopt them to a wider crowd, although they mostly removed the sexual content (the original versions of the fairy tells are quite lewd with babies sucking needles out their mother's hands to wake them up from that century long sleep instead of a prince's kiss) they also worked on the endings. Those stories that served as lessons btw still had happy endings, especially for those following the rules. Their intent after all was partially to teach people that it is good to follow the rules

                            Finally, I did not pay much attention to the Dark Ages, but considering their first story was published in 1812, several centuries after what we in general call the Dark Ages, I am a bit curious. Mind you, Napoleon and the Industrial Revolution are great settings for a campaign, but I just read Dark Ages and dismissed it as an interesting product for me. The Dark Ages as a setting do not have a big draw on me. So why call the product Dark Ages? Then again, I am probably missing something...
