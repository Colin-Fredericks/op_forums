    Remade in Spaaaace!

    04-12-2025, 08:46 AM
    I released Shallow Graves, my Player's Guide to Deviant: The Renegades, in July of last year, and people seemed delighted by it. One sort-of complaint I heard, though, was that I hadn't been very good about telling anyone I was working on it until suddenly I was nearly ready to release it. So, for those of you who aren't on the Discords I frequent or regular visitors to my Patreon page, I wanted to let you know that I'm quite far along on another STV Deviant product called Black Vans, which is a Storyteller's Guide. In fact, I expect to send it to editing by the end of this month, after which there's all that art, layout, and design work for others to do. Here's a brief summary of what's in it:

    Introduction: Driver discusses chronicle design and narrative techniques in the broadest terms — from the logistics of venue and troupe make-up to different approaches to chronicle and chapter preparation and execution.

    Chapter One: Manual digs deep into the Storytelling system's inner workings and how to get the most use out of it as a dramatic engine. It also explores Deviant-specific systems and offers ways to modify the game's mechanics to make them conform more closely to the Storyteller's style and the troupe's preferences.

    Chapter Two: Engine looks at the Deviant's narrative systems and discusses the ways a Storyteller can use everything from character sheets to Touchstones to conspiracy systems to drive the chronicle's stories.

    Chapter Three: Passengers offers a tool for quickly creating Baseline and Deviant characters who can be dropped into the story at a moment's notice.

    Chapter Four: Models looks at parts of the Chronicles of Darkness that Deviant has avoided addressing head-on in its core and first three supplements. It offers ways of including demons, mages, vampires, and other occult creatures (and Remade derived from them) in a Deviant chronicle — whether as a Storyteller character or a player's character.

    Chapter Five: After-Market Accessories takes Deviant out of its familiar genre assumptions and default gameplay loop and places the Broken in exotic settings. It also serves as examples of ways to alter Deviant systems like Variations, Scars, Instability, and conspiracies to fit into a new genre and includes new Merits, Forms, and other mechanics appropriate to those genres.

    That doesn't seem like enough to justify an entire forum thread, though, so I thought I'd share an excerpt of Chapter Five that I'm particularly proud of, which is the Space Opera genre. However, 10,000 words is too much to post in a forum, so I'll give you the first 20% here, and you can read the rest for free on my Patreon here. Enjoy!

    Space Opera

    A space opera is a story set mainly or entirely in outer space, featuring technological and/or social advancements in faster-than-light travel, futuristic weapons, and sophisticated technology, often against a backdrop of galactic empires and interstellar wars with fictional aliens. Historically, it has existed at the intersection of science fiction and fantasy — a soft sci-fi with magical elements or with a reliance on technobabble that may as well be magic. Although it can have elements of horror, it's much more inclined to melodramatic relationships, daring adventures, and galaxy-spanning politics. Here we will focus on four elements:

    Grand Scale: The universe is a big place, and even if action is constrained to a single galaxy, it is far grander than anything humans have ever experienced. Without technology indistinguishable from magic, interstellar travel would be impossible, much less as commonplace as it is. Humanity has spread out among the stars — with a population in the tens of trillions — and yet they are still dwarfed by the vast array of alien civilizations with which they share the galaxy.

    Conflict: War is virtually endemic, and while in some places it expresses itself as cutthroat diplomacy rather than interstellar navies, it's no less intense for that. Conflicts arise both within interstellar empires and between them.

    Subjugation: Planets and star systems are constantly changing hands, and their inhabitants — whatever their species — are expected to serve today's victors as loyally as they served yesterday's. Although this submission is often grudging, the invaders harshly put down any resistance to their rule.

    Wonder: As unspeakably vast as the universe is, human explorers regularly encounter phenomena that defy their understanding and draw them into their mysteries. Countless interstellar civilizations have risen and fallen, and some have left behind traces of their passage.

    Setting: Humanity and the Void Between

    A Deviant chronicle set in the space opera genre has the following features:

    Soultech

    Astronomical distances coupled with hard limits on the speed of travel between points trillions or quadrillions of miles apart were enough to make even the most starry-eyed space enthusiast despair. But that was before the genesis of soultech. For centuries, fringe scientists studied the phenomenon of Divergence, which seemingly unleashed more energy from the human body than anything of comparable mass could contain — short of a macroscopic matter-antimatter annihilation. While the precise understanding of why the human soul can yield such tremendous energy when it is cracked remains elusive, designing technology capable of harnessing it ultimately proved within reach. The resulting field, known as soultech, allowed humanity to catapult itself into an interstellar civilization. This has placed Deviants at the center of all human empires — sometimes as rulers but more often as soldiers on battleships, in starfighters, or as part of invasion forces. "Soultech" is often used in the generic sense of any technology that only functions when operated by Remade.

    Soul Gear: This includes soul weapons (soul guns, soul swords, etc), soul armor, and any number of other pieces of soultech personal equipment. They are Prototypes (Clades Companion, p. 56) that can only be used by Broken.

    Break Drive: Interstellar spacecraft are equipped with break drives, which harness the energy of Remade crewmembers to achieve "breaks" — instantaneous jumps across the vast distances between star systems. With a few rare (and still highly experimental) exceptions, humans are the only species capable of powering a break drive.

    Soul Ship: Also known as "break ships", spacecraft with break drives that have no Remade crew aboard are veritable sitting ducks. Their hulls are no less durable (nor are they any less massive), and they're still capable of performing crude orbital maneuvers and of maintaining life support, but none of their soultech systems function, including shields, thrusters, soul cannons, and sweeps.

    Exos: Exos (sing. Exo) is short for "exoskeleton". Exos are a form of power armor that ranges in size from merely as large as a wheeled ground vehicle to the size of a small skyscraper. They require a single Deviant pilot, and they can be as heavily armed and armored as a small soultech spacecraft. The majority are humanoid, deployed by empires as ground assault vehicles that emulate human motion and magnify the Variations of their pilots. Less commonly, they resemble terrestrial vehicles such as tanks, seagoing vessels, or aircraft. Although some Exos can fly, or even achieve orbital velocity, they lack break drives and so aren't suitable for travel over interstellar distances. Like soul gear and soul ships, Exos require calibration.

    Calibration: All soultech (soul gear, soul ship systems, and Exos) requires calibration before it will function. Calibrating Exos and soul ship systems (other than break drives) excises a Variation from a Deviant and installs it in the soultech. This permanently burns out the capability from the Deviant except when he is using soultech. calibrating a soul cannon based on a Cutting Firearms Lash causes the Remade to permanently lose access to his Lash, for example, but he can still use any soul cannon, Exo, or other soul gear. Whenever a soul ship is constructed, it's a common (but by no means universal) practice to have the Remade who calibrated it become permanent members of its crew.

    Breaking

    A break drive can transport a ship to any point in space whose relative coordinates are known to its navigator. This comes with a few caveats: First, because light can take centuries to reach the observer, a star or planet's location is never precisely where it appears to be. Second, objects in space are in constant motion, which makes it easier to make a break for a point near a star system than it is to break for a planet orbiting a given star. Third, unless the traveler knows both his origin point and destination well, she must first determine the direction and relative distance, which involves parallax navigation. Finally, not all space hazards are visible at a great distance.

    Practically speaking, this means that breaking directly from the orbit of one planet to the orbit of another several light years away is impossible. Instead, a soul ship must make several consecutive breaks with short stops at each interim point to take readings and plot the next break. While one approach is to make breaks of decreasing distances, gradually "zooming in" on one's destination, this is seldom done except by those exploring uncharted space. It's far more common to break to one of a few thousand guide stars, which have minimal hazards, well-charted planets, and, importantly, nearby settlements that can render aid in the event of an emergency. A break drive failure a few light minutes from a space station is inconvenient and expensive; one several light years from the nearest inhabited planet likely means lifelong exile, at best.

    Alternatives to Break Drives

    The break drive is a uniquely human technology, but other space-faring species have developed their own forms of faster-than-light (FTL) travel. Most consider their technology off-limits to humanity. A few are willing to share the fruits of their technology for a price. Outsiders can pay exorbitant fees to travel through borrowed gateways or wormholes, and a handful of otherspace-traveling space captains are willing to haul entire ships as cargo through the dimensions their ancestors so carefully charted. However, it's more common to find non-humans in possession of stolen soul ships and enslaved Deviants than members of gateway-, wormhole-, and otherspace-traveling species willing to permit human travelers to use this technology.

    Gateways: Several species have created gateways that link distant places in a network. Each is a ring several miles across — large enough for all but the most massive ark ships to pass through them. They rely on entangling two identical rings and hauling one or both to a distant location. Ever after, anything entering one ring exits the other. Because of the time scales involved in transporting rings many light years, civilizations that have built networks of these are all unspeakably ancient. Unlike wormhole-diggers, transiting gateways aren't cut off from their home civilizations during the journey; gate ships carry the connection to their homeworlds on their proverbial backs, after all, and a lone gate ship can become a hive from which an entire fleet of warships emerges to meet any threat it might encounter. Systems with two or three gateways are crossroads, while those with dozens of them are natural commerce and diplomatic hubs. Several gate-builder species have forged alliances and connected their networks of gateways together. Gateway civilizations are somewhat prone to fracture; the destruction of a single gateway (whether accidental or intentional) can sever an arm of a bustling empire for millennia. When the gate-builder civilization re-establishes contact with a lost tributary, it might find their cousins sunken into barbarism or extinct. If they survived and flourished, they might be unwilling to return to imperial rule.

    Wormholes: By charting and stabilizing naturally occurring points of pinched time-space, some species have forged interstellar empires. The process of locating and exploring a wormhole is considerably faster than constructing a gateway, but the hole-diggers can't predict where a newly discovered wormhole might lead. Some connect to empty worlds with untapped resources. Many are dead ends — doors into empty space or barren, planet-less stars. A few lead to technologically advanced worlds whose inhabitants respond to the incursion with overwhelming violence. Wormhole civilizations that expand beyond a few systems usually possess propulsion systems that approach light speed. Even so, and even with some of the most advanced AIs and cryogenic technologies in the galaxy, wormhole civilizations are the smallest, with large empires only emerging in regions of space where wormholes naturally proliferate.

    Otherspace: Some species have learned to access alternate universes with compressed or simply different dimensions, such that travel between distant locations in real space can be achieved in a matter of hours or days. Although humans often refer to this as "otherspatial travel" or simply "O-space", most of these civilizations have extensive maps of many different otherspaces. Travel by otherspatial routes can be even faster than breaking, but it's not without its hazards. First, some real space laws of physics don't apply the same way in otherspaces. Second, because otherspatial travel is so reliant on departing from a precise point in real space and returning at an equally precise point in the otherspace, miscalculating a jump point can be inconvenient at best and deadly at worst. Without detailed charts, otherspatial travel to a destination that isn't random is unthinkable. Third, while otherspaces are less populous than real space, they aren't completely uninhabited. In addition to the strange otherspatial beings that live in them, otherspaces are often havens for rebels, pirates, and other undesirables. Otherspatial civilizations arise from adventurous species whose members are willing to throw themselves into dangerous environs in hopes of filling in a little more of the infinite map of the many universes through which they travel. They're also likely to have the most advanced materials technologies — a necessity when a favored metal or polymer exhibits unexpected properties in the otherspace its ships are exploring. For this reason, although otherspaces are numerous, most ships are limited to traveling only through one, and none of them can travel through all otherspaces without risking complete molecular breakdown.

    Hybrid Technology: Virtually no species has independently arrived at multiple methods of FTL travel. In rare cases, however, species with differing technologies have acquired one another's technology, whether through conquest, espionage, or diplomacy. Hybrid FTL holds many advantages over reliance on a single technology, but implementing it requires an investment in infrastructure that even the mightiest empires were once reluctant to make. The arrival on the galactic scene of humans with their break ships has radically changed the calculus, however, and several empires have forged alliances with former rivals to build an FTL meta-system that enjoys all the benefits of gateway, wormhole, and otherspatial travel.
