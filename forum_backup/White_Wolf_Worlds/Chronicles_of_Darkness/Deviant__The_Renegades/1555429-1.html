    People have tinkered with crossover between Chronicles of Darkness games for as long as they've had two splats' core books to rub together. The temptation is understandable. Although the themes and power levels vary considerably from one game to another, they all share the same core Storytelling system. Also, some players are plain more excited about playing as one kind of monster than as another, and sometimes a Storyteller allows this in the interest of ensuring that player's participation (or just because the concept is so awesome that she can't resist).

    For a long time, Chronicles of Darkness games didn't deliberately encourage this. Some made mention of other splats but usually portrayed them as potential Storyteller characters or external factions rather than as potential characters players would run. Beast was the first attempt at creating a game whose protagonists could serve as a bridge between splats. The later Dark Eras books featured multiple splats in the same era, which although arguably intended as an "or", often served as an "and". The Contagion Chronicle was the only supplement to fully embrace crossover, and while the release of a book about supernatural diseases couldn't have had more unfortunate timing, I thought the basic premise was really cool.

    When Deviant came out, I heard a lot of comments about how it could be used to create "pseudo-splats" – monsters with origins in the same myths as the other core books but with a different direction. It enabled werewolves whose transformation was a monthly curse, for example, or vampires with much-simplified mechanics (making them suitable for Storyteller characters or troupes that didn't own a copy of Requiem). Of course, then people came to the Discords with questions about how you'd do a mage in Deviant, and I could only laugh uncomfortably (because the answer is "that's really hard").

    I also heard a lot of observations about how members of a lot of other splats could run Web of Pain conspiracies. Ordo Dracul, alchemists, Seers of the Throne, and more could easily wind up at the head of a conspiracy – or at least participating as agents within one. Although the official position was that there wasn't enough space in the core to address this overlap (the Pleseus Guild was specifically name-checked in the first draft of the core book's fiction, in fact, but we cut it to avoid immediately implying crossover that we wouldn't be addressing), I couldn't deny that they had a good point. You'd have to figure out a way to account for variations in power level between different game lines, but as a narrative went, it was a reasonable one.

    When I sat down to consider what a crossover section would look like in a Deviant Storyteller's guide, I ultimately settled on a few different goals:

    1. For troupes with access to one or more of the other Chronicles of Darkness games, I wanted to provide architecture for crossover play, whether the chronicle focused on the Remade (or on another game line's protagonists), had a fairly equal mix of two or three creature types, or was a complete monster mash.

    2. For troupes who lacked those books, I wanted to provide enough information to introduce a "lite" version of a vampire, werewolf, or other creature – whether it was one played by the Storyteller (recurring ally/antagonist/foil or monster of the week) or one of the players.

    3. I wanted to address ways to include other splats in a Deviant chronicle, including the roles they might play in the Web of Pain.

    4. I wanted to use the exercise to introduce new optional systems that Storytellers could borrow, modify, or use as starting points for rules specific to their own chronicles.

    That was a lot to pack into a Storyteller's guide, especially given I'd need to do it for each of 10 different game lines. I couldn't deliver the full Mage or Demon or Changeling experience in the ~3,500 words I gave myself for each splat, but it's now a lot easier to build a vampire or a cursed werewolf or a local sorcerer who sometimes helps the cohort than it was when I started.

    Today's other free Patreon sample shows off an example of one of the Lesser Forms associated with Beasts (and patrons get the full text of the chapter's introduction, as well as the Beast and Changeling write-ups).

    Web of Pain

    Many monsters successfully integrate into the Web of Pain, and not all of them are Baselines or Remade. Conversely, some monsters have their own counterparts to the Web of Pain, which might coexist with it, oppose it, or even integrate with it. Supernatural creatures who find themselves in positions of power in a conspiracy are much less likely to be its de facto prisoners than are Devoted. However, they fill a similar role mechanically:

    • Supernatural creatures can be added to a conspiracy's roster of occult agents and assigned to a Node with an alternative to the Recruit Devoted Project (Devoted Companion, p. 29) that is specific to their particular splat (such as when using Privileges).

    • A conspiracy that has occult agents can draw upon their connections to their own kind to include functionally identical versions of them in a Send Agents action, using the Shock Troops effect (Devoted Companion, p. 23), except specific to that splat. Those who survive the scene return to their usual business after lending their assistance.

    Threat Level

    Like Remade, occult beings are built with a Threat Level in mind. Each supernatural type has a default Threat Level based on its capabilities and vulnerabilities at character creation. Compare the Threat Level of the chronicle with the Threat Level of the creature:

    • If the creature's Threat Level is equal to that of the chronicle, the character is a typical "starting character" for that type of occult being. If the troupe has access to the game line that features that creature, players (or Storytellers) who wish to play one may use the character creation rules for that game. If the troupe lacks the game or wishes to use simplified, Deviant-focused systems, start with a Baseline character (i.e., skip Step Six of the character creation process in the Deviant core book) and apply the appropriate Greater Form and all its Lesser Forms.

    • If the creature's Threat Level is less than that of the chronicle, apply the template/Forms as above first. For each point by which the chronicle's Threat Level exceeds the creature's, the creature may spend up to 8 Experiences to expand its capabilities. A creature type that is Threat Level 3 in a Threat Level 5 chronicle, for example, has all its Greater and Lesser Forms and can spend 16 Experiences.

    • If the creature's Threat Level is greater than that of the chronicle, do not apply the full template/Form (i.e., skip Step Six of the character creation process). Instead, apply the Greater Form and a number of the creature type's Lesser Forms equal to the chronicle's Threat Level. The character may purchase the other Lesser Forms for 8 Experiences each. If the troupe has and is using the game line that features the creature type, he enjoys the full benefits of the splat once he has purchased all its Lesser Forms.

    Greater and Lesser Forms

    Occult creatures have at least one Greater Form and one Lesser Form. Some also have Scar Forms.

    A Greater Form represents a true non-Deviant character. Except under highly unusual narrative circumstances, a character should only ever have one Greater Form, which includes the Deviant template itself.

    A Lesser Form represents a fraction of the power of a Greater Form ― approximately equal to one Threat Level. A character can have any number of Lesser Forms, but all Lesser Forms should come from the same supernatural creature type (except with Storyteller permission and a strong narrative explanation). A Threat Level 3 creature, for example, usually has three Lesser Forms. If purchased after character creation, a Lesser Form costs 8 Experiences.

    While all supernatural creatures have some inherent weaknesses, some have a Scar Form. This is a package of serious vulnerabilities that partially offsets the advantages the being's occult nature grants. It acts as a reversed Lesser Form, offsetting the Experiences or Threat Level cost of one Lesser Form. A Scar Form can't increase a character's starting Variation Magnitude, only reduce the cost of Lesser Forms within the same creature type.

    For example, Prometheans are Threat Level 3 because they have four Lesser Forms and one Scar Form. A character with the Promethean Scar Form and two Lesser Forms would be Threat Level 1, while one with all four Lesser Forms and no Scar Form would be Threat Level 4.

    Non-Deviants and Beats: Although occult creatures have some inherent disadvantages, none emphasize Scars nearly to the degree that Broken do. Each game line has its own criteria for when characters earn Beats, but we don't have space to reproduce them all here. For that reason, if a character has a Greater Form, consider using these general rules of thumb for when to award a Beat:

    • All characters earn a Beat at the end of a chapter.

    • Early in a chronicle (or soon after introducing a character with a Greater Form), award a Beat whenever the character behaves in a way that draws attention to the ways in which they are different from Deviants ― drinking the blood of an unconscious enemy (for a vampire), for example, or asking probing questions about what it means to be human (for a Promethean).

    • Award a Beat whenever the character acts on a trait in a way that creates a complication for themself or their cohort. This could mean adhering to an Anchor, interacting with a Touchstone, or using a power in a way that endangers the character or bystanders.

        Until Deviant came along, Demon was the game line for which I had written the most words, so it will always have a special place in my heart. Compressing the mechanics of demonic forms was an interesting challenge, but I think the parts I had the most fun with were the bits that interact with Cover, which is what I'm sharing today.

        The bonus free Patreon excerpt has some Geist goodness for you (with full text for both splats available for patrons).

        Integrity Trait: Cover

        Every demon is an impostor pretending to be human. The Unchained has a human body, human relationships, a job, possessions, and all the other trappings of humanity, but it's all an illusion. A demon's Cover rating reflects how successfully his stolen or cobbled-together life holds up to scrutiny — whether he has a birth certificate, a social media presence, old teachers who remember what kind of student he was, and so forth.

        A demon's Cover rating starts at 5 and can be as high as 10. If the demon's Cover rating falls to 0, he loses the active Cover permanently (the Sanctity of Merits applies) but resets Cover to 5; if he loses his last Cover (or if he lacks both the Demon Greater Form and the Legend Lesser Form and loses his only Cover), he can no longer hide his demonic nature and gains the Catastrophic Glitch Condition (p. XX).

        As long as the Unchained plays the role reflected by the Cover he currently wears, he finds it easy to hide his nature. He compromises his Cover when he acts out of character, whether it's telling Baselines that he's a demon, exhibiting skills inconsistent with his artificial backstory, or turning into a biomechanical monstrosity in full view of witnesses or cameras. Whenever he compromises his Cover, the demon rolls Wits + Manipulation.

        Likely Modifiers: High Cover rating (+1 if 6-7, +2 if 8-10) or low Cover rating (–1 if 2-3, –2 if 1). Exhibited overt supernatural powers (–2) or took demonic form (–3) while in the presence of Baselines. Potential witnesses were drunk, high, or in another state that might lead them to disbelieve their senses (+1).

        Roll Results

        • Success: The demon gains the Minor Glitch Condition (p. XX).

        • Exceptional Success: The demon increases her Cover rating by 1 (to a maximum of 10) or regains a point of Willpower. Either way, she takes a Beat.

        • Failure: The demon loses a dot of Cover and gains the Minor Glitch Condition.

        • Dramatic Failure: The demon loses a dot of Cover and gains the Major Glitch Condition (p. XX). She also takes a Beat.

        Compromise: The use of many demonic powers constitutes an automatic compromise, triggering a compromise roll as usual (or one with an additional –3 penalty if the power is a "major compromise"). Unchained with the Demon Greater Form or the Legend Lesser Form roll Wits + Manipulation normally. A character who lacks either Form instead suffers the Minor Glitch Condition (p. XX) — or the Major Glitch Condition (p. XX), if it is a major compromise.

        Lesser Form: Legend

        This Form reflects the demon's ability to adopt a perfect human disguise and integrate herself into society — or a Deviant's supernaturally reinforced alter ego. It has several effects:

        Cover: The Unchained has a complete Baseline persona with all the details of a life lived from birth to the present. The demon is always considered to be "present" in the life of his Cover; even if he takes a weeklong trip to investigate a God-Machine project, his coworkers won't notice his absence, his friends will automatically insert him into their memories of any social gatherings he would have attended, and his children will remember him telling them bedtime stories. He has the Cover Integrity trait, and this is in addition to his normal Integrity trait if he lacks the Demon Greater Form.

        • Legend: Once per chapter, when the Unchained needs access to a Skill or Merit in which he has no dots but which is consistent with his current Cover's identity, he may gain three dots in any combination of appropriate Skills and Merits until the end of the scene.

        • Multiple Covers: A demon with multiple Covers may switch between them once per chapter as an instant action taken while he is alone and unobserved. The new Cover replaces his current appearance in his present location; he doesn't teleport himself to where the artificial person would be.

        • Embodiment: Once per scene, if the demon's behavior remains consistent with his current Cover and it creates a significant complication for himself or his allies, he increases his Cover rating by 1 (to a maximum of 10). This cannot create a new Cover.

        Emotional Control: The Unchained has perfect control over her physiological responses to her emotions. All attempts to judge the demon's emotional state, detect lies, or assess desires based on her involuntary physical indicators automatically fail, instead reading what the Unchained chooses to express. This even affects supernatural attempts to discern the demon's emotions, desires, or candor.

        Natural Aptitude: The demon receives the Eidetic Memory Merit (Deviant, p. 96) for free and is fluent in all human languages that have native speakers.

        Spoof: Whenever the Unchained is subjected to an effect that would potentially reveal her supernatural nature, she may choose to "read" as a Baseline.

        This Form grants a single Cover. Each additional Cover costs 4 Experiences.

        Lesser Form: Unchained Society

        Escaping the God-Machine's service is a beginning. Avoiding its attention is progress. Building a society of Unchained is success, and the demon has established herself as a part of that society.

        Aetheric Resonance: The demon recognizes Infrastructure and Icons when she perceives them with her own senses, and she can automatically sense an expenditure of Aether, the use of angelic powers, or the activation of a God-Machine project within 100 yards/meters. Once per chapter, the Unchained may either determine the general intent of Infrastructure/Icons (or relevant demon/angel/God-Machine powers) or extend her perception of active use of powers to one kilometer until the end of the scene.

        Agenda: Unchained motives for defecting and refusing to return to the God-Machine fall into four broad categories. The demon chooses one:

        • Inquisitors seek to understand some part of the God-Machine's operation, whether to keep clear of its servants or to use that knowledge as a bargaining chip with other Unchained.

        Once per chapter, the Inquisitor may suggest a connection between two seemingly unrelated things and have them be related (subject to Storyteller approval), or she may add +3 to a Mental Skill roll.

        • Integrators wish to serve the God-Machine, but on their own terms or in their own time. They don't hate their creator or its angelic servants, but they have some reason to remain Unchained.

        Once per chapter, the Integrator may defuse a violent situation, causing all aggressors to put down their instruments of violence until the end of the scene (a peace he and his allies can no more break than their opponents), or he may add +3 to a Skill roll to evade, outwit, persuade, or research someone who regards him as an enemy (but not to attack).

        • Saboteurs intend to destroy or frustrate the designs of the God-Machine.

        Once per chapter, the Saboteur may suggest or set off a chain of random events that inserts chaos (and possibly an Environmental Tilt) into the current scene (subject to Storyteller approval), or he may add +3 to a Physical Skill roll.

        • Tempters seek worldly power, influence, and comforts.

        Once per chapter, the Tempter may choose to receive VIP treatment at a location that isn't actively looking for her or her companions (including conspiracy facilities), or she may add +3 to a Social Skill roll.

        Gadgets: The Unchained can imbue mundane objects with their Embeds and Exploits, resulting in a Gadget usable by anyone. The demon may purchase Gadgets based on an Embed/Exploit she has at a cost of 1 Experience — or 2 Experiences for one she doesn't have.

        Once per chapter, the Unchained may replace one Gadget with another with the same Experiences cost, but this is a compromise (p. XX). Replacing a Gadget causes the existing one to lose its properties or pass to a new owner outside of the cohort.

        Pacts: Demons can enter contracts with anyone who willingly (i.e., without supernatural mental influence being involved) signs them, representing virtually any kind of agreed-upon exchange between the parties. Pacts allow Unchained to create and improve Covers while granting the signatory useful gifts. Negotiating a pact requires at least an hour and is usually resolved using the Social Maneuvering system if the demon needs a specific person to sign who may be hesitant to do so. Assuming he finds a willing signer, the Unchained can forge a pact to accomplish one of the following:

        • Friendly Pact: The demon crafts a pact primarily to give a temporary benefit to someone — whether it's an ally or a party she owes a favor. Spend a point of Willpower. Until the end of the chapter, the signee gains up to four dots in a new Social Merit or increases his rating in an existing Social Merit by two. This can't affect Deviant-only Merits or the Fast-Talking Merit.

        • Commodity: Among the Unchained, pacts are a fungible commodity, and the demon crafts a pact with the intent of transferring it to another of his kind in exchange for a favor. Spend a point of Willpower. Until the end of the chapter, the demon gains up to two dots in a new Merit or increases her rating in an existing Merit by one. Except with Storyteller approval, this can only affect the following Merits: Allies, Contacts, Library, Mentor, Resources, Retainer, Safe Place, Staff, or Status. Multiple pacts can affect the same Merit.

        • Reserve: Rather than cashing it in or selling it right away, the demon saves it for later. Spend a point of Willpower. The Unchained adds the pact to his reserve and may use it later for the Commodity or Patch Job effect.

        • Patch Job: This requires the Legend Lesser Form. Spend a point of Willpower. Exchange five pacts for a new Cover or exchange any number of pacts to increase the Unchained's Cover rating by 1 per pact (to a maximum of 10).

        • Soul Pact: This requires the Legend Lesser Form. The demon convinces someone to sell himself to her entirely, signing the pact in his own blood. This always requires Social Maneuvering, typically with a large number of Doors; even the most naïve Baseline isn't usually willing to sign one of these without considerable cajoling. If successful, spend a point of Willpower to gain the signer as a new Cover. The demon may instead keep the soul pact in reserve, as above, in which case it counts as five pacts or can be cashed in later for the new Cover.

            Remember earlier how I said I always knew mages would be tricky? I was right. However, it wasn't impossible. The Techniques system from Shallow Graves makes it a whole lot easier to emulate improvised magic than could have been done with the core book alone. Moreover, rendering Awakened magic as several separate Lesser Forms allows a Storyteller to pare down the splat to cover a range of lesser sorcerers – apprentices, hedge wizards, or occult scholars who have learned just enough magic to make themselves a danger to themselves and others.

            Hunters are the splat most likely to befriend or run afoul of a Deviant cohort, and you can read a bit more about them for free at the Patreon. Full text of both splats is available to patrons.

            Arcana

            Mages divide the power over which they have mastery into 10 Arcana (sing. Arcanum). By default, a mage's rating in an Arcanum is 0, meaning she has no power over it. Awakened who have learned an Arcanum have a rating in it ranging from 1 to 5 dots.

            Whenever the mage gains her first dot in an Arcanum from any of the Lesser Forms, she chooses four Variations within its purview with the approval of the Storyteller. She may cast spells emulating those Variations at Magnitude 1. Variations like Hyper-Competence and Cursed Flesh that are actually collections of related Variations must be selected as individual spells for each specific Variation. Hyper-Competence (Brawl) 1 is a different spell from Hyper-Competence (Persuasion) 1, for example.

            Whenever she increases her Arcanum rating, she increases the maximum Magnitude of these Variations by 1.

            Lash is a special case. A Bruising Lash is only available at Arcanum 3 or higher, and a Cutting Lash is only available at Arcanum 4 or higher. Only Gentle Lashes are available at Arcanum 1 and 2.

            Spells can be used to improvise Techniques, subject to the normal once-per-chapter restriction.

            Death: This Arcanum concerns cold, darkness, decay, enervation, ghosts, and souls.

            Fate: This Arcanum includes blessings, destiny, fortune, hexes, oaths, and probability.

            Forces: This Arcanum commands electricity, fire, gravity, heat, light, movement, sound, and weather.

            Life: This is the Arcanum of animals, disease, food, healing, metamorphosis, Physical Attributes and actions, and plants.

            Matter: This is the magic of alchemy, crafting, gases, liquids, shaping, solids, stasis and transmutation.

            Mind: This Arcanum concerns communication, dreams, emotion, hallucination, language, memory, Mental and Social Attributes and actions, mental projection, and thought.

            Prime: This Arcanum commands Awakened magic, illusion, Mana, and truth.

            Space: This is the magic of conjuration, distance, names, scrying, separation, sympathy, and warding.

            Spirit: This Arcanum concerns animistic spirits and otherworlds.

            Time: This is the magic of prophecy, change, postcognition, prophecy, time contraction and dilation, and time travel.

            Awakened Magic

            The magic of the Wise involves the mage imposing his will on reality to make his vision (what the Awakened call the imago) real. Only a rare few accomplish this in the first place, and it takes long practice to sustain the imago for more than a few minutes at a time.

            By default, a spell has the following properties:

            • Casting a spell requires an instant action and a successful roll using the mage's Gnosis (minimum 1) + dots in the Arcanum of the spell. The mage must choose to either release or contain the Paradox the spell generates before making this roll (see below).

            • The spell hits one subject of Size 5 or less (i.e., valid targets), or an area equal to an arm's-length circle around a point.

            • The mage must be touching the subject of the spell, or be casting on herself, or must make a successful Gnosis + Athletics/Firearms – Defense attack with a range of 10/20/40.

            • Instead of wielding a non-Directed spell himself, a mage may choose to grant it to a target as with Sacred Flesh (Boon), allowing them to enjoy the benefits as though they had the Variation during the duration. The benefits last until the end of the scene (or until they are dispelled, or the mage dismisses them as a reflexive action) and count the mage's Scar Power as 1. Directed Variations and effects (including a ranged Lash) can only be wielded by the mage, not granted.

            • An unwilling or unknowing sapient creature can't be targeted if their relevant Resistance Attribute exceeds the mage's Arcanum rating + 1.

            • If called upon to make a Scar Finesse roll to exercise exacting control over a Variation, the willworker uses Gnosis (minimum 1) + Arcanum rating.

            Reach and Grasp

            A mage's Grasp represents the safe limits of his magic. When casting a spell, a mage's Grasp is equal to his Arcanum rating. A spellcaster can increase his Grasp by 1 for a given spell by undertaking a ritual lasting at least an hour.

            Every spell has a Reach based on how difficult it is to cast. By default, a spell's Reach is equal to the Arcanum rating (i.e., Variation Magnitude) at which it is being cast. A mage who wishes to overcome the usual limits of her magic can do so by increasing its Reach. Some of these parameters we'll describe later, but some are universal to all Awakened:

            • Range (+1 Reach): The spell can be targeted at sensory range.

            • Potency (Variable): Increase Scar Power and Reach on a one-for-one basis.

            • Spell Capacity (Variable): If the mage already has active spells, add Reach equal to the number of active spells.

            As long as a spell's Reach doesn't exceed the mage's Grasp, it remains fully within her control. If it exceeds her Grasp, she risks Paradox.

            Paradox

            Paradox is the fruit of hubris. If a spell's Reach exceeds the mage's Grasp, he must choose either to release or attempt to contain the Paradox. Either way, the Storyteller rolls the spell's total Reach (not just the amount by which it exceeded his Grasp) as a dice pool (the Paradox roll):

            • If the Paradox is released, each success imposes a Persistent Scar of a Magnitude equal to the Arcanum rating of the spell on someone in the scene of the Storyteller's choosing (other than the mage). This lasts until the end of the chapter (until the end of the scene for non-Baselines), although the Storyteller may extend it until the end of the story (or end of chapter for non-Baselines) or make it permanent (or end of story for non-Baselines) by spending an additional one or two successes respectively. If the Paradox roll achieves more successes than the spellcasting roll, the Storyteller chooses whether the spell fails outright or succeeds but creates a significant complication.

            • If the mage attempts to contain the Paradox, he instead contests the Paradox pool with his Wisdom (or one die if he lacks the Mage Greater Form). For this spellcasting, the mage suffers a Controlled Scar of the Storyteller's choosing with a Magnitude equal to the successes on the Paradox roll in excess of those on the Wisdom roll (to a maximum of Magnitude 5). If the Paradox roll achieves more successes than the spellcasting roll, the Storyteller chooses whether the spell fails outright or succeeds but imposes a Persistent Scar of her choice on the mage; this Scar has a Magnitude equal to the Arcanum rating of the spell and lasts until the end of the chapter.

            • The Scars and complications produced by Paradox rolls should reflect the Arcanum of the spell and the mage's intent or state of mind. The player may make suggestions, but the Storyteller is the final arbiter.

            Dissonance: Baseline witnesses erode Awakened magic. If the spell is Subtle, reduce the spell's total Reach by 1 at the end of each chapter it (or its target) was in the same scene as a Baseline; this diminishes a spell factor of the Storyteller's choice. If a spell's Reach is reduced to less than zero, it is dispelled. If it was Subtle but shared the scene with many Baselines, or if it was Overt, apply this at the end of each scene. If it was Overt and many Baselines witnessed it, apply this at the end of each minute (each turn in action scenes). Baselines rationalize or forget any Overt magic they witness, although they still suffer a breaking point.

                I think it's fair to say that Mummy and Promethean are weird games in that they include some quite experimental mechanics and gameplay loops. Mummy doesn't believe in linear character advancement (or time). Modeling the variability of Roles and Refinements in the space allotted was an interesting challenge, and I'm sharing the result of that today.

                You can also suffer Sybaris by witnessing the awe-inspiring miracles called Utterances, which are available for free at the Patreon (and the full text of both splats is available to patrons).

                Transmutations

                Transmutations are supernatural powers granted by the Refinement Lesser Form (p. XX), transforming a Promethean's humours to yield potent tools for her Pilgrimage. Each Transmutation comprises four-tiered Alembics, Scar-free Variations that express the Transmutation's alchemical principle:

                • The first tier is a Persistent Magnitude 1 Variation. If Toggled, it gains the Reflexive keyword.

                • The second tier is a Magnitude 2 Variation usable once per chapter. If Perpetual, it becomes Toggled.

                • The third tier is a Magnitude 3 Variation usable only once per chapter. If an Adaptation is used to reset this Variation, it inflicts a minor Instability each time after the first that it's used during the chapter. If a character has the Promethean Greater Template, she must instead spend 1 Pyros on top of the usual reset cost. If Perpetual, it becomes Toggled.

                • The fourth tier is a Magnitude 4 Variation usable only once per story, with each additional use in the same story inflicting a medium Instability (or costing 3 Pyros, if the character has the Greater Form). If Perpetual, it becomes Toggled.

                Alembics come in many forms, and the player and Storyteller can determine which Variations work best with each character's Transmutation and Roles; each Transmutation's general principles are noted below.

                Alchemicus: Internal and external innovation through alchemy (e.g., Matter Control — Shallow Graves, p. 95).

                Benefice: Fostering community and connection (e.g., Refuge — Shallow Graves, p. XX).

                Contamination: Finding, purging, or encouraging impurities (e.g., Deadly Ichor).

                Corporeum: Achievingphysical perfection (e.g., Superhuman [Attribute]).

                Deception: Blending in with (or hiding from) Baselines (e.g., Mimicry).

                Disquietism: Redirecting or inflicting Disquiet (e.g., Creeping Dread).

                Electrification: Controlling electricity and machines (e.g., Electrokinesis).

                Luciferus: Inspiration and light manipulation (e.g., Blessing).

                        Information Warfare

                        While many terms in Deviant: The Renegades are perfectly serviceable, some have negative connotations world powers would prefer to avoid inserting into public awareness. They've deprecated these terms and replaced them with more marketable ones. Oftentimes, they've kept more acceptable terms for the same thing.

                        Divergence: This term has fallen out of use. While humans are still capable of Divergence in the traditional sense, it's rarer than ever before, and few scientists have any interest in the crude methodologies of the past. Advertisers instead focus on and promote product groups called Platforms. When speaking about the process more generally, the most common terms are variations on the words "Upgrade", "Alter", "modify", "improve", and "transform".

                        Clade: "Platform" refers to the products that grant Variations (Upgrades), rather than to those who have them, but given the problems that often arise when mixing Upgrades of different Platforms, it has become nearly synonymous with the traditional term "Clade".

                        • Cephalist: "Psychic" remains the dominant general term, but earlier generations of influential Cephalist Devoted have cemented the terms "Biokinetic", "Clairsentient", "Pyschokinetic", and "Telepath" into the popular lexicon. Today's Progenitors install psychic powers using neurosurgery, patented cocktails of hallucinogens and anesthetics, or through classes or rituals prefaced by dire NDAs. Upgrades administered in this way are typically described as Gifts.

                        • Chimeric: Despite efforts to coin a new term like "Host" or "Recipient" — as well as a desperate attempt to elevate "Hybrid" — the one that has stuck is "Splicer". The transplantation of vat-grown organs, limbs, and tissue is the most common approach when working on this Platform, although injections of bioengineered organisms are poised to overtake such inelegant methodologies within the next decade. Upgrades administered in this way are typically called Splices.

                        • Coactive: "Infused" and "Vessels" are the most common terms for those with Upgrades of this Platform. Once the province of mystical conspiracies, mastery of the principles on which these Divergences operate has rendered the physics and physiology of them less arcane. A clinic that services Infused requires careful consideration of its location — proximity to an appropriate otherworldly portal, favorable geomantic properties, or a hardened electrical grid and backups to prevent the holding tank of captured spirits from escaping. Upgrades of this Platform are typically called Infusions.

                        • Invasive: "Cyborg" is the overwhelmingly dominant term, although some insist on calling both the Platform's Upgrades and recipients "Bionics". For the most part, installation matches the popular depiction of cybernetics in 20th century science fiction — surgical replacement of living tissue with machines and the implantation of microchips and other tiny electronic devices under the skin. Installing occult artifacts has proved more difficult, although engineers are working on it. Upgrades of this Platform are called cybernetic implants, which most render either as Cybernetics or Implants.

                        • Mutant: The official market-speak for a recipient of Upgrades of this Platform is "Artisanal", but most of those in the biz call them "Customs". Both evoke the personal touch this Platform's Upgrades require. Careful screening is a necessary step that allows an organization to personalize the Upgrade to the recipient's precise genetic sequence, ensuring that the injection that comes at the end of the process will result in gene-deep modification of the customer, rather than deadly anaphylactic response. Mutant Upgrades are known as Therapies.

                        Deviant: As a general term for those who have had Upgrades installed in them, "Deviant" is deeply deprecated, and "Broken" is unthinkable. "Remade" lingers on, but "Upgraded" is more common, and "Altered", "modified", and "transformed" also enjoy regular use. The Upgraded of the present age are hardly recognizable as the technological descendants of yesterday's Broken. The process is no longer random, and while rare installation complications are possible, they're seldom fatal. Moreover, manufacturers have the means to reverse unwanted transformations, although Upgrades are expensive enough to install that few lay out the additional capital for Downgrades (no manufacturer offers money-back guarantees that cover those installation costs, even if a few are willing to refund the cost of recently installed Upgrades).

                        Variations: The most common catch-all term for Variations is "Upgrades", although "modifications", "Alterations", and "improvements" all see use. Other terms are largely Platform-specific (see above). Like Variations, Upgrades have limitations or side-effects. Unlike them, these side-effects generally don't worsen unless the Remade has a new Upgrade installed or replaces an existing Upgrade with a more powerful version.

                        Touchstone: The installation of Upgrades doesn't involve soul-shattering trauma that blasts away the recipient's sense of self. The Altered don't require or have Loyalty or Conviction Touchstones. Nothing prevents them from deriving satisfaction from little indulgences and grand gestures. In practice, however, the Upgrades are an expensive investment that places the transformed deep into debt, and merely paying the interest on those debts is an undertaking that demands most of the Altered's time and energy. Instead of Touchstones, Upgraded have Sponsors and Patrons (collectively known as collectives), each of which has a Figurehead who serves as the Remade's primary contact with the Sponsor/Patron as a collective group.

                        • Sponsors: These are collectives that expect or demand that the Upgraded perform work for them. Most often they are an employer who knows very well what modifications the Altered has and fully expects her to use them in the company's service. Others are those who bankrolled the installation of one or more modifications — be it a government agency, lender, former employer, or rich relative. Job assignments from a Sponsor are usually issued with clearly delineated objectives and rules of engagement. Refusing them has consequences — and failure is frowned upon — but as long as the Remade made a bona fide effort to complete the mission a Sponsor is still inclined to pay her. Sponsors more closely resemble Loyalty Touchstones.

                        • Patrons: These are collectives to whom the Upgrade owes nothing but who will reward the Remade's service with money or future favors. They can include gig employers who pay handsomely for contract work. Rescuing strangers from street toughs or stealing from the super-rich and giving the proceeds to charity can win friends who turn out to have valuable connections and unexpected resources. Job assignments from Patrons are usually more flexible in how the Altered carries them out, but they tend to pay poorly unless the transformed is wildly successful. Patrons more closely resemble Conviction Touchstones.

                        Conspiracy: The conspiracies that once defined the Web of Pain are a thing of the past. Most are now publicly traded companies and conglomerates that openly advertise their products to potential consumers. A minority are government agencies that closely guard the secrets of their Upgrades for national security reasons. Most of the cults and fringe religions that dabbled in the Divergence never found a way to mass-produce Deviants and are rapidly dying out. People generally want a predictable transformation, not one subject to the whims of chance and as likely to kill them as to Upgrade them. The Remade instead find themselves entangled in a Network of Sponsors and Patrons that make bids for the cohort's time and talent.

                        Instability: The changes wrought by installing Upgrades don't make the transformed prone to disintegration. Instead, the Stability track becomes Credit and measures her capacity to endure Pressure, which is the sum of debts owed to various interested parties. Increased Pressure doesn't affect the side-effects of the Altered's Upgrades (her Scars), but nor is it a pathway to increased occult power. If the rightmost box of a character's Credit track is filled with major Pressure, her creditors have decided to remotely disable (or forcibly repossess) her Upgrades, which they successfully do at the end of the current chapter. This form of End Stage doesn't supercharge the modified's Upgrades, but its only cure remains successfully completing a mission for a Patron, which represents a big score that wipes out a crucial debt.

                        Devoted vs. Renegade: Although their mechanics are identical, current parlance is Suits vs. Freelancers. Suits are those who do more work for Sponsors than for Patrons, while Freelancers are the opposite. Upgraded can switch "sides" once per story and may do so even if they have the "wrong" ratio of Sponsors to Patrons (i.e., a Suit with more Patrons than Sponsors or a Freelancer with more Sponsors than Patrons).

                        Icon: Although this term is commonly used, it means something very different in this genre. An Icon is a user's virtual self or avatar, which exists simultaneously with the user's physical body. It can assail (and be assailed by) rival Icons and can often interact with machines and Upgrades to influence the material world (often rendered as Realspace). Iconspace is parlance for the virtual world that overlays our own, which often serves as a battlefield between Icons.

                        Node: Like Icon, this term carries a different meaning in this genre. A node is a data access point that permits rapid Iconspace travel between Realspace points or that serves as a gateway into an artificial environment in Iconspace that has no corresponding location in Realspace.

                                Heroes

                                While "cohort" still sees use as a collective noun for the players' characters in this genre, "Deviant", "Remade", "Broken", and "transformed" don't describe them. Instead, they are referred to as heroes in-world and protagonists within this section.

                                Those who have gifts like heroes but have turned them to selfish or evil ends are villains. Inhuman creatures or beings that present a threat to the heroes' interests are monsters. Collectively, they are the chronicle's antagonists.

                                Origin: Heroes come into their power by many paths, all of which fall into one or more of the categories Origin represents in Deviant. However, this does not shape the protagonists to the degree that it does Deviants. Instead, a hero's Origin reflects a combination of Heritage and Upbringing. Heritage is birthright. Usually, it refers to the sapient species to which the character belongs and is evident in her physical traits. Beast-born have animal-like facial features, for example, and pixies have vestigial wings. Upbringing is culture. It reflects early education and experience — the games they played as children, the skills their caregivers considered the most basic and most important, and what their relatives and neighbors valued or reviled. Heritage and Upbringing are an ongoing conversation carried on from generation to generation. They bleed into one another, such that one can easily be mistaken for the other. A human raised among gray fractals looks human, but he may learn to perceive ghosts the way many members of his adoptive family do, or perhaps he has taken on (or uses makeup to mimic) some of their natural pallor. Nor are Heritages and Upbringings monolithic. Some shifters are deft at adopting disguises, for example, while others manifest tools of wood and metal from their living flesh. Similarly, the children of parents of different Origins could exhibit the traits of one, the other, neither, or both.

                                Training: Heroes manifest their abilities in ways as diverse as any of the Clades, and traits normally associated with Clade can often be entangled with those of Heritage and Upbringing. In place of Clade, heroes have Training, which is a combination of Profession and Vocation. A character's Profession is her mundane education, training, and experience — one that prepared her to practice a trade that would have allowed her to live a quiet, productive life, even if it was as a drunken gambler. Her Vocation is the specialized training and unique experiences that make her well-suited to the life of a hero. For some, Vocation is a natural progression from Profession. The acolyte at the temple receives a mission from the lips of the goddess she serves — and the supernatural abilities she will need to carry it out. For others, Vocation is a stark departure from their intended Profession. A farmer whose village is burned by raiders might take up arms and become a warrior to protect others like it from future attacks. A hero carries both sets of experiences with him and makes use of everything he has learned. Nor is a character locked into a Vocation once he chooses one. Some heroes are dilettantes in many disciplines (and that dabbling defines them as much as the single-mindedness with which other heroes pursue their Vocations), while others make the decision to surrender one set of supernatural advantages in favor of another.

                                Ideals: All heroes live according to a personal code. This has five aspects — Loyalty, Honor, Service, Conviction, and Adversity. Each character has one Ideal of each type. Importantly, the cohort shares at least one Ideal (other than Loyalty):

                                • Loyalty: The bonds between members of a cohort are forged in blood and trust, and they are not broken readily or without consequences. Heroes protect one another and stand with one another. This is especially true of members of their cohort, but it extends to other heroes who request (or quite obviously need) aid. A hero doesn't leave her companions to die and doesn't betray them to an enemy.

                                • Honor: Heroes have principles by which they live. They must abide by a set of precepts, whether they are prescriptions (actions they must take) or proscriptions (actions they're prohibited from taking). Those who adhere to this sacred code stand as exemplars of heroic ideals. Those who stray from it stain the reputations of all heroes and risk a swift slide into tragedy.

                                • Service: Most people can't do the things heroes make look so easy. A hero is obligated to protect the innocent and help the needy as best she can. This doesn't demand constant self-sacrifice and unfailing selflessness, but it requires that the hero seldom refuse reasonable requests and that she not discount out of hand those that serve a clear common good while being within her skillset.

                                • Conviction: All heroes are fierce opponents of something, although exactly what depends on the hero. It could be something tangible like dragons or vampires. It could be a pattern of behavior, such as theft or murder. It could be something abstract, such as corruption or poverty. When faced with someone or something that embodies his Conviction, a hero either rises to the occasion or regrets letting the opportunity pass.

                                • Adversity: Heroes aren't perfect. They have limits and make mistakes, but these don't diminish their claim to the title of "hero". Indeed, repeatedly facing, and rising above, their fears and flaws is a recurring theme in the lives of all heroes.

                                    I clearly missed a trick by not scheduling this one two days later, just so I could make the Christmas at Ground Zero joke. Ah well. Today we're talking about the Post-Apocalypse genre. I love the elegance of the Resources Merit, but this is one of those genres where it sucks the tension out of a story, so I quickly realized I would need to provide systems for modeling the scarcity that is a feature of the genre, which is what I'm sharing today. As it turns out, some of these systems made a good balancing device for genre-hopping chronicles involving time travelers, visitors to otherworlds, and more, so they wound up in the chapter's introduction.

                                    Resources

                                    Otherworldly vendors seldom accept currencies from worlds not their own. The almighty dollar avails a visitor nothing in the Underworld, and you're not going to be able to pay for dinner on a distant planet with your American Express card. Barter is the norm, which creates three obstacles:

                                    First, commodities are much less portable than cash or credit cards. Even jewelry is heavy, and it's hard to make change for a diamond ring. Characters can only carry so much before the weight of their trade goods starts to slow them down.

                                    Second, travelers to otherworlds usually don't have a secure place to store their valuables. Anyone can be robbed and lose everything of value they're carrying at the time, but for a visitor, this is much more likely to be everything they have until they can return home to resupply.

                                    Third, barter is inefficient. It's rare that a visitor will receive the full value of what she is offering in trade. Less technologically advanced societies usually don't have much to offer in exchange, and more technologically advanced societies usually won't consider the visitor's goods worth trading.

                                    Systems

                                    • Resources: Characters with the Resources Merit can't call upon it when in an otherworld.

                                    • Capacity: Characters can reasonably carry or wear a number of items of Size 2 no greater than their Strength + Stamina or their Size (whichever is greater) without difficulty. A character who exceeds this suffers the Fatigued Condition after six hours of carrying the burden. A character can't carry more than twice as many Size 2 items as their Strength + Stamina (or twice their Size, if greater). Three Size 1 items count as one Size 2 item. Items of Size 3 or more (but which are still portable) count as two Size 2 items. A vehicle can carry its Size in Size 2 items, in addition to its driver (and any equipment she is carrying); passengers (with carried equipment) subtract their Size from this capacity.

                                    • Barter: An item can be bartered for one of lesser Availability with a successful Social action (a Presence or Manipulation + Persuasion roll resisted by Resolve is the most common). Acquiring something of equal Availability generally requires an extended negotiation that can be modeled with the Social Maneuvering system.

                                    Advanced Technology

                                    Time travelers and aliens from more technologically advanced worlds often bring some of their tools with them. Whether it's a shotgun in the Dark Ages or walkie talkies on a distant planet, these high-tech devices can give a visitor a considerable advantage. However, the production and maintenance of advanced technology relies upon the availability of other advanced technology. A gun becomes useless in the absence of ammunition, and creating ammunition for a modern firearm requires the availability of materials and tools suitable for the job, and those tools and materials often rely on the availability of other tools and materials.

                                    Advanced technology deteriorates quickly without regular maintenance, and this maintenance is usually only available in places where that technology is readily available.

                                    System: At the end of any chapter in which a piece of advanced technology is used, roll a dice pool equal to 6 – Availability. On a failed roll, the device's owner takes a Beat, and the device ceases to function or breaks until it can be repaired using tools and materials at least as advanced as those used in its creation.

                                    Durable: Some advanced devices are built to keep working even in the absence of adequate maintenance and materials. Increase the device's Availability by one to increase the dice pool above by two — or increase it by two to increase the dice pool by four. So, an assault rifle (Availability 3) normally rolls 3 dice (6 – 3 = 3). A durable one becomes Availability 4 but rolls 4 dice (6 – 4 + 2 = 4) or becomes Availability 5 and rolls 5 dice (6 – 5 + 4 = 5).

                                    Prototypes: Prototypes purchased with Resources or otherwise acquired without spending Experiences are subject to the rules for advanced technology; eventually, they will break, run out of ammo/battery, or otherwise cease to function as intended. Prototypes acquired through the Prototype Merit aren't subject to this deterioration.

                                        Soultech

                                        Astronomical distances coupled with hard limits on the speed of travel between points trillions or quadrillions of miles apart were enough to make even the most starry-eyed space enthusiast despair. But that was before the genesis of soultech. For centuries, fringe scientists studied the phenomenon of Divergence, which seemingly unleashed more energy from the human body than anything of comparable mass could contain — short of a macroscopic matter-antimatter annihilation. While the precise understanding of why the human soul can yield such tremendous energy when it is cracked remains elusive, designing technology capable of harnessing it ultimately proved within reach. The resulting field, known as soultech, allowed humanity to catapult itself into an interstellar civilization. This has placed Deviants at the center of all human empires — sometimes as rulers but more often as soldiers on battleships, in starfighters, or as part of invasion forces. "Soultech" is often used in the generic sense of any technology that only functions when operated by Remade.

                                        Soul Gear: This includes soul weapons (soul guns, soul swords, etc.), soul armor, and any number of other pieces of soultech personal equipment. They are Prototypes (Clades Companion, p. 56) that can only be used by Broken.

                                        Break Drive: Interstellar spacecraft are equipped with break drives, which harness the energy of Remade crewmembers to achieve "breaks" — instantaneous jumps across the vast distances between star systems. With a few rare (and still highly experimental) exceptions, humans are the only species capable of powering a break drive.

                                        Soul Ship: Also known as "break ships", spacecraft with break drives are veritable sitting ducks when they have no Remade crew aboard. Their hulls are no less durable (nor are they any less massive), and they're still capable of performing crude orbital maneuvers and of maintaining life support, but none of their soultech systems function, including shields, thrusters, soul cannons, and sweeps.

                                        Exos: Exos (sing. Exo) is short for "exoskeletons". Exos are a form of power armor that ranges in size from merely as large as a wheeled ground vehicle to the size of a small skyscraper. They require a single Deviant pilot, and they can be as heavily armed and armored as a small soultech spacecraft. The majority are humanoid, deployed by empires as ground assault vehicles that emulate human motion and magnify the Variations of their pilots. Less commonly, they resemble terrestrial vehicles such as tanks, seagoing vessels, or aircraft. Although some Exos can fly, or even achieve orbital velocity, they lack break drives and so aren't suitable for travel over interstellar distances. Like soul gear and soul ships, Exos require calibration.

                                        Calibration: All soultech (soul gear, soul ship systems, and Exos) requires calibration before it will function. Calibrating Exos and soul ship systems (other than break drives) excises a Variation from a Deviant and installs it in the soultech. This permanently burns out the capability from the Deviant except when he is using soultech. Calibrating a soul cannon based on a Cutting Firearms Lash causes the Remade to permanently lose access to his Lash, for example, but he can still use any soul cannon, Exo, or other soul gear. Whenever a soul ship is constructed, it's a common (but by no means universal) practice to have the Remade who calibrated it become permanent members of its crew.

                                        Breaking

                                        A break drive can transport a ship to any point in space whose relative coordinates are known to its navigator. This comes with a few caveats: First, because light can take centuries to reach an observer across interstellar distances, a star or planet's location is never precisely where it appears to be. Second, objects in space are in constant motion, which makes it easier to make a break for a point near a star system than it is to break for a planet orbiting a given star. Third, unless the traveler knows both her origin point and destination well, she must first determine the direction and relative distance, which involves parallax navigation. Finally, not all space hazards are visible at a great distance.

                                        Practically speaking, this means that breaking directly from the orbit of one planet to the orbit of another several light years away is impossible. Instead, a soul ship must make several consecutive breaks with a short stop at each interim point to take readings and plot the next break. While one approach is to make breaks of decreasing distances, gradually "zooming in" on one's destination, this is seldom done except by those exploring uncharted space. It's far more common to break to one of a few thousand guide stars, which have minimal hazards, well-charted planets, and, importantly, nearby settlements that can render aid in the event of an emergency. A break drive failure a few light minutes from a space station is inconvenient and expensive; one several light years from the nearest inhabited planet could mean lifelong exile or worse.

                                                        From the moment I mentioned genre-swapping Deviant, we all knew I was going to arrive at superheroes, and here we are. The genre is enormous, and settling on exactly what flavor of superheroes I wanted to focus on was no mean feat. In some ways, I found I couldn't decide and ended up making it seven different settings depending on the current public sentiment towards the Remade. Today I'm going to show off the Sentiment mechanics.

                                                        I've also posted the genre's modified and new Merits as a free excerpt at the Patreon (and patrons can view the full text).

                                                        Sentiment

                                                        Sentiment is a measure of the public's collective opinion of Remade on a continuum from Glorified (the most enthusiastic) to At War (the most hostile). A chronicle's Sentiment will shape the cohort's interactions with the public and public institutions. The Storyteller should determine Sentiment in consultation with the players, as this will color all superhumans' interactions with the public and public institutions.

                                                        High and low Sentiment grant mechanical benefits — whether only to Devoted/Renegades or to all Diverged. Sentiment also determines what kinds of behavior move it more positively or negatively.

                                                        Glorified

                                                        Baselines expect Remade to solve all their problems and to spare them every hardship and inconvenience. This sentiment is most appropriate in a setting that explores the stultifying experience of being a public resource who faces constant public scrutiny and unrealistic expectations.

                                                        Improving Sentiment: A superhuman saves or restores many lives or preserves something of similarly great significance to many people (such as a landmark or priceless work of art).

                                                        Worsening Sentiment: A significant harm occurs when the Diverged conceivably could have been present in the scene to prevent it.

                                                        Remade: In addition to the benefits of Positive (see below), at the end of each chapter, the cohort generates Effort that they may invest in conspiracy Projects. The amount of Effort is equal to half current Standing (rounded up).

                                                        Devoted: In addition to the benefits of Positive, once per chapter, the Devoted may negate the events of a single scene for the purpose of worsening Sentiment.

                                                        Positive

                                                        Baselines expect superhumans to use their supernatural talents to further the interests of humanity (although exactly what that means varies from one Baseline to the next). Positive Sentiment is most appropriate in a setting where Remade are superheroes with the full support of a number of Baselines, whether they're government authorities, members of a spiritual community, or a conspiracy.

                                                        Improving Sentiment: The Diverged save a life, prevent destruction of property (including public property) or natural beauty, or apprehend a Renegade. Alternatively, one or more of them risks harm or sacrifices something significant in the service of the established order and public good.

                                                        Worsening Sentiment: A superhuman is visibly present in the scene and doesn't intervene to prevent a significant harm or to accomplish a good deed (even a small one). Alternatively, harm occurs while no Remade are present, and none publicly acknowledge it or help with recovery.

                                                        Remade: All Diverged benefit from the Qualified Immunity Merit (p. XX).

                                                        Devoted: In addition to the benefits of Favorable (see below), the Devoted may take a conspiracy action once per chapter, treating conspiracy Attributes as equal to Standing + 2. If unused, this action is lost.

                                                        Favorable

                                                        The public regards the Diverged as interesting and exciting, like a new technology with a lot of untapped potential. Favorable Sentiment is most appropriate for a setting in which superhumans often play at being superheroes but haven't gained widespread support from Baseline institutions.

                                                        Improving Sentiment: Diverged use their Variations in the service of others or the public good without the expectation of reward. Alternatively, they accept compensation or donations (in advance or after the fact) to save a life, to prevent destruction of property (including public property) or natural beauty, or to apprehend a Renegade.

                                                        Worsening Sentiment: The Remade publicly try but fail to prevent a harm or accomplish a good deed (whether compensated or not).

                                                        Devoted: These superhumans wield influence within Baseline society by making friends and proving that Remade can be valuable to their communities. The Devoted receives two dots of Allies, Anonymity, Contacts, Mentor, Reputation, Resources, Retainer, Safe Place, Staff, or Status (all are in Deviant except for Staff, which appears in Devoted Companion — p. 18), which can be shared by other Devoted in their cohort.

                                                        Neutral

                                                        Either Diverged are such a new phenomenon that Baselines haven't yet formed a collective opinion of them, or they're polarizing figures with a close to even mix of strong proponents and detractors. Neutral Sentiment is most appropriate for a setting in which Divergence is a new phenomenon (or one only recently entering the public consciousness).

                                                        Neither Devoted nor Renegades enjoy any special benefits due to Sentiment.

                                                        Improving Sentiment: A superhuman uses his Variations in a context that shows them to be useful and beneficial (even if he's paid to do so).

                                                        Worsening Sentiment: A Remade uses his Variations in a context that shows their limitations, the danger they represent, or the thorny moral and ethical issues they raise.

                                                        Unfriendly

                                                        The Diverged have captured public imagination, and Baselines everywhere shake their heads as they recount the latest news stories about people with dangerous powers engaged in reckless or criminal behavior. Unfriendly Sentiment is most appropriate in a setting where superhumans are mistrusted or face prejudice.

                                                        Improving Sentiment: A superhuman publicly embraces the principle of serving Baselines and/or Baseline institutions, or encourages other Remade to do so.

                                                        Worsening Sentiment: One of the Diverged uses her Variations to commit a nonviolent crime, or her Variation use causes minor property damage (such as flattening a car or destroying a city sidewalk) or serious injury to a Baseline.

                                                        Renegade: Superhumans use their abilities to accumulate resources and attract those who crave proximity to power or wield supernatural influence of their own. The Renegade receives two dots of Allies, Anonymity, Contacts, Mentor, Reputation, Resources, Retainer, Safe Place, Staff, or Status (all are in Deviant except for Staff, which appears in Devoted Companion — p. 18), which can be shared with other Renegades in her cohort.

                                                        Hostile

                                                        The public blames the Remade for much of what goes wrong in the world, and superhumans with known identities live under the constant threat of harassment if not outright mob violence. Hostile Sentiment is most appropriate in a setting where Diverged face violence if they are recognized for what they are, incentivizing secrecy and subtlety.

                                                        Improving Sentiment: A superhuman doesn't use his Variations when it's obvious to witnesses it would have benefited him to do so.

                                                        Worsening Sentiment: Diverged use their Variations to commit a violent crime, to commit a serious nonviolent crime (such as a bank, casino, or jewelry heist), or to cause moderate property damage (such as burning down a house or causing several city blocks to lose power). Alternatively, their Variations kill a Baseline or hurt many Baselines.

                                                        Remade: All Remade enjoy the benefits of the Armed and Extremely Dangerous Merit (Deviant, p. 100), which applies against all Baselines not expecting to encounter superhumans.

                                                        Renegade: In addition to the benefits of Unfriendly (see above), Renegades count their Scar Power, Scar Finesse, and Scar Resistance as one higher (to a maximum of 6).

                                                        At War

                                                        Baseline society regards Diverged as an existential threat to humanity and is actively attempting to eliminate them — whether by "curing" or exterminating them. They might still use Devoted to further this goal, but loyal superhumans are merely lower priority targets. At War Sentiment is most appropriate in settings where Remade are openly hunted and killed as monsters by Baseline governments and vigilantes alike.

                                                        Improving Sentiment: One of the Diverged is nice to a Baseline without expectation of a reward, even if her kindness costs her nothing.

                                                        Worsening Sentiment: A superhuman uses her Variations to cause major property damage (such as destroying a landmark or blowing up a skyscraper) or widespread injury and death to Baselines.

                                                        Remade: In addition to the benefits of Hostile (see above), all Diverged quickly learn to use their powers creatively in order to survive, and may improvise a Technique once per scene instead of once per chapter (Shallow Graves, p. 199).

                                                        Renegade: In addition to the benefits of Hostile (see above), once per chapter, a Renegade may gain access to two dots of the Manticore Companion and/or Prototype Merits, which may be shared with other Renegades in her cohort to gain access to a more powerful Manticore/Prototype.

                                                        Moving Sentiment

                                                        The previous section provides examples of actions that increase (improve) or decrease (worsen) Sentiment. The more positive the current Sentiment, the more kinds of actions worsen Sentiment, and the more negative current Sentiment, the more kinds of actions improve Sentiment.

                                                        • Actions that improve Sentiment at higher tiers of Sentiment also improve Sentiment at lower tiers. Saving the lives of dozens of people (which improves Sentiment at Glorified Sentiment) still improves Sentiment if Sentiment is Neutral or At War, for example.

                                                        • Actions that worsen Sentiment at lower tiers of Sentiment also worsen it at higher tiers. Destroying a cherished landmark (which worsens Sentiment at At War Sentiment) still worsens Sentiment if Sentiment is Neutral or Glorified, for example.

                                                        • Actions that improve Sentiment at lower tiers of Sentiment don't improve it at higher tiers. Giving sweets to children has no measurable impact on the public perception of superhumans when society expects Remade to save lives without compensation.

                                                        • Actions that worsen Sentiment at higher tiers of Sentiment don't worsen it at lower tiers. Failing to halt a runaway train on the other side of the city (which worsens Sentiment at Glorified Sentiment) isn't going to be much of a public relations hit in a world where Diverged are the leading cause of train derailments (current Sentiment is At War).

                                                        Actions only affect Sentiment if they're witnessed by Baselines who don't personally know the superhumans involved.

                                                        Changing Sentiment

                                                        Through concerted effort, a cohort can change public perception of all Remade. While in principle any superhuman can shift Sentiment, maintain focus on the actions of the players' characters (and those of their close allies and major antagonists) when determining the direction Sentiment shifts.

                                                        • At the end of each story, Sentiment can stay the same, or it can improve or worsen by one step, depending on the events of the story. If Remade mostly took actions that could improve Sentiment, it will improve; if they mostly took actions that could worsen Sentiment, it worsens.

                                                        • At the Storyteller's discretion, Sentiment may improve or worsen by one step at the end of a chapter that had an especially noteworthy event in favor of moving Sentiment.
