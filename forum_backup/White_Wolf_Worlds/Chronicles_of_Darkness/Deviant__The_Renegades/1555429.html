    Black Vans - A Storyteller's Guide to Deviant: The Renegades

    11-13-2025, 07:07 AM
    You thought you escaped. You thought you were free. After six months without hearing anything from them, you thought for sure that they had forgotten about you. But then you spot a black van in the street near the place you’ve been squatting, and your heart pounds. Your adrenaline, and your rage, surge. You remember all the terror, all the blood, all the death. You haven’t needed to dig shallow graves in close to a year and a half, but that black van brings back all the awful memories.

    But it’s just one, ordinary-looking black van, right?

    Then you spot another across the street from the coffee shop where you work. And another a block away from a friend’s house. And yet another near the park where you go for your morning jog. After a day or two, you can no longer deny it: They’ve found you, and soon the conspiracy’s agents will come.

    In case you don't know me, I was the co-developer of Deviant: The Renegades and the line developer for it after that. Because the IP owner stopped approving new Chronicles of Darkness projects from Onyx Path Publishing, I have decided to continue the Deviant line on the Storyteller's Vault. I released Shallow Graves in July 2024, and my second offering, which is currently in 1st proofs, is Black Vans – A Storyteller’s Guide. I anticipate a full release in early 2026, and in anticipation of that, I will be sharing excerpts of the text a few times a week until release day – starting this Saturday.

    Today is the cover reveal to whet your appetites.



    Onyx Path Freelancer and former Deviant line developer.

          Today I'm sharing a bit of the introduction – a summary of its contents, really. Shallow Graves and Black Vans were the product of about 18 months of work apiece, but I've had their seeds in my pocket since before I turned in The Clades Companion and The Devoted Companion for editing way back in what we can cheerlessly describe as the During Times.

          I had a very long time to think about what I would want to include in a Deviant player's guide and in a Deviant Storyteller's guide, and the only question in my mind before CofD approvals stopped was how many words I'd be allowed to include. As it turned out, both projects went from a "propose and hopefully get approved' situation to a DIY one. I expected that to be stressful, but it turned out to be liberating. Shallow Graves is exactly the player's guide I envisioned for Deviant, and I feel the same way about how Black Vans turned out as a Storyteller's guide.

          I have, I hoped, taken some of the greatest hits of ST-facing CofD supplements (1E and 2E) and drawn them together into a single, 185,000-word book (for context, a typical CofD core is 200k-215k words). The random story hook characters of Damnation City and Tome of the Pentacle? Yes. The cross-genre play of Mirrors, The Mage Chronicler's Guide, and The Demon Storyteller's Guide? We're gonna do some of that. The crossover options of Dark Eras and The Contagion Chronicle? Yes, please. The optional mechanics of Mirrors and Hurt Locker? LFG.

          I've also thrown in a bunch of advice that is broadly applicable across much of the TTRPG hobby, because I've always felt that we tend to assume everyone picking up a Storyteller's Guide knows how to plan and run a roleplaying game, and maybe that does a disservice to folks who are just starting out.

          The summary below reflects that peculiar and eclectic pedigree. If you want to see more, I've also posted the first part of the chapter fiction at my Patreon, which you can read for free (and patrons have access to the full text of the novella, if you're so inclined).

          How to Use This Book

          This supplement for Deviant: The Renegades covers a wide range of topics. It requires the core Deviant book, and we strongly recommend access to its other supplements: The Clades Companion, The Devoted Companion, and Shallow Graves. The contents of this book are as follows:

          Introduction: Driver discusses chronicle design and narrative techniques in the broadest terms — from the logistics of venue and troupe make-up to different approaches to chronicle and chapter preparation and execution.

          Chapter One: Manual digs deep into the Storytelling system’s inner workings and how to get the most use out of it as a dramatic engine. It also explores Deviant-specific systems and offers ways to modify the game’s mechanics to make them conform more closely to the Storyteller’s style and the troupe’s preferences.

          Chapter Two: Engine looks at Deviant’s narrative systems and discusses the ways a Storyteller can use everything from character sheets to Touchstones to conspiracy systems to drive the chronicle’s stories.

          Chapter Three: Passengers offers a tool for quickly creating Baseline and Deviant characters who can be dropped into the story at a moment’s notice.

          Chapter Four: Models looks at parts of the Chronicles of Darkness that Deviant has avoided addressing head-on in its core and first three supplements. It offers ways of including demons, mages, vampires, and other occult creatures (and Remade derived from them) in a Deviant chronicle — whether as a Storyteller character or a player’s character.

          Chapter Five: After-Market Accessories takes Deviant out of its familiar genre assumptions and default gameplay loop and places the Broken in exotic settings. It also offers examples of ways to alter Deviant systems like Variations, Scars, Instability, and conspiracies to fit into new genres, and includes new Merits, Forms, and other mechanics appropriate to those genres.
          ​

                      Black Vans' introduction is no two-page handshake. Instead, it's a short chapter in itself that offers advice for planning, organizing, and running a Deviant: The Renegades chronicle starting with the basics – the considerations of venue, troupe size, and chronicle length. This includes suggestions for external tools to use between gaming sessions to enhance play, which is today's excerpt.

                      At the Patreon, I've posted another free excerpt about how to use knowledge of your personality and that of your players to decide how best to approach planning and running a chronicle that maximizes your advantages while compensating for your weaknesses (and patrons can read the whole introduction).

                      Externalities

                      Not all events in a chronicle need take place during regular gaming sessions or focus solely on the players' characters. In addition to the optional vignette (Deviant, p. 230) and flashback (Shallow Graves, p. 167) rules meant to flesh out the Deviants' backstories and antagonists at the table, troupes (or individual players) can use any or all the following.

                      Diaries: This could be a diary kept in-character by one of the Remade or just his player's reflections on the Broken's experiences, theories, and lessons learned. It can be shared with the entire troupe, only the Storyteller, or with a select few trusted friends (or Touchstones). The discovery or theft of the diary can even serve as a plot point within the chronicle.

                      Epistles: One of the Deviants sends letters or reports to a party outside of the cohort to brief them on the cohort's activities. Although similar to an in-character diary, epistles imply a relationship with their recipient. Who are they? Do they write letters back? What happens if someone intercepts the character's missives?

                      Bluebooking: In eras past, some roleplayers would buy bluebooks — small, inexpensive notebooks of the sort used for essay questions on college exams — and pass them back and forth between gaming sessions, each one adding a bit more to a scene involving two or three characters engaged in an in-depth conversation or similar interaction. Contemporary bluebooking is more likely to take the form of email or forum threads — or as a series of DMs, in real-time digital play. Bluebooking is a great way to explore intra-cohort relationships (or relationships with significant Storyteller characters) without using up a lot of time at the gaming table. It lets the troupe focus on following plot threads or addressing other problems that involve the entire cohort, while still giving individual players an optional way of getting to know one another's characters.

                      Co-Storytellers: Most roleplaying games assume a single Storyteller is responsible for planning and running a chronicle. This need not be the case, however. A Storyteller who excels at tightly plotted stories can benefit from a friend who digs designing challenging antagonists or who can feed other ideas into the mix. Internet communities have long served as sounding boards for Storytellers looking for advice or ideas, but co-Storytelling is a more formal arrangement — one that can allow the participation of someone who lives too far away to join an offline or real-time digital chronicle. For this to work, it's important that the co-Storyteller(s) have a way to stay current on chronicle events — whether through plot summaries, text logs, or recordings — and that they be in regular contact with the primary Storyteller so that they can pitch ideas back and forth.

                              Chapter One unpacks game systems, starting with the core Storytelling System. Some of this concerns existing systems, such as the Investigation rules (which you can read for free at my Patreon). Other parts offer advice for introducing new mechanics, such as the sample rules for training and commanding animals and Manticores, which I'm sharing below. Patrons also have access to the full text of the "mundane" half of the chapter.

                              This chapter lays the foundation for a lot of what I end up doing in Chapters Four and Five. When I was a kid, I had an English teacher who constantly told us that it was important to know the rules so that we knew when to break them. Chapter One provides some examples of ways to break (or at least modify) the rules, but it also spends a lot of time talking about the rules as they are. When we get to the last two chapters, though, you're going to see some wild stuff that redefines important game mechanics. I can't wait to share some of it!

                              Training Animals and Manticores

                              The Clades Companion (p. 19) divides animals into three broad categories of intelligence: dull, smart, and near-sapient.

                              Dull animals can't be trained, as they are creatures of raw instinct incapable of processing human commands. They include reptiles, fish, and invertebrates, as well as many birds.

                              Smart animals can learn and so can be trained. Most mammals are smart, as are some birds.

                              Near-sapient animals are exceptionally intelligent and capable of solving problems and expressing preferences. Few animals are near-sapient — dolphins, great apes, and a handful of others.

                              A Manticore can be much more intelligent than other members of its species.

                              Humans generally regard themselves as the only truly sapient animals on Earth. Manticores granted full sapience are Deviants with the Uplift Form (Clades Companion, p. 16).

                              Training an animal — even one from a thoroughly domesticated species — takes a lot of time. Working dogs, for example, require one to two years of training before they're ready to be service animals, assuming their training starts when they're young enough and that they receive consistent training. Wild animals or ones who are less well suited to training take longer and are sometimes impossible to train.

                              Basic Commands

                              A character with the Manticore Companion Merit (Deviant, p. 101) that reflects a smart or near-sapient companion, or one who has a smart animal as a pet, is assumed to have previously provided rudimentary training so that it knows how to obey the following commands when she issues them:

                              Attack: The animal attacks a target indicated by its handler until it suffers bashing damage in excess of its Stamina, any amount of lethal damage, or until commanded to do otherwise.

                              Come/Heel: The animal comes to its handler and follows her until commanded otherwise.

                              Down: The animal stops making noise and sits or lays down (handler's choice) until commanded otherwise.

                              Leave It: The animal drops whatever it is carrying or stops interacting with something it was handling.

                              Stay: The animal retains its current position until commanded otherwise.

                              Training a smart animal to obey these commands can occur during downtime lasting at least six weeks, as long as the character devotes at least an hour a day to the training. This is shortened to four weeks if the character devotes three hours a day to the training and lengthened to eight weeks if training is irregular.

                              Advanced Commands

                              In addition to basic commands, domesticated smart or near-sapient animals can learn to obey more complex commands. Teaching a smart animal to obey a new command requires a month of practice. Smart animals can learn a number of advanced commands equal to the handler's Animal Ken dots +1, while near-sapient animals can learn three times as many commands. Wild animals can't be issued advanced commands; they only behave in these ways when they choose to.

                              Fetch: The animal will fetch an object the handler throws or indicates within the scene and bring it back to her. As a separate command with Fetch as its prerequisite, the animal can be ordered to bring the object to a place or person in the scene that the handler indicates. As a separate command with Fetch as its prerequisite, the animal can be ordered to go find an object, animal, or person familiar to it (or one it identifies through Recognize) and bring it to its handler (or to someone else it recognizes whom the handler indicates).

                              Frenzy: Once given the order to attack, the animal will continue to do so even if injured — until it's either incapacitated or ordered to stop.

                              Guard: The animal will protect a person or place indicated by its handler. If it's approached by an unfamiliar animal, stranger, or other intruder, the animal will attempt to alert its handler. As a separate command with Guard as its prerequisite, the animal will also execute another order it knows (such as to attack) if the intruder persists — chosen when its handler gives it the order to guard.

                              Herd: The animal will attempt to control the movements of a target or class of targets its handler indicates — such as keeping a child or flock of sheep away from danger or driving a bear into a trap.

                              Pack: The animal will obey orders issued by one of its handler's companions (other members of the cohort, family members, etc.), although the handler's commands take precedence, and the animal must be familiar with these people before it will take orders from them. As a separate command with Pack as its prerequisite, the animal will obey orders issued to it by anyone who gives an appropriate signal (such as a hand sign or command word), although the commands of its handler and her companions take precedence.

                              Perform: The animal will respond to various commands to take physical actions, such as to beg, roll over, or "speak". As a separate command with Perform as its prerequisite, the animal can be taught to execute a complex series of actions, such as navigating an obstacle course.

                              Recognize: The animal can be ordered to identify and interact with objects it has good familiarity with, although it can get confused by objects that resemble each other. This recognition is imprecise and reliant on the animal's senses. A colorblind animal can't be ordered to identify something as green, for example. As an additional command with Recognize as its prerequisite, the animal can recognize objects its handler describes, although the description must still be in terms the animal's senses can discern.

                              Respond: The animal can be ordered to respond to certain situations, executing its other orders whenever circumstances demand. The handler's player can issue orders even when her character could not do so, but the command roll suffers a –4 penalty. As an additional command with Respond as its prerequisite, the penalty is only –2.

                              Variation: The Manticore activates or deactivates a Toggled Variation or directs a Directed Variation as its handler indicates.

                              Advanced commands don't come naturally to most animals and require regular practice. For every month that goes by during which the animal doesn't practice its advanced commands for at least an hour a day, its handler suffers a cumulative –1 penalty to issue these commands (to a maximum of –5). This penalty fades at the rate of –1 per month once practice resumes and can be reduced by –1 per week with a regimen of at least three hours per day.

                              Near-sapient animals require more attention than merely smart ones. Insufficiently stimulated, these creatures become bored and surly. Keeping them cooperative requires at least three hours per day, and penalties for not practicing advanced commands accumulate at the rate of –1 per week (maximum –5). However, penalties fade at the rate of –1 per week once the animal is receiving adequate attention.

                              Issuing Commands

                              Issuing a command the animal knows when it's in non-stressful situations usually succeeds automatically. In action scenes or other unfamiliar environments, issuing a command requires a successful Presence + Animal Ken action.

                              Suggested Modifiers: Animal is hungry (–1) or injured (–2). Command is in keeping with animal's natural instincts (+1 or +2) or in defiance of them (–1 to –3).

                              Training an AI

                              Training an AI works similarly to training an animal, although most AIs don't have bodies with which to execute common tasks. Issuing commands to an AI requires an Intelligence + Computers action, and the number of advanced commands one can learn is based on Computers instead of Animal Ken.

                              The Beast Mastery Variation (Shallow Graves, p. 105) provides a supernatural means of commanding animals, but the system presented here is primarily geared towards players who have purchased the Manticore Companion Merit (Deviant, p. 101) or have a pet (potentially one that's a Loyalty Touchstone) and want to teach it to carry out useful tasks. Because this training likely occurs during downtime, that process is largely abstracted, while the act of issuing commands is what carries the risk of failure. In-world, situations in which a command roll is called for are likely to be chaotic and stressful for the animal. From the perspective of a system within a roleplaying game, these are the situations where failure is likely to be more narratively interesting.

                              Onyx Path Freelancer and former Deviant line developer.

                                  While I understand this is an extreme simplification for game purposes, this is a lot of misconceptions about animal behavior.

                                  There are definitely reptiles, fish and invertebrates that can be trained IRL to do your Basic Commands. They need specialized training due to species, but still can do it.

Ants have a collective intelligence that has been baffling scientists for a time now that we thought of looking into it, astoundingly superior to what would be expected from their individual behavior, and better than humans at some puzzles.

                                  I'd at the very least give rules for teaching a single specific task to a Dull animal, with no regard for its apparent complexity, especially if the character is an exceptional trainer.

                                          The second half of the first chapter focuses on rules hacks for the game's Remade-specific mechanics. This includes an example of a full rework of those systems to remove broken souls from the equation (available for free at the Patreon), but also guidance on starting characters with additional Experiences.

                                          Deviant handles character advancement in a weird way as compared to other Chronicles of Darkness games. Because Instability can, in effect, permit a free increase in Variation Magnitude, awarding a few (or many!) Experiences to each character and calling it a day just doesn't cut it. Instead, Black Vans offers the option below. As always, the full text of the second half of the chapter is available to patrons.

                                          Character Creation

                                          Storytellers may adjust the trait allowances for characters during character creation, allowing players to play characters who start out more powerful and experienced than usual.

                                          Extra Experiences

                                          Most traits can be augmented by granting bonus Experiences that may not be used to purchase new Variations, and which may carry additional limits on what traits they can improve. For every 8 extra Experiences, treat the Standing of the cohort as 1 higher when building the conspiracy (or introducing a new one).

                                          [table]

                                          Rank Bonus Experiences Effective Standing

                                          Survivor 8 +1

                                          Established Renegade 16 +2

                                          Renegade Role Model 24 +3

                                          Potential Threat 32 +4

                                          Serious Threat 40 +5

                                          Living Legend 48 +6

                                          Conspiracy Bogeyman 56 +7

                                          [end table]

                                          Storytellers should clearly indicate any traits that can't be purchased or improved with bonus Experiences, or are otherwise limited at character creation:

                                          Acclimation: This is arguably the most powerful trait in the game. Consider restricting initial Acclimation to 2 regardless of starting Experiences (or Shallow Graves Forms) or forbidding the purchase of Acclimation with bonus Experiences entirely. To reflect a cohort that has achieved significant self-mastery, consider awarding one or two dots of Acclimation for free, reducing the bonus Experiences amount accordingly — capping Acclimation at 3 or 4 or leaving it limited to 2.

                                          Attributes: These affect Scar Power/Finesse/Resistance, which makes them common purchases in Deviant chronicles. Consider limiting Attribute purchases to no more than half the bonus Experiences. Attributes cannot be increased above 5 dots without the addition of the Superhuman Attribute Variation (Deviant, p. 131).

                                          Merits: The ten-dot restriction on Merits during character creation normally forces some hard choices regarding these remarkable quirks and capabilities. Consider interactions between Merits (especially ones with an Acclimation prerequisite, such as Learned Magnitude) and any Variations/Scars, as these can become quite potent.

                                          Skills/Specialties: These reflect a character's mundane capabilities and have minimal impact on Variations or Scars. They make a good way to reflect characters who were elite prior to their Divergence. Skills can't be increased above 5 dots.

                                          Forms: Some of the advanced Forms in Clades Companion and Shallow Graves can grant inexpensive access to additional Variation Magnitude. Only initial Variation Magnitude and starting Merit dots no greater than Standing can be used to purchase Natural Variations (or additional Variation Magnitude for a Homunculus — Shallow Graves, p. 65)

                                          Threat Level and Standing

                                          Deviant never explicitly assigns a number value to its named threat levels. A Hyperplasia threat level chronicle features a Standing 2 conspiracy and Renegades with 6 Magnitude of Variations each (including 1 Magnitude from Origin), but depending on how players spend their Experiences, they could average 9 or 11 Magnitude of Variations long before they destroy their initial conspiracy.

                                          When Black Vans refers to "Threat Level" or "Standing" when talking about the cohort (rather than the character creation process or conspiracy), it is doing so to indicate the average total Magnitude of Variations possessed by the cohort. A Threat Level 1 or Standing 1 cohort is one whose members average fewer than 6 Magnitude of Variations, for example, while a Threat Level 7 or Standing 7 cohort is one whose members average 18-20 Magnitude of Variations (including one from Origin).

                                          High-Standing

                                          Because starting Variations are measured in Magnitude and not Experiences, they need to be handled separately from other traits. For Renegades with levels of power that exceed even Metamorphosis, Storytellers can use the Standing chart (Deviant, p. 235) to assign Magnitude of Variations and determine the conspiracy's traits. If the conspiracy has Standing 8, for example, each Remade begins with 20 Magnitude of Variations (plus one additional Scar-free Magnitude based on Origin) This works best in a one-shot or short chronicle, as Broken with very large numbers of Variations are often difficult to challenge and have limited options for advancement.

                                          Magnitude for Experiences: Consider allowing characters to exchange starting Magnitude of Variations for starting Experiences. For every 3 Experiences, reduce starting Variation Magnitude by 1. This never removes the Scar-free Magnitude from Origin.

                                              Shallow Graves explored the character creation process and character arc decisions from a player perspective, the first half of Black Vans' second chapter looks at these traits from a Storyteller's perspective. This includes taking the raw material of character traits and background details and crafting them into an interesting story that will be satisfying to the player running the character. One perennial challenge is a character whose personal history speaks of someone with more skills and knowledge than can be represented by a starting character's Attributes, Skills, and Merits.

                                              Available for free on the Patreon is a discussion of how to convey failure in a way that doesn’t make a character seem inept when their traits support competence or even mastery (as well as the full text of the chapter’s first half, for patrons).

                                              Cooler in the Backstory

                                              Sometimes a character has capabilities or connections in her backstory that are not reflected in her traits. A player wants her to be a brilliant surgeon or a master thief or an Olympic athlete, but the dots just don't add up. One reason this happens is that players often like to play characters who were remarkable even before their Divergence. Mostly, though, this reflects the realities of character creation. Even Remade with the Variations to go toe-to-toe with a Standing 6 conspiracy have the same number of starting Attribute, Skill, and Merit dots as one facing a Standing 1 conspiracy (but see p. XX). At other times, a character's appearance or manner comes with social advantages, stigmas, or both that aren't readily reflected with mundane traits — such as a very young or very old character. There are several ways to explain this discrepancy:

                                              That Was a Long Time Ago: The Remade possessed remarkable skills, but they have atrophied with disuse or simple age. At the age of 65, the legendary stunt pilot isn't capable of the aerobatics of her youth, for example.

                                              Unsung Casualty of Divergence: The Divergence greatly diminished the Broken, stripping him of finely honed skills. This isn't reflected by Scars, simply in the apparently reduced traits implied by his backstory. This adds another layer of tragedy to the Deviant's circumstances.

                                              That Was Before the Accident: Not everything needs to be blamed on the Divergence. Some injuries leave lasting damage. A torn knee ligament can be career-ending for an athlete, and a severe blow to the head can cause a variety of disorders affecting anything from motor function to emotional regulation.

                                              Holding Back: The character's capabilities are still there, but circumstances conspire to make them shine less brightly during the early part of the chronicle than they did in their backstory. This works best when the difference between the backstory version of their capabilities and the character creation version isn't huge, or concerns abilities that are unlikely to come into play until later in the chronicle. The player's early Experience expenditures are largely geared towards making the character creation version of the character into the backstory version.

                                              Natural Variations (Optional Rule): The Clades Companion introduced the concept of Natural Scars and Variations (p. 8), which reflect inherent qualities not tied to the Divergence but which closely resemble Scars and Variations. These are easy to adapt to other purposes, including reflecting a unique mundane capability. Without an appropriate Form, Natural Variations are only available at character creation, must be entangled with an appropriate Scar, can't be of Magnitude 3+, and count against the character's starting Magnitude of Variations. They must be Persistent and Subtle (with the exception of Perpetual Gigantic 1 or Perpetual Miniaturization 1) and must be capabilities a Baseline could conceivably have. They don't count towards limits on Magnitude of Clade Variations (for better or worse) and aren't considered supernatural. Powers that manipulate or shut down supernatural abilities and powers do not affect Natural Variations. This includes Variations and Adaptations that increase or decrease Magnitude or suppress Variations entirely, as well as Instability, so Natural Variations never improve during play. Powers that directly oppose the effects of a Natural Variation do so automatically and without a Clash of Wills.

                                              Natural Scars (Optional Rule): Natural Scars can be used to reflect a permanent injury, disability, or inherent disadvantage unrelated to the Divergence. They are wholly mundane and not affected by Instability; the only way they may manifest, worsen, or fade after character creation is due to the events of the chronicle (such as an arm being amputated or a child "outgrowing" Omega). Natural Scars must be Persistent, are not counted towards the limits on unentangled Scars, are usually of low Magnitude (rarely 3+), and generate Beats when they create complications for the Remade or her cohort. Without an appropriate Form, Natural Scars cannot entangle Variations.

                                                  If the first half of Chapter Two focuses on building a chronicle around the players' character sheets, the second half explores ways to motivate a cohort once play has begun. This includes today's preview, which provides potential answers to an important question: "How do I keep the Renegades from killing off my villains too quickly?"

                                                  The bonus free preview on Patreon explores ways to motivate a cohort without leaning on conspiracies and Touchstones to do so (and the full text of the rest of the chapter is available to patrons).

                                                  Conviction Survival

                                                  Once a Conviction Touchstone enters the chronicle — whether he was on a character sheet from the start or is someone who earned the Renegades' ire along the way — quite often one of the Storyteller's greatest challenges is keeping the cohort from killing him before he can serve as a proper antagonist. Endowed as they are with superhuman abilities, a group of Deviants can overcome most individual opponents who attempt to stand against them. A Storyteller can deploy many different techniques to ensure that a Conviction Touchstone's first on-screen appearance isn't his last:

                                                  Obscurity: The Touchstone is the hidden mastermind behind the plots against the cohort, such that the connection between her and the events she orchestrates may approach undetectable. She could very well arrange red herrings so that others are blamed for the events she orchestrated. Merely confirming her involvement poses a challenge, and as long as she's far enough off-screen, bringing her to the rude justice of violent demise is off the table.

                                                  Absence: The villain remains off-screen but makes no secret of his involvement. He may simply send the cohort threatening messages, whether they're packages delivered by a courier who has no connection to the conspiracy or phone calls delivered to bystanders who hand one of the Renegades a phone and nervously explain that "it's for you". If some conspiracy presence is needed, he works through intermediaries who do his dirty work for him — and who take any lumps the Remade may dish out in retaliation. Often, these are conspiracy goons, but they're nearly as likely to be unwitting catspaws or even innocent bystanders manipulated into the position by the Touchstone's machinations. Killing these proxies does no lasting harm to the cohort's enemy or the conspiracy he serves.

                                                  Leverage: If the Conviction Touchstone must be present, it's safest for her to do so when it's clear to the Renegades that the cost of opening hostilities against her is much greater than any advantage gained from doing so. It might involve a hostage, the remedy to a problem the cohort can't solve without the villain's help, information they can't get anywhere else, or dire warnings that if they hurt her it will put them in the sights of law enforcement or provoke a strong reaction from the conspiracy she serves. The first of these works best if the antagonist can both prove that the hostage is within her power (whether through physical proximity or a live feed that can serve as proof of life) and that her inaction (rather than her action) will cause the victim to be harmed. This is what's known as a dead man's switch, and is used to disincentivize ambushes and unexpected heroics. For leverage to work, the cohort must believe she has it, must believe her capable of making good on her threats, and must regard the threatened consequences as worse than allowing the Conviction Touchstone to have her way.

                                                  Alliance: Rather than resorting to threats, which tend to put even Baselines on edge, to say nothing of Broken, the Conviction Touchstone reaches out his hand in friendship — whether temporary or lasting, feigned or genuine. This works best if he can offer the cohort something they want (or need) even more than his blood, such as help taking down a worse villain, committing an act of sabotage against the heart of the conspiracy, or working together to escape enemies who have imprisoned them both. As with leverage, this works best if killing the Conviction Touchstone will create significant complications for the cohort that his continued survival allows them to avoid. The danger of this strategy is that he's trying to broker a truce or alliance with Broken. Even if the cohort agrees that he's more useful to them as a friend than as an enemy, it only takes one dissenter to leave the Conviction Touchstone bleeding out in the snow of the frozen wasteland they've all been stranded in.

                                                  Repentance: The Conviction Touchstone sees (or at least pretends to see) the error of their ways. They make apologies, promise to abandon bad behavior patterns, and otherwise say whatever they must to convince the Deviants that they aren't such a bad person after all — or at least not one that is beyond redemption. This usually reads as insincere and desperate if the cohort has their enemy in their power; a villain pleading for their life when staring death in the face seldom convinces a neutral witness, much less overcomes the all-consuming loathing of the Remade for a Conviction Touchstone. It's more effective if this repentance comes after a show of good faith, such as if they rescue the Broken or her Loyalty Touchstone or commit some act of radical defiance against the conspiracy. Clever Conviction Touchstones might orchestrate assassination attempts they can thwart dramatically in hopes of forcing a reversal from Conviction to Loyalty — or to at least lower the priority the Deviant places on their death.

                                                  Retreat: Linchpins who survive their first few encounters with Renegades quickly learn that the best place to be in a fight with Remade is literally anywhere else. An antagonist can put in a brief, on-screen appearance to issue ultimatums, make threats, or bark orders at conspiracy agents and still leave as soon as actual violence erupts — or preferably before. She must assume that her enemies will prioritize her, and so any escape must make it difficult to follow her. It could be a secret passage, an idling car, or a waiting helicopter, and is almost always accompanied by a large number of goons or an impending catastrophe that draws the cohort's attention away from her long enough for her to make her escape.

                                                  Ruthlessness: When violence is likely or inevitable, a smart villain takes pains to stack the deck in his favor. This could take the form of numerous and heavily armed backup, fortifications and controlled environments that afford a home-field advantage, or advanced personal equipment and Icons. If the Conviction Touchstone is a Deviant or has other occult connections, his advantages likely include supernatural powers. Likewise, exploiting the cohort's Scars or using their known proclivities can strengthen his negotiating power. Enough of these taken together and prominently displayed can convince the Renegades not to resort to violence in the first place.

                                                  Possum: A Renegade is usually single-minded where his Conviction Touchstones are concerned. He'll often fight against overwhelming odds if there is a chance he can land the killing blow on one. An antagonist can use this to her advantage by letting the Deviant have what he wants — or at least allowing him to think he has gotten it. Whether a hidden bulletproof vest wards away the deadly shot or she reacts to a glancing blow as though suffering a mortal wound, going down and then staying down can preserve her from the dreaded double-tap. This works best if the cohort doesn't have the luxury of sticking around to check for a pulse, such as if there are a lot of armed goons still standing or if the environment is unstable or dangerous.

                                                  Surrender: When violence fails, and escape proves impossible, unexpected surrender can forestall death at Renegade hands. Deviants, although not renowned for their mercy, need their Conviction Touchstones, and destroying one is most satisfying on the Remade's own terms. Many Broken entertain fantasies of revenge for years, and putting a hasty bullet in the back of their Progenitor's head before the cops show up may seem insufficiently poetic. This tactic is a dangerous one. It can lead to a lengthy and painful imprisonment. However, a Linchpin with any amount of foresight has contingency plans to arrange for his rescue or extraction from enemy hands, assuming he has no discreet means of making good his escape when his captors' backs are turned.

                                                      Chapter Three concerns the occupants of all those black vans, whether they're Baseline agents or Devoted. The first half presents some simple templates and a bunch of randomized tables to serve as creative prompts for antagonists, allies, and foils – whether because the Storyteller is whipping up a character on the fly or just feeling uninspired when preparing for the next gaming session. Today I'm sharing those simplified templates.

                                                      The free bonus excerpt on the Patreon is a summary of how Variation randomization works (and full text with all the tables a Storyteller could want is available to patrons).

                                                      Traits

                                                      Here we provide a method by which a Storyteller may quickly create a Baseline or Deviant character — or simply select Variations and/or Scars.

                                                      Basics

                                                      Categorize the character you wish to introduce as Important, Significant, Insignificant, or Bystander. This will determine the numerical value of traits.

                                                      Attributes: Assign Primary/Secondary/Tertiary Attributes to either Mental/Physical/Social or Power/Resistance/Finesse categories. Given a 3/2/2 array, for example, you could choose to place threes in Physical Attributes (Strength, Dexterity, and Stamina) and twos in Mental and Social Attributes, or threes in Finesse Attributes (Wits, Dexterity, and Manipulation) and twos in Power and Resistance Attributes.

                                                      Dice Pools: Storyteller characters usually don't track Skills. Instead, they have a few categories of things they're good at doing (such as Guns, Friends Everywhere, or Obscure Lore). For these, the character doesn't suffer untrained penalties and receives a bonus based on whether they're an Important, Significant, Insignificant, or Bystander character. For all other actions, use their relevant Attribute and apply an untrained penalty.

                                                      Initiative Modifier: This is equal to Finesse + Resistance or Physical + Social, minus any penalties for weapons.

                                                      Defense: Usually equal to Finesse or the lower of Mental or Physical. If the character has combat dice pools, use the higher of Wits or Dexterity and add 1.

                                                      Health: This is equal to Size (usually 5) + Resistance/Physical.

                                                      Willpower: This is equal to either Mental + Social or 2 × Resistance. However, most Storyteller characters have limitations on how much they can spend during a scene.

                                                      Speed: This is equal to either Power + Finesse + 5 or (2 × Physical) + 5.

                                                      Other Traits: If the character is a Deviant, they will also have Acclimation, Magnitude of Variations, Scars, and Adaptations. Significant and Important characters (and some Insignificant ones) will have Merits.

                                                      Bystander

                                                      Bystander characters are present in most scenes but don't usually want to be involved. Narratively, they exist to get in the way, serve as hostages, or cause complications more by dint of being there than by any action they might take. A Bystander character might pick up a fallen gun and take a desperate shot, but their chances of scoring a hit are small — although they can add chaos to a scene. Deviants are never Bystander characters, and even faceless conspiracy goons are more capable within their areas of competence.

                                                      Traits

                                                      Attributes: 2/2/2

                                                      Dice Pools: Most of the time, Bystander characters don't have skills relevant to the scene they're in. If it seems reasonable that they would have some training or equipment, give them four dice with those actions (for example, a mall cop attempting to restrain and subdue).

                                                      Initiative Modifier: +4

                                                      Defense: 2

                                                      Health: 7

                                                      Willpower: Bystander characters can't spend Willpower.

                                                      Speed: 9

                                                      Insignificant

                                                      Insignificant characters usually only appear in a single scene. Most Goons are Insignificant. Their Attribute arrays, dice pools and Variations (if Deviants) are usually based either on their Scar (if they're Remade) or on the type of scene they feature in.

                                                      Traits

                                                      Attributes: 3/2/2

                                                      Dice Pools: Attribute + 2 in three areas of expertise.

                                                      Willpower: Insignificant characters can only spend one point of Willpower per scene.

                                                      Acclimation: If the character is a Deviant, they have Acclimation 0. However, the Storyteller may reduce an Attribute category by one to increase Acclimation by one.

                                                      Magnitude of Variations: Standing + 2.

                                                      Adaptations: Insignificant Deviants have but won't use Adaptations.

                                                      Merits: Up to three dots of Merits.

                                                      Mini-Bosses

                                                      Whether they're elite Goons or disposable (but potent) Devoted, some Insignificant characters are a cut above the rank-and-file. To reflect this, increase their effective Standing by no more than the number of members of the cohort. You may exchange some of this additional Standing for Experiences at a rate of 8 Experiences per 1 Standing, spending these on Adaptations (3 Experiences each), a bonus to an Attribute (4 Experiences each), and Acclimation/Forms/Merits (at their normal cost).

                                                      Significant

                                                      Significant characters are ones the Storyteller expects to become recurring characters — although this doesn't always mean they'll survive first contact with the cohort. They might be an occasional ally, a Progenitor's lieutenant, a troublesome monster hunter, or anyone else who might make a guest appearance in a chapter or two each story or could drive a story themself.

                                                      Don't feel compelled to assign every trait from the start. Particularly when introducing a character extemporaneously, even if you hope they'll become a regular feature of the chronicle, it usually suffices to assign only what you need to have figured out for the current scene. You can flesh out additional areas of expertise, Variations, and Merits later.

                                                      Traits

                                                      You may exchange Standing for Experiences at a rate of 8 Experiences per 1 Standing, spending these freely (but not on Variations).

                                                      Attributes: 4/3/2

                                                      Dice Pools: Attribute + 3 in five areas of expertise.

                                                      Willpower: Significant characters can spend up to three points of Willpower per scene.

                                                      Acclimation: If the character is a Deviant, they have Acclimation 1.

                                                      Variations: If the character is a Deviant, they have Magnitude of Variations based on Standing (3 Magnitude of Variations at Standing 1, 6 at Standing 2, 8 at Standing 3, etc.).

                                                      Adaptations: Significant characters have Adaptations as normal. It's simplest to use the defaults in Deviant.

                                                      Merits: Up to five dots of Merits.

                                                      Important

                                                      Important characters are those the Storyteller expects to be a central feature of the chronicle, such as a mentor, rival, or major antagonist. It's appropriate to build these with full character sheets. This doesn't preclude using random generation tables as a creative prompt but using them exclusively from start to finish will probably result in an unfocused concept — especially for high Standing Broken.

                                                      As with Significant characters, it's okay not to have all the details worked out from the start.

                                                      Traits

                                                      If creating an Important character during a chapter, use the following process and then fill out a complete character sheet later. This will mean the final character will usually have significantly lower Attributes than he does when you first introduce him.

                                                      You may exchange Standing for Experiences at a rate of 8 Experiences per 1 Standing, spending these freely (but not on Variations).

                                                      Attributes: 5/3/2

                                                      Dice Pools: Attribute + 3 in up to seven areas of expertise.

                                                      Willpower: Normal Willpower, spent as desired.

                                                      Acclimation: If the character is a Deviant, they have Acclimation 1.

                                                      Variations: Variation Magnitude based on Standing (3 Magnitude of Variations at Standing 1, 6 at Standing 2, 8 at Standing 3, etc.).

                                                      Adaptations: Important characters have Adaptations, whether the defaults or alternative ones from Shallow Graves.

                                                      Merits: Up to 10 dots of Merits.

                                                      Experiences: For major antagonists or other powerful figures, apply an Acquainted/Personal/Obsessive template (Deviant, p. 236) or increase effective Standing by no more than the number of members of the cohort. Effective Standing can exceed 10 but any effective Standing in excess of 10 must be exchanged for Experiences.

                                                          Whereas the first half of Chapter Three concentrated on randomizing the traits of Storyteller characters, the second half does so for their narrative qualities. Today I'm sharing the first several creative prompts for describing Goons, although they could be used to build other Storyteller characters you might need to drop into your chronicle.

                                                          The free preview on the Patreon does the same for some of the 100 conspiracy agents you will meet if you fight the Web of Pain long enough (with full text of the second half of the chapter available to patrons).

                                                          Goons

                                                          Although the word "goon" is frequently paired with the word "faceless", making the rank-and-file antagonists of a Deviant chronicle indistinguishable from one another is boring and kills a potential element of horror. Goons are people, and while their quirky tastes in witty t-shirts and fondness for the music of Beethoven in no way absolve them of culpability for the atrocities they commit and enable, these details can draw attention to the collateral damage the conspiracy inflicts by cavalierly placing these people in mortal danger to boost a bottom line or further some higher-up's personal agenda.

                                                          Despite their relevance to Goons specifically, any of the descriptions below could be applied to Baseline agents, Devoted, or even Storyteller characters with no connection to the Web of Pain. When used to design a conspiracy's Goons, consider ways to establish conformity and eccentricity with your descriptions.

                                                          Conformity: The minions of a conspiracy often have a certain sameness to them. It might be a uniform or logo they all wear or emblazon on their equipment. It could be a requirement that they all wear their hair short and go cleanshaven. Possibly, they favor a particular model of van or gun. Consider the ways in which the conspiracy's agents resemble one another. Try to pick at least some traits that hint at the nature of the conspiracy or how they treat their help. Military-style uniforms and well-maintained equipment speak to the organization's orderliness and internal discipline. Casual attire and unkempt appearances suggest the opposite, and something strange like a strong cologne or matching drug paraphernalia is likely to raise eyebrows and invite questions. A particular presentation could apply to all agents of a conspiracy, those that belong to a specific division or Node within it, or only to those in the current scene. A single squad of a private army could be visibly exhausted because they just finished a grueling forced march, for example, or a specialized kill team might all wear skull-like masks and tactical gloves to intimidate those who might be tempted to defy them.

                                                          Eccentricity: In a conspiracy of indistinguishable assholes, no two goons are truly identical. Some are taller or shorter, have scars or tattoos, carry equipment the others don't have, or are otherwise distinct from one another. Consider giving each goon (or other character in the scene) a distinguishing trait.

                                                          [H3]Example Traits[/H3]
                                                          01-02 Armed: He is carrying or brandishing one or more weapons. It might be a firearm, whether pistol, rifle, shotgun, assault rifle, or automatic weapon. It could instead be a hand-to-hand weapon, such as a knife or cudgel. It could be crude (a shiv, length of chain, or pipe), archaic (a sword or bow), or tricked out with a laser sight or high-tech gizmos meant to improve its range and accuracy. He might also sport a belt or bandolier of spare ammunition or grenades (whether frag, flashbang, or white phosphorous).

                                                          03-04 Athletic Equipment: She wears or brandishes athletic equipment. She might be kitted out for football, dressed for a soccer game, or look like she's about to step onto a tennis court or into a gymnastics meet. She might carry a golf club, baseball bat, polo mallet, or rodeo lasso — whether as a tool for the sport or with lethal intent (and in the latter case, it might be modified for increased lethality, such as being wrapped in barbed wire or studded with nails).

                                                          05-06 Bag: She carries a bag or sack. It might be a purse — whether an elegant little handbag, a practical container for keys, wallet, phone, and assorted essentials, or one so large it could have practically anything inside. Or maybe it's a backpack, whether a school bag, camping rucksack, or rugged military backpack. Possibly, it's a paper, plastic, or reusable grocery bag — or just a sack lunch (or paper bag of dollar bills). It could be a messenger bag, duffel, or fanny pack. It could contain only practical necessities but might include keepsakes, family photographs, a book, or other hints about her inner life that the cohort can discover.

                                                          07-08 Belt: He wears a belt, suspenders, harness, or other contraption of straps and buckles. It might serve the simple, practical purpose of holding his pants up. It could be a means of keeping the tools of his trade close to hand, whether it's a gun, sword, knives, or other weapons, or the tools of a carpenter, plumber, or electrician. It could instead be decorative or a sign of rank — whether it's a prize-winner's rodeo belt or a martial arts belt. A harness might likewise be purely aesthetic, but it's more likely a safety apparatus to protect him when climbing, scuba diving, or parachuting.

                                                          09-10 Beverage: She has a drink in her hand or on a nearby table. Her particular poison could be alcohol (whether in an elegant glass, beer bottle, or paper bag), tea, coffee (in a mug or a clear plastic or cardboard cup), juice, soda, milk, bottled water, or more unusual beverages. She might offer drinks when hosting guests or trying to win someone over. She may also have noteworthy disposal habits; perhaps she tosses empty cans on the ground, meticulously crushes them with her hands, or diligently rinses them out before putting them in the recycling bin.

                                                          11-12 Body Shape: He is notably taller, shorter, thinner, fatter, or more muscular than average. Or perhaps he has a distinctively shaped face, nose, mouth, chin, or ears.

                                                          13-14 Consuming Media: She is looking at or listening to something. It might be a radio (or its contemporary equivalent), whether playing music, talk, audiobook, or podcast. She could be watching a movie, TV show (whether a game show, talk show, soap opera, sitcom, or drama), or a web video (whether short, long form, series, or stream). Perhaps she's looking at social media — whether it's text, images, gifs, or videos. Possibly she's reading a book (print or electronic, fiction or nonfiction, text or graphic) or news (print or online; mainstream, niche, or extremist). Maybe she's playing a game (card, board, or video; casual or hardcore; solo, versus, or co-op).

                                                          15-16 Contraption: They're carrying, wearing, or using a strange device. It could be a high-tech weapon, powered exosuit, or scanner. Perhaps it's biological, with flesh, blood, and pumping lungs, and is somehow both alive and inanimate. Maybe it's magical or otherwise arcane, such as a mystic staff, glowing amulet, or book of spells. Maybe they wield it with confidence, perhaps they have some idea what they're doing but can't fully control it, or possibly they have no clue how it works and are just pushing buttons and praying one of them does something useful.

                                                          17-18 Conveyance: They have a means of transporting themself either near at hand or on their person. It could be skates (or a skateboard), a bicycle, or a scooter (kick, electric, or motorized). It might be a consumer motorcycle, car, or truck. It may be a construction, farm, or military vehicle. Possibly, it's a horse, donkey, or other riding animal. It could even be a wingsuit or jetpack.

                                                          19-20 Costume: She wears a costume. It could be a disguise to conceal her identity or to create the illusion that she is someone she is not. Or perhaps it's suitable for a costume party, sci-fi convention, or movie shoot. It could imply a general type or person or creature, such as a gangster or cat girl, or it could refer to a specific celebrity or character from a TV show or movie.
