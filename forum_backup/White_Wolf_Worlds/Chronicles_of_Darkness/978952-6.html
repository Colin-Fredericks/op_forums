                Gonna throw my two cents in the ring as a proponent of the "grey morality" Technocracy. No, I wasn't indulging in pro-science nerd fascist wish fulfillment. I thought the core idea behind the initial formation of the Technocracy, that magic should be made universally accessible rather than being left in the hands of the Enlightened elite to use or abuse as they saw fit, as really interesting given the metaphysics of Mage. I found the concept of an ideological conflict between the Traditions and Technocracy a lot more interesting than rebels fighting the comically overblown Men-in-Black. I had much the same problem with Werewolf. I like nuanced conflicts between potentially sympathetic people to be more interesting than fighting against comic book evil. I acknowledge that this isn't what the writers intended and that's fine. They were going for grey vs. black where I prefer grey vs. grey. Just a matter of taste.

                I don't, though, think that condescending speculation about the psychology of folks such as myself is fruitful. Not picking a fight here, just warning about the dangers of stereotyping. And this brings me back to the actual topic of the thread. Someone I read recently noted that a lot of the problem with discussions is that people assume that other folks have the same priorities as them. This leads to folks tarring those who disagree with them with ridiculous claims like "Republicans hate women" or "Democrats are against religion". I would simply ask people to recognize that other folks might have different tastes, perspectives, or priorities than themselves.

                Some people roleplay as an escape from their lives, to have fun and lose themselves in a fantasy setting. Some people do it out of a deep desire to explore other perspectives, challenge their preconceptions, and push themselves past their comfort zones. Some people roleplay just to hang out with their friends and be social. Some roleplay because they like problem-solving, tactics, and optimizing their character. Each of these approaches is equally valid but can lead to very different opinions on how games should be designed, run, and enjoyed. It leads to different opinions on what games are good and bad. Ultimately, what games are good or bad isn't an objective measure. It is entirely based on what you are trying to get out of them. And it is worth recognizing that sometimes, a game just isn't for you. Or that a game that you enjoyed is enjoyed by other people for different reasons and they may take it in a different direction than you like. And that's okay. The old books aren't going anywhere.

                Okay, that's longer than I intended it to be and a bit lecture-y, which also wasn't intended. Just thought I'd try to add a perspective to cool some of the outrage.
