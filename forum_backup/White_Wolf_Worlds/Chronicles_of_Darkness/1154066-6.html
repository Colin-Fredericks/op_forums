    Originally posted by ElvesofZion View Post
    I think that there needs to be something that makes Hunters (who actively prepare for and go out to fight monsters) different from Mortals that happen to deal with a monster when it sets up shop in their community. I do think the content of the playtest as written is problematic (if even just from a reactionary stance), but I think that there needs to be a clear delineation between regular mortals and Hunters. Moreso in 2e then there was in 1e due to the inclusion of so many Merits (Supernatural, MCI, and MicroTemplates) that make the old line between the two more muddied.
    I think it's pretty clear it's meant to be the Code. Hunters dedicate their lives to the Code and change their entire psyche to deal with it. A regular mortal who occasionally deals with the supernatural is just treated like a regular mortal.

    Originally posted by White Oak Dragon View Post
    My purpose with the werewolf sacrificing its life situation was purely to point out that it should be possible to shake someone's faith that the monsters are entirely evil, irredeemable bastards.
    The Code doesn't say monsters are 'entirely evil, irredeemable bastards,' it's just that they don't belong. And they don't. Werewolf specifically is a great example of that, their world is gone.

    If one werewolf dies for a human, the hunter's reaction shouldn't be a "Heh. I guess there was actually one exception to the 'All werewolves are evil man-eating bastards' rule. Clearly that was the only one that ever has or ever would exist, though." That said, the fact that werewolves are dangerous is still a valid reason to keep hunting them. They might see werewolves as tragic figures at that point ("Yeah, I know it's not your fault. I'm sorry, but I still need to put the good of the community first. I wish there was another way."), but they'd still do the job.
    A hunter can see things that way. But does that mean when the next werewolf comes by and starts killing their cell they're going to shrug and say, 'Well, he'll sacrifice himself for me when it's important.' The Code doesn't say you can't see a monster as a 'good monster' but that doesn't make them people. If you really want there's probably an option to change your Code to allow for that, but they're still likely considered a monster.

    'When the Code gets in the way of empathy for the plight of a creature that had no choice in becoming what it is, or one that’s trying its best not to harm anybody, it forces a hunter to confront the consequences of the Vigil he swore to uphold.'

    It's explicitly called out as something that can happen. 'Why am I doing this? Maybe they aren't so bad. Maybe I shouldn't be part of this group of killers.' It's not blocking off character options.

    And then there's the other problem with your example. 'The only good werewolf is one who died for me,' is a ridiculously high bar, especially because this 'redemption' only comes in death.

            Well... I think that exercise has merit. But a huge part of my issue is with the Spade Sidebar and not the breaking points themselves.

            Just to get the ball rolling. Network Zero has issues with the following elements:
            • Causing a person to suffer a breaking point from exposure to the supernatural - self evident.
            • Refusing aid to a fellow hunter in need - VALKARIE has been portrayed as Net0's enemy. So an exemption for them makes sense.
            • Causing significant harm to a person. Net0 have a strong X-Files conspiracy theorist anti-establishment streak. So exposing a corrupt politician online and getting him arrested (is that what's meant by "significant harm"? I'd assume so) seems fitting to me. I can't see Net0 killing humans, but doing an expose, I could see that.
            All in all, the code is not too bad for Net0 but it still requires two modifications in my opinion and the limit is one.

            Originally posted by ElvesofZion View Post
            I think that there needs to be something that makes Hunters (who actively prepare for and go out to fight monsters) different from Mortals that happen to deal with a monster when it sets up shop in their community.
            I think you said it yourself: The difference is proactivity. A Mortal will deal with a monster if they're forced too. But if you're actively looking for trouble or training so you're ready if a monster shows up you're a Hunter. I mean "deal with a monster when it sets up shop in their community" is The Union in a single sentence.

            Admittedly most Hunters will have more differences than just reactive/proactive. But IMO being proactive is the minimum you need to distinguish between a mortal and a Hunter.


            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
            as it forces confrontation with some of our easiest dark instincts in othering people and justifying our actions, particularly given that, yeah, sometimes there are plenty of surrounding reasons that are justified.
            I have several thoughts on this point:
            1. Games that explore themes about othering and justifications but use monster's who're hardwired to prey on people and cause suffering as the stand in for the quickly other go into thematically weird and often incoherent territory. You either end up ignoring the parallel because there's no Always Chaotic Evil monsters in real life so there's no real reason to compare the fiction to reality, or the game ends up implying or skirting dangerously close to implying that othering is actually justified because the other really as bad as racists claim. This becomes especially bad when the PCs stand in for full on Nazi racists, not just "I'm not racist but" racists.

            2. The presentation as written is actually very bad at forcing confrontation with our darkest instincts since it presents a single Code, and single hunter mindset in the Spade Sidebar. A far better way to force confrontation would be to encourage every player to modify their character's Code, at least to some extent, and present a verity of different opinions for players to consider; all to maximize the amount of time they spend thinking of it.

            3. We already have Heroes to explore the niche where monster hunters are as monstrous as the monsters themselves, and whether or not it's intentional a lot of people saw strong themes about othering in Heroes. Why do Heroes twice rather than explore something different? Or at least explore many different perspectives of which a Hero like perspective is part but not the whole.

            4. The Hunter core had 12 different groups, and many more were written later. Each had different personalities, ideologies, goals and methods. I can't see one theme working for all of them, nor can I see why you'd want to tie them all into one theme rather than explore many different themes.

                                    Now, here are the Breaking Points, or Tenets:

                                    * Causing significant harm to a person
                                    * Allowing harm to come to a person in pursuit of your Vigil
                                    * Causing a person to suffer a breaking point from exposure to the supernatural
                                    *                                     Refusing aid to a fellow hunter in need
                                    *                                     Killing a person
                                    * Learning that a monster harmed a person when you could have done something to stop it but didn’t
                                    * Putting the well-being or autonomy of a monster over that of a person
                                    * Gaining or bestowing power from an obviously monstrous source
                                    * Betraying a fellow hunter
