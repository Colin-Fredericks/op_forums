    Originally posted by Anteros View Post
    I've heard a lot of people mention Captain Planet in relation to W:tA, as if the ecologist theme of the game somehow made it silly or goofy. I don't get how taking seriously a very real and very deadly issue is supposed to lessen the horror of the game.
    Still, apart from this, it seems to come down to aesthetics? Like the punk athmosphere is seen as 'diluting' the horror?
    The feeling that many have is that it doesn't take the issue seriously. Pentex might as well be run by Hoggus Greedly and Dr Blight, and adding to the comparison is the way that Tribe is handled so ridiculously. It's both an ethnic caricature complete with phenotype and an opt-in socio-political faction. With the fact that everyone is going to want to play a different Tribe, you've got a multi-ethnic band of do-gooders fighting for Gaia (another reason for the comparison) against villains who want to make the world gross and dirty because they worship pollution. That's not taking environmentalism seriously, that's turning it into a Saturday morning cartoon. Much like Hoggus Greedly and Dr Blight, Pentex doesn't want to poison the world because it's cheaper than safely disposing of their industrial waste or finding alternative sources of cleaner energy; they do it because Hexus told them all about Toxic Love.

    And presenting that kind of world outlook doesn't really do well to convey a sense of hopelessness or paranoia. I mean, keeping just in Apocalypse, half the reason the world is so garbage is because the Garou Nation is what is clinically known as "pants on head incompetent". They're like a nation of Batman. Sure, they have the power to fix things and make the world better, but instead of actually approaching problems sensibly, they'd rather just rip and tear like the Doomguy. Punching out Pentex is a lot more fun than actually working to socially or politically undermine them. The few factions that do try an approach outside of CRINOS SMASH are derided for it, or even accused of being Weaver tainted. The fact that Black Spiral Dancers exist isn't surprising, what's surprising is that the whole Nation hasn't caught on to the beat. It's hard to feel like you're fighting despair when OOC it's patently obvious that the "Good Guys" are incompetent at everything they do. And at a certain point it becomes less hopeless and more pointless.

    Contrast this with nWoD, where, yes, the True Fae don't want to eat the world and the Exarchs don't care about you personally. But really, neither did Pentex, or the Sabbat. That Tzimisce? He turned your family into chairs because he hated you. The allies turned Fomor? They were given the Uruk-hai treatment because you screwed up. Events in nWoD actually matter, often to you personally. They're not "this is the way the world has always been", they're "the world is now effecting you." You pissed off the Seer, you made someone mad. And even then, just because things are smaller in scope doesn't mean they're less hopeless or paranoid. Once you've dipped your toes in the water of the Supernatural, you know that you're not safe. Walking to your car at night you might get grabbed by some blood sucking corpse, possessed by a Spirit, attacked by a living shadow that eats flesh, be victim to a hellhound, learn that chupacabra are real, get snatched by faeries, or any number of things. You have ever reason to be paranoid. If you don't belong to a faction, you have even more reason to be afraid, as the Firetouched might capture and try to convert you, the Seers might threaten your family if you don't do as they say, a Reaper might think your soul looks tasty, the VII might make you part of their spooky hivemind. Hell, being part of a faction may make it worse, as suddenly you have to deal with politics. Who's to say your rival in the Consilium won't compel the pizza guy to bring you a bomb? You're a thorn in the prince's side, but why does he keep licking his lips whenever he looks at you? This is also ignoring the existentialist threats, which by and large seem to outnumber oWoD and be handled better. The Abyss, the Seers, the Strix, the Idigam, the Host, Spirits, Ghosts, etcetera etcetera. They don't need to directly threaten to destroy the whole world--and even in oWoD no one really wanted to do that--to be meaningful and threatening.

    "Breathing room" from silly Vampire Illuminati vs Vampire Gangbanger conspiracies and Captain Planet villains doesn't mean a world free of paranoia. The Black Spiral or Hand isn't going to go after you personally for no reason, but they might still see you all on your lonesome and feel like you'd make a good snack. That kind of paranoia hasn't really changed.
    Seriously though, Hunter and Changeling are the only nWoD that I can see as horror, wih perhaps Prometheans when Pandorans wake up. Otherwise things are too impersonal and random to set a horror mood.
    Originally posted by Anteros View Post
    It does feel rather random to me? I mean, what are the chances for those things to happen? Do people live in dread of those things happening to them?
    Yes, when it does happen you have some very personal horror, but there's no real feeling of anticipation of those particular fates, isn't it? Who live their lives in fear of being Claimed? or breaking time? Those are freak events or personal tragedies, that do not really define the game's mood, unless that's what the ST is going for.
    I agree about helplessness. I just find that a futile effort is worse than no effort. Might just be me, though.
    Who lives in fear of the events you mentioned? Does your vampire really expect to randomly have a Tzimisce turn his family into furniture? Those examples aren't even random, they're often the core theme of the game.

    Since for once Ephsy and I are on the same page, and because it's the kind of thing that I was talking about just last page, a few examples:
    I'm going to brush over it, but in Old World of Darkness your main source of Horror is ostensibly the fact that the world is shitty and you can do nothing about it. Ancient conspiracies and pollution cults and people who... want magic to be available to everyone in the form of technology (how terrible!) rule the world. Lip service is paid to how scary and powerful these groups are, but the attitude is definitely one of PUNK. You're rebelling against the status quo. You're fighting back against the simplistic pastiche of corporations that oppress the world, or telling your elders (literally, Elders) where to stick it. By and large, though, you don't have anything that holds you back from being a Vampion.
    In Requiem, you're dealing with your own inner demons. You struggling nightly with the fact that you're a monster, or at the very least a parasite. The more monstrous you become, the harder it is to even interact with normal people. In 2e, you have to keep your friends and allies close, because they keep you grounded and closer to the Man than the Beast, but they're in danger because of how close they are to you. Not just danger from your rivals or enemies, but danger from you. You might harm them, whether by snapping at them and Frenzying, drinking them too deeply, or simply hurt them in the same way that an alcoholic hurts those around her. Doing the things you need to survive--murder, destruction, using unnatural abilities, joining the social groups--makes you feel less and less human, which in turn makes it harder to act human. You can harden yourself to the things you do, but each time you do you gain a bane, and in doing so become more of a monster. This isn't even touching on the actual supernatural threats vampires face, in both editions: VII, Belial's Brood, sectarian infighting, the Strix, and every spooky disgusting bloody thing in Wicked Dead.
    In Forsaken, you aren't even human anymore and really you never were. You have to find balance between your shifting nature and more to the point you have to keep the balance of the world around you. You know that you did nothing wrong, but that doesn't mean you don't have penance to pay for the crimes of an ancient ancestor. But more than that is the fact that you are a weapon. Not a safe, secure weapon, either. You're not a rifle stored with the safety on and the ammunition separate in a locked case. You're dynamite, left to sweat and break down. Every time you go into battle, you're juggling those sticks. Something--someone--is going to fucking die, or damned near close to it. And if you lose control, everything is going to explode and the people you want to protect are going to die at your hands, and you'll wake from it with your face ear-deep in your little sis' chest with the taste of her heart still on your lips, and you'll have to live with just how good her Essence tastes. 2e of course strengthens that theme. Your lack of control may spread. When you snap, everyone you fight with snaps as well. You all become a surge of fur and muscle and death and blood and viscera. More than that, your Pack is more than just nine foot death dealers. It's frail and fragile mortals, who may not even know they're a part of something that puts them in danger. Will you threaten their wellbeing when you go after a rival Pack? Will the Host or the sorcerer or the unkillable corpse think twice about wearing their skin?
    In Awakening, it's easy to ignore the horror. Easy even for the characters themselves to ignore the fact that the world is a prison meant to contain the vastness and power of the untempered soul. It's easy for characters to forget that when they impose their will upon the world, they risk becoming convinced that their way is not just the right way, but the only way. Not just their political beliefs, but their personal take on how physics and nature should be wrangled into submission by their will. If becoming a mad scientist isn't horrifying enough for you, there's always the fact that your Magic is inherently inimical to the world. You impart your will, but watching you work it causes the cursed minds of those around you to reel in pain, and threatens to destroy Magic itself one bite at a time as your careless use of power brings taint and ruin into the world. But all that you need is just out of Reach, just a little further... And of course there's the temptation of power. You've had a taste, sure, but the first taste is always free. Are you willing to sell your soul? Sell someone else's soul?
    In Lost, well, you already feel there's horror in Changeling, but you seem to think the main source of concern is the Gentry. The truth of the matter is that they aren't a problem. They may not even know you're gone, more or less care about one little broken plaything. No, what you should be worried about as a Changeling is the fact that your life is in tatters. Sexual assault is the go-to metaphor, but this is a metaphor. You weren't just raped, you were raped by a hurricane that not only tore our your heart and made it so you'd never love again, but tore your house apart and threw you into the wilds, except none of the people around you were any the wiser. You're an outcast now. Your life is no longer there, and may even have been taken over. You're constantly given the reminder that you are no longer human, and more than that, there's the worry that the things you know might not even be real.
    In Created you're broken. A silly little thing that thinks it might possibly maybe be almost like a human. The world around you burns and boils and flinches at your unwholesome, broken presence. Everyone around you can sense, even if they can't see, that you're flawed. They know the deep dark secret that you harbour, the fact that you're not "real". They know that you live a half life, missing some integral spark of humanity. It's something that you feel, too, but that yearning need doesn't exactly come with instructions. The only way for you to ever fix the problem is to try whatever you can to overcome your existential angst and ennui.
    In Hunter you also seem to consider that horrifying, but what do you find horror about it? Is it the fact that the monsters are stronger, faster, possibly even smarter than you, with supernatural powers that you may not be able to match, much less comprehend? Or is it the fact that you're driven to do horrible things to protect people? You're unable to snuff out the candle and go back to sleep. You're unable to rest when you know the things that lurk in the darkness. What are you willing to do to keep them in the dark--to chase them far away from the firelight? What knowledge will you use, no matter how forbidden? What laws will you break? How far will you go in the name of a supposed greater good? Even if you tell yourself that you did the right thing, will you be able to sleep at night, or will the faces of those all too human monsters stare back at you in fear and surprise and judgemental anger whenever you close your eyes? Putting aside the things you do, what about the things you've seen? That walking corpse last week went down easily, but the grisly scene it left behind will stay with you.
    I'm not going to bother doing the rest, this comment is already pretty long, Geist tends to get called out as not being horror, but there's a lot there with the nature of the Geist, I just don't think that it gets enough attention or mechanical weight. I know nothing about Mummy, and I feel Beast has Geist's problems. Demon's horror is very similar to the horror of oWoD, but it tends to eschew the whole stereotype-as-player-type aspects, and focuses more on the thriller aspects of maintaining cover.

            Originally posted by Dataweaver View Post
            conceptually at least, Mage works far better as a crossover-friendly game than any other, Beast included.
            Hunter is best! Not only does it encourage you to play with the other splats, it provides rules for creating them. Sure, 1e's Dread Powers weren't nearly as developed, and it wasn't until supplements that monster creation became more detailed, but it's the idea presented in Hunter that makes it the best. (Or I guess Hunter, since you're making me look bad with your adherence to formatting). That's why Hunter will likely always be my favourite game.

            That said, my pet peeve with respect to Mage is how it's designed with its own themes being paramount and with little to no regard as to how that might affect crossover play. Which is fine in and of itself, but sticks in my craw when the developers then insist that other gamelines should conform to Mage's way of doing things. In particular, the relationship between mages and mortals, and the relationship between Mage and Hunter: in Mage, mortals who haven't been changed by the supernatural are usually Sleepers, and thus at an overwhelming disadvantage when dealing with mages. Which arguably is very appropriate for the themes of Mage. But then you get into Hunter (incidentally, arguably the main rival to Mage in terms of crossover potential), which has as one major theme the notion that ordinary humans aren't nearly as helpless as they seem, and you get statements from the Mage developer that if he has any say in the matter, hunters aren't going to be able to find any chinks in the “Sleeping Curse” glass ceiling that prevents them from having a fair shot at confronting an out-of-control mage. It's Hunter; the whole point of the game is to surprise the supernatural denizens of the ChroD by achieving things that they think are beyond your grasp.
            I feel that's a little disingenuous but also a benefit, for the most part. Disingenuous because Dave doesn't think anything outside of Mage should conform to Mage. He's said that the Lobotomy Tactic wouldn't work in Mage, it would just produce a Mad One, but that it's a Hunter Tactic. He's also said that Hunters can't get passed the Sleeping Curse because no Sleeper can. (Although I do think that Quiescence is a bit arbitrary when it keeps coming back even after the fact, though if I understand it right that's the Exarch's fault). No mortal, Hunter or not, can avoid Quiescence unless they're a Sleepwalker, which requires at least 1 dot in any inherent supernatural ability, or the one dot Sleepwalker merit.
            And if you ask me, wow, "I keep dealing with all this Supernatural shit" sounds like a perfectly valid justification for taking the Sleepwalker merit. Although he's also said (in relation to Sundered World and the Wise) that knowing someone is a wizard is perfectly fine. It's just seeing the Magic they work that's a problem.

            As for the "but also a benefit" part, I feel like it makes Mage--or any game, since Hunter does it as well--a better game when it worries primarily about it's own thing. Yeah, keep the big stuff together (it would suck if every game used different Spirit rules, and I do feel it's arbitrary that the Primordial Dream is part of the Temenos but apparently the Skien is not the Oneiros) but there's no reason that Mage should care about other games. Likewise, Hunter is my favourite precisely because it plays fast and loose.
