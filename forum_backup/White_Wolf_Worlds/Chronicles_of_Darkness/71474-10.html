        I wonder if part of why Descend demons don't feel like demons, to some, is that it's become the habit to think of angels and demons as opposite sides in a war over humanity. Which isn't the case here - humanity is more like the environment than the goal. Individual humans might be important resources, or even catalysts for falling - and individual demons may care for individual humans, or even humanity as a whole - but for demons as a whole, humanity is incidental to the Fall, to the Descent, and to Hell.

        I'm one of those to whom they do feel like demons. But thinking further over the human-as-environment vibe, and the Gnostic aspects of the setting, while it's easy to think of angels as the archons, in a sense, from the demons' perspective it's humans that are the archons. It's humans who are the main builders and maintainers of angelic infrastructure, it's humans who summon angels into the world, and it's humans who determine the bounds of Cover and what a demon can't and can't do without risking it (at least when it comes to the grossly out of character aspect, as it's human expectations that mostly determine this).

        Charlaquin:
        It is an illusion created by the Demiurge - a godlike being that for various reasons has an interest in keeping us believing in the illusion, in order to keep us separate from God. The true reality (which is to say, God) exists beyond this illusion, and understanding this brings us one step closer to escaping the false reality and returning to God.
        Interestingly, if we equate the God-Machine with the Demiurge, by implication we're equating Hell with the True God.

                                                    I don't think you have to understand Gnosticism to enjoy D:tD any more than you have to understand Gnosticism to enjoy The Matrix. However, if you're not at least casually familiar with it, you're unlikely to notice that the Agents are an allegory for the Demiurge's Angels. Similarly, you may not recognize the Unchained of D:tD as resembling Fallen Gnostic Angels. Now, in The Matrix it's not a particularly important plot point that the Agents are like Angels, but in D:tD the Unchained are the central characters, and are intended to be interpreted as Demons. Therefore, not being able to easily recognize them as such has a much stronger potential impact on one's enjoyment of the work. Obviously, if you miss that particular connection, but enjoy it anyway that's awesome, but I think its still a valid criticism to say that the Unchained don't come across as Demonic to a lot of people. That probably could have been avoided if the book had focused a bit more on explaining what makes the God-Machine's Angels Angels other than the fact that the book says they are, and what makes fallen Angels Demons other than the fact that the book says they are.
