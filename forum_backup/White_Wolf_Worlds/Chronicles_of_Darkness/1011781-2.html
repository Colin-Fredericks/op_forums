                    The discussion of RolePlay vs Rollplay is a tiresome one, and I can't writ much today, so I'll try to be brief.

                    Its bullshit. A ridiculous statement coming from people that try to hide the flaws of their work under the elitist premise that superior gamers should challenge their acting abilities, as if they were on the run for the Oscar, instead of in a room with half a dozen friends and snacks to tell a story no one else will ever know of.

                    cWoD NEVER favored roleplay as a system. NO SYSTEM favors. That is what you need a system for, to take your place when you can't do that yourself. I played it without rolling a single die a lot of times. I also played CoD, M&M, Shadowrun and D&D (all editions so far) in the very same way. YOU don't need dice when you're in the mood for roleplaying and not rolling dice.

                    What cWoD did most often was the contrary. By creating rules for social interactions, and bad ones at that, they made a lot of groups rely on dice when it wasn't needed. I have a friend who distanced himself from Vampire the Masquerade for years because of an ST who asked dice for EVERYTHING, and that was possible precisely because the system lends itself to such interpretation of the rules.

                    nWoD was far more sounding as a system. And yes, this is good for people who wants to play dice. Because it is a rule that ACTUALLY encourages drama. Because in WoD your only encouragement to accept some challenges is to be a "cool kid who plays hardcore". "The unbalance is to be realistic, if you're a 'real' player you'll embrace the hardships and take the weakest choice". This is freaking bullshit. A system that encourages drama is one that mechanically rewards drama. Yes, it is. Encouragement is recompense. You don't encourage a kid to swim saying that you'll break all the toys and shit in the water while the kid is swimming.

The Conditions system rewards drama by giving a Beat every time you resolve the Condition, that is, every time you make shit happen, instead of simply play along with current status quo. I fail completely to see where this goes against roleplaying. I said too much, and my hand is hurting, so that's it.

                            I would have more to say but my phone is about to die and I don't want to lose this post.

                            EDIT: Ok, got a charger. So, Conditions and Tilts are nothing new. Both WoD and CofD have floating modifiers and other effects. Take, for example, Presence/Majesty. Whether you're playing Masquerade or Requiem, you can use it to entrance a victim, causing them to feel trust and affection for you for a matter of days or weeks. The specific mechanical effects of such a trance and precisely how long it lasts varies from edition to edition and from game to game, but in all cases, you have to remember what the effects of Entrancement are, and those effects stick with the subject until the duration expires. Requiem 2e just puts those effects on a card and gives it a name. The most significant difference is that in Requiem 2e, these lingering effects are one of the primary ways of gaining experience. But it's a simple matter to change that if you don't like it. Just hand out Beats the same way you would exp (a Beat has mathematically the same purchasing power as a CofD 1e exp).

                            The other big difference is that some (but not all) lingering effects in CofD 2 have opt-in aspects that reward you with a Beat (or a Willpower or something if you're awarding Beats the old fashioned way) for opting into them. Now, that's something that I can understand people not liking. It does require the player to make decisions for their characters that they might not make if they didn't have access to information the character doesn't have, which some folks find immersion-breaking.

                            Take, for example, Spooked. Anyone with an ounce of genre savvy knows not to go off alone to investigate the strange noise, so players making decisions for their characters based solely on what would be best for the character (as a self-interested character probably "would do") never leads to characters going off to investigate noises on their own. Unless the player just feels like doing something that's going to cause problems for the party for the lulz, which is generally bad RPG etiquette. And that makes it kind of hard to tell a horror story. So, CofD 2e gives you a Condition that encourages players with Beats (or Willpower or whatever) to decide their character gos off alone to investigate the strange noise, even though a self-interested character probably wouldn't, without being the troll player, by making it something that helps the party. Now, for some people, the fact that the system incentivizes players to make decisions for their characters that self-interested characters might not make kills their immersion. And while I don't agree with those people, I can empathize with them. But again, this isn't really a matter of system getting in the way of roleplaying. It's a matter of taste in roleplaying styles.

                                                    Ok, here's some GMing 101. Any time a player declares an action, the GM should go through a mental checklist. It looks like this:

                                                    1. Is there a reasonable possibility that this action could succeed? If yes, go to 2. If no, it fails. Go to 5.
                                                    2. Is there a reasonable possibility that this action could fail? If yes, go to 3. If no, it succeeds. Go to 5.
                                                    3. Is there a consequence for this action failing? If yes, go to 4. If no, it succeeds. Go to 5.
                                                    4. Determine the appropriate dice to roll to determine whether the action succeeds or fails, and ask the player to roll those dice.
                                                    5. Narrate the results.

                                                    So, in your chasing someone down the stairs scenario, I see a possibility of success (jumping down the stairs could get them closer to their target), I see a chance of failure (they could mess up the landing), and I see a consequence for failure (falling on their face). So, I would ask the player to roll some dice. If the system has rules for jumping, I'll use those to figure out what dice to roll, otherwise I'll improvise a roll to call for. In your lying to someone scenario, there's not enough information to answer clearly. How well the player acts out lying really doesn't have any impact on the outcome, what is more important to me is the disposition of the NPC, and what specifically the character is saying to lie to them. For example, if the NPC trusts the character implicitly (either because the character has built a strong relationship with the NPC, or because the NPC is just really gullible, or there is some power or ability influencing the NPC's judgment, or whatever), if the NPC has no reason to suspect the character of lying, or if the player words the lie in such a way that the NPC would have no reason not to believe them, then there is no reasonable chance of failure. The action succeeds, and I narrate the results, no dice needed. If the character is hooked up to an EKG or the NPC has some sort of lie-detecting ability active, there is no reasonable chance of success. The action fails, I narrate the results, no dice needed. In this situation, the consequences of failure are obvious - the NPC knows the PC is trying to lie to them - so we can move on to the final step. If we've gotten this far, I'm probably going to ask for a Manipulation + Subterfuge roll.

                                                    Originally posted by Peraxis View Post
                                                    While I agree that not really dice doesn't mean more/better roleplaying, I think in some ways it forces you to try to RP better because you don't have dice backing up your described actions.
                                                    I don't really agree with the concept of "better roleplaying." Roleplaying is simply imagining yourself as another person and/or in another scenario, and making decisions based on how you think you or that other person would behave in that scenario. If you're doing that, you're roleplaying, if you're not doing that, you're not roleplaying. There's really no room for doing it "well" or "poorly." You can act well or poorly while roleplaying, but I don't think a player's acting skill or lack thereof should impact their character's chances of success.
