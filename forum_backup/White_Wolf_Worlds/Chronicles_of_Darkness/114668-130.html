        How does Words of Truth (Prime 2) actually work?

        The spell itself states that "The mage speaks with tongues of fire, and the world listens. So long as the words the mage speaks are objectively true and the mage herself knows them to be true, all subjects of this spell can hear her and understand her clearly, regardless of distance, noise, or language barriers".

        However it then states that the spell cannot be used for the testing of theories, such as stating "Frank died when he fell off that cliff", when the result is ambiguous, the spell failing if you do not know the Truth. However this itself causes more issues with regards to the potential for the spell to actually be used for Truth detection when yo u consider the phrase "So long as the words the mage speaks are objectively true". If you truly believe that Frank died when he fell off that cliff, say for instance you saw his broken and mangled body below, then the spell should function and you should seem to be stating the truth when you make that claim.

        However what happens if he in fact did not die, but instead had weaved a cunning illusion? Would the objective truth that he is alive disrupt the spell and render it incapable of being used? This may in many situations not be available for use for theory testing, however when you apply judicious usage of memory modification, you can quickly ensure that you believe whatever you say, and would thus be able to use the spell.

        So instead, is Words of Truth only capable of communicating sincerity of intention and belief? If so, then the objective, Supernal nature of Prime's Truth aspect seems to be flawed from the beginning and open to rampant abuse by less than perfectly moral and forthright Mages, who wish to trick others into believing that their sincerely, personally held opinions and perspectives (including those which they fabricate to serve their needs) are the truth.

        Apologies, this may be a less than "simple" question.

                                Originally posted by Prometheus View Post

                                "...and the mage herself knows them to be true..."


                                I would say that the difference between "knowing" someone is dead and "knowing" the truth about your religion is that one instance allows you to figure out the truth, at least in theory (a literal "reality check" for the possibility of the ideas' existence in context of the rest of the world, the rules of the world must allow the check), while the other is inherently about what may be a very sincere faith, but at its core is still just faith.

                                In other words, it's about the faith/proof dichotomy. There is a reason for why the Obrimos tend to be so logically minded. Prime is the Arcanum of Truth and truth has to be internally consistent by definition, *always*. And logic is pretty much a mental exercise about keeping things consistent. By trying to persuade someone about something that you can't logically connect to reality, you are already leaving out a key component of the spell symbolism.                                 At least that's what I keep telling myself to justify how the spell can be used without breaking it.
                                So what happens is that the game stops while the players have a philosophical debate about the nature of knowledge? :P What exactly counts as "knowing" something remains a highly contested question in epistemology - what level of certainty do you need to have in order to be able to say "I know X" rather than just "I believe X"? Can you "know" X when the truth is actually not-X, if you had excellent reasons for believing X to be true but were wrong through no fault of your own? If you believe X for spurious reasons but happen to be right through dumb luck, can you be said to "know" X?

                                In any case, I think the definition of "faith" you are working with is a bit muddled - or at least, is not the sense that the religious people I know would use it in this context. As I read it (and feel free to correct me if I've misinterpreted you), you seem to be proposing "faith" as a sort of epistemic category in which a) "faith" statements are not subject to the same standards of proof or disproof as other statements, and b) they cannot be held with the same level of certainty as other statements. This would seem to put them in a similar category to, say, statements of personal preference, like "pineapple is the best fruit"; it would be silly to ask whether that statement is "objectively true", and if someone cast Words of Truth and then said that, I would expect the result to be a big fat nothing.

                                The thing is, in my experience, the vast majority of people who hold religious beliefs don't regard their beliefs that way. They think that their beliefs are objectively true (even someone holding a belief along the lines of "all gods are real for the person believing in them" believes that that is objectively true). And they think that they have compelling reasons to do so, whether those be elaborate intellectual systems, historical accounts, personal experience, or what-have-you. You don't have to find their reasons convincing - I don't - but I don't think it's reasonable to say that the truth or falsehood of those beliefs is intrinsically unknowable. They are, after all, making a more-or-less specific claim about the cosmos: that it contains a being or beings fitting the description in the Qu'ran (or in the Bible, or the Talmud, or whatever body of religious doctrines they hold to), which has done such-and-such things and will do other things. These claims are difficult (to put it mildly) to empirically test with the tools available to us in the real world, but that doesn't mean that that they are in principle things impossible to know. (Indeed, in the case of religions that promise an afterlife, we all perform a fairly unambiguous check of their truth at some point: we die. We are not, thereafter, in any position to communicate our findings, but either we experience the predicted afterlife, or we don't.) So if you have a spell that explicitly checks against objective truth (and a lot of religions define their deity or deities as the foundation of all truth), I don't think "well, that's just faith" works as a justification for the spell not to apply. If "just faith" puts religious beliefs in the same category as statements like "the colour blue is awesome*", then in the sense that's relevant to their adherents, "just faith" means they are false.

                                Now, I agree that allowing Words of Truth to test religious beliefs breaks the setting horribly. I just don't think this approach works as a way to get around it. (My personal solution is to houserule out all references in the spell to "objective truth", full stop; it convinces people that you are sincere. Much easier to fit into the setting, and, IMO, also more interesting. YMMV, naturally.)

                                * It is, by the way. OBJECTIVE TRUTH! *Casts Words of Truth*

                                Right, so now you have a world in which either:

                                a) Islam is objectively true, and everyone listening knows it;
                                b) Islam is objectively not true, and at least one person (the caster) knows it, because if it were, then the spell would have worked.

                                (You can fill in any religion you please in place of Islam, with the same result; I picked it because it has a very short and catchy statement of its central doctrine.)

                                Either way, the truth or falsehood of that religion in now a settled question. And this can be repeated by any mage with Prime 2 and religious convictions of any sort. Any new mage can be told, with absolute assurance, "oh, yeah, that's the One True Faith", or, alternatively, "yeah, we haven't found the One True Faith yet, but these definitely aren't it."
