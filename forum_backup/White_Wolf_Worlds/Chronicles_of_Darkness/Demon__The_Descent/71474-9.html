                                                            So, I'm going to go ahead and throw my hat into the discussion. I am right onboard with Arcane Arts when s/he said that absolutely everything about D:tD screamed Fallen Angels to me. Of course, I am also fairly familiar with the concepts of Gnosticism, and I'm noticing over the course of this conversation that familiarity with Gnosticism is a common theme among people who don't see a disconnect between Descent Demons and Fallen Angels. I'm going to have to concede that if the game relies on understanding a concept that is far from common knowledge in order for its themes to mesh with their expectations of the central supernatural type, it's the book's problem, not the people's. This is far from unheard of in nWoD. A lot of people can't get past the Animistic themes in Werewolf (myself included). A lot of people had trouble getting just what the heck mage was about (myself included). Later expansions to those lines helped bridge the gap for a lot of people, but many were simply not hooked by the core, and therefore not invested enough to bother reading the books that helped solve those issues. It's too bad Demon seems to be following that same pattern, but it's really not unexpected. The terms "Demon" "Angel" and "God" all have so many different meanings to different people, many of which bear little resemblance to one another, and all of which are highly specific. It was inevitable that the people who did not ascribe to conceptions of those terms similar to what ever version the game emulated would have a hard time getting invested in the game.

                                                            As a side point, lot of people have been asking for more details on Gnosticism. I don't pretend to be an expert, but I have a pretty good working knowledge of the core concepts, if not all of the details, I'll see if I can put it in a nutshell. Basically, what we experience as reality is not real. It is an illusion created by the Demiurge - a godlike being that for various reasons has an interest in keeping us believing in the illusion, in order to keep us separate from God. The true reality (which is to say, God) exists beyond this illusion, and understanding this brings us one step closer to escaping the false reality and returning to God. The original Matrix film was based very much on these concepts, with the machine being the Demiurge, the matrix being the false reality, and the real world being God. The later films got a bit carried away in the cyberpunk overtones and action sequences with Neo using cool powers, so the metaphor kind of broke down. But that's to be expected, since the Gnostic concepts the original was based on don't lend themselves to creating an interesting sequel. It would just be Neo going around showing people that there really is no spoon, and there's nothing special about him that allows him to do these things, he just sees beyond the illusion, and if you just open your eyes you can be free too. And that, according to Christian Gnosticism, is exactly what Jesus was trying to do. According to Christian Gnosticism, the old testament God is the Demiurge, and the new testament God is the true God - that's why He seems to change from commanding vengeful entity to all-forgiving force of love and salvation. Christ wanted to teach people the true nature of God, and that by accepting His word, they could escape from the suffering caused by the illusion of being separate from God.

                                                            Aaaaanywhoo... The similarities between D:tD and The Matrix come from the fact that they both layer cyberpunk overtones on top of Gnostic undertones. The difference is, the Matrix focuses on the struggle of humanity to escape from the false reality, while D:tD focuses on the struggle of former servants of the Demiurge to come to terms with the inherent freedom that comes with existing within the false reality. That's why, to me, D:tD is the perfect Fallen Angel story. Angels exist as one with the Demiurge, but when they fall victim to the illusion of individuality it creates to keep mortals in control, they often prefer the illusion over the truth of existing without that false sense of self. Thus, a Fallen Angel is born.

                                                            Now, whether Fallen Angel = Demon is entirely up for debate. In my book, there is no difference, but I can totally see MCN's point that this particular story is about the process of the fall, where a true Demon game would need to be post-fall. And plenty of very valid arguments can be made that the Gnostic interpretation of Angels may not be the best way to depict them in a game like D:tD, or that the cyberpunk aesthetic is too far removed from the pop culture understanding of Angels and Demons. But arguing "that's not what Demons are" is kind of silly. Not only because Demons are fictional and an author can use creative license and call just about anything a Demon and not be wrong, but also because it absolutely is an accurate depiction of at least one religious philosophy's version of what a Demon is. Albeit with some superficial technological themes on top. As I said, reading this thread has convinced me that going with such a particular and admittedly obscure interpretation of Angels and Demons may not have been the most marketable choice, and extra work should have been done to EXPLAIN the Gnostic concepts that are central to understanding how an Unchained is in any way a Demon. But what else is new, that's a very common issue with core books in nWoD game lines.

                                                            EDIT: To answer your question, Incendax, no. I would not say most people are aware of Gnosticism. It's not really practiced like other religions, because by its very nature it doesn't require any sort of ritual or dogma. Those are things the Demiurge demands to get you to buy into the idea that anything you do in this false reality matters. True enlightenment only requires understanding and acceptance, not practice. So it's not something that most people would have heard of it unless they've done religious or philosophical studies.
