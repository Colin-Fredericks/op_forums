        Yeah, the metaphors aren’t really helping anyone. Much more efficient to just talk about what we’re actually talking about.

        Personally, I’ve never really understood the desire to use a set of mechanics designed for a particular setting to play a different setting. The point of the mechanics is to give you tools to interact with the setting. And I’m a proponent of using the right tools for the job you’re doing. I wouldn’t want to use the d20 system to play a political intrigue game starring vampires, and I wouldn’t use the Requiem rules to play a high fantasy game about recovering treasure from subterranean complexes.

        There’s also a line that must be drawn at some point of what non-mechanical content you consider fluff vs. crunch. To use a simple example, look at the first Merit that appears in the so-called crunch section of Secrets of the Covenants; alley cat. The purely mechanical function of that Merit is to decrease the total number of 8s, 9s, or 10s that need to be rolled in order to achieve critical success on certain rolls by the dot rating. Are the rolls it applies to part of the crunch? I think most people would say yes - the mechanics are incomplete without them after all. But, “rolling to hide, evade, or pursue” is not game-mechanical information. It necessarily references what is happening in the fiction, not a value on the character sheet. How about the fact that it only applies “in your character’s home city streets”? That qualifier is meaningless without reference to the setting, and in fact suggests an urban, probably modern, setting. How about the fact that the Merit requires a dot of Carthian Status? That’s a much more direct reference to the mechanics, and yet says even more about the setting - you can’t know what that means without knowing what a Carthian is. And then we get to the description of Carthians being the vampires most intimately familiar with city streets. Indisputably setting content, yet it takes up half of the text of the Merit, and also contextualizes the mechanics of the Merit.

        And this is just one very simple Merit. The line between game mechanics and setting content only gets blurrier with more complex mechanics like Conditions, or Disciplines. The rules are intimately connected with the stories they are used to tell, such that the categories of “fluff” and “crunch” are ultimately arbitrary. You can point to parts of the books that do not contain mechanics text and say “I want less of this kind of content” easily enough, but it’s much harder to separate the setting content from the rules content on the parts that do contain mechanical text.

                                        I don’t think Onyx Path does focus on “role play over roll play” (*hurk*). White Wolf made its brand on being “the role player’s RPG” in a time when D&D 3e was the industry standard. And like the witticism about not having to outrun the bear, White Wolf didn’t need the lightest mechanics on the market to sell itself that way, only to be lighter than D&D.

                                        Onyx Path has inherited that reputation for being “the story gamer’s alternative to D&D,” but it hasn’t really been their MO for a long time. They’ve long since carved out their niche, and mechanics-light is not what that niche is. And I’m pretty sure they’re well aware of that.

                                                        Originally posted by figurehead View Post
                                                        What does (*hurk*) mean?
                                                        It’s the sound of throwing up in one’s own mouth a little bit. I make it when I use certain expressions that are common in RPG discussions to express my distaste for them. Role play vs. roll play is one of them, and ElvesofZion gave a pretty good summary of why I so detest that phrase. Another one is “good roleplaying.” As in, “I like to award bonus experience for good roleplaying.” These idioms and others like them present a warped view of roleplaying and roleplaying games, which I feel are harmful to the hobby. But, they are so ubiquitous in RPG discussion that it is sometimes more convenient, or even necessary, to use them to quickly communicate my point. So I use an onamonapia to express that, though I am using the idiom in question for expediency’s sake, I feel a little bit gross for doing so.
