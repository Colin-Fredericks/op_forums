      The layout decisions for 2e are such that a bunch of systems that are contained in the 1e nWoD corebook are not reproduced in the rules chapters of all 2e splat corebooks due to not being primary considerations in the design of those lines and/or space concerns leading to an alternative representation.

      2e's mechanics for explosives and explosions are included in Hurt Locker (p134-137) and in Deviant: the Renegades (p204). The mechanics for falling damage can be found in the Chronicles of Darkness corebook (a.k.a the Revised Storytelling System Rulebook) (p97-98), Promethean 2e Core (p212-213), Changeling 2e Core (p190), Hunter 2e Core (p144), Geist 2e Core (p164), Mummy 2e Core (p189), Beast Core (p172), and Deviant Core.

      Basically almost every corebook for a gameline or new edition published after 2015 has the falling mechanics in it; Vampire, Werewolf, Mage, and Demon were all initially released while the IP was owned by CCP Games, who would not allow new editions to be made and thus were initially written as "Chronicle books" to be used with the 1e (new) World of Darkness corebook and the God-Machine Chronicle Rules Update to serve as a second edition in all but name, but the Chronicles of Darkness corebook wasn't compiled until after Vampire and Werewolf had updated Blood and Smoke: the Strix Chronicle and The Wolf Must Hunt: the Idigam Chronicle into Vampire: the Requiem Second Edition and Werewolf: the Forsaken Second Edition, respectively, by which point the layout decision had been made to include some iteration of the core mechanics as a dedicated chapter in each gameline's corebook.

      The specific format of that rules chapter has changed a couple times over the years, but the one in Deviant is both availing itself of the streamlined version that's been more or less used since Changeling 2e and incorporating the widest variety of additional systems for sources of harm and danger (such as drowning/suffocation, radiation, and acid, as well as separating Tilts beyond Poisoned and Sick into moderate and severe).

                              First of, while there are many similarities, overall I recommend to read this as a completely distinct game, because it is dissimilar enough that you may miss a lot of details if you don't read it as a new thing. Like how Requiem vampires has far better senses than VtM ones, with actual night vision and an astounding perception of blood as the main, but not sole, examples.

                              CofD 2nd Edition does have a core mechanic for mortals that addresses how morality and experiences change. Integrity actually lets you define your own list of "sins", here called Breaking Points. What keeps it balanced is that you actually get something out of rolling them, so how frequently you want to roll Integrity is actually an open question.

                              Still, it is extremely important to notice the conceptual differences between VtM Humanity and VtR Humanity, and both and Integrity, which is also why they're now called Breaking Points. Integrity has more to do with resilience to stress and trauma, so to speak, than morality. It isn't about being good or bad, but about going through experiences that shake your sanity and/or emotional stability.

                              Humanity in Requiem has to do with how much you remember how it is to be human and how to pretend to be one of them. Humanity Breaking Points may be crimes sometimes, but they're there less because they're bad things and more because they dampen your connection with humans, making you feel less a person and more a thing of the night. It doesn't take into account your actual feelings on this matter, either, truly monstrous Kindred may still want high Humanity because that makes easier for them to pretend and walk among the sheep, while a staunch defender of human rights may have low Humanity because that doesn't really means they feel any connection or similarity besides being sapient beings and they don't want to pretend to.

                              That doesn't means VtR Humanity doesn't have some Gothic Morality undertones to it, but in general the idea stands, it isn't about being nice, but about having empathy with humans in the more technical definition of the word.

                              All that also means that both Humanity and Integrity in CofD 2nd E have Breaking Points that are brought against you from the outside, not actions you take, and they're important to the game. A Mortal will frequently face Breaking Points through traumatic experiences with the supernatural regardless of what they chose to do about them. And Vampires suffer Breaking Points from things like reaching certain age thresholds, staying torpid for a long time or simply surviving humanly impossible injuries.

                                      Originally posted by monteparnas View Post
                                      That doesn't means VtR Humanity doesn't have some Gothic Morality undertones to it, but in general the idea stands, it isn't about being nice, but about having empathy with humans in the more technical definition of the word.
                                      To draw a mechanical comparison to a later gameline, a vampire at Humanity 1 never rolls better than a chance die on any attempts to understand or relate to humans as anything other than food or threats, and this bears some similarities to the ways unbound geists in Geist: the Sin-Eater are constrained in exchange for their relative freedom of movement and deathly empowerment: the only Social rolls from and toward that aren't limited to a chance die are rolls to intimidate or frighten, and at the most tenuous connection to their Sin-Eater this is also the dicepool used to try and get it to not manifest in response to perceived or real threats and dangers.

                                      Compare also Mummy, whose Memory stat goes down when you act against the person you are now or deny the person you used to be and consequently bring yourself more in line with what the Judges you answer to want you to be, i.e. a brutal instrument of Bronze Age judgment and reappropriator of magical relics who moves only for the satisfaction of the avaricious urges of demonic gods of pain and misery.

                                      Or compare Demon, whose Cover stat wants you to be invested in not letting people connect true knowledge of your secret nature to the on-paper identity you occupy as a rogue archon on the run from the Demiurge that made you, or Changeling, whose Clarity stat contrariwise really wants you to not have to acknowledge the otherworldly life that turned you fae or be confronted with the sort of accidental gaslighting that happens when you insist to normal people that some stuff that doesn't even sound possible (and in many cases doesn't even sound like appealing fiction to the invested) happened to you.

                                      Three of these five gamelines had Rose Bailey prominently on their development team and I know she's spoken directly to at least two of them having the mechanics of their Integrity analogues tied up in the pressures of having to lie about what you are on a daily basis; perhaps relatedly, a trend that emerges as one reads through a lot of personal-scale text in Chronicles is that humanity is broadly aligned with the concept of denial or negation or falsehood and forces thereof, whether it's Heroes in Beast: the Primordial, the Spring Court in Changeling, the Abyssal shard in Mage, hunters in Hunter, or Requiem's deal with Humanity in general and the Strix's beef with it in particular.

                                      It's also notable that Humanity as an Integrity analog is a model mechanically designed to be lost down to a level the character can stabilize at, and that investment of effort (and Experiences) toward maintaining a higher level is something characters can choose to do or not bother doing. Some other monsters work similarly (notably including mages and hunters), while others start low and build higher as part of a progressing narrative (in Geist's case Synergy is actually a Supernatural Potency stat with loss conditions, whereas in Promethean and Mummy Pilgrimage and Memory the character is pursuing understanding of humanity and themselves, respectively) and still others have less entropic models like two-way scales (e.g. Harmony from Werewolf or Satiety from Beast) or damage tracks (Clarity from Changeling or Stability from Deviant: the Renegades).

                                      Requiem loosely attaches becoming a worse person to becoming worse at being "a person," but it also provides the loss of your original human context as another route to that slow slide down the ladder, whether it's the eventual death of everyone who knew you or the encountering a culture that didn't exist when you were alive or just the simple cognitive dissonance of being confronted with the fact that you're dead even though you're walking around (a similar realization also being one of the few things that can cause ghosts to lose Integrity after their death, as well). The moral metaphysics of the Chronicles of Darkness are such that the fabric of a character's identity as a participant in human society and the world tends to react poorly to contradictions of that identity, Walt Whitman quotes about containing multitudes be damned.

                                                  Originally posted by Cain Loup-Noir View Post
                                                  This mean that the st should take in account each pc persona individually instead of using the general hierarchy of sin in the book.
                                                  So the thing is, Chronicles of Darkness does not generally support wholly removing incentives against killing from its Integrity systems for characters who aren't extremely spiritually divergent from normal humanity (and even then). Personally-defined breaking points are generally in addition to the ones printed in the book and the overwhelming majority of human cultures socialize their members to attach a negative connotation to the general act of killing people and build on exceptions later that, mechanically, may make the roll more likely to have a positive result. Gaining new banes simply doesn't practically work otherwise.

                                                  This isn't like Morality, where people could make arguments about the severity of different degrees of assault and theft — a vampire who has deadened themselves to the impact of killing someone in a fit of passion no longer takes the Bestial, Competitive, or Wanton Condition when they do so, but deliberately killing someone is still bad for the Humanity of most vampires who have the average Humanity rating for their age unless they've warped their souls and taken on weird folkloric weaknesses that nevertheless get less severe the better they are at pretending they're human. (And that specific division is likewise important for mages, who reverse the order of planned and impassioned murder on their own ladder because Wisdom is concerned with self-control over a process that can operate subconsciously.)

                                                  I get that player psychology tends to be risk-averse and loss-averse and Humanity costing Experiences to raise makes it feel bad to roll for Detachment if you aren't gleefully along for the ride as the character degenerates, but unless you are killing people all the time this is a problem that literally solves itself once you've managed to restabilize your night-to-night unliving situation and that's part of the reason vampires build sociopolitical machines and make ghouls and thralls and carefully cultivate the spaces they move through and rest in.

                                                  Being forced to do illegal, immoral, and/or unethical stuff is the domain of the desperate, while going out of your way to do it is the stuff of the terminally overprivileged. Neither is good for maintaining relatability to the average living member of your society.

                                                      First of all, in addition to what Satchel said, and considering that narrative and thematic relevance take precedence over everything else, I want to add something as a Psychologist:

                                                      Killing isn't a matter of morality.

                                                      Most societies are against killing outside very specific situations, either as law or just a moral standing, but beyond that there's a mental block against killing we're simply wired with as humans.

                                                      Of course this doesn't means we can't kill. We very clearly can. It also don't mean killing is always immoral, it very clearly isn't. But it does mean that for a non-disabled person in that area (like some sociopaths and psychopaths), killing is ALWAYS traumatic, no exceptions.

                                                      This has even been one of the biggest conundrums of military training through human history. Most fatalities in battles don't actually occur from directly fatal injuries, because very few soldiers can actually force themselves to cause such injuries. The flinch, they pull back, they cause non-fatal injuries if they can. It's all nice and dandy at training and talking, but to actually do it is a lot harder. Most soldiers actually survive loosing battles, and the deaths that do occur are usually due to infected injuries. More soldiers fall to other causes rather than enemy steel.

                                                      And one of the reasons experienced soldiers are valuable is because they're better at getting to do the deed. That's also a big source of trauma for soldiers, and one of the reasons the US military has one of the worse outcomes for veteran mental health is because it cracked the secret to significantly increase disposition to kill. This works, but takes a severe toll on the soldiers (which are then released in society mentally broken, as many veterans know).

                                                      So at the same time that it is an important narrative element the idea that killing is never without trauma, or ok when you're at a very bad place already, it's also actually very real that killing is always traumatic regardless of moral standing or previous deeds. And remember, the main point here is trauma, not morality.

                                                      This mean that the st should take in account each pc persona individually instead of using the general hierarchy of sin in the book.
                                                      The problem here is two-fold.

                                                      First, the overall idea is to have a distinct Integrity-like system that works with the themes of the splat, so avoiding to do the same mortals do is part of the point. The Hierarchy of Sins in Vampire is meant to work within the narrative themes of the game and reinforce just how much a Vampire both is a former human, but is also drifting from it, becoming something else.

                                                      Second, the very fact that it exists to deal with a non-human psyche and a specific sort of consequence. This is not about trauma in a general sense, this is specifically about the kind of shock that may deaden the connection of the character to a human condition they don't really have anymore.

                                                      How a Vampire reacts to emotional trauma in general is given by their behavior, but the very fact they don't have an Integrity anymore and won't suffer the Conditions a mortal suffers for those things is telling. Humanity covers things the character may not really give a damn about, they're not trauma in the sense of being emotionally shocking, but in that they affect their memory of what it's like to be physiologically alive and eat food, instead of being a predator that sucks blood from the living.

                                                      In a sense, it's the very fact that the character doesn't suffer a regular Breaking Point from murder anymore that makes them susceptible to a Humanity Breaking Point when doing it. It's not their emotional, moral or rational stance on the act of killing, but the fact that it feels like killing a vermin, now, and different of a human no amount of thinking this is bad will make it feel bad.
