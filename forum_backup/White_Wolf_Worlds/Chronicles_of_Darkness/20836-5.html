        Oh good, I was worried I'd double post.

        I thought I should share my ideas/notes for the Viziers just in case someone else wants to write them. Most of it is how they differ from Savants, seeing as how they can be disturbingly close conceptually. The Viziers (puppet-master/adviser) and the Savants (revolutionary/teacher) can appear to be different points in the same philosophical cycle. This is not so.

        The big one is that Viziers rule by proxy (if they rule at all), while Savants 'rule' by legacy (or to put it in more obscure terms, meme).

        What this actually entails has sort of already been mentioned, having been discussed on pages 4 (splat outline) and 5 (virtue). The bottom line has not though. To put it in the simplest terms I can; Savants are not the philosophy of scholars and Viziers are not the philosophy of con men. They can be but it is not inherent. Remember that their virtues (Rationality for
        Viziers, Resourcefulness for Savants) does not directly support these roles. So instead, I looked at how these roles support their respective virtues.

        It's simple really, Viziers are planners, schemers, working their networks of information and wealth two steps ahead of everyone, proactively pursuing their agenda. They are careful to keep their goals grounded in reality. Which also makes it easier to deal with your average Joe Blow, since they keep abreast of such affairs. If I had to anolog Viziers with another groups splat, it would be Demon's Tempters.

        Think of the proverb: 'give a man a fish and you feed him for a day; teach a man to fish and you feed him for a lifetime'.
        Vizier's give the fish because it efficently solves the immedite problem and reinforces the man's dependance upon the Vizier (or at least his web of influence).
        Savant's teach the man to fish...and asks said man 'why didn't the other [Vizier/Dragon] teach him?'

        And so the seeds of the revolt are sown.

        Sorry, delved a little deep to get accurate influences.

            I haven't read everything in the new book, but here are my thoughts on some of what I've seen so far.

            I don't really like the Caitiffs. At least not the name (surely we can do better than stealing from VtM?) and their being considered a Philosophy of their own. They seem a lot more like the absence of a Philosophy. Also, the connection to Huang Long being through honesty and humility makes Caitiffs seem "better" than the other Philosophies--like they're the only ones who have the right idea, while the other Philosophies are hobbled by delusions of grandeur and a need to hang on to at least some of their old glory. Given that the whole game centers around dragons as metaphors for rulership, elevating the ones who run away from the power and responsibility of being Oroboros--who made the decision to become Oroboroi, but couldn't cope with the consequences of that choice--seems rather...incongruous. Even more so to associate those dragons who run away from what they are, who try to be human even as their methods of sustaining themselves is the most animalistic and monstrous of all, with "honesty".

            (I kind of slipped into "in-character" voice there--while I don't like the Caitiff as presented, I don't actually have the contempt for them that the last couple of sentences might imply. They're a valid character option, but I don't think they should be a Philosophy unto themselves.)

            Perhaps it would be better for Huang Long to be associated with something else--or perhaps no Philosophy at all, as the Yellow Dragon was often considered to be "above" the other directional beasts in some way in Chinese folklore. This being the case, if a Philosophy ends up being created for him/her/it, it probably shouldn't relate to "honesty" or "humility"--if anything, arrogance would probably be a characteristic flaw. "Stability" would be more appropriate, as well as more resonant with the original myths.

            I also am somewhat leery of Savants being associated with anarchy--my original conception of them owed much to the Confucian ideal of the scholarly junzi, or "gentleman" (an "ideal man", probably of high rank, whose understanding of propriety and correctness not only in people but in the workings of society and the world gives rise to a peaceful and orderly society) and Plato's concept of the philosopher-king.

            Honestly, chaos and anarchy strike me as something that the enemies of the Oroboroi would be more likely to endorse than the Oroboroi themselves. Even a the leader of a rebellion doesn't want rebellion in his own ranks. One must remember, also, that the end goal of a rebellion is the replacement of a corrupt order (or at least, one with which the rebels disagree) with a better (less disagreeable, from the rebels' perspective) order. (Generally, one that has the rebels on top.) It's not an endorsement of disorder, or a condemnation of the rule of law.

            Furthermore, the Four (or Five) Beasts are not actual in opposition to one another, in terms of North-South/East-West or any other arrangement. They represent the cycle of seasons the five elements, the general climate and terrain in those directions in ancient China, and the astrological influences of their respective regions of the sky, all of which were complimentary rather than oppositional. (This is a thing in Chinese philosophy in general--opposites are seen as complimentary parts of the same thing, rather than two separate and intrinsically opposed things. Dualism is more characteristic of Western philosophy.)

            All this in mind, the dualistic opposition comes across as kind of forced, at least to me.

            Dragons also need a major antagonistic force. Something that shakes things up, shocks them out of their routines, forces them out of their comfort zone, makes them do interesting, story-worthy things that break out of the mold of "survive, maintain Province". All the Chronicle books are based on this logic: the Strix are a threat that completely upends the vampiric playing field, the Idigam are an Outside Context Problem that none of the tribes of the Uratha are equipped for, Mysteries (otherwise known as Really Weird Shit) just will not leave Mages alone, nor they Mysteries, the angels and Infrastructure of the God-Machine are literally everywhere...so what do Dragons have? Neither the Einherjar nor the Knights of the Bloody Chalice are a good fit for this, IMHO: they're both just variations on the premise of Hunter, and a principally human antagonist just doesn't come across as the massive threat that the Strix, the Idigam, and the God-Machine are to vampires, werewolves, and demons and mortals respectively. Without something like that, the game just isn't as compelling.

            We need an antagonist that's tied into the mythology of the Oroboroi, but that isn't of the Oroboroi. Something so powerful and inimical to dragonkind that mortal enemies will call a truce and work together to survive it. Something that has ties to dragons, has an interest in them, has a history with them...something that makes them feel what an ordinary human feels when faced with a dragon in True Form. Frantic. Desperate to survive. Fragile. A feeling of "no this can't be happening they're not supposed to be real they're just stories oh god I don't want to die!"

            That kind of antagonist.

            Without that sort of looming threat, Dragon is much less compelling as a game unto itself, as opposed to something you read for the ideas, raid for mechanics, and crossover with other games. YMMV, of course; this is just how I see it.

                        Actually, the original Philosophies work very well as Pure/Belial's Brood/Scelesti style antagonists. It's something I suggested in the thread on the old forums:

                        Originally posted by Exploding Frogs
                        Another way to integrate the current Embers Philosophies into the rewrite would be to make them an antagonist faction, centering around different forms of tyranny and oppression: Oroboroi who refuse to learn from the Collapse and want to indulge in being dread god-monsters to the mortal cattle. (As opposed to the protagonist Philosophies, which apply lessons learned in the Collapse toward a mutually beneficial relationship with their subjects; even the Imperators are interested in authoritarian rule as a means to make a nation great rather than to parasitically leech off of the populace and luxuriate in their own power.

                        Ideas on how this would work with each Philosophy:

                        Ash: "Obey me, or be destroyed. Don't think I mean it? Neither did this guy, and now he's going to be an example."

                        Blood: Control their subjects with tangled webs of conflicting loyalties and obligations, playing them off of one another and preventing them from rising up.

                        Bone: Aggressively maintain the status quo, on the one hand maintaining everything in reasonable condition ("the trains run on time") but on the other crushing innovation and change.

                        Sweat: Ruthless taskmasters who relentlessly drive their subjects to create monuments to their glory and takes away what few luxuries they are permitted in the form of tribute and taxes.

                        Tears: Sadists on a power trip who love seeing their subjects squirm. Pointlessly abuse power specifically to cause suffering.
                        Some other things that I think need to change:

                        * The jade weakness. Oroboroi aren't werewolves; it doesn't fit any of the mythic or folkloric associations for them to have a particular substance they are vulnerable to. Particularly not jade, which was thought by the ancient Chinese to be a pure/noble/divine substance, in much the same way that dragons were themselves thought to be pure, noble, and divine beings. It's not mandatory for a WoD splat to have a substance that is especially harmful to them; it's only used if it fits the folklore from which the splat is drawn.

                        * The pseudo-scientific and pseudo-historical explanations for splat traits and powers. Much like midichlorians, they're answers to questions no one asked, and they actually hurt suspension of disbelief. Don't explain how the Heart works as a blood-pumping organ. Don't go into involved explanations of the physical space taken up by the Heart and Furnace. Don't explain where every bit of terminology came from in-universe. This is a game about fire-breathing dragons, for Pete's sake. I guarantee you that if you leave these things unexplained, no one will notice or care. And the explanations you've used clash horribly with all the talk of divine right, tyrannical god-monsters, and the soul of the world.

                        * Relatedly, the organ removal in exchange for higher Furnace. Firstly, see above re: pseudoscience. Second, there's no basis for it in myths and stories about dragons. Third, it presents a serious obstacle to role-playing and encourages roll-playing (players should not have to expend mechanical resources to have their characters express emotion.) Fourth, it excessively penalizes Furnace increase; the Heartbeat is more than sufficient on its own.

                        * Typhoon. Myths and stories of dragons credit them with influence over the weather, but this is generally portrayed as being something entirely under their control. This is clearly a matter of Ablutions and Edicts, not uncontrollable side effects of high Furnace.

                        * Mandate is not nearly as divorced from human morality as advertised. The higher ranges should have more to do with being unquestionably In Charge and having a well-maintained province than being benevolent or beloved by the people; the lower ranges should be more about abandoning a ruler's dignity, authority, and self-respect than about being a petty dictator. Petty dictators can be competent rulers (witness Machiavelli's The Prince), and someone can be loved by the people and still be running the country into the ground.

                        * Philosophies are an X-splat kind of power, not a Y-splat kind of power.

                        * Sparti. First, the correct word is Spartoi. Second, it's probably too powerful: creating supernaturally skilled humans out of thin air on demand? Really? Third, it comes across as vaguely cartoonish. I'd suggest something like turning "the fertile soil" into a metaphor for "a pregnant woman", thereby forcing dragons to wait for their Spartoi to grow to adulthood. Or maybe a metaphor for "a human with great potential", thus making it into the equivalent of Ghouls, Stigmatics, or Sadikh, and setting hard limits on how many Spartoi a dragon can create.

                        * Grafts. They're not especially thematic; they seem more like leftovers from the earlier versions of the game than anything else.

                        * The general portrayal as the Oroboroi as instrinsically and unavoidably doomed to extinction. Give players reason to hope for their characters' future! Let there be a promise of Ascension or Apotheosis: a semi-mythical ascent to the condition of the True Dragons of old, shrouded in mystery, rumor, and outright misinformation but promising salvation for those who can attain it.

                        * Deep Ones. They don't fit in a game about dragons, either in style and tone or in function. They need to go.

                        * Less defined backstory. The New World of Darkness doesn't have a history per se. It has stories and rumors and urban legends. The origins of supernatural entities are vague and uncertain, never spelled out in detail in the books, so that each individual chronicle can come up with their own origins. There is no Caine for the vampires, no Antediluvians, no Enoch. Or if there is, it's just one of many, many stories they tell of where they came from, and who can say whether it's the truth? Atlantis is an allegory for the world used to be different before the Fall, not a history of a literal island-nation of mages that existed way back when. The Uratha begin every story they tell with "This story is true.", after the fashion of many orally-transmitted cultures. That doesn't mean that the stories are literal histories of events that happened, but rather that they articulate the truths the Uratha live by through metaphor and symbolism. The Oroboroi should be no different. They know that once upon a time, dragons ruled the world; they know that now, they must hide from mankind. They have an inkling that their power was stolen from greater beings even as they themselves stole their power from their Antecedents. They feel the Mandate of Heaven deep in their bones. They gather scraps of memory, dream, and nightmare from the Treasury, yet who can say what of that is true or false, and what of the true is history and what is allegory? Scatter stories of their origins throughout the book, but feel free to let the details contradict one another: only the broad strokes of it are consistent. The rest is for the players and Storyteller to decide at the gaming table.
