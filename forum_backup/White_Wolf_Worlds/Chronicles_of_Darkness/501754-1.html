            Originally posted by StSword View Post
            That's a real easy question to answer- so someone who would have fun playing a seemingless changeling can do so.

            And it's hardly a great loss being seemingless- so far all we know is that you lose out on an advantage and a disadvantage, and some contract affinity which is no great loss if you aren't planning on buying those contracts anyway.

            Unless something drastic is added to seemings, then being seemingless is a minor disadvantage at best.
            While you're correct that the absence may feel overstated, I think it is a bigger one than alluded here. From David's remarks, the majority of Contracts will be of the universal variety. The way those work, going by the Outline,* is that you buy the Contract for 1-5 experiences (depending on its rating). Each universal Contract has one or more favored Seemings (6 is called out as rare), for which it gives an additional or focused benefit: if you're a member of a listed Seeming, you get its version free when you buy the Contract. If you're not, or you want to also buy another Seeming's version, you can pay an experience to learn it from a member of another Seeming. If you have no Seeming, you always have to pay that experience to learn from someone else if you're to get an extra benefit from it.

            At the end of the day, while it's clear there's a gap, the degree to which this point matters will depend on how good the favored Seeming benefits actually are. If they're more of a nice touch, then it's probably not a significant impediment. If they can make a Contract much more useful, I can see how that could ruffle some feathers.

            That said, with the emphasis on agency, I'll probably actually be okay with having a difference like this. Probably. It'll depend on one very important question to me: if a Changeling clearly reclaims his or her life and agency in a significant way but also really doesn't line up with any of the six Seemings, what happens? Does the character just get the closest Seeming to the manner of escape that will fit (which is less than ideal, but okay), or does she wind up Seeming-less? If it's the latter, I feel like that breaks the metaphor for me: having a Seeming feels less like a manifestation of the agency a character reclaimed, and instead feels like that's true only if the character's reclamation was one of the 6** specific "right" ways to go about it. The achievement isn't diminished for those who manage it, but it would lose a lot of luster for me.

            Of course, that concern also depends on all six Seemings collectively feeling like they leave sizable gaps in ways to reclaim agency that aren't caught by any of them. That's impossible to say without seeing all the write-ups and any lead-in to the Seeming section, but I'd be lying if I said it wasn't a mild concern: the three we've seen feel just a bit too specific to me.

            Hmm... Didn't really intend for that reply to morph into a perhaps poorly written expression of my likely baseless primary source for 2e trepidation, but there we go. ^_^o

            * See the relevant section:
            Originally posted by 2e Outline, on Universal Contracts
            These have a single name. They’re lone powers. They have dot ratings, you buy them at their dot rating. So, a three dot contract costs 3 experiences. These each have one or more favored seemings, where they do an additional or focused thing. You could theoretically list six favored seemings. That should be rare. If you have that seeming, you get it for free. Otherwise, you have to find a member of that seeming to teach you, and it costs one experience.
            ** Yes, and eventually 7, possibly 8.

                            Stoic Endurance is not an act of Agency. You don't get a gold (or should that be red?) star for just sitting there. You don't escape barb wires by just sitting there.

                            Even if I ignore some of the real world parallels(Which I'm going to, because 'Stoic Endurance' in those circumstances makes me furious), the Wyrd is a narrative force. Protagonists aren't protagonists because they sit there, but because they are pro-active agents. A character who does nothing to handle the conflict and instead idly prays for its' passing isn't going to be rewarded.

                            I mean, don't we get pissed at characters who get all the reward when they do nothing for it? Don't we hate heroes who haven't earned their heroism in fiction, the stories that tell us we should praise them just cause? The Wyrd is no different.

                            The one who sits, the one who waits, the one who accepts-there is no Path to the Tower of Revolution for that one. And when they escape, they are going to have to come to terms with that, until they develop the adolescence needed to confront their world.
