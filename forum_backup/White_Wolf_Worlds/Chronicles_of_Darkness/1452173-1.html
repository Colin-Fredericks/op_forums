    Originally posted by Penelope View Post
    What do you think of this?
    Just in general, it's frequently perceived as rude to ask one member of a minority to "answer" for the actions of another. As I said before, we're not a monolith. Judaism, more like an ethnic group than many religious groups, has no centralized leadership. There's no head rabbi dictating what is, and isn't appropriate Jewish behavior. The only religious authorities Jews recognize are: parents until their child is acknowledged as an adult by religious standards (the Bar Mitzvah ceremony), and the rabbi(s) an individual Jew chooses to follow (this is actually something that extends past religion in Jewish philosophy; Jews are expected to follow the advice of experts when they ask experts for advice, and thus are also told to be careful in picking their experts).

    So... while I think I get where you're coming from, please try to think of better ways to go about these sorts of questions (and this doesn't just apply to Jews).

    My Jewish (Israeli, actually) friend told me that he tried pepperoni pizza once when he was younger and liked it even though he’s always kept kosher. He said the reason he did that is cause it’s not really a sacrifice to give it up if you don’t know what you’re missing.
    So, I'll address two things here. First is my best attempt to relate Jewish theological philosophy to the situation. Second, I'll address this as, "HA, if you saw this character in a work, how would it make you feel?"

    The first one is going to get a bit lengthy, so I'll start with the short version: While Judaism would consider this a transgression, and thus it would require ritual cleansing of the body and the soul to avoid negative divine judgement, most Jewish philosophers would say that it's more important to value the commandments of our faith from a place of wisdom than one of ignorance. It's better to transgress and know what not keeping Kosher is like if that helps you appreciate Kashrut, than to follow the laws by rote and have no real attachment to them.

    In Jewish moral philosophy, there are only three transgressions that have no exceptions. That is, every other rule in Judaism comes with caveats, and exists in a hierarchy of levels of transgressions, mitigating factors, and so on. It's important to note that Judaism does not phrase these three transgressions as inherently ones that come with specific punishments at mortal hands (though we do that too), but rather these are the three sins that you should die before you commit: murder, sexual violence, and idolatry (not going to get into that one in detail, but this is its own very complicated subject).

    So, where does keeping Kosher fit into all this? Surprisingly low actually. As long as it's just between you and the Lord, eating something unkosher is ultimately a fairly low down sin. There is no case where, if you had to pick between two transgressions, eating something unkosher isn't the clear choice to make. Even when it wouldn't be clear, it's a coin-flip between two equally less important ones. Remember, this is the maximum punishment for breaking these laws (again, just for yourself): say a prayer and take a ritual bath. That's all you have to do before you can reclaim your position as a Jew-in-good-standing to participate in Jewish ritual life. You can see why the rabbis do not think harshly of exploration of transgressions on this level in younger Jews.

    Of course, things get more complicated when other people get involved. Taking your impressionable younger sibling to your pepperoni pizza party is not OK. Rabbis and other prominent Jews are held to a higher standard lest their actions led others astray by creating a sense that it is not a transgression (however minor of one). And so on.

    And, to come from the other part of this, Judaism has a long standing internal debate over two seemingly contradictory commandments: regular ritual practice, and only performing ritual practices if your heart in committed to them. Not saying the Shema prayer every day is a transgression, but saying it if I don't mean it is also a transgression... so what do you do? This is why Jewish theologians heavily stress allowing young Jews to explore the lesser transgressions and such grey areas in religious law. The only real solution is to let people find that balance through experience with the faith.


    Onto part 2:

    This is extremely common, and if you write a Jewish character that hasn't done something like this, we can kinda tell your exposure to Jews is limited. Doubt is natural, teenagers are rebellious, so Jews tend to have a few transgressions under their belts.

                                    I really wish I could find the article I read the other day about the wendigo. It explains a lot of the "what's the big deal" parts of this conversation very eloquently, and from the perspective of someone with actually cultural and scholarly authority.

                                    So the wendigo is part of a belief system. One that's still practiced today.

                                    People from outside that system take the wendigo and adapt it to their fictions, their novels and games. And it changes, like a game of telephone.

                                    Other people are exposed to those fictions and are inspired--not by the original concept, but by the fiction that was in turn inspired by the original concept--to include the wendigo in their own works. And it changes more.

                                    Soon, there is a substantial body of work--art, stories, online pseudo-mythology--that all uses the name "wendigo". They share some common themes (giant evil deer-headed zombie/skeleton monster, etc).

                                    At this point, more people outside of the original culture are familiar with the deviant art/D&D version of the wendigo than the original. What's more, *they don't even realize that what they consider a "wendigo" ISN'T the original*.

                                    And a cultural myth, a story that's a part of a living, breathing system of belief (and an extremely serious and important one, at that) is altered. Damaged in a way that it's people had no hand in and cannot repair.

                                                    Let's look at a fairly simple and direct comparison: Rock & roll vs. hip hop.

                                                    Rock & roll and hip hop are both musical genres that were created by black American subculture.

                                                    Rock & roll was heavily appropriated. First by white American culture, and then by white culture in general, and by global culture. Hip hop didn't stagnate into some static art form. It's constantly evolved, and frequently from positive cultural exchanges rather than apropriative ones.

                                                            Please stop using the past tense. This is happening around the world, right now. It's not something that only happened in history. You, whether you see it or not, are a beneficiary of colonialism. You are gaining at the expense and harm of others even if your country wasn't one of the big colonial powers. Other people are suffering for your life to be better. Allowing the distance between you and them to shield this from your cultural framework only helps perpetuate the harm being done.



                                                            That's easy to say when nobody in your family has had their creative works stolen from them for others to profit from. Everything is pretty when you can't see the pile of bodies trampled on to make that beautiful artistic work.



                                                            This isn't really a good faith response to the issue of cultural appropriation.

                                                            Let's look at a fairly simple and direct comparison: Rock & roll vs. hip hop.

                                                            Rock & roll and hip hop are both musical genres that were created by black American subculture.

                                                            Rock & roll was heavily appropriated. First by white American culture, and then by white culture in general, and by global culture. Extremely few of the creators of rock & roll got rich, while a lot of white people got rich off them. And rock & roll didn't get dynamic for it, it got static. For the first 30 years or so of its popularity, with the originators pushed out, the appropriators were just remixing the same material over and over for pop audiences. When you think of the revival of rock & roll's creativity in the 70s and 80s, it wasn't because global exposure to the music led to all sorts of innovations... it's because white people starting adding black and latin innovations to their work. The only reason we have hard rock and heavy metal, is because white people starting appropriating the blues to spice up how dull rock & roll had become in their hands.

                                                            The hip hop and rap artists grew up with that history. So they fought it. They pushed to keep control over their work, push against appropriation, and to make sure that other artists that wanted in &quot;earned&quot; it instead of took it. Are there non-black hip hop groups? Of course there are. But it never stops being a black cultural expression that other people are knowingly engaging in. The pioneers of it are acknowledged and elevated instead of shoved aside for more mainstream faces. Black people haven't been shut out of the economic and social success their creativity generates. Hip hop didn't stagnate into some static art form. It's constantly evolved, and frequently from positive cultural exchanges rather than apropriative ones.
