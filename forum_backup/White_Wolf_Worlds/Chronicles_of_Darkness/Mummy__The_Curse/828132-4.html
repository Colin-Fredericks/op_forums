        Some questions to ask of Bound of the Black Land:

        1. What do they call themselves? "Sin-Eater" is a very instantiated term, so what would be culturally significant enough to be used to refer to the Bound? Ideally, I think it should be a term that both resonates with the culture of the time and place, but also a term that has fallen out of use and has been recycled by the Bound.

        2. Sin-Eaters are always breaking laws. What metaphysical laws are they breaking in this age? What cultural mythoi are subverted by their very existence? I mentioned Osiris earlier; would comparisons invite sanctification or blasphemy?

        3. What are geists? Beyond what the book says geists are, what are geists to Sin-Eaters here? When we think of ghosts nowadays we often think about emotional or psychic resonances. To the Kemetics, ghosts were an aspect of the soul, right? The Sheut or Ba or something detached from the rest after death? Is that how occultists view geists? How does that color impressions of the Bound? Do they have too much Ba? Or did they lose their Ba in the Bargain and have it replaced with the geist's own?

        4. Are there any Bound Conspiracies afoot at the time? Does the Black Land have representatives of the Dzimba Woye? Or is it still too early for them? How about a Great Cult that goes all the way back to Irem (or at least claims such)?

            1. "The Bound" is quite possibly an appropriate term, but a word that had fallen out of use by the First Turn is 'Gala', a specialized Sumerian priesthood specifically meant to lament things, and were associated with Inanna and Ereshkigal.

            2. The Gala were not easily defined in Sumerian society- they had no fixed gender identity, and were trained in a dialect of Sumerian used specifically to render the speech of goddesses called "eme-sal" the "fine tongue" or "high-pitched voice". Despite that, many Gala were male and had large families. Their entire purpose was to lament, to appease the dead and ensure that the living continued to remember and sacrifice to them. Sumerian and Egyptian belief was that those without sacrifices suffered an uncomfortable life in the Underworld. In the CofD, this is literally true, as offerings and remembrance of the dead provides Essence. In Egypt, I'm not so sure they'd compare themselves to Osiris/Azar, but it is entirely possible they'd connect themselves with his dying and rising. This would make the Egyptian people rather upset, seeing as after being resurrected Osiris dutifully marched off to Duat to take up a throne there. The common response would likely be to cry "Blasphemy!" at least later. In the First Turn, the Osiris cult was just forming, and there was no real concept of homogenous or national religion. Association with Osiris would call upon his nature as God of Transition, aiding ghosts in moving on. Association with Anubis would call upon his nature as guide and protector of the dead. Osiris is the lord of the land of the dead, but it is Anubis who is god of the necropolis.

            3. In Sumeria, they are the Gedim- simple as that. However, exactly what they are made of is a point of contention. Some Sumerian texts implied that the human soul was made up of three parts, and that two thirds of it moved on upon death, but the remaining third- which is either purely mortal or purely divine, it's hard to tell which -survives and becomes imprinted with the memories and emotions of the dead person, and are sustained by offerings. A Gedim risked becoming an Utukku by escaping the Underworld or being summoned by a priest, at which point their nature would be changed by the secondary transition and would become something else.

            To the Kemetic, they would be a strange kind of Akh. The Akh was the reunion of Ba and Ka that went on to the afterlife. The Ba was the personality of the person, while the Ka was their vitality- the Ka was lost during death, and restored by proper funerary rites and sustained by offerings. The Kemetic funerary priests had no way of knowing for sure whether the Se-Akh, a ritual to reunite Ba and Ka, worked properly, but it was implied that you could "die a second time" because of a botched ritual. Perhaps a Geist would be an Akh that either became powerful, or, is corrupt. It is no longer a sustained continuation of the person it was, but because of lax funerary practices or the destruction of its tomb, it was forced to wander. They might say that the Geist is an Akh without Ren- a dead person who has lost their name, and thus the core part of their identity. They tie themselves to the living to take advantage of their Ren, and possibly transition to the afterlife as a whole person, and in return the living person gets power- a second Ba and a second Ka, something that only gods are supposed to possess. This would be a disquieting situation to a devout follower of the Pesedjet, but some might feel pity for the corrupt Akh, even as they fight to keep a hold of their own Ren. Sin-Eater Manifestations are the manipulation of different parts of their soul. There are eleven Manifestations, and I think most of these map to the parts of the soul in Egyptian belief.

            4. Don't quite remember the Dzimba Woye, but there is no way there isn't a Conspiracy. The Ancient Egyptians were thorough in their attempts to separate the living and the dead, although paradoxically, they didn't have a concept of death as such, only a distinct and final transition that is not deviated against. Such a deviation is against Ma'at. The Sumerians, the Canaanites, etc. all believed in death as a final transition, and they didn't believe in a potential paradise like the Egyptians did, but they also didn't separate the ancestors from the living the way the people of Kemet did. Heck, they didn't even really think of a ghost as being the same person, but a different creature with the person's memories. Doesn't appear that they made an important distinction between the two, however, and the ancestors were still a part of regular life. The Kemetic, however, banished the dead to the west bank of the Nile and there was minimal contact with the ancestors beyond specifically going to the tomb and making offerings there. The living go on to their own lives, and leave the dead in their necropoli.

                        It is Setem, yes, but they were less priests of Anpu and more generic funerary priests who wore Anpu masks while embalming the dead. In Hieratic, it would be spelled Stm, because the language doesn't naturally have written vowels.

                        And now, the different Sin-Eater Manifestations and how they relate to the Kemetic anatomy of the soul. Several of these are the more immediately recognizable parts of the soul anatomy, especially since they're the Pillars of the Arisen soul- Ab, Ba, Ka, Ren, Sheut. The others are less well-recognized. The Khat is your natural body, while the Sahu is your spiritual replacement of it. The Khu is distantly related to the Akh, being the immortal part of a person that helped catalyze the Se-Akh and put the Ba and Ka back together in the Sahu. Alternatively, the Khu actually was the Akh, and it was a separate part of the soul that lived in the Sahu and needed the Ba and Ka to properly animate it. Whatever the case, the Khu was the immortal, imperishable, 'Divine Spark'-like part of you. Sekhem is sometimes also said to be a part of the soul, the radiant life force of the body while the Ka was the animating force of the spirit that could separate out into a different entity and could be the source of ghosts.

                        The anatomy of the soul is, understandably, complex and contradictory in Kemetic writing, and doubly so with modern Egyptologists who just have the texts to go on.

                        Boneyard: Ba
                        Caul: Khat
                        Curse: Ren
                        Marionette: Ka
                        Oracle: Khu
                        Pit: Sheut
                        Rage: Ab
                        Shroud: Sahu

                                                            Originally posted by Aiden View Post
                                                            Also, hey, I'm curious. I was reading more of the Mumviri and it mentioned they're from the Land of Punt. That inspired me to look up more about the place, but there was very little to go on. Can you tell me a little about how you envisioned the place?
                                                            yeah, Punt wasn't an easy one- not only there is little to no information about it, it looks like CofD took a different path from the general consensus about its location (from what I could gather, most researchers locate it in the Horn of Africa, but CofD seem to placed it in South Africa and connect it to Mutapa, as it was hinted by Dave). Using that connection, I've made some research and decided to base its concept loosely around the Shona people and their culture. When writing the Mumvuri, I've thought about a sophisticated culture based around harsh divisions of totems and tribes, connection to the spirit world and some sort of "high shamanism", including lineages of all kinds of shapeshifters which are contained to certain tribes and a "Parliament of Beasts" where the leaders of all local Breeds meet together in the shadows of the world. In that culture of spirit animals and werebeasts, the Mumvuri are an anomaly- they belong to a totem which should not exist, yet that totem is enough to give them proper presentation. It is a wild land, a sophisticated land, a place of dark mysteries and shiny treasures for those who dare to pass the guardian beasts and undead witches of Punt.
