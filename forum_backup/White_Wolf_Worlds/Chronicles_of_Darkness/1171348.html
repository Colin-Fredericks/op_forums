    Starting Scene Advice and Your Favorite Openings

    12-28-2017, 06:14 PM
    Hello all!

    I really need your help on two questions, one somewhat specific and the following question a more broad extension of the first. I'm a ST for a local geek bar here in Vegas, and I am starting up a Chronicles game! The players are very interested, and I'm excited as can be allowed. However, there are a few things I really would appreciate help discussing.

    The problem is that we have been playing 5th edition for a while, and I am worried the transition to Chronicles might be a little difficult. I've talked to them about this, and they are very much on board with trying the Chronicles mindset, but I really want to start them off right. So, I need your help in helping me figure out a starting scene for them. Our story is very loosely formed right now, but the PCs will stay mortal for sometime, to help mitigate any "power fantasy" holdovers, and it'll be set in Las Vegas and surrounding areas. I'm trying hard to create a scene that sets up how weird the world is, gives them a reason to get and stay together, but most importantly reinforces how different Chronicles is compared to something like 5th edition.

    And this leads to the more general and fun question! What kind of opening scenarios or scenes do you enjoy for your games? I love starting scenes that indirectly involve the players, as I tend to frame them in way where the greater threat drags the players into the drama unintentionally. I think this builds tension fairly well, as it shows the players are out of their element, and that whatever they are facing may be greater than expected. For example, the players accidentally witness a murder by some winged thing in the park, and that winged thing also sees them before it flies away. Now the players are forced to deal with this murder because who knows what that creature may attempt!

    Quick summation: I'd appreciate some of your help in creating a starting scene for a bar game set in Vegas, and what openings scenes do you like in general for your games?

      I've never STed a game, so I will stay far away from backseat-driving your story.

      As for the second question, my own ST usually starts scenes off simply: She'll describe the environment and then let us PCs look around and ask questions about what we're seeing, etc. It's nice because it helps us situate ourselves and our characters, pick up the tone she's trying to give it, and figure out what our options are. My character once randomly decided to browse a late-night store our ST mentioned that sounded like a throwaway piece of imagery (she was describing the street my character found herself on after a tense scene prior), and it ended up containing two important plot items.

      She sneaks in plot a lot this way. We have learned to fear her Perception roll requests, because we can never tell if a botch means we're gonna step in something gross as soon as we start walking or if a hidden enemy now gets to keep spying on us. Or if a fail means we missed an important clue...sometimes she doesn't even remind us to make a roll. We have to ask for it ourselves o_o If our ST was running that story, there would be a real chance we'd miss the winged being and/or the murdered body entirely

          As 'experienced' CoD ST, I would look over what you really want to highlight in this opening scene. You want to mark how worlds from 5th edition are different? Then focus on that. Make Intro scene, that describe 'story problem' taken from CoD universe 'typical' phenomena - ghosts, spirits, vampires, yeti, etc. You want to mark this scene as start of horror that CoD universe story - in it's roots - is, first and foremost. Show some NPCs and at least one of them getting killed by phenomena story is about. You then end cut scene and start normal 'party building' of Players Characters that are coming together to solve the case of monster killing humans. You only try to describe things vague and not 'showing' proper monster directly, so players will not be sure what kind of monster the phenomena in the end is.

          Great examples of those you have in Supernatural TV Series - each episode starts with this short 'Intro scene' that shows someone is dead, killed by monster - and then it's interrupted to move to showing PCs 'taking case', collecting 'evidences' and 'lore'. finally confronting 'monster'. For me, it's essence of CoD games. Just see first 'Intro scene' of Supernatural - I would just end it on women burning. You can just watch other episodes for more opening ideas.



          Other idea is in CoD Rulebook - you have 'Missing Personas' scenario, page 110. You can also use this to start chronicle.

          Next ideas and advice will need to know WHAT you plan for chronicle in general, now.

              I like Media Res, start with the characters in a scene were they are already involved then explain how they got there afterwards.
              ​To use your winged murderer example, I would start by having it make an abbreviated attack on the characters asking their players to respond, then after it flies off I would tell them that they were just walking in the park when they stumbled into its path and oh yeah, you witnessed it attacking someone else before going after you.
              ​You can even ask the players why their characters happened to be in the park that night if you want, but by starting in the middle of the action you avoid Chesh's problem of the characters totaling missing your hook by one dice roll.
              ​As a side benefit you also avoid the attention hog at the table spending an hour explaining what his character had for dinner before the plot starts.

                      I tend to start with a murder. It sets the tone, gives the characters something to investigate and shakes up the social order of the circles the victim was part of. It may or may not tie in to the overarching plot of the chronicle.

                      Then to emphasise the difference from something like D&D I'd stress the modernity of the setting. The police are investigating as well, checking CCTV and questioning witnesses, and the players' characters may have some similar resources as well.

                      Similarly if there's combat make it feel dangerous - someone getting hit and losing half their Health and getting the resulting penalties will make each hit feel more significant.

                          My take on it to start is to highlight the mundane and the strangeness.

                          CofD isn't so much about supernatural phenomena forming a hidden world as in cWoD. Instead, it is about weird phenomena being a part of the same world as everyone else, a layer of mystery behind all things.

                          Get a mundane situation to unite the characters. They can work at the same place, have a common relative, be traveling to the same place for their own reasons. Then, get some seemingly normal but worrisome things to move them. A murder, a robbery, a stalker. The problem shouldn't sound as supernatural at first, though it can sound as weird. Even a corpse drained of blood can look as the work of a mundane killer, and a technique I like is, in fact, to throw a scene that seems too obviously the work of X splat, but that was actually built by other things through mundane means (I'm fond of an alchemist that used human blood as an ingredient, and got the players thinking for most of the story that it was a vampire, until they started to notice that some things where not adding up for this).

                          Supernatural aspects should be "hidden in plain sight", but start subtil. The first scene can have entirely mundane explanations, and as the characters progress they start to see strange things. Get it in a way that a character that wants to find a mundane explanation to it all can achieve to do this for most of the game. Characters that believe too much in magic can also get it wrong easily. In the end, the main goal is the feeling of unknown.

                          It doesn't have to go away with splat PCs, either. The CofD's world is a versatile place where many things can happen, and very few people get true answers. As you gets deeper in the mysteries, as you get more and more answers, you just keep finding things that defy whatever you think to know about the world.

                              Attach people together, to the mystery and to their own characters. Supernatural comes later, if at all if your running mortals.

                              Here are some prompts to help get the creative juices flowing(for mortal because mortal is a pretty good place to start);
                              Foreword: Make sure your players are comfortable with any topics your bringing to the table, as they might be a sensitive subject.

                              Black out in the Asylum: have the players be doctors/orderly at an Asylum when the power goes out, the electronic doors open and the wackos get out of their cells. Trapped in a dark Asylum in the woods the players --staff in the break room during the night shift-- must either find a way out of the Asylum, call for help or play hero and turn the generator back on and save the facility from going mad. As the night goes on strange things start happening, something is in the woods and not all the doctors have been upright as they first appeared(why is everyone looking for doctor Warren?!). Each player holds a peace of the mystery as seaming normal day time events hold the secrets to this night of horrors.
                              Pretty much a 'video game' start. Players all meet in the break room when the power goes out, things are going mad and they have to survive.The worst thing that can happen at the start is the whole, "I'm the leader!" argument and they key to avoiding that is to keep the pressure high enough that they know they can't just sit around and argue about it. The keys here are
                              1. [*=1]Let the players feel both unsafe, but not on rails. Know that mad people are coming for them, were they are is unsafe and they have to decide as to what to do. Also remind them splitting up never works, probably by having a guy run out the break room and become a Wilhelm scream. THEY WORK HERE so they know were things are and how to get there. Everyone knows the elevator is out of order, the doctors know were they keep the keys to the drug cabinets, and the cleaning staff know all the secret little places("Your the maintenance guy, you don't need to look for the key to the basement, you have it on you"). Let them make their own plan and follow through. [*=1]Thunder and lightning; don't throw baddies all the damn time. Throw in moral choices, puzzles and other things to keep players interested. (Remember just because someone was locked up in an Asylum doesn't mean they are a danger to society.) Do you let the junkie have the medicine cabinet key in exchange for safe passage through D block? Or do you try to fight off the junkie and his crew knowing the mobsters who pretended to be mad to get out of going to prison are coming for you, guns in tow? Maybe option C? [*=1]A bigger mystery: Something is going on in this Asylum. Something each player has gotten a hint at during the day but now walks in all its naked glory during sun down. Figure out what your horror is and work backwards, giving each player hints to what is going on. Here are some fun ones.
                              1. [*=1]The Mad Scientist: An oldie but a goodie. Someone has been doing horrible things to the inmates in the name of their twisted science. Hints during the day could be odd benefactors, heated arguments with the head of staff overheard through locked doors, a strange 7 foot 13 bodyguard who never eats, sleeps, blinks or large amounts of odd things being ordered/going missing from the medical cabinets, etc. A night time result of this could be anything from special mad men, patchwork creatures, reanimated corpses or just the aforementioned body guard hunting you down. [*=1]The Demon: An unholy force is attached to this place. The deeper you go the messier it gets. Like a sickness it spreads and festers, starting as hallucinations, then foresight, then the twisting begins. The longer the night lasts the more twisted things the players run into, and the more signs of infection appear. Daytime hints could be anything from patents predicting the future, knowing things they couldn't possibly know, people acting strange or just patents being taken to Quarantine for no explicable reason. [*=1]The Fairy: There is something in the woods and the owner of the Asylum is on its side. Its here to make a deal and no one sees it coming. Daytime hints are mostly the deals its made, and the odd rituals the owner insists are performed. A patient in solitary appears in the garden inexplicably and stranger still is missing a foot he has no memory of having in the first place. A janitor gets in an argument with a doctor and gets fired, in a week the doctor is dead and a new doctor takes his place, one with a familiar face you just can't quiet place. The black out is the perfect time for the fairy to make some fresh deals and to collect on what is owed. [*=1]The Haunting: EVERYONE WAS DEAD ALL ALONG. The key to getting away with this is to litter clues throughout the thing, without just copping out. The ghosts dress cleanly while everything in the 'real' asylum is filthy and old. The characters don't own modern technology but the ghosts have 'sci fy' stuff, The 'real' asylum generates new rooms, rotting floors and places move around. Bonus points for having the Asylum be rebuilt multiple times and the players walking into it in different time periods. Warning, if preformed wrong, or right peoples still might see this one coming and even if they don't still might be made you used such a cheesy set up.
                              About last night: The players are all collage students sharing a house in suburbia and wake up with something wrong with each of them, and a blank spot were last night should be. Not long after the story begins strange things and the 'wrong thing' gets worse and worse. Its a race against the clock as the players try and save themselves before they become something unrecognizable.
                              Simple horror story set up. The hard part is keeping the players together and moving, better with people who like to role play. Some little hints:
                              1. [*=1]Give them a trail. No, really make sure they have something to do to solve the mystery. A lot of people make the mistake of not planting obvious evidence at the start so players just flop around getting angry. I can't stress this enough, this set up only works if they have someone to chase and something to do. [*=1]The ordinary; I find this set up works well when the supernatural hides behind the ordinary, and now that the players are crossing the threshold they can see the other side of things. The back room of the crappy video store you thought was for the adult entertainment really leads to a cult meeting ground or something. [*=1]The Transformation: Is the hard part. The thing that is wrong with everyone could be anything from a clockwork implant to a need to drink human blood. The goal is to make normal life harder as the change progresses and to start asking questions about the world they live in, themselves and the most common one, why on earth would I want to give up awesome powers? the big reason to do this set up is...
                              1. [*=1]Splat Introduction: Time to transition to the real game line you have been wanting to run! Are they all secretly prommies who have been brainwashed by alchemists running the collage and only now have started to hear their clockwork hearts? Have they gone through the first change but are so in denial they have denied harmony all together and thus have been having blackouts all month? Are they budding mages under the watchful eyes of the seers? Are all their wrong things side effects of breaking a promise with a fairy and now hes come to collect? You deiced!
                              We take this to our graves: The players all did something horrible together and have sworn to take it to their graves. Probably murder lets be real here. The players must work to hide evidence as investigators close in and the supernatural take interest in our plucky heroes.
                              All this set up needs in character creation is two things;
                              1. [*=1]a reason not to skip town. Because everyone moving to the four corners of the earth makes the story become a pain to tell as players are forces to either be AFK 80% if the time or control side characters who pad out the story. A police investigation or living in an isolated place helps. [*=1]The promise to take this to their graves. I would literally put it on a card that characters who break the promise are going to end up like people who get laid during a slasher flick. Yes its meta but we want the players to be the ones fighting to keep the secret and its no fun when someone goes 'whelp, the moment you guys turn around I'm going to the cops lol screw you guys' and everything devolves into PvP or the story ends.
                              Once you got that out of the way time to tally a list of clues to dispose of and the deeper mystery attached to those clues. Before long the players will come to the realization that as they protect themselves, they are hiding a deeper mystery that could do far worse then one accidental murder. Was the guy they killed even human? Were they under their own control? Are the investigators investigating you or something worse?

                              Have fun, be creative and I wrote way too much for this post.
