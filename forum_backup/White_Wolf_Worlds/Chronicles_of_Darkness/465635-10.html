    Originally posted by Whithers View Post
    The classic meta can be imposed - and I prefer that Meta for both opportunity and consistency. The Classic Meta unified the cosmogonic expressions of the entire world.
    I feel pretty confident in saying that neither cWoD, nWoD 1E, or nWoD 2E has had a unified cosmology. Each individual line (cMage, nMage, cDemon, nDemon, etc) has its own themes, many of which touch on different and sometimes contradictory forms of spirituality and parts of the human experience.

    I think it's an error to consider the GMC a sort of overarching canon in the nWoD that has spiritual implications for the whole universe. The nWoD is mechanically and thematically modular, and the GMC is made up of a lot of different story elements and ideas that can add to a game. None of them are mandatory. The book shies away from presenting even a canon version of itself, instead providing multiple possible origins and reasons for existence.

            Originally posted by Satchel View Post

            I think one of the more salient disjunctions is the completely different Influence caps, honestly.


            I wasn't sure whether I had overlooked something when I couldn't find/remember non-Fallen angels in my few brief forays into cDemon's material, so it's good to get confirmation of that.

            The fact that the Unchained draw their starting demonic forms from their angelic forms (which run across the whole demonic/angelic spectrum) and have a looser standard of Virtue and Vice seems like it can be leaned upon in attempting to emulate Fallen's demons; ditto one of the common Integrator views of Hell in combination with some exiles being warped in mind and/or body due to flaws in their summoning.

            Bumping up the God-Machine's degree of secrecy, incorporating the book's suggestion for introducing the Unchained into a running game via broken storage/transport facilities, and leaning on the idea that someone on the old forums brought up that the Machine is something made to do an undesirable job (which itself has resonance in the Idealists' true divinity movement) could go a ways toward evoking Fallen's take on God.

            Any other notable facets of the games' points of interface come to mind?
            The Agendas are very similar to the Factions of the fallen.

            DTD Agendas:
            Integrator - Rejoin the God-Machine on one's own terms.
            Saboteur - Blow up God.
            Inquisitor - Find out what the G-M is up to.
            Tempter - Forget the G-M, build an awesome existence right here.

            DTF Factions
            Faustian - Exploit humankind for one's own benefit. (A bit similar to Tempter)
            Cryptic - Figure out Lucifer and God's secret plans. (Much like Inquisitor)
            Luciferan - Find Satan, rejoin him, defeat God. (Kinda like Saboteur)
            Ravener - Destroy all of the works of God, including humans (No real analog)
            Reconciler - Gain forgiveness from God. (Similar to Integrator)

            Another thing that's similar is the necessity of Pacts. DTF demons need pacts to sustain themselves with Faith. In return, they grant humans access to their powers. DTD demons need pacts and souls in order to hide from the G-M.

                I'd say that the Ravager faction is a better parallel to the Saboteur Agenda than the Luciferans are; the latter don't have a counterpart in Descent because Descent doesn't have a counterpart to Lucifer.

                A major difference between the two games is that the God of the Fallen is, at worst, an absentee landlord, and is very easy to portray sympathetically (making the Reconcilers a very attractive faction); meanwhile, the God-Machine of the Descent is very difficult to portray sympathetically, as it is by default an inhuman tyrant who hunts down and destroys former agents who dare think for themselves and views humans either as cogs in the machine or as utterly unimportant. This makes the Integrators one of the least attractive of the Agendas, and only playable (IMHO) as "rejoin the God-Machine in order to change it from within".

                                                    On Demon: the Fallen chat - and it's been a long, long time so I could be way off base here - I think it's a mistake to categorize Earthbound as the line's main antagonists. The main antagonists in D:tF are demons, and I don't mean quite in the same way that vampires have to deal with other vampires. The protagonists are assumed to be part of the group of Demons that, upon taking a new body, were shocked out of their hellish ways by their host's remnants of humanity, becoming a mix of the demon they are, the angel they were and the human they took over. The vast majority of demons that now have bodies did not experience this moment of clarity and are still the twisted, tormented monsters that dwelled in Hell for so very, very long and they want to spread the suffering..

                                                    My opinion on nWoD stuff is entirely unoriginal: I encourage people to treat the fluff as a toolkit to be used as much or as little as you want for your individual games. I like hammering cosmologies together, which is why for my Demon game the God-Machine's origins very directly involve the Exarchs and the Principle and indirectly reference the gods of the Shadow and the Others from Arcadia, but I also have a cosmology for games that only involve Vampire/Geist/Promethean/Mummy with a heavy emphasis on the Inferno supplement. As much as cosmology wank is fun - and it's so much fun - the only time I think you're obligated to have any mention of the God-Machine around is when you're playing Demon*. Otherwise, it's all up in the air for each campaign.

                                                    * I'm sure there's also probably a way to play Demon without the G-M but at that point you're putting in a lot of work for little gain, IMO, so I have no really good advice.

                                                            • The fact of transformation and consequence, poetically and sometimes literally expressed as the most volatile classical element and other sources of power.
                                                            • The fundamental web of interconnection between all things as manifest in emergent storybook patterns in the rational and irrational presentations of un/reality.
                                                            • The incomprehensibly vast social organism that reflects things of mystical-emotional potency in the world and the strange collection of laws and customs that feed and bind them.
