                Originally posted by Wraith_Magus View Post
                In any event, Mary Shelly was very much a part of the Romanticist, anti-rationalist wave. The subtitle of the original Frankenstein was "The Modern Prometheus" (gee, wonder where the gameline got its title...) as a specific allegory to the nature of how the monster was created as an attempt to steal the powers of God, as a mad trespass. Frankenstein has been used as practically the defining example of the debates over ethics in science specifically because of its overt themes that have basically been the crux of every argument against research that have been laid down before or since.

                Now, you might look at it with your own eyes, Death of the Author and all, and put other themes into it, and if you want to do that, then more power to you, you can rewrite the morals of the story to fit your own worldview, and play Promethian to a different theme than the author intended. In fact, I generally find that to be the best way to really enjoy the game, which is sort of why I prefer a Dustbowl version of Promethian to a "Let's dig up Mary Shelly's corpse for one more dance" theme. Still, the original themes of the work were very much part of that general Romanticist zeitgeist, which is precisely why her works were so popular and famous in their day. I could write a whole freaking OTHER giant post or two on how popular villains are generally reflections of the underlying social malaise of the era that produced them, but that's neither here nor there.
                If we're going Death of the Author here, I'd say you're relying more on it than any critic who awkwardly raises the point that Ms. Shelly wasn't that anti-science.

                Part of the main theme of Frankenstein is that the Monster could have been good, or at least he thinks so. He's certainly brilliant; he taught himself a human language and became quite fluent in it simply by watching a human family. Frankenstein himself is responsible for his Creature's actions, in that he was utterly unwilling to deal with the responsibility of the knowledge he brought. The scientist is a modern Prometheus, but his a pretty piss-poor substitute for the Greek fire-giver. Frankenstein hoards his knowledge, squanders it, even on his deathbed he thinks it's devauled if he chooses to share it with Walton (all he says is that electricity is involved, but not how). Hence, the genie goes back in the bottle, because it was never let out in the first place. Victor shouldn't be playing God because of some strange idea of cosmic order-it's that he in particular is really bad at it, and doesn't actually consider the ramifications of doing so. Hence why I like to say Young Frankenstein is the most faithful adaptation of the book, because its own inference (that a Frankenstein who cared for his Creature ended up in a much better place at the end) is entirely logical given the themes and actions of the book's characters.

                And more importantly, the context. Shelly was a suffragette before their time, and had suffered a stillbirth before that point. Frankenstein creates life to prove he doesn't need a woman to do so, and not only does that prove a poor idea for the child, he also evades his responsibilities as a father, thus turning the book into an indictment of deadbeat dads and a metaphor for stillbirth. Also, her professed belief that any excess of obsession invariably ends tragically...in reference to politics.

                I'm not saying there isn't "men choosing to steal god's work" there, but it's a lot more nuanced than what you seem to think. And of course, there's also the fact that Promethean alters one of the themes completely; the Monster's desire to become human isn't a hopeless quest at all. That flips one of the morals on its head right there.

                As for Roanoke, I hope they bring up the fact it's the most likely explanation is that the survivng colonists moved over to live with the Chowanoke and interbred so much it was impossible to tell any English ancestry among their children. Could raise some interesting themes.

Considering ths is coming from the woman who Vindication of the Rights of Women, there is very little chance that Shelly intended Frankenstein as anything but science positive.
                                    Mary Shelly absolutely wrote Frankenstein first and foremost as a horror story relating to "man's transgressions". You can read right on Wikipedia her own written introduction to the book, which includes the line, "Frightful must it be; for supremely frightful would be the effect of any human endeavour to mock the stupendous mechanism of the Creator of the world." (Not to mention the movies, where they ham it up on this specific point, about how Frankenstein declares he now "knows what it's like to be a god!")

                                    Now, can there have been other motivations, like Nyx is saying about bitterness over her husband's being unappreciated, or a feminist jab? Yes, others, ones that weren't so utterly on-point as the one she specifically listed as her primary inspiration, but that doesn't relegate this to back-bench status. A story can frequently carry with it the social baggage of its day completely without intending to, (like, say, anything with blackface in it...) but that doesn't change the main thrust of a work. But Mary Shelley wasn't being coy about what was supposed to be the horror in this horror story of hers: It's about men playing God, she says it in the introduction in her own words.

                                    Now, you might not LIKE the facts, you may want to use Death of the Author to insert your own take on it all you want, but that doesn't change that "science is meddling in God's domain" not only was its overt, stated theme, or what everyone recalls about it, but also its primary method of delivering horror. (When people make a movie about a man carrying a baby, it stars Arnold Schwarzenegger, and is very generously called a "comedy", rather than intending to horrify people with the premise.)

                                    The horror was that of Galvanism actually succeeding, which people feared it would. The idea of raising the dead at a person's whim horrified people, and to the uninformed, it seemed like it might be possible. (And seriously, it's not like that's gotten old. Again, the only thing that changes is what sort of pseudo-science they blame, because people nowadays know that just sticking a fork in the power socket doesn't bring the dead back to life.)

                                    Now, is Promethian a different game than Mary Shelley's book?

                                    Yes, absolutely... because Mary Shelley's book really isn't all that relevant to the story that is being told. The fact that you have to go to such ridiculous lengths to try to apologize for or reinvent the ONE THING everyone knows about this story shows just how severe the cognitive dissonance becomes. That's sort of the point, here. That's why I don't like setting Promethian in Mary Shelly's time, because it chains Promethian to its original creation, and doesn't let it develop on its own.

                                    Making Aztec Mummy instead of Egypt and supplying rules for how an Aztec mummification can work and be powered would let the game line expand beyond just reliving the same base concept of Egypt. We have a ton of things pulling Vampires out of just what Bram Stoker did. There are different traditions for Promethians, already, detailing the likes of Jewish golems. Promethian's themes have completely outgrown mere fear of lightning. So why does it have to be forced to keep going back to the same Shelley well?

Mary's missing journal entries were the notes of what they did and were taken by Polidori and lost (to make it into the hands of future demiurges).
