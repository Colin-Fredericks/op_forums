    Originally posted by Verlan View Post
    Awakening is as Gnostic as Kult is. The broad outline is there, but the specifics of the cosmology is not. You do not have Sophia messing up the process of manifestation and you do not have Yaldabaoth keeping fragments of the Pleroma in a stupor. Awakening does not adhere to a Gnostic cosmology, and frankly, that is fine. To actually claim that it is Gnostic, however, is incorrect. It would be like claiming that Rain World is Buddhist.
    Adding to Tessie's point, I don't think Blavatsky, Jung, the Neo-Platonists and the like need to qualify as more than a broad outline for Awakening to be Gnostic in the sense that most people who are not speaking in the role of religious scholars mean, which is in the same sense as the Matrix is a Gnostic setting.

    In regards to your latter points, the setting of Chronicles of Darkness as thaumaturges, as displayed in Second Sight. Mages and occultists co-exist in the same world. Thus, the primacy of the Supernal cosmology of Awakening necessarily has repercussions for real-world religions.
    Mages dealing with the Supernal has as much repercussions for real-world religions and occult practices as people with cash interacting with a world where most of the population uses vouchers and the barter system; magic systems that aren't Awakened use of the Arcana leverage established connections in a way that mages use as mnemonic aids. Without the transgressive steps off the beaten path necessary to pursue the Mysteries, practitioners of mortal magics interact with Supernal cosmology from roughly the same perspective as anyone else with one or more feet in the shadows — they're using an existing system directly where mages are building all their spells from scratch in a volatile manner (or developing their own system at a slower pace through Legacy Attainments).

    The hubris of the Awakened is most consistently portrayed as the belief that satisfying their curiosity is an end worth any means (and incurious people do not Awaken in the first place); if a character's beliefs about souls are never tested they can go on thinking what they like about it, but the Sects constitute a society with taboos specifically relating to souls as the setting describes them, and the breadth of possible magical acts are such that if you've been taught by the most knowledgeable group of mages in the setting and want to look into topics relating to the magical beliefs of the culture you were raised in then you have the tools to do so whether those beliefs survive the investigation or not.

    Finally, not all mages would necessarily have nonmagical upbringings. There is no reason why individuals with a magical background could not Awaken.
    We have explicit discussion of the reasons why knowing about the supernatural generally makes a character less likely to Awaken, but yes, this is technically true.

        Originally posted by Tessie View Post

        Gnosticism isn't a singular religion of belief system. It's a collection of several different religious traditions that share specific themes, with wildly varying actual cosmologies. What you have described is one gnostic system.
        Not to mention that we do have the Fall (Sophia introducing imperfection to the world), and the Exarchs (Yaldabaoth/Demiurge) who are working to suppress the Oracles (Pleroma) who in turn guide humanity (through the Watchtowers Awakening people) in the mainline Diamond belief system.
        I know that there are many Gnostic systems. I only used that given one as an example. I used that example to illustrate how the cosmology of Awakening is not Gnostic apart from its epistemology. This is nothing new. In fact, most strains of western ceremonial magic have been Gnostic in regards to epistemology, but not cosmology. Essentially, one has to clarify which meaning of the term Gnostic that one is referring to. The term Gnostic is generally used to refer to religious systems that are predicated upon the importance of religious experience to the attainment of liberation, and Awakening does match that definition of the term. As I have pointed out, under that definition, certain religions could be defined as Gnostic, such as Jainism, even though their cosmology does not match the traditional Gnostic model.

            I think that bringing up Blavatsky and the like is actually quite pertinent. Theosophy and Neo-Platonism are topics that are distinct from Gnosticism per se. There are a great many similarities, of course, particularly in Theosophy, but the environments that they developed in were fundamentally different. Theosophy owes more to the occult lodges of the late 19th century and early 20th century than it does to Gnostic philosophy. Furthermore, it was influenced by Orientalism. Neo-Platonism did have some Gnostic influences given that it was post-Greek, but again, it was its own philosophy. I think the main problem is that books tend to generalize many religious and occult traditions into a single undifferentiated mass, when in reality they each had their own philosophy and identity. I think that some generalization is fine, but the matter becomes more troublesome when the Awakened are depicted as fundamentally more knowledgeable regarding the universe than the cosmologies that the writers based them upon.

            As for the matter of mortal occult practitioners, the problem is that they are depicted as not walking off of the beaten path, as is the case with the Awakened. Religious trailblazers are nothing new. Mages themselves are based upon such individuals. By supposing that mortal occultists do not have access to the Mysteries in the same way that mages do, one is necessarily making a significant cosmological presumption. Furthermore, the idea of thaumaturges utilizing already-manifested systems does not match the history of occult traditions. Magical systems grow and develop, like many things in the world. It feels like the presentation that you are describing renders thaumaturges essentially reliant on Awakened mages for the development of their magical systems.

            Finally, in regards to your last point, I do recall that Sleepwalkers are described as less likely to awaken than Sleepers. However, I do not remember if that is established as something that is known to be true, or something that is theorized by the Awakened.

                Verlan, have you read Signs of Sorcery, the book that talks about how the Awakening, one of the best-understood processes of becoming a supernatural in this setting, works? Have you looked at the explicit varieties of experience that Awakenings entail that include contending with the search for impossible truth and a novel confrontation with the Lie? Do you seriously believe that I or the books are positing that magical traditions got all their ideas from mages just because mages do not strictly require a magical tradition to work miracles?

                Mortal occultists objectively have less access to the supernatural than monsters like mages do. This is demonstrated by the fact that monsters with an innate connection to other worlds with Irises tend to at least have simple means of bypassing the Keys a mortal would otherwise need to determine for each individual gateway, to say nothing of magical senses like the ability to perceive specific frequencies of Twilight or the instinctive draw toward other monsters of their type. Mortal magicians are operating in the same sphere of development as krewes in Geist and sorcerer cults in Mummy, the former of which involves building a mythology for a death-religion whose teachings include magically efficacious ceremonies and the latter of which involves dangerous experimentation or tracking down hidden records of processes that work.

                A mage is a feedback loop of occult strangeness and the examination thereof. Symbolic support can help them bootstrap their workings higher and stretch the limits of their capabilities, but they can command the dead and twist destiny with nothing more than an exercise of will and a little luck where an unAwakened soul would need to gather reagents and seek out locations resonant with their goal. The two may theoretically both tap into the same force (in the sense that both use "magic" or "the divine" to do the things they do, but the former does not invalidate the latter's spiritual beliefs any more than vampires coming up with the Longinian Creed for their own purposes invalidates the Catholic doctrine of transubstantiation. The world exists and progresses with or without the Awakened putting their thumb on the scales.

                        This is a nonsensical statement without defining what you mean by "the mystical experiences of mortals" and "more ontologically correct." In a setting where the supernatural is pervasive and mages in particular know it's nigh-on inescapable, a mystical experience could be a psychological breakthrough, a bout of mental illness, an ordinary eureka moment in the right context, deliberate or inadvertent communion with local or extradimensional supernatural entities, or any number of stranger things, and the only distinction the Awakening has over any of those is that it interfaces with symbolism that taps into the Truth and potentially ends in a character becoming a mage.

                        I am just concerned about mages being portrayed as possessing more correct cosmologies than mortals, particularly when such cosmologies are often a stew of various religious elements mashed together without the context of the original magical systems.
                        A game set in a cosmology where there are several consistent major supernatural forces that do not change with human culture on the timescale of a mortal lifespan and an organized group of wizards has been studying the supernatural as a whole for thousands of years cannot reasonably lend gameline-level weight to the cosmology of every cultural perspective on the world and its magic.

                        This is a setting where "mages have met the Astral manifestations of the gods they worshiped and either become disillusioned with their faith or pinned their hopes on a True version of those deities in the Supernal" has been a thing for some time, to say nothing of Temenos realms making magic that conforms to their conventions easier or Supernal Verges and Emanation Realms having odd physical laws and magical objects within their boundaries; magical traditions can have supernatural truth to them without needing to have a direct line to Real Ultimate Power™.

                        As an aside, I also feel like some parts of Signs of Sorcery are not really understandable.
