    Soul Mechanics and Religious Mages

    03-16-2024, 06:16 PM
    How do you handle the disconnect between the theologies of player characters and the manner in which the game's mechanics treat souls? In particular, the ontology of the soul that the setting presents is difficult to reconcile with Mormon and Jain models of the universe. Have any issues regarding the the topic come up in your games? It seems like the easiest option is to just suppose that what the game treats as a soul is not necessarily a soul, but I am curious to see how others might have approached the problem in their games.

          Originally posted by orathaic View Post
          No, I'm not sure what the issue is.

          The game is not the beliefs of Mormons or Jains, it is a game setting. I don't expect things to work in it the way anyone believes it works in reality.

          Also, what is the contradiction you see? Nobody knows what happens to souls when a person dies.
          I think the main issue is that it would render role-playing such characters difficult. Though 2e tried to move the Obrimos Path away from religious archetypes, they are still associated with the Obrimos Path in the text, and so one has to wonder how exactly they are supposed to role-play such characters.

          As for your question, there a few main problems at hand. One of the major ones is that souls can be destroyed in the setting. According to the doctrine of the Mormons and the Jains, that is impossible, and so in the setting those religions are objectively incorrect. It is also mentioned that Supernal magic cannot create souls, though it is unclear whether that is possible at all. I do not quite recall if the books actually suggested the souls have an origin, which would allow one to play a Mormon or a Jain without being objectively incorrect in the setting. In both of those religions, the soul in uncreated. Another problem is one that is more specific to Jains. The nature of souls being different between beings is something that is not present in Jain doctrine, but it is present in-game, as seen in how various supernatural beings are noted to have different spiritual principles.

          Also, this is not just about those two religions in particular. There are plenty of other religions whose doctrines would not fit with some of the mechanics present in the game. I mainly bring up the question as it can bring up issues at the table. If someone comes in with an idea for a character, only to realize that their character will have to abandon their previous religion two sessions in, for example, it is a practical concern.

              Geist has a historical setting in its 2e corebook where the local dead culture has to deal with the fact that the Islamic faith of that place and time does not believe in ghosts, mostly by either focusing on the practicalities or styling themselves as a different sort of being.

              I should imagine a sufficiently dedicated religious monster, particularly of the sort whose notable factions include "there ought to be a religion exclusively for people who can do True magic because Sleepers get everything wrong," can come up with a reason why the things mages and other monsters tend to describe as a soul does not appear to exhibit the qualities ascribed to the soul in the religious texts available to those still susceptible to the Lie.

                  Mages Awaken to the fact of the Lie and that much of their world is comprised of it, and there's plenty of reason to believe the religions of the world, if ever any of them held truth, very clear don't have all of on this side of the Path. Dedication to religions after the fact often don't have much rooting in the fact that they believe it so much as they can draw power from it. Any reasoning beyond that is a matter for individual mages to deal with.

                  Speaking as an ex-Latter Day Saint, It's not particularly difficult to roleplay. You thought you knew everything, then you learn better, you decide if you can keep lying to yourself or cut what is good and useful from what wasn't, and then move forward a wiser person. Mages already do that regularly-religion's just another thing you you awaken to the reality of.

                              As for your question, there a few main problems at hand. One of the major ones is that souls can be destroyed in the setting. According to the doctrine of the Mormons and the Jains, that is impossible...
                              First, having a mistake like this in doctrine is something a character can easily rationalise.

                              Souls can not usually be destroyed without magic. One Mage might decided that Mages using magic is Godly and believing God is omnipotent that awakening has brought them closer to God, another might decide that destruction of souls is Demonic and by implication that all mages are against God - becoming a Banisher.

                              A third Mage might decide that is something wrong with the description which mages use for souls, and that they aren't what their religion is talking about (after all souls are indestructable) and search for True Souls.

                              A fourth Mage might decide their entire religion is built on the lie and used by the Seers to enforce the Tyranny of the Exarchs on the world - becoming a crusader against The Father.

                              A fifth Mage might decide they have true insight into the nature of their religion and create a sect within to lead people towards the Truth. Summoning Supernal Beings as part of regular rituals to 'prove' the have God on their side...

                              I see this less as a problem and more as an opportunity. But ultimately Mage is a gnositic game of Truths about the world, if you don't like some of those truths you are free to modify them at your table.

                                                      A little late to the party, but it seems pretty clear to me that what the Awakened call a soul is not the same thing these religions call a soul. In the setting, a “soul” is not the sole vehicle of a person’s self. Having your soul removed doesn’t immediately remove your personality and volition, and you can even swap souls with basically no side effects. So, by my reading, “souls” aren’t even unique eschatological phenomena. They’re more an interchangeable medium that merely plugs the person’s actual “self” into their mortal form. Removing it is like cutting out parts of a person’s brain: it severely inhibits the person’s ability to function, but it’s not actually relevant in an eschatological sense.
                                                      The fact that the hypothetical “soul soul” isn’t detectable or manipulatable by Supernal magic might only reinforce the religious view of souls as inviolate and eternal.

                                                      The longer I study science the more I am convinced that it is functionally indistinguishable from what our ancestors would refer to as sorcery. And I would know, being both scientist and sorcerer.

                                                          As others have said before, I think that it is pretty easy to see individual mages using that sort of reasoning in regards to the problem. I think that the larger question then is why the books use the term soul to refer to an animating essence in the first place. In general, I feel like the established cosmology of the setting tends to overshadow the worldviews of individual mages. The core rulebook exemplifies this pretty well. In the beginning, the book clearly differentiates mortal magic from Supernal magic, and yet the book also constantly references mortal religious traditions when it speaks of mages. Also, Awakening does not really do a good job of establishing that what the game's mechanics refer to as a soul might not match philosophical models of the soul. For example, the whole philosophy of the Tremere in 2e is reliant upon the idea that they really do strip themselves of their souls, and are thus able to identify with a monistic world-soul. The Tremere very much interact with the soul presented by the game's mechanics. If anything, the Awakened should be very aware of the fact that what seem like distant, abstract questions are actually empirical in nature.
