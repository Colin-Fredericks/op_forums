    Going with Abyss as Primodrial Chaos / Starting Darkness is in themes with many religions. Just see this paragraph on site dedicated to Norse mythology.

    The Old Norse word gap means the same thing as it does in modern English: a void, an empty space. The meaning of the ginnung element, however, is far less certain. The best guess anyone has come up with so far is Jan de Vries’s suggestion of “magically-charged,”[2] a theory that has gained widespread acceptance.[3] This would certainly be in keeping with the interpretations propounded below.

    Chaos and Cosmos
    This perfect, uninterrupted silence and darkness has close counterparts in other mythologies from around the world. To cite but one example, most of my readers will no doubt be familiar with the famous words of the first chapter of Genesis, which describe the state of the universe prior to the intervention of Elohim in Judeo-Christian mythology: “And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep.” The opposition between the well-ordered, just, and beneficent cosmos on the one hand and the lawless chaos that surrounds it is perhaps one of the most common themes in religion and in human consciousness more generally.[4]

    In the pre-Christian religion of the Norse and other Germanic peoples, this chaos-cosmos split is expressed as an opposition between the innangard, that which is orderly, civilized, and law-abiding, and the utangard, that which is wild and anarchic. Plowed fields are innangard, but beyond the fences that surround them and mark them off reigns the wilderness, the utangard home of the giants. These anti-cosmic forces are constantly trying to drag the Aesir gods, their work, and their ideals back to chaos (and at Ragnarok they succeed, at least for a time). While the wilderness is utangard enough, the “capital” of chaos, as it were, is Ginnungagap; the abyss is the ultimate destination to which the giants want to bring the world.

                            If I understand it correctly, the idea that the universe (in the broadest possible sense) emerged from the Abyss goes something like this: "before the beginning of the universe, the universe didn't exist. The Abyss is defined as that which doesn't exist. Therefore, before the beginning of the universe, the universe was part of the abyss."

                            Where this breaks down, for me, is the very idea that the universe has to have a beginning at all. Pretty much every creation story begin with the premise that, while the world as we know it doesn't exist yet, there are still things that do, and which have a definite reality. It makes a lot of sense to me that, just as the biblical God was already there "in the beginning," when the world was without form and void, the Supernal is eternal even if the physical world may not be.

                            In other words, the Supernal didn't emerge from anything; the Superal is and was and will be, world without end and all that. Indeed, beginnings and endings are merely symbols which exist within the eternal truth that mages call the Supernal Realms.

                            That being said, this in no way precludes the possibility that the Abyss can grant powers that would be impossible to replicate through Supernal magic. I'm fact, it *must* be true that there are abyssal spells and attainments - magics that never were and should never be, and which violate whatever transcendent principles define and govern the Supernal itself.

                                                    But that's just my point: they're not "before the universe"; these myths suggest, essentially, that the universe was around already before the world we currently live in existed. The Bible talks about darkness and waters - these aren't weird, formless, indescribable things - they're not even the absence of things altogether. They're things that are still around in the world today - explicitly so, actually, since God makes night from the darkness that's already there, and makes seas from the water that's already there. If you want to see what this primordial universe was like you don't have to go to the Abyss, all you have to do is dive deep underwater without a light.

                                                    Likewise, the Ginnungagap lay between Niflheim and Muspelheim, two realms that are still around and very much real in the context of Norse mythology, and which are defined by heat and cold - two perfectly ordinary properties differentiated perhaps only in intensity from the heat and cold experienced by people in Midgard.

                                                    There's a deeper issue I take with your reasoning though: If the Supernal is merely the "ordered" part of the Abyss, then the Fallen World, being explicitly a myth-of-the-cave style shadow of the Supernal, must be so too, as must, presumably, basically every other known part of the CoD universe. If that's the case, then the Abyss is the universe (as well as being the Abyss, to the extent that the Abyss can be said to not be part of the universe). Which may be exactly your point.

                                                    But at that point I have to ask... so what? Calling the whole universe the Abyss, including those parts which do not have the properties normally associated with the Abyss, is basically just using a different word to refer to the same thing. It would necessitate that we come up with some other term to refer to what is currently referred to as the Abyss, since it's a qualitatively different part of the universe when compared to the rest of it. And now we're just playing with semantics, and haven't actually learned anything at all about the nature of reality in the CoD universe.
