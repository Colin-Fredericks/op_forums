    Acts of Hubris Questions

    06-04-2023, 07:05 PM
    I'm currently running and playing in a very slow, but entertaining, solo game of Mage. However, as it my first-time playing Mage for any considerable length of time, many questions came up during the last Chapter which the RAW doesn’t seem to explicitly address. Therefore, I would love to get some input on these matters from more experienced players and STs. Specifically, I'm playing a starting character, a Free Council Thyrsus. The Thyrsus infiltrated a compound of a cult hidden in an old bomb shelter. Before entering the compound, he buffed himself with a Life Veiling spell which veiled him from all living beings (akin to Shadow Walk) and a Life Ruling spell which made his unarmed attacks more potent and Lethal (like the Reach effect of Gossamer Touch). Inside the compound, all hell broke loose and the Thrysus found himself needing to incapacitate a few Sleeper cultists:

    1. Should the Thyrsus attack a cultist using his now-Lethal fists, is it considered an Act of Hubris? What about if he filled the cultist's health track with Bashing or Lethal Damage? What if, during the rest of the Scene, he heals the cultist so that the cultist's entire heath track is only filled with Bashing damage? Is it still considered an Act of Hubris even if it's no longer "violence that leaves its victim with long-term injury"?

    2. While keeping the effects of both spells in mind, is there any way the Thrysus could incapacitate a cultist without it counting as an Act of Hubris? In other words, is putting them to sleep, paralysing them or otherwise preventing them from moving counts as "Forcing a sapient being (whether a Sleeper, spirit, or anything else) to act counter to its interests"?

    3. ​If the Veil spell expired in the middle of a room filled with cultists, causing the Thrysus to magically appear in front of them, does he have to make a separate Act of Hubris roll for every Sleeper cultist because they each suffered an individual Breaking Point?​

      Wisdom is all about how precise and in control of the consequences the mage is. So looking at your questions individually:
      1. Inflicting bashing damage would only be a sin for an Enlightened mage. A medium tier mage should probably only risk Hubris if they didn't cause permanent harm or trauma. So they are not bleeding out, haven't lost a limb, didn't die by falling unconscious in an unsafe place or were left in a position where their rivals could kill them, etc. Because killing them could spark vendettas, cause a power vacuum, bring more attention to the mage than they would want, etc.
      2. Incapacitating is not an Act of Hubris. Making them stranded in a Realm Invisible, having them suffer a Breaking Point, binding them to a place or similar is. However, doing it without care may become one, a worker dying because he was put to sleep and fell into the assembly line is an Act of Hubris, because that shows lack of control and deliberation.
      3. I would just roll one Act of Hubris. The player made one Unwise plan, they get one mechanical consequence, that should keep things proportional.
      If they were in the Enlightened tier, for example, they could simply use an AoE Coaxing the Spirits with the command "gun safeties set" or "tie shoelacess to each other" if they are equipped for melee. They could even call the police, since they are all Sleepers, or even cut the power to have a secondary plan if the Veiling failed.

      Hope that proved helpful.

          Originally posted by KaiserAfini View Post
          Inflicting bashing damage would only be a sin for an Enlightened mage.
          I think the apparent common thread of hubris against Enlightened Wisdom is "incidental potential consequences of one's magic", and that once a person is actively intervening in another person's life and wellbeing it becomes Understanding.

          Originally posted by KaiserAfini
          Because killing them could spark vendettas, cause a power vacuum, bring more attention to the mage than they would want, etc.
          I find that a strangely utilitarian idea of the potential consequences of killing people as it would inform Wisdom. Frankly, I'd say a mage's peers should be very disturbed by them if they express a sentiment of "killing is bad because it might cause later inconveniences".

          I tend to think of it in terms of what a mage could perceive in a life through the Supernal. Like the Acanthus who can look at one and see the thorns denoting all the myriad possibilities that life's future could entail. I imagine somebody looking at all of those closing off as the life ends prematurely, and I think it's a mage who's fallen rather far who doesn't grapple with the question of whether they can really comprehend the weight of the potential that has been destroyed.

          That and how a mage should consider the matter of whether or not killing somebody makes the mage themself less inhibited about killing people.

          Originally posted by KaiserAfini
          Incapacitating is not an Act of Hubris.
          I think it's easier for direct incapacitation to not be Hubris than killing is, but still a bit context dependent, based on the exact method and how fully the mage examines the situation beforehand.

          For one thing, with the "have them suffer a Breaking Point" alternative, I think there's a question of where their mind goes when they come to and try to figure out exactly how they came to be incapacitated.

          I would say that the overall description of the scenario provided certain involves some Act of Hubris, because if all Hell broke loose then that means a mage involved themself in a situation not properly within their control where risk of receiving or inflicting violence was in play. I find a scenario like that to be too chaotic to exactly be compatible with Understanding level Wisdom.

          Which isn't a bad thing, mind, because as the book points out, most mages fluctuate around that level. I think people can get a bit hung up on Acts of Hubris when the game is supposed to be about engaging with them, stumbling around them, and occasionally playing a character who endeavours to learn from them.

          After all, there are only three tiers of Wisdom. I think an effective basic rule of thumb really ought to be that if one is playing a scenario where the mage got themselves involved in something messy and dramatic where a lot of the variables in play weren't expected or weren't under their control, it probably entails some Hubris against the middle tier. Being at Enlightened Wisdom is the level at which one is considering things from a distance, planning meticulously, and using magic to set things in motion from afar (or has been doing so up until the point where a narrative draws them into the messy and dramatic shit), and at Fallen you're technically hovering close to the edge of Rapture but for most practical purposes are probably just somebody who has been involved in so much messy shit without stopping to contemplate it that you've become inured to that. Like, you have to do purposefully heinous shit (or at least things that cascaded out of control horribly) to really become Rapt, so it seems like a level characters will likely bottom out at, at which point it's really more of the social consequences of being seen as somebody with so little self-control (as well as the greater difficulties of containing Paradox).

                      Inflicting a temporary Condition or Tilt is generally no issue. Did Fate magic make coffee fall on your eyes and given you the Blinded tilt ? It hurts, but you will be fine. Did a a Force spell disguised as a pressure point attack Stun you ? Eh, you will live. Did your make the thugs fall asleep in their warehouse with a Mind spell ? Better than starting a shootout, they will probably think it was sleeping gas.

                      The interpretation of against their interests is also important. Taking free will from someone is Hubristic, but context is king. But if the mage is in a security room and sees someone that is about to be sniped, using Mind to force them to duck is fine.

                      Aggravated damage is terrifying beyond how close it brings characters to death. Its very difficult to heal, so much so that not even werewolves have a way to deal with it quickly, and they are extremely tough. Any Conditions inflicted by it are of the permanent type, Blind means the character lost an eye, Arm Wrack means their limb was destroyed. Mend will not restore it, Regeneration is a temporary fix. You would need a something like Transform Life to gain axolotl's regeneration and wait for your body to heal it naturally (or some other specialized spellwork to make the body regrow it), all while avoiding Disbelief.

                      I would also count something like an indefinite spell inflicting Chronic Agony as permanent damage as well.

                      Regarding when to roll it, page 87 of the core book does not mention it should be delayed, so its rolled immediately after the action. Because a mage going Megalomaniacal or Rampant will probably add fuel to the fire, making the situation worse or the cleanup more difficult. It makes the immediate consequences of Hubris much more visible and immediately tangible to all allies.

                      Design wise, Wisdom is all about being precise and mindful of the consequences, its trying to push players into a space where they are rewarded to take an approach that is not Big Hammer Theory. When they do fail the Degeneration roll, its setup so the spiral of Hubris and climbing back to a Wiser state lead to interesting story moments.​

                              One thing that I think for determining adjustments to Acts of Hubris is that coming up with rationalisations or edge cases is the wrong way to go about it. The way I see it, if a character is trying to hard to justify why their actions are technically in the right, that's actually leaning a lot more into Hubris, because it's trying to absolve of responsibility.

                              I think the circumstance where Wisdom can be reconciled with doing violence is not so much making the case for it being ethically justified as the character having an objective (preferably not a totally self-interested one) that the violence is a pretty firm necessity to accomplish, that they've got a comprehensive understanding of the situation that allows them to reasonably make that judgement call, and then they pursue that end with directness and conviction. I'd say that the use of violence merely because it's expedient is not Wise, whether or not it entails deadly force (for one thing, I think mages have a lot of the avenues of insight to recognise how death is not the only bad thing that can happen to a person if injury is inflicted upon them, and that even if their body heals in relatively short order their mind is something else).

                              In that sense, it can be possible for the mage who uses violence on a person because the point is for that person to die to have less hubris than the one who did it to sweep the person out of the way of some other end (even if there are other ways in which it's difficult to end a life without that being hubris).
