                        I feel like some potentially problematic associations around the phrase "real name" in this context can be solved if the word "real" is replaced with "birth". I think there's some level of unpleasantness around an implication, no matter how unintentional, that the properly gendered name that a trans person takes is some how less real at some sort of metaphysical level than the incorrectly gendered one they were given at birth. Even if we can handwave it as being about something else entirely by talking about supernal sympathy and the locking in of symbols with names, it still feels kind of wrong in ways that are hard to describe.

                        And I'm not sure I'd have been as bothered by this prior to having watched Sense8; specifically how Nomi's mother's uses her pre-transition name Michael as an attack on her identity. Thinking of a bunch of transphobic mages going "yeah, but we can *prove* that whatever you may want to be calling yourself now, your *real* name is.." is kind of disturbing.

                            I think Eolirin's point is a valid one, but I do think it's worth pointing out that these "real names" or "birth names" are emphatically not a good thing because of the way sympathy works. It's not about the name that you want to be called by, it's the one with really strong association with you, desired or not.

                            I don't know if they kept it this way in 2e, but I remember reading somewhere in 1e that if you go by a certain name (not necessarily a Shadow Name?) long enough, it can develop sympathy with you. Maybe that's influenced by your desire to identify with it or not. I think a description of that name being a shackle tying you to an identity which is not wholly of your own making is sort of in-keeping with it.

                            I feel this dips into questionable territory, but considering what Mages can use your sympathetic name for, I don't think it's completely inappropriate that the symbolic act of violence that is willfully ignoring a trans person's identtiy could be a component of the usage of your name against you.

                            That said, for groups that are still uncomfortable with it, I would just handwave it based on the idea that whatever name other people most strongly associate with you is the "real name" for the purpose of sympathetic casting, so if you've been identifying as Kallista when your assigned name is Keith, Kallista carries more sympathy with who you are as a person.

                            That said, I also think this sort of thing could also step on the toes of the Shadow Name, which is honestly a nice way to claim an identity with your own agency as opposed to the one being used as an essential GPS tag/collar/tool of your destruction for any Mage that knows it.

                                        Just to be clear, my issue isn't so much with how real names work in Mage, because it's a horror setting, and the idea that your birth name is a shackle that can be used against you is perfectly cool. My issue is entirely with the fact that the book, in an out of character fashion, is giving implicit weight to the point of view that that birth name is some how more real and legitimate than the identity of the character through its use of the word 'real'. It's not a real name in any way that'd matter to a person's sense of identity, it's just the label they were given at birth. And I think conflating those concepts is problematic in the context of people who suffer the same sort of conflation around other aspects of their identity.

                                        It makes me uncomfortable, which is fine for the mechanic to do, it's a good way to explore that kind of identity issue and the prejudices around it, but it's less fine for the book's language choice to implicitly support that mentality even as it sets up the issue to be explored. Edit: to be super, super, clear, I do *not* think there is any intention to do that on the part of the writers, whatsoever.

                                        Also I don't think this is necessarily a huge deal or anything either, just something to perhaps consider, but I'm also not transgender, so I can't really speak to how big a deal this would be to someone who is.

                                        Edit: also, to be clear about the metaphysics, given the sorts of source material Mage draws on, the birth name being the thing that gets locked into place has strong thematic weight; names have power, and the idea that things without names are more malleable and unclaimed, and that naming them locks them into more static shapes and grants power over the thing being named is very much in keeping with a host of important inspirations for the line. Using other things or tying it to identity related things is obviously fine, as a choice by the ST, but it does abandon those thematic associations. I just think drawing a cleaner line between 'this is what naming a person who has no name does in the setting's metaphysics' and 'this has anything to do with a person's identity' is maybe worth looking at; this thread provides plenty of examples of people struggling with completely separating the two, when, metaphysically, they have nothing to do with each other, and I think the word choice 'real' is the core of that conflict. It's really just your birth name, the first name that was used to describe you. It's locked, it never changes, it's a name, and names have power. It's not your identity, it's not the core essence of you.

                                            I kind of agree that "birth name" or maybe "child name" is probably a better way to describe it, even if sometimes it's not the very first name you were given. My explanation to players that the reason it lends such potent sympathy isn't because it's tied to your identity, so much as because it is tied to your origin, your childhood. It gives someone power over you because they know the name you were called before you had the power and agency to name yourself. It lets them symbolically usurp the place of someone who has known you since you were a child. Or, alternatively, you voluntarily allow them to retroactively share such a place. I really liked the angst of how the protagonist of the Imperial Mysteries intro fiction was unable to share her legacy because she didn't know her own birth name. I don't remember if that's actually in the rules, but it suggests that the mentor-student relationship must be cemented through trust, symbolized by a ritual "I wasn't always called The Nemean" disclosure.
