    Storyteller Beats - Storytelling My First Mage the Awakening Chronicle

    I am prepping to start my first Mage the Awakening chronicle tonight. Our session tonight will be a "Session Zero" with an introduction to the game, character creation, and chronicle planning.

    I am pretty excited to be playing a CoD game again and a little nervous. Any advice for a storyteller diving into his first Mage the Awakening chronicle?

      Any time someone asks me this, I always say the following:

      0. The Golden Rule: Always have fun. If something isn't fun, don't do it.

      1. Learn to improvise. You'll never find things go perfectly smoothly. Be ready to roll with whatever happens.

      2. Tempt your players with A-Beats. Any time their Obsession or something that fits into their character's larger interests has an opportunity to advance swiftly come up, offer them an A-Beat to act in a (preferably less-than-ethical) manner to further that agenda. For example, breaking into the office of an archaeologist to ransack their records because one of their last findings looks like something from a Ruin.

      Have fun!

      Edit: Gonna throw in a bit more advice, actually.

      3. Have your players figure out why their cabal came together, what their purpose is, and what their rules are. Cabals aren't just groups of buddies who got together Because. They're a mystical compact and secret society a group of Mages formed to use for mutual benefit and/or out of shared ideals. Not only does this help give players something to keep everyone on the same page, but it also gives you hooks to hang stuff on.

      4. Have a built-in dispute resolution system. Players will inevitably have different ideas on how they want to do things. Have a dispute resolution system everyone agrees on; not only OOC but in-game, preferably with some mechanism to ensure that, if decisions consistently go against one person, they can get something out of it. For example, if a person is the only one opposing a vote, say, they can demand some condition on the course of action to be taken.

          Favor speed over accuracy, at least at first. It's fun to really dive into the system and do everything exactly right, but sometimes it can be a drag. Down and dirty spellcasting is perfectly fine when you don't want to spend three minutes working out a spell, especially if there's not a lot of consequences.

          Seconding Axelgear's bit of advice there about getting the players to figure out how they know each other. One thing I do is have everyone go around in the circle with the question "How does your character know the character sitting beside you?" until they have interesting backstory for the group.

              I have a lot of advice, but no time to formulate it all right now. How much time do we have before you get to your session 0?
              Anyway, unpopular piece of advice: in beginning, avoid plots that directly involve other Mages. Why? Because Mage-on-Mage interaction demands MASSIVE system mastery. It's ok when these interactions are friendly or very limited, but antagonistic interactions with other Mages are really really challenging to wrap your head around, especially in the beginning. Plots focused about investigation and "players versus a Mystery" are much, much more beginner GM friendly, and they also leave much more room for experimenting and fucking-up for your players.
              Oh. Hmmm. I've remember that there was a thread about "how to prepare a Mystery" - I made a pretty big post in it about how Setting Mystery can impact your play, and other things. I'll search for it later and link it.

              EDIT

              Originally posted by WHW View Post
              By the way, I also strongly suggest limiting other Mage characters at the start of the journey. Introduce new elements slowly. First, let the character interact with just the normal world, but using their magic; let them explore for a bit how does it impact their normal life. Throw a mugging at them, provide opportunities for unethical uses of their power; it's a good tutorial section (failure won't make artifacts disappear forever in maws of abyss, there is no rival mage waiting for your misstep) and it will help the player fell *different* from other people. Give them potential to go on a power trip.

              Then, introduce something supernatural. Something small, but rewarding. Goetic daemon possessing one of their patients, a strange spacial distortion making people wait forever in the line, maybe something dangerous to "normies" but not really to the Mage. This should happen after the player and character are somewhat comfortable with using their powerset.

              Then, you can introduce bigger and bigger slices of mystery, but don't drop any mages yet. Remember, in 2nd ED, "Magical Society" is much more than Mages - it's thousands and hundreds of misguided Sleeper cultists, Sleepwalker workers, Supernatural-Merit Mortals and so on. Let the player find one of these out, and follow it to the magical society.
              Perfectly, this should also correspond with the character facing a problem that lies outside of their power set, which will make them want to find someone who can aid them in their thing. Meeting another Mage should be special in itself, and not something introduced lightly; using too much Mages tend to distort the game away from investigating Mysteries into playing ball with "Consilium Politics" written on it. And Mage - on - Mage conflict (not necessarily combat, but all of the magical interactions and stuffs and things) will complicate the game very, very much, as Mage-Mage interaction is incredibly complex and unintuitive. Horrors, Spirits, and other things like that are much more manageable "opponents" than having to wrap your head around 10001 magical gambits and stratagems.

              Ideally, your Mage won't be "recruited" into the Order. After some interactions, he should *want* to join them on his own.
              Originally posted by WHW View Post
              For smaller, more Quest-Like Mysteries, here are some thoughts.

              First, you always need a Buy-In. A reason for characters to get interested in tackling the Mystery. Game provides you with a lot of tools for that - Arcane XP, Aspirations, Obsessions are all signs of good goal-oriented design - game is constructed in a way that provides you with both both gameplay and story tools to encourage your characters to chase the Mystery.
              From your design stand point, you need a way to introduce the Mystery and make it a potential option without revealing too much or shoving it down the throat of your players. This is harder than it sounds. In my case, that's why we really like our homerule that Quiescence doesn't flat out erases Sleeper's memories of magic or rationalize them as not-supernatural; in our games, it just conceals the Supernal Magic as anything else that makes sense to the victim - be it magic, UFO, science or religion.

              So in our case, one of possible ways to introduce a potential Mystery and make this very first step already interactable is to plant seeds about it in Sleeper testimonies. Searching through blogs, stories, accounts and rumors is fun and can be procedural while still providing variety.
              In this first phase - let's call it Discovery Phase - you need to also consider a few things. How old is the Mystery? How many people are aware of it? Who else is currently investigating it? What makes people aware of it, what contributes to obfuscating it? Is larger Mage Society aware of it? Does it fall under someone's "territory"?

              After you write these things down, proceed to Investigation Phase. Investigation Phase is basically about poking it with the stick. This is the meat of everything. But, beware!
              This Phase is mainly composed of two things - trying to understand the Mystery and dealing with it's whatever effects. Mystery that just lies and patiently waits for you to solve it is not very exciting and doesn't really use potential the game gives you. So it's important to always look at your Mystery in search for untapped potential.
              Investigating the Mystery usually means observing it's impact on the world, using both mortal and Magical means. To design it good, you need to really understand what your Mystery is about, and create a clear projection of it in your head - do that and then start writing down as many possible symptoms, consequences and signs of the Mystery as possible. More the better, don't feel obliged to use them all. It might be a good idea to use this material to revisit Introduction Phase design.

              After you have a good picture of how the Mystery impacts the world (never be satsified with just one thing - always try to have multiple impacts, otherwise Mystery will feel flat. If it really just has to do One Very Specific Thing, then make sure to have multiple examples of that Very Specific Thing running around - for example, if all it does is reanimating animals, then have a reanimated beloved pet, reanimated animal who got shot after scaring the neighbours, and reanimated animal who is out for blood. If Mystery does one thing, show that this one thing can mean multiple things depending on context), you are set pretty good. Make sure that you prepared yourself to manifest these interactions using game engine somehow; write down Conditions, prepare monster templates, figure out spell-like effects, and so on.
              Again, be very careful of making Mystery just a dormant thing that waits for players to look at it and be understood at their pace. While these are OK from time to time, it's easy to make them the default, and it makes them a little boring.

              What is very important in Investigation Phase is that Mages are...very...good at figuring things out. While Mysteries are hunted exactly because they usually are something that won't be understood simply by casting a Knowing Spell, don't overdo the "trying to understand" phase. Make it gradual. Players should start with basic idea of what the Mystery is, and then expand their understanding by experiencing the Mystery over the adventure.
              If your Mystery isn't very spicy by itself (not every Mystery will have symptoms that result in dramatic and exciting scenes), it's a good idea to spice it up using other means - like rival investigation groups.

              Third, final phase is Dealing With It phase. After your players understand the heart of Mystery, or at least get as close to it as possible (not every Mystery is solvable! Sometimes "solving a Mystery" means "ending it so it won't do more harm, understanding it be damned"), you need to have figured out...what next?
              Basically, it's good to have a goal. As noted above, "solving a mystery" in classical, murder mystery sense isn't really always applicable to Mage and their understanding of Mysteries. So you need to basically define for yourself what you think "solving this Mystery" should, could be. If it's a Mystery about evil wardrobe that each children and then spit out their twisted shadows to lure in more victims, "closing/destroying the Wardrobe" is a satisfying SOLVED! condition.
              ...and of course, this is very fun, you can have multiple possible SOLVED! conditions, and these SOLVED! conditions can be very different and often - contradictory or mutually exclusive.

              Using our Evil Wardrobe example, one way to SOLVE! it is to "destroy it". However, someone else might decide that "harness it's power and engineer a way to make Wardrobe Children loyal to me" is also a viable SOLVED! condition.
              Again, try to think about as many ways to SOLVE! the Mystery as possible. SOLVING! the Mystery always should DO something and CHANGE something. While it's ok to have, from time to time, a Mystery that just ends with "oh, now I get it", it's not really that great to have it be the case all the time. After all, Mage is all about consequences; if it doesn't have any, it ain't SOLVED!.
              Figuring out multiple SOLUTIONS! is also good, because it can give you more ideas about possible motivations and conflicts; again, allied investigation team might suddenly turn into rival team simply because they have a different idea what to do about the Mystery when people finish poking it with magisticks.

              It's also good idea to think about "rewards" that come with SOLUTIONS!, though "consequences" are a better word imho. Still, always try to have something tangible, touchable and potentially manifestable inside rules as a product of SOLVING! the Mystery. While majority of your rewards for finishing a Mystery will be Arcane Beats and Beats from Aspirations/Obsessions related to solving it, don't feel constrained to make it the only possible currency. One SOLUTION! might reward you with more Beats, while other might reward you with a Condition, Merit or other "something", like an ally or a way to convert children into Wardrobe-Controlled Agents of our Cabal. This is a very large space that often remains untapped and wasted. Don't let it go to waste.

              You should also note that many Mysteries don't "puff" out of existence after being SOLVED!. This is why it's important for them to have some kind of consequence. SOLVED! Mystery will probably remain in the city and keep influencing it, though Mage society might now be aware of what it does and use it (or take measures to minimize it's impact). Before you introduce a Mystery, always think about it's possible lingering effects. If you don't want it to be A Thing That Will Stay, you need to build in a safety measure that will allow you to explode the Mystery after it outstays it's welcome.

              Oh, and it's always good idea to constantly be looking at previous phases after you are done with your current prep session. Often, you will find a way to improve the things that you figured out first using information you figured out later.

              It's also useful to think about Milestones in your Mystery. These are basically major points in the course of dealing with Mystery that signal moving forward. Mysteries sometimes can make feel players helpless and stuck, thus identyfing points that signify their progress and emphasizing them to make them feel accomplished might increase their enjoyment and engagement with Mystery.
              Designing around Milestones is useful, but you can also use ":fishing for Milestones" in your notes as a way to have another thing to do with things you already wrote down.
              Milestones don't necessarily need to be something you design you adventure around, but it might still be a good idea to figure them out - they are very useful for pacing and sessions-construction.Achieving a Milestone is a great place to end the session and keep people hyped up. A Mystery that is a constant struggle might be fun for some groups and in general fun thing to do from time to time, but from my experience, a lot of people will get tired by being constantly in the role of someone who is always in the dark. While it's genre-appropriate, be careful about it. People like knowing, people like feeling that their actions are not wasted. Balance sense of progression and mysteriousness.


              *
              I know that a lot of this might be contradictory to, say, some of the "mystery" guidelines you can find about RPG games. In fact, I think it might be contradictory with some CoD-specific ones. It's ok. I find them not fitting my style, and my advice is a fruit of that style. I personally enjoy Mysteries and RPG games the most when there is in fact something behind the curtains, actual world to explore and things that are hidden from my sight, but very hard and very in-place. For people like me, "figure it out as you go" or "leave it in flux" is anathema to what we seek and being reminded of these things often kills our immersion and reminds us that we aren't exploring something "real".
              ...so my advice basically boils down to "figure out all the things".

                      Oh, and ask your players if they are interested in any preexisting Legacies or if they want to create their own. If they aren't sure, or they want to create their own, ponder upon homeruling away the extra difficulty of founding your own Legacy. Legacies offer a considerable power boost (even if it's very upfront), and getting your third Ruling Arcanum is something that many players want to do ASAP (I very rarely see players who focus on their two in-Path Arcana; 3/4 of times, I see people put 3 points into one of their starting Arcanum, then 2 points into something outside of it, and focus on that pair, ignoring the "third wheel" 2nd native Ruling Arcanum), and they are supposed to form "mini orders" and "kind of almost Cabals"; hearing that your player want a specific Legacy 3 months into a game where you *know* that there is no such Legacy in the city can be a pain, both for you and the player.
                      As for why I suggest lifting the penalties on creating your own Legacies...there is *one* Legacy in the corebook. That's it. There is no more support for Legacies in 2nd edition, so you are going to homebrew your own Legacies anyway, so why not let your players tailor fit their Legacies for themselves?
