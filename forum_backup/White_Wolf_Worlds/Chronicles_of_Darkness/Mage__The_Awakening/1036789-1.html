    The examples here contain a huge amount of moral luck, something I'm not a fan of. If what a Wisdom break reflects is a loss of self-control, there shouldn't really be a difference between accidentally beating someone to death with a baseball bat in a blind rage, between beating someone severely but not quite managing to kill them due to circumstances, and even just feeling that same blind rage and being about to act upon it when circumstances intervene. All three represent the Mage experiencing the same things, losing just as much control, but the latter isn't rolled for. Consequentialism doesn't have much place in what is ostensibly a deontological concept (which goes double where the Supernal is involved).

    Likewise, talking about a lack of intent in the aforementioned killing in a blind rage seems odd when we have two Paths whose entire schticks are "You are what you feel/want" and "Your primal instincts are very much a part of you". If you beat someone to death in a blind fury, you wanted to kill (or at least seriously hurt) them in that moment. Just because your rational brain realizes this is a terrible idea and you'd not have done it doesn't mean that it was very much an action triggered by the piece of meat and salt that is you. Humans like to separate out actions we consider irrational as somehow not being who we are, and that just isn't how reality works. The parts of you (and me) that are dumb, panicky, and that we often wish we didn't have are still very much part of us.

    If Wisdom represents consequences, why are only negative consequences rolled for? Why aren't positive ones? Why is saving a life not rolled for when taking one is? Is spontaneous healing more of a sin than planned healing?

    If Wisdom represents some discord between the Mage's idealized self and the reality, that's fine, but that's a very different system; one that would require significant tracking of what the character's idealized self is and how it changes with time (or whether it does at all). If Wisdom is how good you are at being ethical, that, to me, would be a better system. It also allows more Orange-and-Blue morality as the oaths a Mage swears may become more important to them to maintain the health of their soul than basic common decency. Sadly, such a system is clunky buuut... It might work.

                            Posting my thoughts on this on a paragraph-by-paragraph basis:

                            But there is a difference between beating someone out of rage, and feeling that same rage. Beating means that the mage chose to act on that impulse. That doesn't sound like luck to me.

                            The Mastigos Path is more than "You are your desires". The warlock, after Transgressing the boundaries of morals and ethics to Confront her own desires, must then learn to control it. Without that last step, she is a hollow husk whose thoughts "hardly inhabit one's brain". The psychonaut gains Insight/Gnosis by learning just what desires he possesses, and then learning to wield them as sometimes-literal weapons.
                            Likewise, the Thyrsus Path is more than "You are an animal running on instincts." Contrary to its most common stereotype, the Path celebrates the specialness of humanity, and all of its endeavors and civilization, which includes the myriad ethics and laws humans crafted to maintain a working society, which has served well in ensuring the survival of the species.
                            So yes, mages of all Paths realize that acting without control, without prudence, without Wisdom, is a part of themselves that they must nonetheless strive to tame.

                            You are describing actions done in negative and positive intent, not negative consequences and positive consequences. Actions done in negative intent are an Act of Hubris because it means that the mage is both willing to solve problems the quick and dirty way instead of thinking things through, and thinks that she can get away with whatever harm she causes. Actions done in positive intent are usually not an Act of Hubris, because Wisdom is about self-control in the context of "Don't go around harming people with your magic". Did the mage kill that annoying coworker who pissed him off, who happened to be a serial killer when not at work? Positive consequences, but the action was done out of negative intent, so it's an Act of Hubris. Did the mage save that bleeding victim of a traffic accident, who happened to be a pedophile not detected by the police? Negative consequences, but the action was done out of positive intent, so it's not an AoH.

                            I'm still firm on the idea of Wisdom being about self-control in the face of the possiblity of doing harm by your own actions, so I can't quite comment well on Wisdom-as-discord or Wisdom-as-ethics at this point.

                                            If you're going to quote the whole post, it makes it easier to read if you separate responses into smaller sub-quotes. Not huffy or anything; just a suggestion.

                                            Originally posted by 21C Hermit View Post
                                            But there is a difference between beating someone out of rage, and feeling that same rage. Beating means that the mage chose to act on that impulse. That doesn't sound like luck to me.
                                            There is a reason why I said "circumstances intervene". If you experience the same level of rage but can't act on those desires due to circumstances outside yourself, in what way have you kept yourself any more under control?

                                            If a Seer by the name of Blood Moon is holding the severed head of your apprentice and tosses it at your feet, your character might beat Blood Moon to death in defiance of the protocol of the meeting. If the character receives the aforementioned head in a box, mailed to their sanctum, they'd be just as likely to want to beat Blood Moon to death but, if any meeting were to come next, they might've cooled off enough to constrain themselves.

                                            The character, in both cases, experienced just as much fury and anger and rage and loss but in the latter case was powerless to act upon it.

                                            "Moral luck" is a term meaning when two actors are essentially identical but have different outcomes due to circumstances, and wherein only one of them is blamed for it. For example, if two people meet at a bar, get drunk, and drive drunkenly home, but only one of them has the misfortune to encounter a group of children playing in the dark, swerves to avoid them, and hits a bystander, we only consider that one guilty of a crime (or at least the specific crime of manslaughter) despite the other one ostensibly committing the exact same actions that would render them morally and criminally culpable. That is moral luck; where external circumstances appear to affect the morality of your actions despite you having no control over them.

                                            If Wisdom is about self-control, the sorcerer experiences no less of a loss of self-control in one circumstance than the other; in both cases, they are overwhelmed by grief. It is simply that there is only one scenario where they can act upon it. Only one would end up making a Wisdom roll despite an identical loss of self-control. Hence, moral luck.

                                            I was not saying that any Path believes that lack of prudence is a way to live, but more that they challenge the conventional meanings and understandings of it. A Mastigos may believe in a need to control one's desires but they just as frequently see that control only as a way to prevent that desire from inhibiting some greater desire (whatever that may be). In other words, a Mastigos who really loves to get drunk only abstains from descending into alcoholism because they love chasing the Mysteries more, or know the consequences of extensive drinking and are willing to extend some abstinence so that they may enjoy getting drunk more times than otherwise. Likewise, the Thyrsus Path often sees animals as pure in their intents and actions; if they want to kill, they kill; if they want to fuck, they fuck; justifications are often just window dressing for the more honest truths behind it all.

                                            These things mean that the idea that your desires aren't wrong because the idea of right and wrong don't exactly exist; only the consequences and if they're desirable. Prudence, then, is differentiated from Wisdom. If the aforementioned mentor decides that their grief demands a life for a life, that can and possibly should be justification enough for them. You can say that it won't bring their apprentice back or what have you, but if their grief wants it, why shouldn't they feed it? It's what they want. Sure, you can say that they should focus on healing and such, but why? Is there some objectively better way to be? If they believe that vengeance is a necessity for justice, why do your beliefs overrule theirs?

                                                Axelgear the way I understood it, when Wisdom finally clicked for me, was that there actually isn't a good reason to not use magic (most of the time) and that, for the most part, Wisdom breaks pertaining to Acts of Hubris aren't about what the Mage really feels at all. It's about the other symbols in the equation. The lower your Wisdom, the less you consider the reality of other things in the Fallen World, and the more you see them as just Supernal Symbols. What makes a given thing an Act of Hubris is that it impacts other symbols, and those self-defining disagree rather vehemently with being redefined. The one thing I can see in common with every Act of Hubris is that it either harms reality in general, or harms the internal reality of another self-aware being, whether through causing Integrity Breaking Points, killing them, or forcing them to act counter to their normal behaviour and desires.

                                                At Enlightened Wisdom, you are extremely sensitive to the reality of other thinking, feeling beings, and what your power over the underlying Patterns of the Fallen World can do. When you use magic to accomplish something that you could easily do with your own natural aptitude or learned skills, then it's Hubristic because you feel the need to rewrite existence (and potentially risk breaking it) to do something you could easily accomplish yourself. If innocent bystanders are at risk, you also risk Enlightened Wisdom. It explicitly mentions bystanders so if you intentionally target someone for a particular spell that doesn't cause Paradox or a Breaking Point, you're pretty much fine.

                                                At Understanding Wisdom, those things don't bother you anymore. You are a Mage, you understand the Truth, why shouldn't the world bend to your will? Magic flows a little more freely from you, and you don't particularly care about potential collateral damage anymore. Things that impact your self-image, your reality, the reality of others, and the reality of the Fallen World in general, are Hubristic. Damage another's Integrity through Breaking Points? Hubris. Force someone to do things they normally wouldn't or that will actively harm them? Hubris. Inflict long-term damage or committing premeditated murder? Hubris. Don't contain a Paradox and risk the stability of reality? Hubris. Ripping apart your own soul to make soul stones? Hubris.

                                                At Falling Wisdom, you can't even see other sapient beings as people anymore. You completely deny their reality. "They're just Sleepers/Ghosts/Spirits/Goetia" you think to yourself. They don't have any internal Truth that you should care about, they're just a pile of Patterns and symbols for you to warp to your heart's desire. The only things that affect you anymore are gross crimes to reality. Letting a Supernal Being fall to the Abyss, deal with the Abyss' agents, and destroying an Awakened Soul, which, when you think about it, is sort of the same thing as destroying a potential Supernal Being. At this point, the only thing whose reality actually impacts you is the Fallen World you live in, and once you become one of the Mad, even that is another pile of Patterns and symbols to play with.

                                                The way I see it, Wisdom is already a pretty Blue and Orange Morality scale, because to my interpretation it's really about symbology and respecting the integrity and reality as other pre-existing, self-defining symbols. Mages see symbols as Truth, the form is the thing, so if you try and force a symbol that self-defines, that declares its own Truth, to become something else, to take on a reality that is detrimental to its existence, then you are damaging an expression of the Supernal. It's also important to note that nothing in the mechanics suggests that a Mage who succeeds a Breaking Point actually feels guilty. They gain an Arcane Beat, representing greater self-knowledge, and they move on. They also gain an Arcane Beat just for risking Wisdom to begin with, so they actually get two Arcane Beats out of the situation. Same with failure, except failure encourages them to embrace their Supernal nature, it encourages them to use as much magic as they can, and to completely disregard the fact that other sapient beings are actually alive. They start to lose the forest for the trees. Megalomaniacal and Rampant also, notably, change the Mage's Anchors, encouraging an even more dramatic shift in their way of thinking.

                                                TLDR; Acts of Hubris are about denying the reality, and will, of other self-defining symbols and the Fallen World. The lower your Wisdom, the more you see a person as a Pattern, until you can completely disregard the existence of just about everything and everyone, unless you cause, death, maiming, or actively harm reality through the Abyss or destroying pieces of the Supernal World. Acts of Hubris can be enlightening, but failing the roll means you slip into the trap of seeing magic as a solution instead of a tool, or of completely denying the reality of other people. Losing Wisdom means a sudden, unusual shift in the topography of your soul that encourages you to fall down the rabbit hole of seeing individuals as Patterns instead of people.
