                            The first trick to running a crossover game is the same for any other game with more than one player, avoid overlapping roles. It's okay to have, for example, two heavy hitters if the story is combat intensive, even better if one is a brick and the other a back stabbing type, as long as each character gets their chance to shine.

                            ​From personal experience I think everyone is way off base thinking the Mage will dominate every encounter, usually the Mage ends up taking a supporting role. He doesn't use life to boost his strength to superhuman levels and make himself a one man army, instead he uses it on the Werewolf and gleefully watches as it chews its way through hardened steel like it was tissue paper.
                            ​The Mage doesn't boost his presence to become the most persuasive person in the room, he boosts the Deava than watches as the Vampire dominates entire crowds.

                            ​If the players approach every situation as a competition to prove their character build is best than yes, somebody is going to dominate and leave all of the others frustrated (at my table it's usually the guy who always rolls lucky at dice). If instead they approach every challenge as a group, with the character written for that role taking the lead (and a variety of challenges aimed at different character's strengths), most players won't care who took the last shot but will be satisfied with their own contributions.

                                                Originally posted by maryshelly View Post
                                                I was thinking about crossover. Such as a pack of Iron masters hunting Mages as prey, or the encounters Elder Vampires can have with Supernaturals as suggested in Thousand years of night.
                                                Well in this case you just have to plan things out like you would with any antagonist. Look at the character sheets belonging to your players and assess their capabilities, then build challenges accordingly, ideally avoiding a 'rocks fall, everybody dies' scenario unless there's a suitable warning, 'the crevice ahead is shielded from above by dozens of boulders held up by a flimsy-looking pole.' From there you use the group's strengths. For vampires it's manipulation and politicking, an elder vampire didn't get to become one by rushing headlong into combat at every moment, they've let their enemies tie themselves up in knots dealing with other issues and taken them out one by one, seduced them over to the vampire's side, or just outsurvived them. Werewolves are more of an iminent, physical threat, but they don't really jump into the sacred hunt on a whim, otherwise they'd be horrible spirit hunters. They do research and reconnaisance and prepare for their hunts accordingly, and against larger threats are more likely to work on harrying them down, taking out allies and contacts and crippling infrastructure until they take down the target. From a mage point of view, on both sides, protecting oneself from these things is easier theorized than done, as everyone has family or friends and relies on other members of the cabal, or outsiders, to make up for their shortcomings. Sure, a cabal may be a group, but the mentor who advises them in times of need is alone. Their sanctum may be good for ritual magic, but what happens when the enemy already has someone on the inside?

                                                If you want specific advice for how to develop a mage antagonist for a specific game line, I suggest you make a thread in the appropriate game's forum. If you want advice for making antagonists OF a certain game line for a mage game, you should probably make a new thread devoted to the topic.

                                                But, I was also thinking about general power level, and how Mages affect the world relative to other Supernaturals, and how they compare to the human race ( the baseline humans in the core chronicles of darkness book)
                                                Flatscans really got the shaft in second edition. Everyone has gone up in power, and it looks like each book is increasing the distance between them and regular humanity. They have gotten a lot of more powerful offshoots, however, like wolf-blooded, the micro-templates from Hurt Locker and isolated cases of strange powers. In my experience, running my own game and reading others, at a certain point, humans in mage stories are going to be relegated to background. They're there, players might investigate one but they're under no obligation to actually deal with them, and PCs only really have to be aware when it becomes a danger that might cause paradox. Of course, this can be a weakness, as there's many enemies and dangers that can hide within humanity. And obviously as storyteller you can shake it up as much as you want, by making mysteries revolving around humans, eventually it's going to be easy enough to get around.

                                                In turn, however, you start dealing with other issues. Goetia, spirits, Abyssal whatevers and Supernal intruders, the many varied interruptions with the Fallen World, and of course other mages. One of the big themes of the game is the mage versus herself, so you'll always be able to play with that.

                                                Mage, all the supernatural games really, are a significant detour from usual level 1-13 fare. Once you start a game with what you think is a good antagonist for 'beginning characters' only to see a pack of werewolf PCs converging on them and realizing your 'boss' is going to be dead in one turn you can either balk or start planning appropriately. Take advantage of PC weaknesses, read their aspirations and backgrounds, see what the players want to happen and make it possible, though not without a hitch. Maybe they want to fly off to the astral plane and build a castle, in which case humanity, or at least humans, are out of the picture entirely.

                                                            Mages, Mumies and Demons are more powerful than other gamelines. That has been established and is intentional design. So what should you do about the rules in a crossover: nothing.
                                                            That a Mage of the same "level" is more powerful than a Vampire doesn't make the game less playable. If you have a PC-group only from one splat it is fairly easy. You adjust the NPC to your needs. A starting Mage might be a formidable antagonist for a group of Vampires, but of course you don't throw an Archmage on a group of freshly changed Werewolfs.

                                                            If you have a group of different splats it get's a bit more tricky, but there is actually a game mechanic dealing with powerlevels: experience. Either just give the non-Mage splats some additional experience or take some away from the Mage (e.g. take 2 Arcana dots away at creation and limit max Arcana dots to 2). You might have to tweek a little there, but if you want all your players to feel equally powerful that is by far the easiest solution. And the major advantage is that it just works with all the rules, without changing anything that might turn out to totally break the system.
