            Making the analogy that like an addict low Wisdom Mages are more likely to not give a shit about the consequences of their magic is fairly apt.

            Mages are addicts, they’re addicted to Mystery and their Obsessions in particular. It’s pretty much one of the core themes of 2e. Damage done to Wisdom as a pursuit of those addictions indicates how much a Mage tries to mitigate the destruction they do in service to those addictions.

            High Wisdom Mages are just as addicted, but Wisdom is a sliding scale, there are high functioning addicts and high Wisdom Mages would fit in that category as or until they gradually slide down the slippery slope and want only to satisfy it.

            Wisdom measures that level of how much they let the addiction control them, vs. the other way around. Most Mages slide to a certain point beyond which they won’t typically fall, but as an addict there is always the risk they might just take that one further step down.

            Mages can fall all the way to being Mad by letting their addiction rule them, and thus break free of the constraints of Wisdom. Mages can also become addicted to Wisdom itself as well which leads to a different kind of NPC status as they Ascend at or above Wisdom 10 and become/add to the definition of what Wisdom is in the first place. Which can lead to all kinds of fucked-up-ness for the Mages left behind.

            Basically, there’s probably an Ascended Mage whose to blame for why Wisdom works like it does and you’re probably hosed because of them. There are no real winners in the Wisdom game.

                    Because the things that you have to do to lose Wisdom at Falling are on the level of extreme disregard for things beyond your self and your Obsessions in the first place. If you want to argue that evil is inherently foolish or vice versa, you're welcome to it, but pragmatism alone is generally enough to set the average mage to the task of not drawing attention from powerful and/or undying beings or scourging their loved ones and cherished holdings with the brush of magic. They're people, at first, and a will to power does not get to the point of petty monomania without losing a lot of what makes a person between Point A and Point B.

                    Also, I entirely disagree about the Mad. Cause and consequence are like the chicken and the egg. Their Obsessions consumed them because they were foolish and their Obsessions drive them to commit ever greater Acts of Hubris.
                    If you're going to point to the book's game-mechanical definition of what makes a character Mad, you'd be well-served to not put this argument immediately after a statement that ignores how Obsessions interact with the Wisdom system. Any Obsession makes Acts of Hubris done in its service more likely to break bad and the things that set Obsessions are not character-facing save in the broadest possible sense. You get Arcane Experience for pursuing your Obsessions and you get Arcane Experience for testing your Wisdom, and the game uses those facts to represent these as things mages will move towards in the course of a normal game.

                    Also, the game disagrees: at 0 Wisdom you become one of the Mad, full stop. No other requirements.
                    You don't become one of the Mad because your Wisdom hits zero because your Wisdom doesn't hit zero through the casual activities of being a mage; the stuff that damages Falling Wisdom is egregious, and you don't get to that level without putting a lot of effort into your Vice and Obsessions and very little into shoring up your grip on your mystical faculties, which doesn't happen without you already not paying much mind to the consequences of your actions.
