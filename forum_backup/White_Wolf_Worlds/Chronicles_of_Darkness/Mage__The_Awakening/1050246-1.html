                    Yeah, an actor, playwright, singer, etc., who uses their medium to convey an active philosophy in an attempt to uplift humanity and guide them to something better would be a Hiereos. Bardoi are people whose sole contribution is in making something pretty. A good actor may indeed be someone of real skill and ability, but if all they're doing is telling someone else's story, the Ladder doesn't consider them that valuable; they're not really masters of their own fate nor guides of any sort to others.

                    It doesn't matter, in other words, if a singer can give people goosebumps with their rendition of Handel's Glory to God (not really a solo piece, I know, but not the point); it's Handel who captured that beauty and ideal in the first place and put it to paper. Handel would be a Technos, the performers merely Bardoi.

                    It's not the medium that matters, in other words, but what the person brings to others.

                                from Plato's Republic in wikipedia-

                                Concluding a theme brought up most explicitly in the Analogies of the Sun and Divided Line in Book VI, Socrates finally rejects any form of imitative art and concludes that such artists have no place in the just city. He continues on to argue for the immortality of the psyche and even espouses a theory of reincarnation. He finishes by detailing the rewards of being just, both in this life and the next. Artists create things but they are only different copies of the idea of the original. "And whenever any one informs us that he has found a man who knows all the arts, and all things else that anybody knows, and every single thing with a higher degree of accuracy than any other man — whoever tells us this, I think that we can only imagine to be a simple creature who is likely to have been deceived by some wizard or actor whom he met, and whom he thought all-knowing, because he himself was unable to analyze the nature of knowledge and ignorance and imitation."



                                as far as I'm seeing it, the Ladder of Things pretty much corresponds to the ideals of Platonic philosophy- my sister has learned about it in her degree, so she may know more about it, but I do remember that according to Plato, artists are found near the bottom of the world in spiritual means, as they never truly make, only imitate the world around it, and by doing that he steps away from the Idea.

                                                "Platonicaly" speaking, it doesn't matter. They are still making art- and art only mimics, not creates. It is about discussing what "art" truly means, and in the eyes of classical Platonic view (as far as I understand it), the act of art is a lesser one. Art never truly make, only copy, and by acting as if it creates when it doesn't is what places artists so low in the Platonic "social order". "Inspiring people to see the truth through art" is not, as far as I'm aware, a part of the Platonic philosophy- as inspiring the masses is the role of philosophers. The world view of the Silver Ladder in that context is strongly based around Plato's paradigm, including considering soldiers and their like as more important to society than artists.

                                                Now, I'm not saying that artists inspiring people to see beyond the Lie is not a good concept- only that it is not a part of the Platonic philosophy (as far as I'm aware- again, I study chemistry, not philosophy), and if the Silver Ladder were to base itself strongly around Platonic ideals, than it would not accept artists as having an high place in the social order. It is a matter of a world view- and I imagine that even in the Silver Ladder itself there are discussions and disagreements about how truly is that social order is valid.
