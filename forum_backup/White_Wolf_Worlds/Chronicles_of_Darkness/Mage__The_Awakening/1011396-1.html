    Honestly, I feel that Fate magic would gain a lot if it had a similar concept of Kharmic Debt, where when you manipulate luck using your powers, luck has to *come from somewhere*; every lucky break you arrange for yourself is luck stolen from someone else. Current Fate is just...all upside, no downsides, free meals, just lucky breaks, no consequences unless you homebrew some. It's more wish fulfillment than personal horror/dark urban fantasy.
    So yeah, I would be totally for some kind of energy build up that accompanies heavy Fate magic (kind of like fucking around with Forces should unshape local weather patterns, Mind will traumatize people, and so on), with Arcadian Tornado being a neat example.

            Your powers having a terrible price isn't really one of Mage's themes, though. The idea that having control over Fate (and using said control) having innate negative consequences is something the setting seems to deliberately not have (or at least doesn't state up-front). The bad stuff that comes from monkeying with Fate and the horrible chains of unintended side-effects aren't because the gods decided to fuck you; they are your fault, absolutely and decidedly. If it's just an automatic thing that happens no matter what, it removes the whole idea of trying to balance Wisdom with action and instead makes it a question of total abstinence for any sane person or reckless abandon for everyone else.

            Mage gives you a really shiny, pretty rope and goodness, look at that, you've made it into a noose.

                    Originally posted by wyrdhamster View Post
                    Which is why Life is Strange works only on seemingly notice when comparing to Mage
                    Well, yeah, it isn't Mage. Any time you draw parallels to another piece of media, there will be parts that work and parts that don't.

                    Originally posted by wyrdhamster View Post
                    - Hurricane is foretold by yourself so it's not really your own consequences, it was premeditate orchestrated this way.
                    Max saw the storm in a vision, but she didn't know that using her time travel abilities would be what caused it until near the end of the game. Really, the thing that makes it un-Magelike isn't the fact that she had a vision of it (future vision is a pretty standard Time power), it's that the storm seems to be more of a cosmic karma thing, like Chloe was "supposed" to die, and saving her created this kind of karmic backlash of fate that would kill thousands more. But, as WHW observed, that's not really how Fate works in Awakening. There is a way to interpret it that fits better into Mage, but it's still not perfect, and honestly I doubt you'll like it anyway as you seem to have strongly disliked the ending of the game.

                    Think about it this way. When Max awakened, she saw the watchtower. (the lighthouse) As an Acanthus, her Astral Journey took her through time, showing her a glimpse of a possible future, though at the time she couldn't quite make sense of it. The first time she used her magic to travel back in time and save Chloe, she unknowingly Reached way beyond her grasp and released the Paradox, not knowing any better. The released Paradox created an abyssal incursion in the form of the storm, though it took time to get to Arcadia bay. Other stuff happens, Max saves Chloe some more, figures out how to contain the Paradox from her spells, (hence the nosebleeds and such), but eventually, the Abyssal entity in the form of the storm reaches Arcadia bay. Luckily (or perhaps unluckily) for Max, she has time travel powers. She can go back in time and stop herself from casting that first over-Reached spell, so the Paradox never gets released, and the Abyssal storm is never created. But, doing so will mean Chloe will die. That is the kind of decision Mages have the responsibility to make.

                    Obviously, yes, the ending is a little contrived. In a more open-ended format like a tabletop RPG there would have been nothing stopping Max from back and warninf the people of the impending storm rather than stopping the storm from happening. But the format is limited, and the options presented create a significant moral dilemma. But, if we examine the other options realistically, are they really any better? How many people are going to believe Max about the storm? Are the lives of the people who don't want to take some teenager's word that they need to evacuate worth less than the life of one girl, just because she's personally important to the player character and they're not?

                    The salt is real. Sorry, but I don't see this implication from interpreting Life is Strange as a Mage story at all.

                            Life is Strange - IMO, it was about a 14-year-old moving through the 5 stages of grief after seeing someone she may have not even known killed in the bathroom but in any event, let me run two scenarios by you for story seeds:

                            1) What if Max never left the bathroom? The entire game is her having a psychotic break after watching a total stranger shot and killed. She makes the entire story up as an escapism to deal with the harsh existential crisis that just slapped her in the face.

                            If this is the take away from the game then it becomes a picture of an innocent dealing with denial and you could spin it into an innocence game as a 14-year-old is trying to cope with watching a girl get merc'd in the bathroom by diving deeper and deeper into her onerios and denying reality. If she fails to accept that Chloe is dead, she becomes a banisher or never awakens though if she accepts it she becomes either Mastigos or Acanthus.

                            2) Another inception style mind twist that's more Moros, what if Max is actually Rachael Ambers? She's been tossed into the dirt after having a bullet to the portion of brain that handles with cognitive self-awareness and the game we play is her Ego (Max) and her Id (Chloe) trying to solve the death of the personae (Rachael). The maelstrom then signals the brain death, while Max's powers are all part of the Rachael searching her own memories without the capacity to identify her Self. Chloe's death is the death of natural function which is why she nearly dies disaster after disaster an ounce of self-preservation the entire game.

                            With this logic you could say that it’s the awakening of either Moros or Masitgos. Another angle, you could say that Rachael is an incredibly powerful, gifted, or fated Mage and this event is a threshold seeking with her untimely demise being the end of a mystery play.

                                    Char, if you feel that this should go to different thread, could you extract this post, post I quote, and the post Axelgear was responding to and create something like "Price of Magic"? I'm talking about it here, because I feel Wyrds objection to Life is Strange is very relevant here, but I can see why it could fit it's own thread more.

                                    Originally posted by Axelgear View Post
                                    Your powers having a terrible price isn't really one of Mage's themes, though. The idea that having control over Fate (and using said control) having innate negative consequences is something the setting seems to deliberately not have (or at least doesn't state up-front). The bad stuff that comes from monkeying with Fate and the horrible chains of unintended side-effects aren't because the gods decided to fuck you; they are your fault, absolutely and decidedly. If it's just an automatic thing that happens no matter what, it removes the whole idea of trying to balance Wisdom with action and instead makes it a question of total abstinence for any sane person or reckless abandon for everyone else.

                                    Mage gives you a really shiny, pretty rope and goodness, look at that, you've made it into a noose.
                                    Yeah, but look at other Arcana.
                                    When you decide "I want to get some zombies going" in the morning, corpses don't pop up into existence from nowhere. You need to go dig some graves, kill some animals, buy them from shady sources, and so on; you are interacting with the world in questionable ways that might have consequences, intrude into private space of other people (like, who gave you right to animate dead bodies without consent of the previous occupant or their family?) and so on. Your zombies don't pop up conveniently into existence from nowhere; it's the same reason why people in DND 5 have problems with Necromancer Wizard archetype being core, as it's main function and gimmick is creating undead, which is problematic as *tons of characters find that practice to be wrong and evil*.
                                    Is it evil in Mage? No, but it can lead you to do "evil" things and trample over others in order to fulfill your selfish desire.
                                    Similar case with second major "point of power" of Death; Ghost manipulation. Ghosts allow you to do stuff, a lot of stuff, but you are treading on slippery ground here; do Ghosts deserve their own rights and right to be undisturbed? How do you (aside of mechanical Rank) differentate if this is a Rank 1/0 Ghost which is totally fine to rip apart for Mana whenever you feel thirsty, or if is it self aware enough that doing so would be monstrous cruelty? What gives you right to basically enslave and mind rape Ghosts to do your bidding?
                                    And third, well, Souls. Try poking at someone's Soul using Death and not be called a Left Handed prick.

                                    Mind is similar, as pretty much anything that isn't targeting you is controversial and potentially "monstrous from the core"; your Mind magic will probably leave behind broken people, people who got violated by your magic in worst ways possible even if you had great intentions. What is worse? Mind Controlling someone into telling you everything, or interrogating them and punching them few times? You can have a discussion here, a dilemma, and again, game provides you with tools to "hang yourself" without explicit "GM is targeting me help there is nothing like this in the books".

                                    Spirit is probably the best example of "giving you a tool", as you have a whole living, breathing ecosystem that *will* react to your manipulations, and your actions will have consequences. You have a whole subsystem to track these consequences and put them in motion; it\s still very abstract, but if you change local resonance from "Murderlicious" to "Vomit Inducing Sweet", you can only blame yourself for Murder Spirits going elsewhere to feed. It's logical and introduced into the game. For other Arcana, like Forces, you can extrapolate using "real world rules"; if you are fucking with electricity, it's easy to figure out you could cause unnecessary damage in local equipment. Playing with weather will unshape the weather patterns, and so on.

                                    Fate and Time are abstract enough that there is nothing really to extrapolate from, and they also don't have anything similar to "Spirit World Economy" to help you check how you are hanging yourself. System doesn't help you with telling a story about how you are cutting a good luck here and taping it with a Fatetape to that person here; it helps you tell stories about willing Event X into reality, without asking "how do you do that?", "who suffers because of that?" and "what are going to be consequences?"
                                    There is never a reason to ask yourself if it's right to use your Fate/Time magic, because unlike other Arcana, there is no one hurt by your actions. All frills, no bitter pills. You want that guard to open the doors? Mind will literally violate him to do so; Spirit will violate a spirit to violate him to do it; Fate will just make it happen, because why not.
                                    For a path that talks all about consequences and interconnections, your magic is consequence-less and not connected to anything. It also lacks the tool to introduce these to the stories without it feeling like "GM targeting" and "GM punishment".
                                    And then you have a situation like this thread, where "consequences of your Magic" are seen as unfair punishment when introduced into a story. While you can argue that "a good GM will just introduce it and their players will just be happy with a good story", a lot of players will feel like it's undue punishment and gm dickery, because *there is nothing in the mechanics of the game that told them about their spells having those consequences."
                                    If I'm going to ever have patience to try fixing Fate, I would do something similar to Sympathy System, where you are targeting individual threads of Destiny and Fate, and if you want to make someone who is Lucky Unlucky, you need to either find an Unlucky person and steal it (like you steal Sympathy) or, if you are good enough (read, Fate 4), directly transform Luck into Unluck. And so on.

                                        Fate gets you the desired result through whatever channels allow that result to come about fairly rapidly — the path of least resistance is not the path of fewest complications, which is part of why the Acanthus tap into archetypes that trend between flighty take-the-problems-as-they-come sorts and obsessive micromanagement fiends.

If your players think "I'm going to take a gamble and have my desired outcome accomplished through whatever means are easiest with this spell" having a ripple effect is unfair, pretty much nothing is going to convince them otherwise in a framework where magic exists in a populated setting full of moving parts that don't exist purely to facilitate the aims of their spellcasting.

                                                        The problem is that Life is Strange is written from such a non-Acanthusian attitude that's hard to parallel it or comment on it through those lens on this particular point.

                                                        Now, being perfectly clear, I think the ending is bullshit, a sore strike against an otherwise beautiful game. But If I have to interpret the game through the lens of Awakening, it's not an Acanthus story, but a Moros story. It's a game about transformation and transition (only by virtue of that fucking ending, but still those things none the less) where in Max's desire for permanence is fundamentally shown to be a Lie-that life is fleeting and that eternity is a lie. Throughout the story, Max is trying to just sort of keep things together and to return her world to the way it was as a kid, and even improve upon it with her choices. Now, her choices do change things-this is the reason the ending pisses me off, because it strips away all of that-but if you pay attention to the major beats, even before then, pretty much there's a limit based on the details, and the ending wipes away all illusion of meaning in the grand scheme of things. In the end, regardless of how you have to go, Max's story is about how you have to let go of something, because the world just keeps changing, and to cling one way or another just means the loss of everything-and really, the world will move on without the small town of Arcadia Bay, without Chloe-without Max.

                                                        So, yeah. LiS is a terrible Acanthus story because of it's end, but it's a great Moros-with-Time story. The biggest problem is that up to that fucking god damned ending, it played out like an Acanthus story.

                                                        But then again, Life is Strange is not an Awakening story.
