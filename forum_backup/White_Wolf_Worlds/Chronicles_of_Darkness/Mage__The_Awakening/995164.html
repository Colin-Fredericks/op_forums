      Resist the urge to make them cryptic for mystery's sake. No mentor worth their salt deliberately hides information from their students without a very good reason. If you feel like having a mentor know something would actively harm the game, then don't have them know it.

      Related to that, mentors are not omniscient by any means. They don't know what their students don't tell them and are not going to just blurt out every bit of relevant information at the drop of a hat.

      Mentors aren't also doing what they do out of charity (usually) and, even then, they have lives of their own; needs of their own. Consider what a mentor is getting out of their relationship and how much time and effort that would likely make them invest in their charges.

      So, for example, say you need information on werewolves. If your mentor doesn't have terribly much knowledge about werewolves, they might give a basic schpiel ("They're vulnerable to silver, aren't they?") and offer you access to their library, allowing an extended roll to find information. If your mentor is something more of a specialist who has had contact with them before, they might give their own stories, riddled with personal biases and anecdotes, and then offer the same if they can't answer the apprentice's questions. They might also be willing to offer assistance.

      For a menial favour, as above, the mentor might not ask for anything up front but an expectation of repayment is there. That is, the apprentice will do something for their master. This might be delivering a package or bringing back a piece of werewolf fur if they go hunting one. Generally speaking, whatever this favour is, it should be something the character can do on screen and which adds colour and possibly plot hooks to the adventure. As a second example, perhaps your mentor isn't particularly canny about werewolves but knows someone who is - an old Sleepwalker who works for the Mysterium - that they owe a favour to; something about mending their old books. If the apprentice goes and does the favour, perhaps they'd be happy to lend information?

      Then you can introduce the Sleepwalker librarian, either as a fun recurring character or to have them already be dead when the characters arrive; a victim of werewolves who didn't want this interloper poking around and sharing their secrets with others.

                          I'm going to have to disagree with AinSoph here.

                          The best Mentor character I've ever seen or had the pleasure of having as a PC was actually the antagonists of the story. A Mentor is not an Ally or a True Friend (Unless you get those merits for the same person.) They always have a clear reason for why they're mentoring the PC, and while that relationship doesn't have to be antagonistic it also doesn't necessarily need to be one of friendship or even respect (In fact, the more complicated the relationship the better I say.) The only thing you need to do for a Mentor NPC is to have them actually impart knowledge and wisdom onto the PCs in some way. As long as you do that, you'll be golden.

                          Case in point: That Mentor character I mentioned earlier did certainly teach my character a lot about the Mystery plaguing the setting, he left behind a trail of clues that lead to understanding the threat (An Inferno Demon), how to banish it (Left behind a complex ritual), and how to track him down (True Name.) What he kindly left out was that he was the one who summoned the Demon in the first place, and that by banishing it my character was essentially nullifying his soul contract with said Demon. Best Mentor I've ever seen.

                                      LokiRavenSpeak,

                                      I don't mind you quoting my posts, so long as you use the full intended quote.

                                      For instance, my point with that last post was that all a Mentor truly needs to be is useful to the PCs, and so long as they're that then it really doesn't matter what relationship they have with them. Making every single Mentor-Apprentice relationship one of cordial smiles removes a valuable tool from the ST's arsenal. To illustrate this point, let's use the example you yourself gave: Dr. House, Supernal Mage.

                                      Let say you made a Dr. House Mentor for one of the PCs, or better yet a Mentor for the entire Cabal. Here are just three things you could do to make this NPC not only an enjoyable character for the PCs to interact with, but also memorable and important to the story:

                                      1) The MentorGuffin: All Mages are addicted to Mysteries, but all humans tend to specialize towards different interests. In this Chronicle Dr. House just so happens to be the Consilia's expert on Irisis. So when a new Mystery crops up centered around a mystical door that leads to another world, the PC's Mentor suddenly becomes the friend that all of the Orders desperately want. How does that effect the Mentor, how does it effect the PCs? What could the PCs do to garner favor with either their Mentor or Order?

                                      2) Tangible Results: This is a double edged sword, but who wouldn't want a sword with two edges? Have the Mentor's lessons be focal points for some sessions. How does he train them? What is his relationship with each Apprentice? And, when they call upon his help for their own personal needs how does he respond? How does he want to be payed back? What would happen if the PC's went over his head? How would the good Dr. respond?

                                      3) Only Human: Finally, don't forget that the Wise are still profoundly human. This even includes Dr. House, Supernal Mage. What are his hopes, fears, goals? What has he given up to gain the knowledge he has now? How has that knowledge effected him? Who is an important part of his past? Are they still a part of the Consilia? Just why is he such a dick? And, what is he willing to show the PCs and what must they discover on their own?

                                              When my players spend their resources on a merit like Ally or Mentor I prefer to let them define the nature of the relationship, so long as it's consistent with the dot investment made; I also give them first crack at coming up with the details of the character, assuming the merit isn't being applied to a preexisting NPC.

                                              If I decide to add complications to the relationship, I say so, and I give the player the option of taking back their xp investment.

                                              I do this because I want to operate on a basis of trust. Contacts and Allies and Mentors are a boon to the game: they give players a way to define their character that lies outside of stats and gear and personality quirks, and they give GMs a useful story hook - but they can only do that if players take them! I don't want to load them up with so much baggage that it trains my players to avoid them; nobody's Iron Stomach merit is secretly plotting to kill them.

                                                      My players generally don't buy mentor dots. As a way to jumpstart and further the plot, I usually give them a free mentor that gets the PCs going and then dies. IE: The Obi-Wan Kenobi type mentor.
