    Originally posted by Errol216 View Post
    I'm curious, Nirgal. Have you ever tried playing without XP? Like, fill out a character sheet, and add random stuff whenever the ST says it's okay to, but just don't ever reward XP. For anything.
    i reward exp by what i consider appropriate and considering how fast i want to make the players progress. i usually have the directive that what you earn in-game doesn't need exp coverage and what you get with exp needs in-game coverage, for ex: if you want a contact in the police departement go ahead and in-game find those contacts, if you find a way to achieve that you will get to write those contacts on your sheet; you want a safe place? go ahead and make arrangements, buy a house, find a good trusted security system installer etc; if you get a magical item in-game it's yours no need to spend exp to be able to use it.
    on the other hand if you get a magical object thx to exp you have to justify in-game why you have it, often such things are possible in the "downtime" between two stories or chapters.

    i award exp for partecipating in events (you always learn at least something), for creative thinking or for anything that moves the story forward, for the introduction of interesting elements or even sidestories, for playing your character above the average making difficult decisions to follow your character personality or because the character learned something (like studying).

    all these rewards are more narrative than mechanic, part of the fun is to see your character learn and change (not necessarly becoming more powerfull) and this is what i give exp for, my players want to have fun and thus they just try to roleplay at their best (ofc someone is better at doing it than others but that's no problem if you commit yourself) because they want to roleplay and not because i give them exp. Infact i usually give exp every 4-5 session that is often 1 month and half real life time.

    p.s. that's how my group handles exp in every game not only mage.

                                        Alright, so this is a big philosophical split among much of the RPG community. It's been described in a lot of different ways in the past, with varying degrees of accuracy. "Gamist" vs. "Simulationist", "Director Stance" vs. "Actor Stance", "Roll-playing" vs. "Role-playing", "Metagaming" vs... err... "Not Metagaming", etc. What it comes down to is the fact that some people prefer players to make decisions exclusively based on in-character information, and some don't care if players use out-of-character information to help them make decisions. For some folks, if the player's desires and the character's desires are not in-sync 100% of the time, it breaks immersion and harms the experience. Chronicles of Darkness Second Edition is not the system for those people.

                                        Here's the thing, Chronicles of Darkness is a collaborative stoytelling system first and formost. That's why it's called "The Storytelling System." The system priorities rules that get everyone involved in creating a dynamic and dramatically interesting horror story over preserving the synchronicity between player desires and character desires. It concedes that players will always make decisions based on out of character information. Like, literally it's impossible for human beings to ignore information they have access to when making a decision, because when you try to, you're still using that information to make your decision, you're just using it in the negative. e.g. "Well, I know trolls are weak to fire, but does my character know that? Maybe I should cast magic missile instead of fireball" is still a decision made based on the fact that you know, out-of-character, that fireball will be more effective against the troll. So, rather than trying to fight basic human psychology, Chronicles of Darkness works with it. Instead of asking players to pretend they don't know that going off alone to investigate a strange noise in a horror story is always a bad thing for the character who does it, and do it anyway because it's "what my character would do", it just encourages the player to want to go off alone and investigate the strange noise, because that's what happens in horror stories and players can find a way to justify absolutely anything as in-character if it's something they want to do.

                                        Now, some people don't like that design philosophy. You're absolutely not alone in disliking 2e because of these kinds of mechanics, it's just that most folks who feel that way don't stick around these forums for very long, cause the vibe here is very 2e-centric. But, if you prefer to keep players' desires and characters' desires in-sync, that's fine. 2e won't be the system to satisfy that objective without a lot of house ruling, though. You may want to consider using 1e as a baseline and bringing over elements of 2e that you like.

                                                    It sounds like you run games in a manner very similar to myself. Story forward, focused heavily on the collective roleplay element over adherence to mechanical rigor. That's pretty hard to do, btw, and if you're pulling it off that's great. Honestly it sounds to me like you don't NEED all those rules. Everything you've said tells me you and your group have a strong dynamic, maybe strong enough that you can just ignore the stuff you don't like. That's kinda how the game is designed, too. Very few systems are so integral to play that they cannot be excised or downplayed. I run without Beats, never roll Integrity analogues (because I trust my players to know when they've hit a breaking point), and very rarely have to pull out Tilts or Conditions in any mechanical sense (again, the players handle the narrative effects all on their own), hell I've run a couple of sessions diceless because no one felt there was any need to roll.

                                                    And there isn't anything wrong with any of that. The truth is that the system is just a crutch, a way of handling with dots and dice what may be difficult to achieve with pure narrative. But if that crutch gets in the way there is no shame in tossing it out the window.

                                                    If you are interested in system tweaking, I'd actually recommend you check out the Storypath System Spoilers for Scion 2e. It is similar enough to CofD that many elements can be ported over, and is clearly laser focused on creating streamlined roleplaying experiences without getting too fiddly with dice.
