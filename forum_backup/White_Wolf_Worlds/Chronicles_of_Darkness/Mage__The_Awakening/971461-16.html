    Originally posted by Geckopirateship View Post
    Again, that's something I've been very clear about wanting to emphasize. I'll try to be more diplomatic about Mage, but it's kind of hard to talk about it without insulting it in some way when I'm trying to fix what genuinely seem like problems to me. I love Geist, for example, but I don't object to the homebrew threads focused on &quot;fixing&quot; it, because I can totally recognize that there are things about it that you can definitely make a case for being problems. There's a difference between kinds of homebrew- this kind, and the Geist fixes, are about correcting perceived faults, which is different from those which are about adding content or making adjustments that aren't made to remedy problems. So, making a homebrew Legacy for Mage or a homebrew Key for Geist or a Dark Era can be done without implying that there are problems with the original, but something like this really can't. I understand that I've been a dick about this in the past, and I'm trying to do better, but there's not really a way to ask &quot;how do I make mages feel more monstrous&quot; without implying that the game as it is makes them insufficiently monstrous.
    The key to not being insulting to the game you are hacking is to focus on personal preference. When in doubt, use "I statements." "I have a problem with X" instead of "X is a problem with the game." "I want to change X" instead of "X needs to be changed." Avoid suggesting that the game is broken. "Hacking" instead of "Fixing". Mage isn't unplayable, you just don't like the way it plays. And that's ok. And changing it so you can play it in a way that you do like is ok. It only becomes not ok when you claim that what you like is objectively better than what other people like, or that other people are wrong for liking Mage as-written.

                    Again, "Magic is Mages forcing their delusions onto reality" is a completely plausible interpretation of Awakening's metaphysics. "Knowing secret truths about the universe" is a Mage's interpretation of their own powers. Nothing says their interpretation is actually right, and there are very good reasons to believe they're not. Fighting against reality itself is one of Mage's major themes (even if it's not one I would choose to focus on if I were to run it). It just seems like what you need is a setting doccument more than extensive hacks. Not to say you shouldn't hack the rules to emphasize those themes if you want to, just that those changes aren't strictly necessary in order to play to those themes.

                    Originally posted by Geckopirateship View Post
                    You say that "mages realize the secret truths the sheeple don't" isn't true in the game. What's your evidence? Where does it say this or imply it? Sure, you're free to interpret mages as being delusional, but the game as written primarily supports the "mages see secret truths about the nature of reality that normal people don't" interpretation.
                    The game is written from a Mage's perspective, since Mages are the protagonists. They are, however, meant to be anti-aspirational protagonists, which we know from word-of-developer and from the fact that it's a Chronicles of Darkness game. For an example of Mages being delusional, look no further than the Atlantis myth. Mage society collectively buys into a story they know for a fact is at best only symbolically true, and at worst outright fiction. The truth is, no such place ever existed. The aspects of reality they call "The Lie" have always been part of reality. The supernal symbols they blame for the existence of those parts of reality have always been a part of the Supernal. The Abyss has always separated the phenomenal from the Supernal. Magic has always been intrinsically harmful to the human soul. Mages have a story that conveniently explains why these facts are only true of this version of reality, which overwrote a previous version of reality that just-so-happened to be an idyllic utopia by Mage standards until those badwrong Supernal symbols (which used to be mages, don't you know) snuck into the Supernal where they didn't belong and ruined everything so hard it applied retroactively. Now, that story could be true. The young earth creationist explaination for dinosaur fossils could be true too, if you're willing to swallow the pill that the universe operated under different rules back then (admittedly an easier pill to swallow when you know for a fact that the rules of the universe are malleable, but still...) But Occam's Razor would tend to suggest Mages are fooling themselves.

                    Personally, I favor an interpretation that says the myth contains a grain of truth, but has been massively distorted to suit a narrative that casts mages in a better light. If you prefer an interpretation that says they're totally wrong, that's just as valid within the setting as-written.

                                                    Originally posted by Geckopirateship View Post
                                                    Off the top of anyone's heads, what would be the major things that would need to be changed to update the Gifted Scientists from the MCG to 2e rules?

                                                    Here are some other goals:
                                                    - Establish a clear link between power and monstrosity. Gaining Gnosis inevitably distances you from humanity and has lots of nasty effects.
                                                    - Use this to balance Mages. Mages should be able to choose between being human and being powerful. They shouldn't be able to get both.
                                                    - A better system of limits and sacrifices. Every Mage should have personal limits on their powers, and doing major effects should require sacrifice of some sort. Unknown Armies is a great example.
                                                    - Actually, "how can I make Mage more like Unknown Armies" is pretty much a good question in itself.

                                                    An idea that would cover a lot of those would be to give Mages Bans and Banes when they gain Gnosis or lose Wisdom.
                                                    I've been toying with stuff way more than I ought to as my own Mage game winds down, but it's kinda voluminous, so I'll share an easy-to-adapt tidbit regarding your first request:

                                                    So, my players found it a bit tricky to keep track of Virtue/Vice and Aspirations and Obsessions. As a way of emphasizing the way mages lose their connection to their humanity as they raise their Gnosis, every normal person in my game has five Motives - one Virtue, one Vice, one Long-Term Aspiration, and two Short-Term Aspirations. Each Obsession a mage has replaces one of those five slots - chosen at the time Gnosis increases. At Gnosis 1 most mages are just going to ditch one of those Short-Term Aspirations, but what do you do when you get your second Obsession at Gnosis 3? Or your third at Gnosis 6. By Gnosis 9, you'll have four Obsessions crowding out all but one of your remaining Motives. Do you retain the capability to feel good about living up to your high ideals (Virtue)? Do you cling to your guilty pleasures (Vice)? Do you continue to pursue major life goals (Long-Term Aspiration)? Or do you hang onto your ability to derive satisfaction from small accomplishments (Short-Term Aspiration)? And what of those mages who convince themselves at Gnosis 1 that they would rather cut out the "sinful" part of themselves (Vice)?

                                                    In consequence, as a mage's Gnosis increases, she becomes more and more a creature of her Obsessions.

                                                    Raising Gnosis makes it harder to advance mundane traits Even if you're not playing for Beats, there is always that moment every other Gnosis bump where the player needs to think about what part of his character's human motivations to cut out forever - which serves as a reminder to the player that his character is losing something even as she gains more occult power. It's a subtle loss of humanity compared to Bans and Banes, but it has the advantage of being easy to implement. As an added bonus, it reduces the mechanical complexity of tracking Obsessions on top of the five mundane Motives.
