                The problem is that it's not a convincing argument in this situation.

                Let me go to a similar case I used in Changeling for some clarification-A Huntsman grabs a newly returned changeling, drags them to the edge of Arcadia, and gives them a choice: Help us or go back. In this scenario, there's a widely understood and oft-accepted premise that Arcadia is The Worst Thing Ever to changelings. Dying is kinder than Arcadia. It is not implausible, hanging on the edge of that cliff, a changeling would be willing to compromise their beliefs and betray their friends, families, and beliefs.

                Now, mind you, that's still kind of cartoonish and not really how the Huntsmen work. They do use scare tactics, but they play a complex game beyond that, taking advantage of the fluid nature of the fae perspective in all it's forms in order to foster distrust in one's self and others and them in you and ideally try to influence a changeling to change their mind on deep, fundamental levels so that even a partial success yields long standing results. But it works because of the premise that we can accept in the comfort of our homes, because we have, for the most part, bought into the books.

                Mage though? Mage doesn't have exterior circumstances like this. This is not a game heavily about the impact of outside influences and how one shifts from one view to another, this is a game about the failures and triumphs of the human will, that change is fundamentally at the hands of the person confronted with it. So we as players, looking in, just see a classic scenario we have seen in media countless times with no real personal experience for why they would say "Sure, I'll join."

                I mean, sure, intellectually we know that the stress and the fear and the confusion and the anger and the everything going on at the time might sway us one way or another, but we've also been trained to know the answer is NO in that scenario, that principles are worth dying for and important and generally we've just seen enough shit that portrays saying "I choose death" in a positive light. And from the comfort of our own homes, secure in our knowledge and faith and feelings and bathrobes, it's easy to say "I wouldn't join." And so when you're trying to convince someone that they might, of course they're going to say no. It's not compelling. It's not an invasive scenario, it doesn't subvert or challenge one's conventional understandings. People know to say No to that. They know to say No to the guy trying to buy you.

                If there is an appeal to make, It is not through blatant displays of force, it's through subtle and insidious manipulations of that which a person takes pride in. The Seers aren't going to put a gun to your head, they're going to find ways to make you question yourself, what you stand for. They will find the cracks in your armor and they will take advantage of that with velvet gloves. Sure, they'll keep up exterior pressure, but they aren't going to compromise the entire's organization as the soft second place.

                If the Seers try to win someone over, the first thing they are always going to do is get someone to give up. Completely on their own accord.

                And, you know, it's hard for anyone to know just how they would give up. The worlds we live in seem very sure to us-they are designed that way, and we are programmed to make it easy to find, because otherwise we'd be gibbering balls of neuroses on overload. These things seem safe and solid to us, and it takes a lot to make them ephemeral. If you told me five years ago that I would be planning on leaving the Mormon Church, I'd have laughed at you. It now seems inevitable to me, but then, it was inconceivable, and that's with me having been aware of a majority of the things that did, in fact, go and play into why I finally got tired of the bullshit.

                And this is half of why my point is you won't know until you're there, because it's inconceivable to see that which is firm around us fade away into smoke. This is why I generally do not posit why Kumiko specifically would. It's a personal affair.

                (The other half of the point is that saying "i wouldn't" basically reads to me as "I'm not examining with myself with a critical eye, looking for weaknesses, and shoring up against them, so by all means go for it", and further stills that those flaws are perceived as possibly not having a place in the Way Things Are and something that needs to be accepted for one's long term transcendence.)

                And strictly speaking, it's also, from an in-universe perspective, not very practical for the Seers to go around doing the "Gun to Head" conversion trick. You might win a temporary ally, but you have also laid the seeds of future shit-kicking going down. The Seers might be a contentious bunch, fighting each other over positions and such, but they still wouldn't want to essentially hire someone in a way that invites them to set them up the bomb at the worst possible time.

                So, yeah, no, if you want to try and open someone to the possibility of them joining the bad guys, don't go with a classical black and white scene we've all seen and know the answer to. Argue from a position that is chartreuse.

                                            I feel like this entire argument is premised on the idea that the Seers are giving you the hard sell. Do we think that they do that, though?

                                            I mean, they recruit people, yes, because they need tools and cannon fodder, but - crucially - they're not rewarded for swelling the ranks as such, but for climbing them, and every new recruit is a potential competitor. And while it's also true that every new Seer recruit is an opportunity stolen from the clutches of the Pentacle, so is a dead mage.

                                            I think it's entirely possible that if someone isn't immediately receptive to the basic appeal of the Seers - to immediate status and luxury - then that person is just crossed off the list permanently. Why expend a lot of energy trying to change someone's mind about letting you share your power with them? Especially when that early reluctance could be a red flag signaling trouble down the road, when power and experience have made them more formidable?

                                            In this take, the stops would only really get pulled out if the person had something the Seers specifically wanted, like a unique insight, a special talent, or a promising destiny.
