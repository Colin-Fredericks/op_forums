            Most everything that was on the spectrum of Umbra from higher to lower has a corresponding set of rules. Whereas there was basically a single axis of weirdness in oWoD, (lower would be negative. Deep umbra would simply be farther. Both fall on the same axis) there are multiple axes in CoD.

            The lower, near Umbra is ghostly Twilight. The lower umbra is the Underworld... and, arguably, some of the Lower Depths.

            The upper near umbra splits into the Inner Realms (Astral) and the Spirit. The near side Twilight for these things is distinct in 2e (and, arguably in 1e) but there are increasing depths to most of these axes, as well.

            The Twilight of mental projections extends into the dreams of individuals and then into the dreams of groups and the world. There are many distinct realms in there and you will likely find much of what you expect from the deep umbra in there. The axis is towards a more universal soul... and, ultimately, reaches to the Supernal Realms through the Abyss.

            Spirits have realms that are linked to physical reality and then stretch off into more esoteric forms.(Can't find my Book of the Spirits, just now, so I can't skim it to spot things that jump out as incompatible, but the vast majority of the fluff is basically applicable.)

            The really out-there deep umbra stuff is Abyssal or Lower Depths... or even Emanation Realms and the Supernal.

            If weird, deep umbra quests are something you enjoy, Stupid Loserman nailed it: The Astral Realms are probably what you seek, so the Book of Spiriits may not be of great value. There's a reason Awakening recast the Dreamspeakers as a Mind legacy.

            Grump, grouse, and/or gripe.

                                Yeah, you want the Astral Realms for that, that's both the signature otherworld for mages and the place where different conceptual realms manifest genre-specific phenomena to suit the ideas the realm embodies. The first edition Astral Realms supplement still has a lot of relevant and useful setting detail for plotting a 2e astral-heavy game, though any mechanical widgets or astral entity (what Mage 2e calls goetia) writeups would need to be rebuilt for 2e. But it's great for context and guides, it fits exactly in with how 2e describes the Astral Realms, and its sample astral environments that receive multiple-page writeups include "the planet Mars as a place of war, as imagined through pulp science fiction, with green men wielding swords and rayguns," and "the ruins of lost civilizations of intelligent awakened housecats or crows, which rose and fell entirely in the world of dreams, purely possible but never tangibly realized."

                                While Mind is the relevant Arcanum for spells which directly transform or manipulate the nature of goetic beings, it's not really necessary for a mage who just wants to project into the astral and go adventuring in the collective unconscious to uncover secrets, chase troublemakers, or influence ideas. The Spirit Arcanum, meanwhile, while important if you want to try to reach into the Shadow Realm, is generally more practical as a means of influencing latent spirits to achieve results in the material realm (which may or may not involve directly transforming or manipulating the nature of those spirits as the means to do so).

                                                Originally posted by Mahjemm01 View Post
                                                Speaking of The Hedge, Proindrakenzol, I have a copy of CtL 2e because I am wanting to have adventures in the Hedge. But I have read that ...idrc...where exactly....The Hedge flays non Changeling and Fae souls while travelling through it.
                                                1e's version of the Hedge had it so that most non-changelings deferred rolls to lose their Morality stats until they left the Hedge, and wandering through the Thorns/leaving the path stripped dots from Morality and Wisdom outright every hour that a character spent wandering; mechanically, this runs the same risks as soul loss for mages, including losing the ability to cast once they bottom out.

                                                Since Integrity stats work so differently in 2e, this is less of a concern than the fact that "the Thorns" means "the parts of the Hedge that are particularly dangerous." Navigating the Hedge in the Thorns is tricky — it's "faster" in the chase that models navigation (you practically need a vehicle or a lot of Speed boosts to have a shorter run to success than the Hedge itself), it rolls more dice in the pursuit, and any Hedgespinning rolls it makes in response to the characters' actions gets the 8-Again rule for generating successes to spend creating complications, to say nothing of the higher likelihood that you are in an actively dangerous environment — and just not being fae makes the process of navigating the Hedge in general harder (there's no Wyrd rating to reduce the number of successes you need directly, the Hedge doesn't subtract anyone's Clarity from its effective Initiative, and mental/emotional Conditions further affect the Hedge's ability to get you lost by winning the chase).

                                                        The Thorns were in 1e as well, it's just that the division of Hedge-type went from "consistent path," "on the path," "off the path," "far off the path" to "consistent path," "most other places," "this is where the Big Bad Wolf lives."

                                                        There's no longer a delay on breaking points and their equivalent and it no longer directly removes dots from the relevant Traits, but the Hedge is set up in such a manner that if it gets what it wants before you do, you're going to get funneled into the Thorns or the front door of Arcadia; Hedgespinning allows its users (including the Hedge) to force other characters' failures to become dramatic failures, dramatic failures in the Thorns result in really bad-news Conditions like Lost or Madness, and negative Conditions tend to play out in the environment in metaphors that tend to incur further breaking points.

                                                        And all this is to say nothing of the fact that one of the easier ways to get anywhere in the Hedge is to accept the guidance of a hobgoblin in exchange for Goblin Debt — a meter that only changelings can reduce without doing favors for the goblins they owe instead of letting it build up to the level of "you're a hobgoblin now, too, have fun sticking to the Hedge until you've worked off your Debt."

                                                        Basically, though, yes, the principal issue has changed the precise flavor of "you will likely wind up trapped in slavery after long enough" that being stuck in the Hedge long-term promises the incautious wanderer.

                                                        World of Darkness: Inferno provides some information on the most consistent-ish denizens of Abaddon, and Werewolf's treatment on Wounds and the Maeljin can be used fairly well conceptually, but the Lower Depths are you're-not-coming-back territory for most games. Phenomena emerging "upward" from the Depths cause a great deal of trouble on their own, usually, because the world's not built for them.
