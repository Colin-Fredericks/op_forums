            Adherence to a universal sectarian law pretty much is the definition of orthodoxy, which started with Edict of Thessalonica in 381 CE that turned Christianity into the State Theology of Rome and outlawed entirely any other religion. Previous to that, the closest thing approaching orthodoxy in the Roman world is superstitio, the belief that someone was worshipping excessively or had unusual devotion. It was considered a personality flaw, and Seneca wrote about it. Augustine of Hippo had access to his treatise on it, but we don't. Another lost document. Exclusionary practices, like Judaism and Christianity, were considered superstitio. It was only after Nicaea and Thessalonica that is became codified in the modern sense.

                        Huh, learned some new stuff. In which case I concede the point, and yes, Christianity would be considered the most successful Mystery Cult.

                        Anyways, on to Collegia and other things.

                        GUILDS: THE EARLY YEARS

                        Remember the discussion about secular Mystery Cults and aristocratic clubs with Mystery Cult structuring? Those are the Collegia, but the slight problem is that collegium can be an extremely broad term in Roman law, and it involves a lot of ancient legalese that I am neither equipped to handle nor inclined to translate. The Wikipedia version is that they were any association with a legal personality in Roman society. There are three broad categories of Collegia, being religious organizations, trade organizations, or burial societies. These Collegia were authorized or empowered by the central Roman government, and the internal structure of the Collegia were based off of Roman government structures on a smaller scale.

                        Later in the Republic and eventually in the Imperial Period, the government tried to restrict Collegia to hereditary positions, or to limit them to political clubs with the express purpose of trying to secure the election of candidates for magistrate positions. Some Collegia were charged with the maintenance of state cults, others were made with the express purpose of teaching, protecting, and spreading a trade. Some Collegia were even made with the express purpose of being a burial society, ensuring proper rites and a memorial for the dead, a kind of immortality in the Greco-Roman world only the better off or the aristocratic had access to. The trade Collegia were eventually instituted in every major municipality as "public utilities", much in the same way that state cults were considered essential to public interest.

                        The upper management (or possibly the only members. I'm not sure and I don't have access to the proper documents to find out) were all appointed to their positions, either by the general membership of the Collegium or by the others in the ruling council. Collegia we actually have memberships for suggest that official Collegia had a number of appointed positions ranging from just two members to up to fifteen. As I said, it's not clear if these are the people in charge of a wider membership, or if these were the only "official" members. I'm inclined to believe that it was a mixture of both, considering the College of Consuls were elected from the Senate (wider membership) while the College of Pontiffs was a group that was made up exclusively of the highest priests in Roman state theology (limited official membership). And then there's the College of Augurs where I seriously doubt there was only a handful of diviners in the Roman world, but it appears as though these are the only "government approved" diviners. As an interesting aside, a lot of skilled craftspeople in the Greco-Roman world were actually slaves, which make Collegia an interesting way that people who are generally considered to have very little power in their society can exert power on the wider world. Towards the end of the Western Empire, most craftspeople were not slaves, as the Collegia had given trades some fairly serious social leverage and prestige.

                        The lack of written records from the fall of the Western Empire and the general chaos of the Post-Roman period seems to suggest that Collegia collapsed in the wake of the centralized government that supported them going up in smoke, but they persisted in the Byzantine Empire and basically did what they had always done. Apparently stonecutters and glassmakers were the only trade Collegia that survived in western Europe. Eventually Collegia evolved into Guilds... or at least that's what a not-insignificant number of historians I read about believe.

                        GUILDS: THE LATER YEARS

                        Much like the Collegia that preceded them, Guilds were tightly interrelated with civic government. Also like Collegia, those high up in the Guilds are also often high up in civic government. Guilds were early vectors of international communication and trade in the Post-Roman period, because as much as these smaller kingdoms warred with each other, a Guild was a Guild, one stone mason was much like another, and the Guilds were essentially parallel governments anyways. This caused... problems... later in history, when centralized government started becoming more powerful again, and Guilds became comparatively less influential. However, they were now wealthy, highly entrenched in society, and frequently armed with mercenaries or using some of their own lesser members as as foot soldiers. Guilds start looking a lot like government sanctioned cartels after a while, complete with splintering and infighting as groups either specialized or generalized, causing conflict as to whether a Guild faction was worth being considered a separate Guild. Regardless, there was eventually some kind of pseudo-feudal system between overarching Guilds and the lesser, specialized Guilds.

                        RELATING BACK TO MAGE

                        So, as has been previously established, Orders are Mystery Cults. There's no getting around it. However, Mystery Cults are also voluntary organizations. You aren't mandated to be part of them the way you are mandated to be part of the state theology. The Consilium then is probably closer to a Collegium, with the official positions appointed or elected by the general membership (It's been a while since I've read the books that are heavy on how Consilia work, so correct me if I get something disastrously wrong) of Awakened, who happen to be parts of Orders. But Orders are not just Mystery Cults, they evolved into something rather like Guilds. Complex, Mentor-Student relationships form the bedrock, initiatory politics produce the intrigue, and Cabals form political blocks within the Orders. Most of the Orders could probably function just fine on their own, but if we accept that they operate more like Guilds, then the specialization means that they associate with the other Orders pretty much because they compete for the same resources, but they all have their own means of understanding and using those resources.

                        The Orders, while not technically political blocks of a Consilium, probably end up functioning that way in a similar manner to the interrelationship between Guilds and civic government. For some, being appointed to a specific position may be a matter of being in the right Order or having had the right Mentor, for others, trying to keep Order politics out of Consilium politics might be something they strive very hard to do. I'm not really a politics guy, I'm an Anthropology guy, so this is starting to get out of my wheelhouse. Next time I get the opportunity to write another huge post for this thread, I'll probably start poking at the Orders a little.
