        You're taking a single line and basing your entire understanding on it. Wisdom is a measure of how the mage uses magic, and it ties back into how well the mage deals with Paradox. But it's not exclusively about the mage's magic. Nowhere does it state that only spells can cause Acts of Hubris.

        "In an Act of Hubris, the mage kills a living being for Mana."
        Blood sacrifices are not made through spells.

        "Two mages in nearly identical circumstances cast the same spell or commit the same action, but to one, his Wisdom is safe while the other risks degeneration."

        "When innocent bystanders are affected by your
        character’s spells or actions, she is at risk as well."

        "Self-mutilating events such as soul stone creation risk degeneration."
        Soul stones are not created through spells. They're results of an innate ability.

        "allowing a Supernal being to be consumed by the Fallen World"
        AoH through inaction. No spell is used for this.

        "If a mage suffers Wisdom loss through use of a spell, she can choose to wipe that spell from future Acts of Hubris;"
        Implying Wisdom loss through means other than spells. If only spells could cause AoH, there would not be need for any clarification that Inurement only applies to spells.

        "Using a Legacy Attainment is never considered an Act of Hubris; the mage’s mystic self is completely attuned to their use."
        A mage's mystic self is not attuned to any mundane actions.

            Supernal Magic is broader than spellcasting.

            Mana, soul stones, supernal beings, are all supernal phenomenon. They're all magical. Degeneration in those cases falls neatly under my thesis that acts of hubris always involve the mage's interactions with spells and other supernal phenomenon.

            Both of your items keying off the word "action" are cases I had in mind where the authors don't want to write "with magic" a hundred times in that section. Relevant actions in that context would be the activation of an imbued item, the summoning of a supernal being, flaring a nimbus, etc... and this covers the remainder of your points too. Those aren't referring to mundane actions, unless of course the ST wants to make use of the flex built into the Wisdom system to make it so and blunt the horror build into the mage template.

            You didn't address how your interpretation of "mystic self" for legacy attainments makes any sense in light of my point above about there being no exceptions for usages of legacy attainments that are "immoral" (according to a Western concept of morality? Eastern? Atlantean?) and also run utterly counter to the fundaments of the legacy. In no sense would a mage's "mystic self" be attuned to such actions... yet they're degeneration free anyway. It's just a nonsense position. You may as well say that they're free of risk of degeneration because reasons. A legacy is not defined by its attainments; the attainments arise out of and in support of the ethos of the legacy.

            Thematically, Wisdom and the Lex Magica being divorced from the common man's perception of morality is a fundamental driver of horror in Mage. A mage of Enlightened Wisdom could be a stone-cold murderer as long he's very careful, calculating, and judicious in how he employs magic, and the letter of the Lex Magica won't necessarily find him culpable for anything so long as his actions don't step on any other mage's toes. Once you've signed your name to a Watchtower, your God is magic and your morality is all about showing it proper respect.

            EDIT: To clarify, this is the meaning of "mystic self is completely attuned to their use":
            "The mage invokes them from her reshaped soul instead of reaching into the Supernal."
            It means that they're effects that arise from the mage's own soul and are not supernal magic. And so, of course, their use has no impact on Wisdom, because Wisdom is only concerned with the mage's actions related to Supernal magic and other Supernal phenomenon. This is the interpretation of this collection of rules that is internally consistent. The other interpretation that you're advocating strikes me as "It doesn't risk degeneration to give it an attractive gameplay advantage, even though that stands in contradiction to the setting." Going by your interpretation, the game should only have allowed legacy attainments to be degeneration free when the use aligns with the ethos of the legacy.

            EDIT 2: Honestly, I just don't get why someone would interpret Wisdom as reflecting even in part a common notion of morality/consience, as if a comprehensible character would ever think "Mudering babies is deeply wrong... but I'm cool with it as long as I use this inured spell or legacy attainment. Then my sense of morality isn't violated so no risk of degeneration!" That's just... random. What does make sense though is a character thinking "My provincial notions of morality have burned away in the fires of Supernal Truth, but I must employ these Truths judiciously lest they consume me completely." In that paradigm, the mage's Wisdom isn't bothered by baby-murdering regardless; the only true sin is the reckless use of magic. That's consistent and comprehensible. A guy doesn't become a Rapt because he got drunk and shot his wife. He becomes a Rapt by using magic so carelessly that he loses all ability to take care in its use. Morals are for mortals.

            Now, this isn't to say that a mage wouldn't feel moral qualms about baby-murdering. We still have to have sympathetic, relatable characters. It's just that these violations of conscience and moral qualms aren't relevant for the Wisdom stat. The bottom line is that Mage does not have a stat that tracks a Good vs. Evil scale, it has a stat that tracks the Judicious vs Reckless scale with respect to the Supernal.

            EDIT 3: But at this point what I really want is for Satchel to prove me wrong by referencing a part of a 2E Mage fiction where a character struggles with Wisdom after a hit-and-run accident, or a DaveB 2E liveplay where he makes someone roll for degeneration after robbing someone at gunpoint, or similar.

                        Conceded, although for the purpose of my own games I prefer the way I've been running it because my interpretation supports the "Mages are monsters too" horror aspect of the setting. I find that in games where characters lose a stat value based on the morality of their actions that they must spend XP to buy back or risk losing their character, it over-incentivizes them to act like goody-two-shoes superheroes and inhibits their willingness to really inhabit their characters. I mean, explain how "Using a human shield" is an Act of Hubris on anything other than moral grounds.

                        And, as noted previously at length, I'm not able to jive the mechanical benefits of inured spells and legacy attainments w.r.t. degeneration into that schema. The fact that a mage used an inured spell or legacy attainment to kill someone in a fit of rage has no bearing on their self control. The intuitive notion of self control is about what you do, not about what tool you use to do it. Inured spells and legacy attainments being free of risk of degeneration end up seeming like gameplay advantages or consessions that run counter to the spirit of the Wisdom mechanic rather than supporting it, whatever hand-wavy throw-away justification is provided. It's like having a law against dumping raw sewage in a river, and finding out it's okay as long as the raw sewage is transported in barrels labeled "soil" because, hey, that law has harsh penalties and you have to cut the waste management companies a few breaks.

                        If Wisdom is about self-control, it would have made more sense for a mage to be able to inure himself to specific misdeeds, and for a member of a legacy not risk degeneration for actions in line with the ethos of the legacy.

                        Ugh, it just seems like a mess. Dish-served-cold murder is an act of self-control. Taking a human shield is a neutral action with respect to self-control. The waters are muddy, and it just looks like "actions that a reasonable person would call selfish or evil are low Wisdom" -- i.e. a measure of morality. This is starting to seem like an Emperor with No Clothes situation. It's like a hodgepodge, where when the authors wanted to throw in a mechanical penalty for an action they wanted to discourage they labeled it as an Act of Hubris for want of any other suitable mechanic. If there is a theme that underpins and unifies all these Acts of Hubris, it's not the one described in the section on Wisdom.
