        Originally posted by wyrdhamster View Post
        Limited human minds cannot predict every possible results - it's also fundament of Wisdom-as-Trait.
        No it isn't.

        If I go out to the store this morning to pick up some eggs, and I have a choice of routes, and I pick one, and then on the way a drunk staggers out from behind a blind corner into the path of my car before I can slow down, that is a consequence of my choice - my choice to turn right instead of left or whatever - and of my inability to foresee the future and the outcome of all possible circumstances. It has jack to do with my Wisdom, however.

        Holding people culpable for the outcomes of actions taken in good faith and without impossible knowledge of the circumstances elevates absurdity to the level of an art form. Under that rubric, every postman who unknowingly delivered a mail bomb, or a ransom note, or a Publisher's Sweepstakes scam, would be some kind of criminal.

        Wisdom is not some absolute measure of how much harm you have unknowingly - or even knowingly! - caused. It is not an evilometer. What it does do is indicate the degree of care a character takes in their choices out of their feeling of connection to others - how likely they are to see themselves and their goals as the only worthwhile end. Going back to my first example, if I hit the man because I was speeding, because I figured that my precious time was way more important than the law or other people's safety, *that* would be a strike against Wisdom.

Then you can say that Silver Ladder, for making every one live in Paradise, would get lowest Wisdom dice pool, as they do not try make anything work under now governing principles of CoD universe.

                    Not too mention lower penalties from planned murder than crime of passion.

                    Naw.

                    The opposite of Wisdom is Obsession, not action, and the best way to conserve Wisdom is not to just offer a teenage, fatalistic shrug and accept every status quo that is presented to you - that's just another path to alienation from your fellow human beings, another path to detachment (in this case, detachment from the struggles and sufferings of the people around you).

                    Every mage has an obsession and every order has its own path to Hubris. An Arrow's preoccupation with self-perfection and endless struggle is no less of a potential pitfall than a Ladder's revolutionary zeal: the danger to Wisdom is in the act of Obsessing, but Wisdom isn't a proxy measure for whether the object of Obsession itself is good, bad, or indifferent. These things are decoupled. Self-knowledge and self-improvement are generally regarded as positive goals - it's certainly hard to construct an ethical schema where they're explicitly and always anti-good! - but low-Wisdom Arrows exist. Domination and enslavement are almost unquestionably bad things but high-wisdom Seers exist.

                            These are the questions that keep thearchs up at night.
                            It's important to consider Wisdom as separate from ethics or morality inasmuch as it's explicitly measuring how likely you are to use magic as a shortcut, and how that separates you from mundane humans. Taking it strictly as written, Wisdom doesn't have any sins for failure to use magic. Early on, the Arcana are not innate to a mage's selfhood - he will still think of himself as a regular person - and later on, at higher Gnosis, it reflects that magic isn't a passive choice but an active one that carries its own risks that may outweigh the consequences of not using it.

                            A freshly-minted Acanthus, for instance, just out of Acanthus High and still getting a grip on even the basics of his arcana, might not realize that he could have done something to suggest the better route to take...but that's not going to stop him feeling horrible or guilty about it when it happens. Conversely, an experienced enchanter who has seen a lot of people's Fates fulfilled or cut short might have a somewhat inhuman sangfroid about the death of one person but would feel terrible about their sloppy Fatesmanship in letting it happen in the first place.

                            The second guy is probably more culpable morally, but I wouldn't rule that he had committed an Act of Hubris. He probably does have a lower Wisdom score in general, though, which is reflected in his detached outlook.

                                I think this is where we run into a problem, since murder is a Wisdom sin, even when done without magic; that certain magic acts are sins when they could be accomplished no other way; that a callousness to one's fellow human beings is not a trait exclusive to magicians.

                                It feels to me that any claim that Wisdom isn't tied to morals and ethics will run against the problem that it clearly, say, values human lives and souls over the lives of plants and animals. It claims to value self-control, but self-control of a very specific type.

                                Wisdom seems to cleave to a very modern humanistic and libertarian view of what is a "normal" human, and values that humanity above all else. It is not, by any means, a schema that'd have been accepted universally before the rise of modernity.
