    Too many rules too few roleplay?

    10-19-2016, 10:21 AM
    I had been thinking about it from some time and finally i decided to open a thread about it:

    with second edition a lot of new rules were introduced, rules that i find not only unneeded but even harmfull for a roleplaying game. The game itself is very complex with how spells work and that's part of the fun, i don't really see the need for even more complex rules to encourage/force players to play.
    Roleplay becomes a mechanic that is even rewarded mechanically with exp: obsessions are just a way to keep control of players and force them to do things that are compatible with the narrator storyline, i can see how this can help gamists and inexperienced narrators but i think that is a way to transform a roleplaying game into a boardgame, more on the subject conditions and tilts... seriously how are needed 10 pages of those things? to keep track you even have cards... and the game promotes you to use those conditions and tilts to exploit for exp.
    To play by the book a scene you have to start piling condition cards (maybe it's raining and it's dark, maybe one or more characters have some spells running), then players start to think that they have to do something because they have an obsession... then comes the social conflict rule system... Seriously what's the need of an in depth rule system to decide the outcome of roleplay? A character needs to convince someone and to do so what he does? start rolling diece... a chance to roleplay becomes a chance to roll dice. Since your character is good at speaking you can brain off and let dice do the talking...

    the new rules encourage players to look at their character sheets and start to think how to wombo combo things to get exp, they have to play their obsession to get exp, they have to cause disadvantages to themselves to get exp and so on, if they manage to do both at the same time win!
    characters are defined by their virtue/vices, their path, their order and now by obsessions, in the end it seems like they are constructed in such a way that every roleplaying moment is a consequence of a mechanic and not the real reason why you play in the first place.
    While playing i find myself telling to my players: ignore this, ignore that, don't worry about this, don't worry about that... practically all those new things are so mechanical and heavy on the game that i find myself playing using only the new rules for spells! And the worst part is that it forces us to house rule a lot! all those new mechanics are not optional but are integrated so deeply in the new system that not using them and wanting to actually roleplay is penalized.

    I am not against rulesystems, i feel the need for rules to avoid too much arbitrary decisions and to give players a solid idea of what they can accomplish, but i don't like when a game becomes more a series of rules than a roleplaying experience.
    Am i the only one feeling that this new course of cod is going too much on the gamist side of the spectrum?

              Obsessions and Aspirations are ways for the player to tell the Storyteller what they want for their character.

              Conditions and Tilts are just a fancy name for Effects, and offer players incentives for playing them up. The only difference between, 'You've been blinded by the flash,' and 'The flash is so bright it blinds you, take the Blinded Condition,' is that you can get experience from the latter. You don't need to pile up Condition cards. 'It's dark and rainy, take a -3 on your attempt to shoot the thug.'

              If you're worried about people trying to get more Beats for themselves than others, then use group Beats. They shouldn't have to cause disadvantages to themselves just to get Experience, but alternatively without Beats they have no reason to ever do anything that might be disadvantageous. In this form, Beats give you a reason to take risks with your character, because even if you fail you get a Beat, as opposed to a failure offering nothing.

                  Aspirations and Obsessions

                  I think these are a great addition to the game. These are neatly divided into long and short-term goals that give information to the Storyteller. What are the players interested in? How are they going to go about fulfilling these goals? What are they aiming at in the short-term (this or the next session) or in the long-term (half a dozen to a dozen sessions from now)? I ask my players to create a storyline of their own. I know...how unorthodox of me to think that players might be interested in telling their own stories inside the giant sandbox that I have lovingly crafted for them. I do have my own stories to tell as a Storyteller, but most of the time I just listen to my players and let them direct the chronicle, while I keep dropping hints about something grander in the background. Sooner or later, the players also become increasingly interested in the stories I have, but I find it very desirable to get players invested in my chronicles. And nothing builds that better than allowing for personalized stories where the player characters play a central role.

                  Condition Cards and Tilts

                  Honestly, I think these are mostly just a way to provide guidance and direction to less experienced players. I use them sparingly, because they can become a pointless hassle, if I just start throwing them around willy-nilly. Like any game mechanic they can become an obstacle rather than a good thing. Personally I haven't found them to be a problem and they do provide a little bit of extra guidance.

                  I think a lot of what you are saying comes down to the fact that Mage 2E is a new thing. I have only run a few sessions myself, and it is a lot to take in. That's why I spent over an hour with all of my players just teaching them how the new magic system works. And I'm slowly introducing them to the rest of it. I think a lot of the ideas in the book are perfectly fine, but they do take time to get used to. The end result is, IMO, better than 1E. However, having only run a few sessions I reserve my right to change my mind later on.

                      I can't say for others, but no. I disagree with you. I personally feel that having a system designed to reward dramatic roleplaying is actually the direct opposite of "gamist." The game is actually changed in such a way that you can't advance as a character if you use a so-called gamist mindset. You get rewarded for failing, you get rewarded for making yourself vulnerable, you get rewarded for helping others succeed at their goals. In fact, the game is designed to mechanically reward Narrativist and Simulationist style of gameplay. In fact, the CofD is not the first game to go this route, but the system for rewarding experience for roleplaying is still rare. Its more rules-heavy, perhaps, than you are used to, but that is different from "gamist" mindset. Perhaps you are opposed to having a large amount of rules.

                      I have to question a certain line. "Then players start to think that they have to do something because they have an obsession." Yes, that's kind of the idea. Its called a plot hook. Characters having individual needs and drives that push them to do more than passively respond to a GM story, usually a combat threat, is usually considered a good thing by most people. Obsessions are really nothing more than a glorified Aspiration, and aspirations are just character goals by another name.

                      You said that every action is a consequence of a mechanic rather than "reason you started playing." People all play for different reasons and get different things out of the game. And I think its awesome that you get xp for playing for the reason you started playing? If I want to play an occult detective, isn't it awesome that I'm getting xp for doing that instead of just xp for fighting monsters? And if I want to get xp for fighting monsters, I can do that too. I think you've fallen prey to the Stormwind Fallacy. You can both get strong, and have fun roleplaying. In fact, the system is designed to encourage both at the same time. That's pretty cool.

                      And, yes, I think Social Maneuvering is fun too.

                                                      I must admit when I went from 1e to 2e I was disappointed with how much fiddlier the system had become, and just how dense that rulebook is, especially as I was dealing with players totally new to nWoD. However, certain updates, Aspirations and Obsessions in particular, or the clarity of focus in Arcana, really quite pleased me and I stuck with the system. And I think the big problem is not that the rules don't lend themselves to roleplay, they absolutely do, it's just that it's not clear how you're meant to use those rules in story. Honestly it's taken me some time to realise that the game relies on proactive players and Aspirations are meant to be ways to encourage players to drop in their own scenes, rather than merely a list of "to-do" items the ST is meant to incorporate into the script of the plot, or that Mysteries are just "mystical things your characters look into" rather than "esoteric equivalent of a whodunnit." Environmental Tilts to me are really the equivalent of traps in a D&D dungeon, they're environmental set dressing that influence mechanics and often problems that need direct solving. If you don't want to focus on environment, you don't have to think too hard about them.

                                                      I was talking to one of my players about this. They said Mage 2e was absolutely not their thing given its density and myriad rules, except they were still having a great time playing with it because I listened to what bits they found interesting and let them play with that. I think that's the core of it, is realising that Mage is meant to be more of a sandbox game taking place over weeks at a time. If players need Willpower they can come up with a scene where they act out a Vice, and you can portray those consequences. Ditto they can take time out to work on an Aspiration or an Obsession. They want to get something from Order contacts, great, that's a scene you can tie into a sequence as they're called back for favours. Indeed, realising that it's meant to be played over narrative rather than immediate time lets social maneuvering make actual sense, because you can let players build up relationships over time, rather than "passing through, can I use your Hallow?" But that realisation has honestly been a big conceptual breakthrough for how I ST my game.

                                                      What I will say I don't like about Mage 2e is that it really, truly, requires players to be totally au fait with the lore, meaning they have to sit down with all those books to get a firm grip on what they want to do. There's no easy way for an ST to dump the required info into their laps without it breaking immersion, or to suggest ideas to them without feeling like you're railroading them.
