    To be honest, I'm not sure why the Ladder gets such a strong reaction. Mages existing brings up the exact same concerns; that people with unanswerable power will potentially use it in corrupt, selfish, or downright cruel ways. The Ladder is just a technocratic response to this situation and the only one that actually managed to set up a legal code and system to punish those who do act irresponsibly or maliciously.

    Maybe I'm just a different sort of person but we already live in a world where the powerful have undeniable amounts of privilege and control over the poor and vulnerable. The horror already exists that someone could threaten, assault, or even kill you and get off with a slap on the wrist at worst; they just have to be a musician or perhaps a wealthy teen who claim that being privileged denied them an understanding of responsibility. We're only just out of an era where the sister of a President of the United States was lobotomized to the mental state of a two-year-old because she was "troublesome" for the family. This, all without the shadowy cabals of sorcerers who do exist in the World of Darkness and who can already wield their power to whatever end they wish.

    The Silver Ladder at least promises - and somewhat manages - a level of noblesse oblige as a part of their mandate, and comes with the unspoken guarantee that, since anyone can Awaken, the top echelons of their hierarchy will be diverse and drawn from a range of backgrounds and perspectives. We can't even get that in government now and the developed world has practiced democracy for how many centuries?

    The Silver Ladder is horrifying because a world where there are unequal power dynamics is horrifying; because power is always and inevitably abused after being granted or acquired, be it in a day or in a century. We just already live in such a world, though, and the Ladder has to be compared to that pre-existing state of affairs. It can't just be thought of in isolation.

    Or maybe I'm just an apologist for technocratic autocracy, who knows.

                For me, the saving grace of the Ladder - what seperates them from common-or-garden oligarchs - is their Obsession with social mobility. In our world, and even more so in the World of Darkness, we have countless people who could soar so high if they could only escape the circumstances of their birth. "American Dream" to the contrary, if you're poor but have the potential to be president or a heart surgeon you'll most likely languish in a dead-end job and die without ever realizing it. Our politicians bleat about opportunities and hold the vanishing exceptions up as proof that the system works, but really? Your average blue collar worker is a slave in all but name.

                The nature of Awakening is a near-random stroke of extreme good luck. The Ladder can't do anything about that (much as they honestly do want to) but that institutionalized inequality and static social class? They have that in their crosshairs, and every Illuminated cult is a weapon against it.

                They're just also paternalistic shitheads.

                                    The talk about the Seers actually reminds me of an old ethics professor of mine who liked to talk a lot about how much easier it is to get into unethical situations by making decisions that are tempting but not immediately unethical. The man worked as a news room manager for about three decades and, in that time, saw a lot of people succumb to temptation.

                                    If you've built yourself up and manage to work your way to an anchor position, you might get offered 80,000 a year to work on some regional public network. There, you know that you have a mandate to provide actual news. Or you can take the 500,000 a year offer to go work for Fox, and I don't think I need to detail what that entails. The problem is, no-one at the time of the offer tells you precisely what you're going to have to do. You know what you'll have to do, it's certainly implied, but it's not in your face at that moment; only the money is. You get to become rich and famous instantly, with a guarantee of being able to publish book after book about your experiences; seeing the world, traveling to interesting places, and receiving a thousand and one kickbacks and rewards for your dutiful service. Your bosses will monitor your social media feeds, you'll be required to live your life a certain way, only use advertiser brands... But you'll be rich beyond the dreams of avarice and live a life that is exciting and interesting, albeit devoid of integrity.

                                    If someone said to a journalist "I'll give you all that to compromise on this story", I imagine very few would agree. But when it's only an implication - a thought for you to deal with in the future - most will take the offer. If they didn't, we wouldn't have Fox, the Daily Mail, the Sun network...

                                    Humans prefer the immediate over the long term. Seers aren't told up front "We'll give you ten grand if you go psychically torture those homeless people". They're given the rewards first; shown the glitz and glamour. When the office job begins, it's mostly benign. When the first real test of their integrity comes, the good things are already there and the bad things wait threateningly on the edges. Easier to stomp someone else's neck than put your own in the noose.

                                    ---

                                    But while I'm rambling, might as well share another point of view here: There's another major Seer motivator that's brought up periodically in the books but not ever really discussed and it's an important one when discussing the Silver Ladder: Fear.

                                    Power is not something people like to share. Willingly agreeing to give others power over yourself is the hardest thing to do. We don't like the idea of binding ourselves; of lashing ourselves to the wills of others. We ostensibly accept institutions that do so but usually only when they're out of sight. The thought of hearing people debate and vote on a bill in Congress or Parliament, or being close to an armed police officer, makes many people squirm, because these are both institutions which - albeit ostensibly serving them - have power over the individual which they have very little immediate ability to act against.

                                    Seers are jealous of power. They want control. They want the ability to orchestrate the world around them. Other people who are empowered in any way are a threat to that. The weaker everyone else is, the stronger the Seer. Every new Awakening is a new threat; a new godling challenging the Seer's supremacy. Keeping others down protects their hegemony; keeps them under the thumb of the Exarchs.

                                    Thearchs walk a fine line in trying to do the opposite; to place an equal yoke around all necks. The Lex Magicka is impressive for the simple fact that it is a collective yielding of power by the Awakened to the polity, rather than maintaining absolute sovereignty. This doesn't seem so impressive to those of us who live under a government that is simply assumed to power by the weight of history, but every new Awakened is a sovereign individual; one that can ultimately likely do whatever they wish without ever fearing restriction. Joining that framework is a choice. That so many choose to do so must be a sign of the impressive power of the Lex and the Consilium system.

                                    It's as impressive a test of someone's nature when they're willing to give up power, perhaps as much so as what they do with it while they have it.

                                                            Originally posted by Neall View Post

                                                            You might as well ask how a defense lawyer with a strong sense of ethics defends someone they suspect is a murderer, or a corporation they know full well is perpetrating a fraud. They do what they can to safeguard their Wisdom, they pride themselves on their excellent work, they tell themselves they didn't make the world this way, and they take home their check.
                                                            As a commercial litigator who's also engaged in a great deal of public interest work, the defense counsel analogy is inapt (and my pet peeve when lawyers are scapegoats or examples of greed and corruption).

                                                            Representing a murderer or "evil" company is not a lapse in ethics and is not justified by lame excuses about "how the world works" or anything for a paycheck. The USA and most Western countries have an explicitly designed adversarial system of criminal and civil justice. Everyone is entitled to criminal defense and representation by counsel. Each lawyer is supposed to advocate only their client's interests, and any failure to do so is actually considered a serious breach of professional responsibility that could cost an attorney his or her license to practice law.

                                                            To use the example of defending a murderer, I would first note it's usually the job of underpaid and overworked public defenders who, as a general matter, tend to hold decidedly liberal and progressive notions of social equality and economics. Under our system of justice, it's not enough to know your client may have committed the acts of which he's accused. Guilt is tested by the State's ability to prove it under accepted rules and procedures at trial. Despite what you may have seen on television, strict rules prevent lawyers from allowing perjury and other misconduct. Matters like innocent until proven guilty, a trial by one's peers, cross-examination of witnesses, clear rules of evidence, etc. are designed to protect people from oppression and tyranny, not promote it. Are there injustices, and are some attorneys better than others? Absolutely. However, these discrepancies exist with any system designed and implemented by imperfect humans. Like democracy as a form of government, such systems may be the worst form of justice, except for all the others.
