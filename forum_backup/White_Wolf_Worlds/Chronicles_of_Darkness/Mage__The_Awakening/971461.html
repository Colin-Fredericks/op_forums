    Emphasizing ambiguity and horror: Gecko's currently unnamed Awakening

    09-17-2016, 03:31 PM
    I've been wondering if there was a way to accomplish my goals for Mage: The Transgression on a smaller scale, by slightly hacking and nudging Awakening. The primary goal isn't just to make it more fun or flavorful or mad science-y, but to really make it feel like a personal horror game about broken monsters. The most important thing is, what would be the best way to run or hack Mage in order to emphasize the ambiguity and horror of the setting? To really make the players question everything, and to create the genuine possibility that a lot of the principles of the setting are either false or much more complicated.

    Here are my goals/ideas so far:

    • The main thing I want to do is present being Awakening as a cursed, monstrous condition rather than a blessing or a "higher state" of evolution, make it so that "Awakened" and "Sleepers" are just terms Mages use to feel better about themselves, and close to or more than half of Mages wish secretly or openly that they were normal humans again.
    • Paradox and Obsessions are both much more dangerous and punishing.
    • Archmastery is equivalent to becoming a True Fae- the Mage becomes an utterly inhuman entity.
    • Much of the setting's assumptions are ambiguous or wrong, and the possibility exists that much of what Mages believe is true is true because their disruptive presence in reality forces it to be true.
    • Moral ambiguities of the main conflict are emphasized. The Seers have a genuinely good case for why they're right.
    • As Mages gain power and lose Wisdom, they must take Bans and Banes related to how they can perform magic, like other supernatural creatures.
    • Crossover possibilities are enhanced, with Mage supremacy being actively fought with nerfs.
    • The subjective nature of the Supernal is emphasized, as well as the possibility that it's essentially a shared delusion that Mages can force into reality. Players only have to pick one of their Path's Arcana, and choose any other as their second ruling Arcana. Experiences of the Supernal realms are vastly different and subjective.
    • Some Mages have no Path. They are stereotyped as being more likely to become Scelesti or Banishers, but the truth of this assumption is questionable.
    • A significant and ever-increasing number of Mages Awaken as Gifted Scientists from the Mage Chronicler's Guide.
    • Presumably other stuff I'll think of later.

    Feedback and suggestions are welcome.

      Here're some more:

      • Many Mages deliberately try to avoid gaining power, because gaining power inevitably removes the Mage more and more from humanity. Obsessions get stronger, Bans and Banes develop, and ultimately the Mage risks becoming an utterly inhuman Archmaster.
      • Mages are stuck between the risk of higher power and the painful compulsion to seek it- their Obsessions constantly force them to delve into paranormal phenomena and seek knowledge, no matter how much they try to resist.
      • The Nimbus grows more powerful as a Mage gains in power as a representation of their growing distance from humanity, acting similarly to the Wake from Leviathan.
      • It's quite hard to effect powerful supernatural creatures, and some are immune to direct uses of magic entirely. Creativity and problem-solving are key.
      • More pessimistic Mages prefer the terms "Hollow" or "Open" to "Awakened", because they more accurately describe what happens. A Mage blows a hole in his soul and desperately fills it with chunks of occult symbols, grabbing onto a watchtower to survive having their mind cast into primordial chaos. The "hollow" nature of Mages is why they're so sensitive to the occult and potent with it: they have "open space" with which to connect directly with the supernatural. Obsessions play into this, too: every Mage feels a deep and aching pain, as if they are missing something precious, and Obsessions are an attempt to fill that hole.
      • The factions now have more of a resemblance to the Covenants in Vampire- they primarily exist to help Mages justify and survive their cursed state.
      • The Silver Ladder is sort of equivalent to the Invictus, using elaborate hierarchies and rituals to manage their condition. They try to frame the Awakened state as a honorable burden, something given only to the worthy. Members of this group are most likely to be Lemurians- those who are trying to bring back a lost magical golden age.
      • The Mysterium seek knowledge with a fervor that surpasses that of other Mages. Some seek it for its own sake, while others look for the secrets of the world in the hope of regulating or removing their condition. They study the past to learn from it. Most historically significant Mages, after all, are primarily famous as cautionary tales.
      • The Guardians of the Veil know just how dangerous the supernatural is, especially the volatile powers of their own kind. They dedicate themselves completely to protecting the Whole not just from direct menaces but from knowledge that might lead them down the same self-destructive path the Hollow tread.
      • The Adamantine Arrow are not simply about warfare or strength. They focus on control and refinement as a means of honing mind and body to withstand the ravages of the Awakened condition. Many are in some way ascetic, seeking enough control to keep the fires raging inside them under control.
      • The Free Council are no longer the primary technomages at all. Every order has a fair proportion of scientifically-focused mages and Gifted Scientists. The Council's connection to mortal occult traditions is much more important to them, because their focus is connection: remaining in contact with the mortal world and humanity so as to avoid drifting away from it.
      • Hm, maybe The Hollow Chronicle is a good name for it.
      • No player is allowed to make a character inspired by Dr. Who or Benedict Cumberbatch's Sherlock. Any character who qualifies as a sarcastic british man is to be subject to close ST scrutiny, and is only allowed a maximum of three quips per game.

      ...you know, I just realized that Mage rubs me the wrong way not because I have a problem with power fantasies but because I've always identified with monsters so much, and seeing something so prominent in my favorite game about monsters that's so...un-monstrous is jarring. Mages just seem like...the cool kids, the effortlessly popular ones who sail through life while the rest of us are scraping around in the dirt.

                                                          Originally posted by Geckopirateship View Post

                                                          Well, being Awakened is a monstrous Condition, and "Awakened" and "Sleepers" are just terms that Mages made up. And I would argue that most Mages do wish to be human, so much so that they are almost all in pretty deep denial about the fact that they're monsters. If you want to play Mages who are fully aware of their monstrosity, you could just play as Banishers. But, if you really want the Awakened to lament their Awakened natures, that's an easy thing to accomplish. Just put that in your setting document and bam, done.

                                                          Originally posted by Geckopirateship View Post
                                                          • Paradox and Obsessions are both much more dangerous and punishing.
                                                          Obsessions aren't punishing as part of a general 2e design philosophy of mechanically encouraging player behavior with carrots, not sticks. By the fluff, Obsessions are something Mages are constantly plagued by and can only resist with great effort and discipline. Mechanically, you award Beats for pursuing your Obsessions so that players are encouraged to pursue them. If you want to add a stick, I suppose you could make players have to spend willpower or succeed a Resolve + Composure roll or something to resist an opportunity to pursue an Obsession. As for Paradox, Paradox is pretty dangerous already. It's more dangerous to reality than it is to the Mage herself, and that's part of the horror of Mage. Every Paradox is your fault, for knowingly using more magic than you could safely handle. Now you have a choice, take responsibility for that mistake and suffer resistant damage and potentially a Condition, or force that responsibility on the world around you, keeping your own body and soul safe but damaging reality itself in the process and risking your spell twisting into something truly dangerous in ways you didn't intend it to be. If you insist on making it harsher though, just adjust the Paradox dice by Gnosis progression.
