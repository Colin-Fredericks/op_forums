        Originally posted by Axelgear View Post
        I certainly wouldn't consider it an obsessive inflexibility if a mortal found out a vampire was feeding on their friends and neighbours and set to kill the bloodsucker; I'd consider it a bit of a lizard brain mentality but ultimately not an inappropriate reaction.
        Once more with feeling:

        Obsessive inflexibility of driving focus is, in fact, one of the defining qualities of most inhuman monsters in the setting. Most gameline-level PCs get ringside tickets to what happens when you smush that trait up against a more rounded humanlike personality and wait for the former to overwhelm the latter.
        Your Obsessions are not your character and your character is not defined by their Obsessions, yes.

        Your Obsessions are, however, that part of your character's makeup that is most like the psychology-or-what-passes-for-it of monsters that have little care for self-control or the consequences of their actions, and they are the part that comes into the most regular prominence among mages as their Wisdom degenerates.

        I am not saying that mages are inflexibly obsessive. I am saying that obsession is the inflexible element of a mage that brings them into alignment with monstrosity.

        Nitpicking about the intensity of my Long-Term Nimbus examples does nothing to change my point that a mage's Nimbus means they are an addict that attracts their drug of choice to them just as surely as a vampire or a werewolf living in a place occupied by other entities is going to have their respective urges to hunt for blood or secure their territory tested against their unwillingness to do those things. A low-Wisdom mage with a poorly-compartmentalized Shadow Name (if they even have one) is going to be running into little curiosities everywhere in the course of their mundane life, some of which will hurt people they care about simply by virtue of some of those people being Sleepers and some of those mysteries being Supernal or Abyssal.

        Babbling about typhoid just establishes that you've been paying zero attention to the definition of "monster" being used. You're welcome to disagree with it, but it is useless to claim that mages aren't monsters in a context where "monster" means, at bare minimum, "occupies a framework of supernatural urges and involvement" just because not all humans shy away from the supernatural and a mage who deliberately does nothing with their power over the things that are drawn to them could conceivably exist. Having typhoid does not inherently give you the ability to detect and control typhoid. A banshee is a monster, and all she does is make a noise when someone is about to die.

        If you refuse to accept that classification scheme, fine, but understand that if you proceed in the discussion without engaging with it then you're talking past the point.

                I think that Mages tend towards obsession very strongly, but not all of them obsess in the same way that you'd usually categorise it. Like, the Interfector in the core book fiction. What's his obsession? It's possible that he has an obsession in a mystical sense, a tangible phenomena that he wishes to solve, but it seems to me that his obsession is to do a good job as that is all he has left. And is that really an obsession? Doesn't feel like it is, in the usual sense. It's what gets him out of bed in the morning, but it consumes him because there's nothing else there, perhaps. And Mage needs to let me play Outis, clearly.

                That's why I like that there's no negative reinforcement to not going after your Obsessions, other than a lack of personal development. Mage's are not instinctively driven like Vampires or Werewolves, neither should they be. If a Mage harms someone in the pursuit of their obsession, then they have no fall back of an inhuman drive to blame. That's all on them. But really, if a Werewolf goes into Kuruth and accidentally kills someone, it's not the same. They are responsible. But at the same time, no Werewolf can truly control their rage, and therefore they can be argued not fully be responsible for it (indeed, they can be argued to be victims in their own way of the Rage).

                Mages are monstrous to me in the same ways that humans are. That is, it's either a meaningless and entirely subjective statement or a poetically rich description of some people's actions depending on how you look at it. Mages have obsessions on their character sheet to reinforce the aesthetic and tone of the game, and if they don't follow them, they don't die or fall apart or whatever. But it is how Mages develop their power. And that's cool, because it gives an incentive to follow your character's obsessions. It's as easy for a Mage to be a good person as it is for the rest of us. They can benefit from not being a good person, just like the rest of us. And many Mages find it hard to rise above their desires, just like the rest of us. Sometimes those desires are admirable! Sometimes not.

                The difference between Humans and Mages is that the implications of their choice to indulge or deny are more far-reaching, due to their power. And that's scary, I suppose, in the sense of being a bystander to a Mage's obsession.

                But I think it's key to remember that a Mage doesn't have to be a monster. A Vampire or a Werewolf is, by it's nature, but not a Mage. Which is why the monstrosity of some Mages is all the more tragic and damning. They could have chosen a better way, but did not. I could hate a Mage for the effects of their blind obsession far more than I could hate a Werewolf for being unable to control it's rage. One is a condition, the other, a choice. And that's cool. Mages get the chance to be pretty bright heroes! Many of them are total bastards, though.

                        Originally posted by Moinen View Post
                        A question for you guys is "How human are mages in your chronicle?"
                        100% human, no question.about it They may be monsters, but that doesn't make them inhuman monsters. The Major explains it perfectly:


                        The reason why mages don't crumble when faced with too much trauma is not because of their inhumanity, but rather due to human's capacity to adapt. Humans in the CofD live in a world filled with monsters that constantly prey on them, so would it not make sense that they would bounce back? That they explore this horrifying world? That's what humans do! Mountains are there to climb. So humans adapt, Awakening to the Supernal to fulfill their potential in a world much harsher than ours, gaining power that far exceeds anything else, gram for gram (even demons and mummies, because creativity is the most potent weapon in any TRPG bar none).

                        Does that make CofD a little Mage-centric? Yes, it does, and it should be so. Why? Because past all the fluff, vampires at their heart signify our greed and lust. Werewolves signify our primal savagery. Changelings signify our paranoia and madness. Prometheans signify our instinct to reject outsiders. Each timeless monster signifies an aspect(s) of our darker nature, that's the whole point of horror to begin with. So what do mages signify?

                        They signify the best and worst aspect of humanity: potential. They are the dark lords that wield the same power as the hero does, forming classic villains like Darth Vader and Voldemort that stick in our hearts for the rest of our lives. Mages are us in every respect, making them the greatest of monsters. Vampires may have greed and lust, but so do mages. Werewolves may have primal savagery, but so do mages. There's simply nothing another monster can do that a mage can't, because mages have been formed from the very core of humanity itself.

                        That's my take on it, anyway.

                                A couple of nitpicks:

                                1. mages don't have to obsess in the same way that a character whose Vice is Lust doesn't have to lust. True, the mechanics are all-carrot/no-stick; but lustful character who isn't played as being lustful is being improperly roleplayed.
                                2. Technically, Prometheans don't signify our instinct to reject outsiders; the torch-wielding mobs do. What the Prometheans themselves signify is immaturity; poor impulse control.

                                As for what negative characteristic mages signify? Hubris; pride, arrogance. A tendency to assume that the world revolves around you. Unrestrained Will.

                                To me, hunters are the representatives of human potential; mages go beyond human potential, with every Attainment distancing a mage from the normal human experience. It's a game about transhumans and the dangers of transhumanism — and one of those dangers is a general lack of fear.

                                The differences between Integrity and Wisdom are often subtle; but they're very real, and very profound. I generally wouldn't classify mages as monstrous (although they tend to have a disturbing sense of superiority that can easily lead to monstrous actions); but I'd definitely classify them among the monsters.
