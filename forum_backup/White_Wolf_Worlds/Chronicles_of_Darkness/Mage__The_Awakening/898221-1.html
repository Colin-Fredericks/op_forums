                    The Ashavan

                    In ancient times there was an order of Persian willworkers known as the Karpani. Keepers of otherwise forgotten mystical traditions predating the revelations of Zarathustra, these magi were vilified by the prophet himself in the Gāthās, the oldest of the Zoroastrian scriptures. Accused of harming livestock, inflicting pains on the innocent, and influencing the good toward evil deeds, the Karpani vehemently opposed Zarathustra during his lifetime. Yet as time went on, the Karpani came to recognize the wisdom in the prophet's teachings, and by the time of Alexander's conquest of the Achaemenid Empire they had adopted much of the philosophy of the Avesta alongside their own occult practices, even if they never truly considered themselves followers of the Good Religion.


                    In the great mystic melting-pot of the Hellenistic World, many of the Karpani were counted among the founders of the Diamond Orders, particularly the Guardians of the Veil and the Pancryptiates, while others, particularly among the more martially-inclined Artestars, would ultimately be seduced by the nascent Seers of the Throne. Yet those most faithful to the culture that birthed their order remained aloof from either group, their loyalties and interests lying with their sleeper kinsmen. Indeed, as conflicts flared between mages in the era of the Diadochi, the remaining independent Karpani turned their back on Awakened politics altogether. Most of these independent Karpani belonged to a moralistic faction known as the Yashtipati, "masters of the Yashts (hymns)", but in time they adopted a new title for themselves: the Ashavan, meaning "bearers of righteousness".


                    For more than a thousand years, the Ashavan were among the most influential and widespread Nameless Orders in the world, albeit a particularly loosely-organized one. Opposing the excesses of the Seers and the Diamond Orders alike, they dedicated themselves to defending and serving their sleeper families and communities. Although ancient tradition forbade them from serving as priests themselves, they developed close ties with the Zoroastrian priesthood and used these connections to nearly monopolize the recruitment of those who awakened withing Zoroastrian congregations. In this manner they effectively dominated the magical landscape of Persia and neighboring lands through the Parthian and Sassanid eras, but the very sleeper connections that had allowed them to be so successful would prove their undoing.


                    The rise of Islam didn't put an end to the influence of Zoroastrianism overnight, nor did it immediately spell the end for the Ashavan, but following the Arab conquest of the Sassanid empire both went into decline. In the case of the Ashavan, this decline was helped along by rival orders of magi, particularly the Seers of the Throne, who were quick to capitalize on the worst impulses of the Muslim ruling class in order to eliminate the Order and its sleeper allies. Never a truly unified power to begin with, the power of the Ashavan was soon broken, and Persia once more became the battleground of foreign magi.


                    Yet just as Zoroastrianism survives to the present day, so too do the Ashavan. A tiny order mostly affiliated with conservative Parsi families in the Mumbai, though a few are thought to have emigrated with their sleeper kin to North America. Their efforts to avoid contact with other mages has given many the mistaken impression that they are hermits or "Zoroastrian monks" of some kind. This, however, reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of the faith; active moral participation in the world is a core element of Zarathustra's message, and the Ashavan embody this by dedicating themselves to spreading good thoughts, good words, and good deeds within their communities. This isn't to say they're all saints - they're as vulnerable to hubris as any magi - but the teachings of the Order attempt to instill a strong sense of moral duty in every new initiate. They tend to use their magic both subtly and sparingly, both in an effort to avoid hubris and because they believe that Paradox strengthens the Lie of Angra-Manyu and obscures the Truth of Ahura Mazda. Awakened of all paths are accepted by the Order, but Moros may fit less comfortably into their ranks, as any interaction with the dead is thought by the Ashavan to cause spiritual pollution.


                    Order Tools: recitation of hymns or devotional poetry, ritually pure fire and water, white ritual vestments

                                Ooh, joining in on this! Currently, running a Chronicle set in Manila, which is home to various Nameless Mages and Orders.So here goes:

                                Mga Inanak ni Hukluban (Children of Hukluban)
                                A gathering of native Acanthi and Mastigoi who worship Hukluban, an ancient Filipino goddess of black magic and witchcraft. They practice ancient blood rites and tribal witchcraft, which Manila's Consilium would consider Left-Handed - if they didn't choose to look askance. Both Seers and Pentacle mages seek out Hukluban's Children for their gift of prophecy, and ability to weave fortune or misfortune.

                                The Knife of Paradise (yep, took it from HtV)
                                The Knife (an imperfect translation of 'kris', a local dagger-like sword) are a small group of Awakened who watch over Manila's Muslim quarter. Almost all have roots in the Consilia of Marawi and Zamboanga. Its leader is an Obrimos who parses gnostic language from the Qur'an. Explorers and scholars, they sometimes find common cause with the local Mysterium when their goals align.

                                Vox Populi
                                Vox Populi are modernist mages involved in Manila's vast call center industry. They see BPOs as a modern human phenomenon, and offshore sites as nexuses of information where they can study Sympathies. Not surprisingly, Mastigoi are the most respected within Vox Populi followed by Obrimoi.
