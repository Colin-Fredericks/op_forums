    I'm New To STing And Will Run Mage 2ED Soon. Any Important Advice?

    12-10-2016, 04:54 PM
    It's an IRL game with only one player. The city is London. The PC my friend created is a seasoned professor of psychology at Oxford. His Virtue is Rational and his Vice is Manipulative. I want to start with him as a mortal and role play his Awakening, but my friend wants him to become a Mastigos, and I'm having a hard time understanding that Path. The PC will later join the Mysterium.

    I would like advice on how to describe his Mystery Play, and how to narrate the process of being discovered by and joining the Mysterium. Any tips on low level Mysteries for the first sessions are appreciated as well.

    Bye.

      Ok, first off I'll give the elevator pitches of the Mastigos path listed on the other thread:
      Mastigoi awaken from inside. Their Watchtower sits fixed in fire and iron and is surrounded by daemonic creatures. They have dominion over the expanses of Mind and Space. Mastigos awakenings deal with trying to break out of the prison of Pandemonium.

      Mastigoï awaken to the screaming of their own minds. They command Mind and Space, arcanas of connection and they realize separation is the greatest lie ever told. They are lords of the immaterial, twisting desires and sympathies to their whim. Some summon their own shackled sins to their aid.

      Mastigos: This character is all too aware of his flaws, something he knows everyone has. This makes him humble and tolerant; he doesn't judge his worth or that of others by their flaws. He also tends to see those flaws as challenges that can make people stronger, but there will always be another flaw.

      Mastigos
      The Mastigos is a Mage who looks at the world through the eyes of relationships. Everyone lies to each other, putting up outward facades while their inner life is in turmoil. They turn away from each other and themselves, refusing to acknowledge necessary truths, which poisons their community. The Mastigos say that, “Distance is a Lie.” They forcibly create bridges where there were walls, and relish in the growing pains that come with having to come face-to-face with both others and oneself.

      And there is also what the book itself has to say:

      Magic is the soul’s journey between Temperance and the Devil,
      through the maze of all desires.
      We Awaken to the reality of our transgressions to confront our joys
      and sins. Your true self is a demon, trapped in an Iron Gauntlet.
      Release it, and it can take you anywhere.

      Mastigos master a world ruled
      by desire, thought, and perception.
      Thought is substance. Space is
      sentiment.

      What this means is that to a mastigos, magic is literally the transformation of the soul's character on the path going back and forth between the ideals of Temperance and the Devil (unfettered will). Or in other words, magic is, to a mastigos, the back and forth character swings (not mood) of perfected, balanced restraint of yourself and your will and letting your desires take you where it will.

      The first extreme is called the psychonaut. Those that gravitate towards this extreme will cut themselves off from outside attachments, suppressing their sympathetic connections and more often than not diving into their oneiros to control themselves.

      The other extreme is called the Warlock, or taboo breakers. They manipulate sympathetic connections to bring their desires to closer to fruition.

      All in all, the Mastigos is a fairly esoteric and arcane path. Mind magic is capable of achieving quite a few ends and Space can take you just about anywhere. For anyone interested in psychology, Mind magic has plenty to offer. For those interested in twisting geometries, impossible spaces (bigger on the inside, left is right and right is down, inverted space that keeps things inside inside and things outside outside), as well as portals and teleportation, Space is what you want.

      As to the mystery play...my group didn't actually play through that part. We did very short descriptions of it (our chronicle was set in london too, though my character was the only one that awoke via a runewalk). I would say that you will want to think of a scene where people's emotions, personalities, and interpersonal connections come alive as their own beings, or demons. The pc will then interact with these creatures as they lead him towards or away from the Iron Tower (I highly recommend having some of the runes for Mind and Space available as visual aids for tatoos or artwork that these "demons" posess or point towards. It's a very evocative aid). If towards then he probably will feel resistance to following these creatures. This represents the abyss in his soul not wishing to gaze upon the supernal truths that Pandemonium would show him. If away, then he should feel a gravitation towards the tower and will need to push past these creatures to achieve his goal of reaching the tower. Either way he should most likely be resisting these creatures and overcoming them. The final part of the awakening will have him sign his name somewhere. If you are actually doing a mystery play and not an astral trip then he will see himself signing his name onto something of importance to the Iron Tower while in the phenomenal world he will actually be signing his name on something that would make sense in mundane reality. For example, while he is signing his name onto the book of residents of the Iron Tower, he could be signing a parking ticket, a credit card slip, or a check. Or perhaps a guest registry at a wedding. Or a birthday card. Whatever it is, at that point he is then a mage and supernatural mysteries are then ripe for the exploring.

      As to the Mysterium, I highly recommend picking up the first edition order book. The second and third chapters are especially useful. The second chapter will give you a decently in depth explanation of what it is the Mysterium pursue and how they pursue it. The third chapter gives lots of juicy details of internal relationships as well as the Atlantean Mystery, a special mystery that is only available to the Mysterium (it's also how Mystagogues progress in rank).

      Good early mysteries would include the pc's first astral dive into his oneiros, a weak goetic creature messing with a grad student's mind, and a door in the university library that has been Spacially veiled so as to be ignored (perhaps there is a Mysterium study room beyond the door, thus when the mage uncovers the veil he is discovered by the Mysterium?).


      Final thoughts? I would remember that a mage is far more than his arcana. Mage sight is important as are his attainments. Also, don't forget to give mundane challenges as well. A huge part of being a mage is balancing his arcane and mundane lives. And don't be afraid to give mysteries that require arcana other than Mind and Space to fully understand, thus pushing for the pc to diversify (eventually, anyways).

      Last but not least, make sure the both of you have fun. It is a game after all.

          I personally may suggest having the awakening not be where he travels astrally to the watchtower but the version where his perception of the world around him is altered. This series of what one would assume are hallucinations you could have either slowly build up or start and stay at full strength. Either way this method in my opinion would present this character because of their life and profession a chance to grown in their awakening and before they even awaken learn the delicate balance between his mortal life and the unexplainable. Of course suggest it to a play before taking to them about it. Page 60 'Waking World dream' and the surrounding section has more information on this type of awakening.

          The idea of a mental professional awakening amuses me because not just in the process of awakening but also in their new life as a mage they may have doubts about their sanity. I have no doubt that at least in the back of their mind they may think they've lost themselves to madness. For that reason I think roleplaying the 'waking world dream' style of awakening may be the best (in my opinion) way to transition from mortal to mage.

          There are also some guidelines for beginning a mage at a lower power level from Dave on these forums somewhere. I think it was 1 dot in each ruling arcana instead of the usual spread and they don't have any rotes, a starting mage character represents a mage who has underwent and completed their training which your PC will not have done. However, feel free to also start him at the standard mage creation instead.

          For how the mysterium may discover him and then be introduced there are two main ways I can seeit unfolding. The first is that they monitor (through fate magic) when a new mage awakens, they may even have some factors in this spell to ensure they are only notified of those who would be good candidates for their order. The there method I can imagine is that in the PCs exploration of their new power they draw the attention of the order, through their actions they unknowingly earn a chance to join an order. Since you want to have the character role play this all I may suggest that after their initiation into the order (1 free stars dot) they gain the standard rewards without needing to spend xp on them, a dot of occult and the high speech merit.

          I have not yet had a chance to run with the mysteries system but it is an investigation in the search of truth at its core. The mystery is some mystical event, occurrence or location. It does not have an obvious source. Each layer of obfuscation must be peeled away from this mystery. For this reason I may suggest doing more than they suggest in the 'building the mysteries' section (pg 93). I would suggest integrating the mundane system of investigation as well. The mystery they uncover may not just be in one lone location and what they learn of it at each location helps them get to the source of the mysteries truth. This is a suggestion more so investigation a mystery is more than turning on mage sight and rolling dice, yes the mundane investigation has dice rolled too but I feel it adds more to the narrative as well. Of course for a basic mystery such steps are not truly necessary but for a grand mystery it should have a grander narrative to it in my opinion. For instance a grand mystery could begin with a mage uncovering or discovering a single piece of god-machine infrastructure. It would rob a good deal of narrative impact if all it took was staring at this initial discovery to decipher the full mystery. Instead they must follow the trail that connects the many parts and overcome the obstacles that would stand in their way. Once they have overcome these obstacles and gathered all these fragmented pieces of information would they truly be able to understand the mystery and the Player be able to appreciate having solved the mystery.

                  Learn to improvise.

                  This is the general advice I give to all STs everywhere and forever. Do it, do it early, do it well. No plan survives first contact.

                  As for the questions you actually asked...

                  Mystery Play

                  Decide how sudden or prolonged the Mystery Play is going to be. They're not all the same; some Mages Awaken in a sudden flash reminiscent of Zen; where the wheels of the universe become instantaneously and transcendentally visible and, in a sudden flash, they realize sublime Truth. Most, though, Awaken over a period of days, weeks, months, or years. Even if it's not immediately obvious, many Mages may realize the first tremors of the act that shook their lives began years before the final act that sealed it; they simply didn't know it at the time.

                  For a Mastigos, Mystery Plays are frequently about crossing a boundary and the question of just what makes you who you are. It's about a struggle with confusion and uncertainty until the mind can stand above or wipe away the maze it stands in; defining itself and eliminating the Lie.

                  I recommend using the Mystery Play to highlight the character's Virtue and Vice; they are both self-defining features. An acknowledgement of both can and should be necessary to cross the bridge. Given you want them to be a mystagogue, perhaps the Mystery presents itself as a unique patient case file that comes across their desk, and from it comes a need to hunt; to chase the lead. Obstacles present themselves that force them to manipulate others to get their way, but they cannot actually unveil the final Mystery without also accepting what their rational mind tells them: That there is a supernatural aspect to this strangeness consuming their life.

                  Once they accept that this is the case, they must perform an act that proves it to themselves; wholeheartedly performing some sort of occult ritual to prove to themselves that it is real. Maybe summoning the devil that the patient in the case file says possessed them and made them commit a brutal crime?

                  That's how you become a Mastigos.

                  Discovery

                  There are honestly good odds this character is already under observation by a mystagogue. Mages can usually recognize a Mystery Play, and someone seeking and investigating some odd phenomenon will draw their attention. Unless vital, most Mages also don't get in the way of a Mystery Play; they know that getting too close can either interrupt it or get them tangled up in it. They watch from a distance and wait until they feel the explosion of magic that accompanies a new soul touching the Supernal.

                  If you prefer otherwise, though, any Mystery that attracts the newly Awakened character will also draw other Awakened and, frankly, they're a professor at a university. Mystagogues will not be far away from whatever first Mystery falls naturally into their lap.

                  Actually joining the Mysterium is outlined well in the Mysterium book itself. A far better question is the offer. Going up to a stranger and saying "Hey, want to come to my Church?" is awkward for most people. Yet that is what an offer to join an Order is. Making such an offer will always vary, depending on the person offering it and the recipient. Think of who the person making this offer is before you decide upon it and go from there.

                  First Mysteries

                  A psychologist would probably be naturally attracted to people with strange talents. The world is full of people who claim to have odd psychic powers. In the Fallen World, some of them actually do. Even if not a practicing psychologist, your player character may know people who are, hear odd stories from them or the literature, and choose to investigate.

                  What happens when a Mage finds out someone has real psychic powers? Sounds worthy of investigation, wouldn't you say?

                      Learn to plan and to prepare. Designing your game sessions with a clear idea in mind will remove the unnecessary fat, planning stuff means you know what probably will happen which means you will be prepared to run it mechanically and roleplaying-ally. Improvising will happen, but in order to improvise well, you need to prepare first. Approach designing your chronicle with dilligence, discipline and good notes-taking. By preparing a living world, beforehand, you will just have to put it in motion and see how it unfolds. In a game like Mage having the whole picture generated and understood is very important, simply because a simple Knowing spell will force you to summon data influence by tons of factors.

                          The two most valuable lessons I learned as an ST for Mage were Focus and Closure.

                          I learned that Mage was a game that required improvisation at a scale no other game had ever asked of me as a GM, and I learned that quickly. I found myself constantly injecting intrigue, mysteries and surprises around every corner -- which is fine to do, for a session. Maybe two. That's how the start of a story should generally feel, IMO, for investigative games. Beyond that point, though, I have to find a way to enforce a "No New Crap" rule. Everything should stay in the same stream; I can't have thousands of branch-off things. Both my players and I got buried under subplots, story arcs, unexplored potential threats, unresolved conflicts and un-attained goals. I have to make sure that any surprises I have planned steer me toward the conclusion of the story.

                          Because stories should end. PC's don't have to win all the time, but they need a definitive ending point to the side-plots or episodic stories. It helps focus me and my players on the stuff we find rewarding if we don't let everything spiral pointlessly out of control and branch off in a thousand directions -- and it helps more if I let mysteries, conflicts and plots resolve naturally instead of constantly throwing twists into them, or constantly revealing new layers of intrigue or surprises. I don't artificially lengthen an individual story beyond, say, the 2nd act.

                          This requires that in my improv I actually have either an active eye on those elements (Focus and closure) and, sometimes, that I actually decide what my intention for a particular antagonist, mystery or plot's scale is. Sometimes I might see an opportunity for a pitiful one-off peon enemy to become a huge game-shakingly big enemy, but I don't do that all the time. I let the little dweebs stay little dweebs. Gives me the chance to let the players get a win under their belts, so the struggle of the Main Plot can continue without them feeling overwhelmed or hopeless.

                          Storytelling is the art of pretending you totally meant for that to happen just now.

                              By the way, I also strongly suggest limiting other Mage characters at the start of the journey. Introduce new elements slowly. First, let the character interact with just the normal world, but using their magic; let them explore for a bit how does it impact their normal life. Throw a mugging at them, provide opportunities for unethical uses of their power; it's a good tutorial section (failure won't make artifacts disappear forever in maws of abyss, there is no rival mage waiting for your misstep) and it will help the player fell *different* from other people. Give them potential to go on a power trip.

                              Then, introduce something supernatural. Something small, but rewarding. Goetic daemon possessing one of their patients, a strange spacial distortion making people wait forever in the line, maybe something dangerous to "normies" but not really to the Mage. This should happen after the player and character are somewhat comfortable with using their powerset.

                              Then, you can introduce bigger and bigger slices of mystery, but don't drop any mages yet. Remember, in 2nd ED, "Magical Society" is much more than Mages - it's thousands and hundreds of misguided Sleeper cultists, Sleepwalker workers, Supernatural-Merit Mortals and so on. Let the player find one of these out, and follow it to the magical society.
                              Perfectly, this should also correspond with the character facing a problem that lies outside of their power set, which will make them want to find someone who can aid them in their thing. Meeting another Mage should be special in itself, and not something introduced lightly; using too much Mages tend to distort the game away from investigating Mysteries into playing ball with "Consilium Politics" written on it. And Mage - on - Mage conflict (not necessarily combat, but all of the magical interactions and stuffs and things) will complicate the game very, very much, as Mage-Mage interaction is incredibly complex and unintuitive. Horrors, Spirits, and other things like that are much more manageable "opponents" than having to wrap your head around 10001 magical gambits and stratagems.

                              Ideally, your Mage won't be "recruited" into the Order. After some interactions, he should *want* to join them on his own.

                                  I ran a 60 session 1e chronicle a couple years ago and my biggest piece of advice is this: Don't try to throw everything into one game.

                                              The conditions/beat system from CofD sets up a really nice feedback loop. Something dramatically interesting happens, the character gets a beat, the beats turn into Experiences, the character gets stronger. Mastigos are awesome at this. Your player will probably realize early on "hey, I can magically make myself blind for a few hours and bump into things" and you may have the instinct of "oh, they're abusing the system." They're not. Steer into it. Let them go blind, then have someone pickpocket them. Have them toss up a spell that makes it impossible to understand language, and then have the local authorities come to them with questions.
                                              I find that it's really helpful when doing 1:1 games to set up a foil for your PC. A foil is someone who highlights and contrasts the character. So, if your player wants to be a conventional Mastigos who unleashes their inner demons, maybe there's another Mastigos who wants to "awaken" everyone around them, whether they're ready or not. Or maybe your player is Manipulative in a friendly way, so make sure there are other characters who are equally manipulative of NPCs. Maybe the player asks for something really minor, just a tiny nudge with Mind magic, but it's the final straw for someone who's used to being worked over and they overreact. Look for opportunities.
                                              I've found that it's really helpful to have some physical trackers for some spell effects. I like poker chips. I have my players take one color for every spell effect they have, so we can track spell control. I use another color, usually red, to track Paradox rolls during a scene. Those rolls are going to start off trivial, but it's easy to push them. It's *especially* easy to push them once you hit Gnosis 3, but your player will probably wander into the breakpoint on their own soon enough.
                                              Finally, I don't recommend that you think of Mage as a game of trying to challenge or overwhelm your PC. No matter what obstacle you set up, they can find a way around it. Mage isn't a game about "how will you win?" It's a game about "what will you give up to win?"
