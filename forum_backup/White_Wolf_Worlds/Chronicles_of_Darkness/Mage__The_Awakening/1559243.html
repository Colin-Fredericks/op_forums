    Questions About Mysteries

    04-06-2026, 09:21 AM
    So, after reading a truly tremendous amount of Mage: The Awakening books, I decided to revive my old account on this forum, which I have not used since 2021, to ask the one place where I can be sure that people will probably have an answer of sorts for me: I don't get mysteries and I do not understand how they should work in actual play.

    That is not a question, but bear with me. Mage: The Awakening Second Edition has five location write-ups; London, Los Angeles, Salamanca, Tokyo, and Tucson. Each is attached to at least one Mystery—whether London's runewalking, Los Angeles' Astral intrusions, Salamanca's libros y puertas efimeros, Tokyo's ansho, or Tucson's Phantom Bells—which provides a central axis of gameplay, kind of like an electrified lamp provides a central axis of "moth enrichment", if you will. Page 93 of M:tAw 2e says the following:

    Any Magical Puzzle, any lingering spell, any otherworldly enigma is potentially a Mystery. The Storyteller decides on the particulars of the Mystery, which break down into three parts: Opacity, surface information, and deep information.

    It proceeds to then define Opacity and surface information, before defining deep information, on pg. 94, as:

    Deep information is the truth of the Mystery, the intent, the Supernal resonance. This information only reveals itself if the mage unravels the Mystery's Opacity and then succeeds in a Revelation.

    Now, this is not super confusing, of course, but in the Storytelling chapter, under the section "Making Mysteries" (pg. 293), the book notes that:

    As a Storyteller crafting a Mystery for your players to solve, beginning at the end and working backward is a good way to start. If you're going for a more traditional "find the clues, sort it out" style of game, the easiest and perhaps most important thing you can do is have a clear image in your head of exactly what happened in the moment of death, theft, or other mysterious event. When a cabalmate's daughter went missing, in the exact moment, what happened? Who was there? What time? Where exactly? The more of these details you have in mind, the easier it will be to work backward even as your players do.

    Later sentences are consistent with this language of "crime"-style Mysteries, noting that "As you've decided on your scene, you know what's happened, so you know there's blonde hair to find. A car to identify. A witness to track down who saw something he wasn't supposed to." and that "The real secret to running an amazing mystery with mages is this: Don't decide who did ahead of time."

    Now, obviously, from the lack of capitalization, it is clear that this is talking about mysteries more broadly, rather than just capital-M Mysteries, but it appears to me that the advice given in this section is, nonetheless, still not directly helpful for the kinds of Mysteries generally presented that mages find themselves dealing with. Of course, such advice is useful for investigating a particular Abyssal intrusion, or similar, but the more broad Mysteries presented in the different location write-ups seem to me, like they would leave me asking what the actual investigation would look like? Am I doing research here, like in Ars Magica where I am trying to figure out the underlying mechanics of a strange magical phenomenon, or are those examples more meant as plot generators, with the Mysteries being, rather, the plots they generate? Am I taking this too literally or missing something incredibly obvious?

    To help me figure out an answer to this, I also took a look at Signs of Sorcery, which expands on Mysteries and, indeed, has a dedicated section to building them. Here, more examples are provided on pg. 29:

    The Hierarch is dead, and the only lead is the rapidly-fading remnant of the spell that killed him. People are compelled to fling their most prized possessions into a deep pool where some forgotten god slumbers. Murder rates have spiked, but is it the summer heat wave or poisonous Loci?

    In this case, examples 1 and 3 seem more immediately actionable to me in terms of the guidelines provided in the corebook, though example 2 still seems a little difficult for me to envision as a Mystery. Again, here the question to me is exactly what a player character would be "investigating", if that makes sense? Are they trying to find out the mechanics of how the forgotten god does this? Do they want to know what it wants them for? Why this is happening? The example of the temporally erased archmage is also very helpful, but I am still ultimately left somewhat uncertain about how to structure, create, and set up these kinds of more "research"-like Mysteries rather than the more intuitively obvious whodunnit-style magical investigation-type Mysteries. As such, after a truly unnecessary amount of words, my question is basically, what is the advice of experienced people here in regards to structuring and setting up these kinds of mysteries, and making them interesting?

    Kindly thank you all in advance, and my apologies for the excessive words. I only ask with so many words, because I am preparing a chronicle, and finding myself somewhat challenged in this regard.

      Well, for start i assure you that, at least for me, the concept of a Narration based on Mysteryes is not easy to figure out.
      You could be inclined to consider it like Detective stories, investigations but with supernatural theme and powers.
      It would not be a wrong approach, but for me it s simply not enough, not for an entire chronicle.
      As a premise, i use a narration style in which i know exactly in advance what happened but not how the player would discovery it, clues are here sure but are not set in stone.
      My approach for a Mystery generally is:
      -I give to the Players a reasonable motivation to come in contact of it
      -First contact, a superficial examination of a supernatural phenomenon that doesn t work as it should, or a seemingly mundane event with no "rational" explanation
      -Players use abilities (skill, mage sight, spells, other actions) to examine various element of the scene.
      -The players, sometimes with helps from npc formulate hypotesies and try to verify them.
      -I add complications, to make things more difficult but still funny, like someone who tries to obscure the truth, a fight with a strange hostile entity, or an npc that reacts in an unpredictable way.
      -Resolving complication leads to the correct hypotesy.
      -If possible now that the player knows they must made a choice, often a difficult one, on what to do with this truth

      All of this keeping in mind that all of this should be described as something marvellous.
      Mages sees thing that a normal human could only imagine.
      Enrich every situation with unusual and stunning details.

      Make the player feel powerful but not omniscent.
      They are the protagonists, they bring change in the game world but they should not think it s a static world.

      I hope it helps.

          I feel like either I'm having difficulty parsing the core of the question or I'm just kind of floundering at the open-endedness of it, but to try and address one of the noted disjunctions, I have a general ludonarrative principle and a comparison with some other gamelines to draw:

          1. While technically any supernatural phenomenon can be an object of study for the Awakened, this is nevertheless a storytelling game, and thus if you as a Storyteller are planning to put in the players' path as part of the plot for a given story (or chronicle), the "crime scene" approach exists because it behooves you to make sure there's a compelling narrative reason for a given Mystery to come into play.

          2. Deviant's systems for forming a conspiracy emphasize that establishing a secret society of the sort that pursues the Remade often entails tasks that are distasteful, unethical, or immoral. Werewolf's treatment on lodges is plain that, in addition to serving as the connective tissue of werewolf society between the local scope of packs and the global/philosophical scope of tribes, joining a mystery cult dedicated to a particular facet of the Hunt comes with secrets of the dirty variety along with the magical ones (and only some of that can be attributed to the greater integration with spiritual "society"). Demon's primary antagonistic force uses mundane actors whenever it doesn't require a magical solution and its selection bias for re-integrating rogue elements is toward those who will betray anyone and anything else for it at the drop of a hat. Vampire is a whole-ass game about playing as a magical being who does institutional corruption as a survival strategy, and its least politically-inclined covenant still needs resources and advances its occult research by methods that explicitly risk the humanity of the ones who perform them.

          The way the setting works, any given Mystery may connect to a magical actor as a target of their agenda or a consequence of their actions, and many of those actors are liable to be bastards by simple virtue of the survivorship bias of how the setting is deliberately engineered. While mages can get by on general obsessive curiosity, the Mysteries that will keep the ball rolling are those that will cause problems to pursue, even if only because it leads to learning things it does the character no good to know.

                          To be entirely clear on that last one, I agree with you in general that Mysteries should ideally have some kind of fallout and lead to juicy drama and players into trouble, I am just mainly interested in seeing some advice regarding, for example, a scenario like the forgotten god slumbering beneath a pool, mentioned in Signs of Sorcery, or deciphering an ancient grimoire written in a language that never was, etc. and how these might be made fun stories for players to engage with.
                          Not having had much actual play experience with Mage specifically, I can unfortunately only speak from a theoretical perspective as a general dabbler-and-diver into Chronicles as a whole, though given that the Changeling game I'm running sort of extemporaneously spun together something I've built into that would probably qualify as a Mystery with some work, it is my hope that as a minor game design hobbyist with a writing-related degree the following is also helpful:

                          Kurt Vonnegut's rules of creative writing indicate that every character should want something, even if it's just a glass of water. Changeling 2e's advice on creating Goblin Contracts starts with "Imagine a problem. Now imagine a goblin. How does he solve it?" In the inverse phenomenon of how the God-Machine's operational biases work (and perhaps a banner example of the priorities of certain Deviant conspiracies), the light novel series Occultic;Nine has the answer to "why were a large number of people compelled to walk into a lake and drown themselves?" tie into an attempt to scam rich people with the promise of immortality to get access to their finances. The Tremere writeup in Night Horrors: Nameless and Accursed describes their hunger — the great hunger of magic — as "the entropy that creates beauty."

                          Even if a Mystery isn't the direct product of a malicious actor, any potential value to be exploited in it, any potential harm it can do, and any sort of wonder to be derived from its lack of mundanity mean someone is bound to have an interest in it. Mages are characters who are prone to obsession in a way that means they tend to snag on Mysteries, because, like the Awakening but (mostly) at a smaller scale, Mysteries reveal the gaps in the Lie, which is appealing on its own merit but also serves as a source of mystical insight (i.e. Arcane Beats) even without any direct practical applications of an individual Mystery, and if a Mystery is related to other Mysteries there is a bigger picture to reveal, with greater odds of running afoul of other actors with their own plans for the world as the scope of things expands.

                          Demon (and, by extension via contact, the Contagion Chronicle) paints a picture of the God-Machine as a vast and nigh-inscrutable edifice of interconnected plans and supernatural phenomena. Werewolf casts the spiritual ecosystem and its related ritual magic as undergirded by a plethora of agreements that reflect the relationships between various worldly phenomena. Changeling gestures wildly and infrequently toward the oaths and bargains that the Fae offer and undertake in the pursuit of their drive to possess the world entire, and the primary otherworld of that gameline's sporadic comparisons to Alice in Wonderland make it unsurprising that it gives the Awakened a particularly deep rabbit hole to fall down by presenting a beautiful alien environment to explore, but the loose drive toward Ascension and the diversity of Mysteries in the world are such that the Hedge is far from the only wrong turn mages can get stuck on.

                          Not all Mysteries are beautiful, awful, weird, connected, and useful, but it's rare to find a Mystery that isn't at least one of those things, and pulling the thread on any of those qualities tends to drag in other story elements that can make complications, even if only for one specific mage. Using trees to build a model of the forest or probing the sacred spring to find where its healing waters come from are pursuits the Awakened are inclined toward both by being the sorts of people who investigate strange phenomena and by being ludonarratively rewarded for doing so — Arcane Beats are the abstraction of "mages who seek out Mysteries advance their Wisdom/Gnosis/understanding of the Arcana/initiation into their Legacy faster than mages who don't" and so a strange incursion of another world or an unusual pattern of deaths resonating with Stygia are big meaty bones to gnaw on for people looking to digest the truths that underpin their reality.

                          Any supernatural phenomenon could be a Mystery, though as the benchmark chart in Signs of Sorcery makes clear, Mysteries with an Opacity below 3 are likely to be conceptually simple and/or lack potency; total understanding of a Mystery means the character knows where it came from or what it's connected to, and building out those connections shows them where more of the levers are. In the absence of practical applications or immediate plans, investigating a Mystery gives a character grist for the mill and potential leads on new things to study or exploit; Scrutiny (and Mage Sight in general) is draining, so basic opportunity cost means most mages will prioritize things that are likely to be worth their time if they have access to them, and many Consilia specifically limit access to big Mysteries for practical and safety-related reasons before the matter of "this is essentially an Everlasting Gobstopper of arcane revelations to pick apart."

                          The short and long of it is that you don't need to worry too much about what "counts" as a Mystery as long as there's a supernatural element in play that can be understood. Everything else is extra points that work like any other plot device.

Secondly, do you believe the example magical weirdness about the various cities in the corebook is meant to be Mysteries in and of themselves, or more like, fodder that a storyteller is supposed to use for inspiration to design Mysteries pinging off it? And finally, do you have any advice for designing Mysteries without a central "whodunnit" in general?
                          To use the Mystery I mentioned at the top of this post as an example, the Chicago of my chronicle has a dream-city made out of a lobotomized titan "underneath" it, gated behind a mix of Infrastructure and Hedgeways, because the Great Chicago Fire was (in some iteration of reality) a proximate cause or effect of the God-Machine setting events in motion to core out a True Fae for resources, and things went wrong in a way that has enabled a closer interface than is usual between the metaphysics of the Machine and the Wyrd.

                          From this core event we have a pair of powerful demons serving as patrons to changeling courts who meddle with Infrastructure and stigmatics under a freehold oath colloquially called "the gag order," as well as the supernatural scaffolding among the city's skyscrapers, the secret passages squirreled away in the Deep Tunnel Project, and the comatose bodies buried in the walls of several key pieces of architecture, to say nothing of the open question about whether the Agencies involved are actually altering the timeline of the city or just rewriting collective memory.

                          Any one of these things by itself could serve as a Mystery, but because they all link together into a phenomenon you can sum up in a couple sentences it's easy to call the Loop the big Mystery of this iteration of Chicago. Elements of the freehold link into separate major phenomena in neighboring freeholds or from elsewhere in the world, and different mages investigating the Great Star could presumably go in entirely different directions based on the tenor of their Obsessions. Maybe someone from the House of Ariadne takes a keen interest in the City White (which hath fled the earth). Maybe a Transhuman Engineer tries to extract the principles of the strange Regalia infection that afflicts tokens around Chicago Union Station. Maybe a mage with an interest in language follows the line of institutional inheritance from one of the splinters of the Coroner's March Apostles back to the neighboring freehold's patronage by twin avatars of Red Wolf.

                          Focused Mage Sight is a tool that directly descends from 1e's mechanics for scrutinizing resonance, and it's not the be-all and end-all of a Mystery's involvement in the story — "solving" a routine haunting still has a ghost in the world to deal with, and while getting a Revelation of the mechanism by which efimeros-worlds prevent visitors from affecting their contents with magic may help you get the hang of Supernal Dispellation, it's not likely to directly enable you to create an Iris to Magical Spain on-demand without crossing the Threshold or tracking down a related Mystery that enables you to do so. Reliance on Scrutiny alone is not going to get you past the temple guardians or solve the coded crossword puzzle you need to win a chance to talk to the spymaster from another dimension, and the costs of Active Mage Sight and Scrutiny mean a character who tries to brute-force understanding of a Mystery is going to be drained in ways that require recovery even if it works in one sitting.

                          My general advice for designing freestanding Mysteries is to come up with supernatural phenomena to include in the story and work backwards to how they got there, or vice versa; one notable part of the mysteries of the 1871 freehold is "why is the urban legend about a frankly improbable disease-related death toll in Nineteenth Century Chicago so persistent?" and the answer relates to an organized group of magical liars wrestling with the history of the city to have a ready supply of false identities, which is more of a "whodunnit?" style of Mystery in a way, but the maze of smoke and time threading between the train tracks and the rooftops is an interesting plot device even without the knowledge that it was used as a vantage point to half-kill a god a hundred and fifty years ago.

                          The typical agendas of the Exarchs and the Abyss make it kind of hard to get out of the headspace of "what's the angle for this occult gewgaw?" but the setting does allow for more benign stuff like "if you read a poem with these qualities in the old auditorium after dark you can see the sounds turn into colored lights" or "every mirror in this house reflects the face of the current owner regardless of who's looking at it and the owner is nobody special" or "despite appearing perfectly level, water collects on only one side of the local swimming pool." Studying these as novel phenomena gives Arcane Beats, as does finding out new stuff about them by bringing them into contact with other Mysteries. Bigger and more complex Mysteries are prone to have more points for an agentic being to be connected to, but simple stuff is easy to use as material for a character to leverage for their own plans.

                              I think for Mysteries with deeper Opacity, there should generally be a context that precludes just standing around for however length of time to repeatedly scrutinise it (not least of which are Consilium regulations that might be wary of somebody messing up and occluding the Mystery a bit), and while the books might not explicitly advise it so, I feel as though even the most thoroughly scrutinised Mystery ought to only provide details of function and nature that can be used to form sophisticated theories of its ultimate origin and mechanism.

                              Like, if you study the Fleeting Worlds of Salamanca, fully unveiling the Mystery won't just tell you outright what is causing something so remarkable to exist, but provide you with a basis to come up with ideas on where else to start looking and what to start studying that might provide evidence of "of, this thing did it, and this is how".
