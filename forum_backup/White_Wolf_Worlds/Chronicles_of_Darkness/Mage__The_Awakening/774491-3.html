    I'd like to add that I, too, have run very long Mage campaigns (one is in its... Third year at this point?) and played in another in its second, and can honestly say that Paradox never had any real bite. In fact, in the game I ran, my players only had one Paradox in the entire playtime.

    With 2e, Paradox is much more of a choice and has much more bite, adding dice as quickly as it does. I've been kind of muddling with 1.5e rules and even in that limited form - using spells far more powerful than they likely are in the book - it's had a noticeable effect on decision-making already.

        My fix for Paradox in 1e was basically make it lethal. Problem solved.

        That said, I really really like Paradox more from what we have seen in 2e.

        Lastly, I didn't do it when I was starting as a ST, but later I started including drawbacks of lugging around active defenses all the time. The biggest and most interesting one is that other mages can detect you automatically when approaching from a 200 yard distance. A pretty significant drawback. On some scenes this meant that enemies know they were coming and where able to escape in time, prepare a quick ambush or put defenses up themselves. Other times this was used to track their routes and find their Sanctum (who they had hidden at the time).

        Thus, players had to be more discerning on when it was a good time to have defenses up and when not to.

        I am also glad that 2e fixed this anyways, I like the 2e method better.

                                                2. So a few points of mana per day, whoo, you're rolling in mana now. It also now costs mana per additional non-ruling arcana application to your Mage Sight, and Mage Armor costs a point of mana for every additional scene you have it active past the first. That isn't even including spell costs, be it for specific spell requirements or use of non-rote, non-ruling magic.

                                                3. Infinite possibilities isn't what Vampires and Werewolves are about, so who cares if they're applicable magic is more focused or not? Working as intended. The additional 3 dots (assuming the unknown factors work like in 1e) will only net you 1 or 2 dots in your common arcana. That gives you access to Compelling, Knowing, Unveiling, Ruling, Shielding, and Veiling effects. Most of those are devoted purely to gathering and concealing information, which is literally a mages bread and butter. It is exactly what they are supposed to excel at over all others (supernaturals of not).

                                                The others give rudimentary control over the things that fall into the arcanums purview, including the ability to shield yourself from some of it's effects. This is important because in 2e mages aren't inherently immune to the effects of their own spells. If you create a fireball without first giving yourself protection from fire you're going to incinerate your own hand.

                                                So long story short, all the additional 3 dots give is a wider breath of possibilities in doing the very thing mages are designed to do.
