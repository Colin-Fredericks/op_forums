    One of the beauties of running a campaign rather than sitting down and writing a book, is that you get to see how another person, given the limited information, actually will work things out. I love watching that. Learned many neat things from that.

    Experienced DM notes:
    Assess your ability to come up with things on the fly. There are two major extremes in DMing: Wingers and Bookers. Most DMs will develop a comfortable style that falls somewhere between these.

    Wingers are only ever successful if they really are truly good at winging it. Bookers excel only if they have accounted for every detail.

    The latter tend to have better descriptions of things and more intricate plots but thinner worlds due to not having all the possibilities for interactions worked out. (Say, random peasants you might find interesting and try to talk to. There are exceptions, I've played with. Man, that can get impressive.)
    Wingers tend to have plots that aren't as specifically defined, so they often prefer a sandbox style, with less actual plot present. Surprising a winger is harder, though, and if they are good at it, they tend to have loads of memorable characters though less often memorable plot. The weakness is details. Since they are coming up with descriptions and such on the fly, they often don't go into as much detail. (You want sweeping eloquence, consistently, you need to edit, which takes time and is preparation.)


    I'm heavily winger but a plot-heavy variant, so mostly have advice on fleshing things out for such.

    Use stereotypes. The world is full of strange people who come from a lot of backgrounds. To get a believable world, then, your characters need to come from similarly diverse backgrounds and be fleshed out characters. Stereotypes exist because sorting people into associated trends is a natural human function. Use that part of your brain to help you generate NPCs that seem real.

    How? Stereotypes are sets that can and should overlap. Factions will often attract people with certain traits. Regions will have others. If there is a global faction of people who have studied a specific art, but you need one from that farming village over there, use the stereotypes of that village combined with the one for the faction and roll in some personal touches including personal goals and history. If a guy gets a visible scar, at least figure out if he likes it and what his explanation is for it. If an NPC dresses differently than most, consider why.
    (Some might prefer talking about this as 'treating backgrounds as characters' or something. Programmer sorts probably recognize this as "multiple inheritance".) These categories, it should be noted, should tend to come in two categories: Physical and Mental. Physical is where certain types of people live / which types of people live in a certain place. This includes details like heritage and ethnicity, obviously. Mental would be things like factions and religions.

    (When setting these up, by the way, it helps to figure out what an exceptional member would be like alongside an average one.)

    What you don't do (unless you are actively trying to reinforce some aspect of society like fear or conformity) is describe your NPCs as these things. Do the processing in your head and then describe and play out the character of your new NPC.

    The benefit is that you can come up with an expected set of traits for these stereotypes and then be able to produce many distinct character with significant differences. If a character represents a conflict between multiple stereotypes, you have something interesting about them. They might hide it or even ignore it, but that's a significant character detail. Figure out how they handled that conflict in their own head, and that's a part of the character. If a regional stereotype happens to require such a conflict, be aware that multiple solutions to the conflicts likely elevate into factions.

    Same goes for making arbitrary settlements: There is a trend among the people present as to what they can and would build. A town can be thought of as a character or as a town-specific regional stereotype. Much as with NPCs, you need to throw in a few quirks and details about personal history. These can normally be pretty vague. Most of the time, it's set dressing, but remember it so it fits the patterns of your world consistently.

    Take copious notes: At the end of a session or right after a scene, whichever works better for you, jot down the details that you came up with and what the players were up to. This helps with consistency. This includes history of a village and such.


    I've actually had players take a shine to a budding romance in an outlying village that was purely a background piece. They decided that they liked this shepherd kid and helped him compete with the mayor's lad for the village beauty. (He was too poor to buy little niceties like the mayor's son, but she did like him.) The village was only there because something had to be, and I did what I describe above: worked out what kind of place it was and the sorts of people who'd live there and, when they decided to visit, fleshed out the NPCs that inhabited it. I intended the scene to take 1-2 minutes. It ran about 2 hrs.

    Which serves as a nice bridge to:

    Don't rely on players for plot: This is more important to bookers, but is still critical to wingers. Players will jump to the proper conclusion in the first scene or miss the plot hook for months in real time. (Had a Cam plot run for just under a year before they unraveled it. 9-10 months!) They will find random NPCs interesting and ignore important figures. Some really are trying to wreck your game, but 99% of the time, they are doing it because that's what seemed interesting to them. If you can spin the plates and keep things progressing, so much the better.

    If you need something to happen, there needs to be a reason for it to occur. Feel free to throw out multiple clues or re-purpose clues they already found but discarded so as to let plot unfold as it needs to. Try to minimize that, though. It's a naughty habit.

    Don't cling to one path: The players might do something that prevents your plot. This is likely, yet doesn't represent a thing to be feared. Contingency planning and alternate valid paths should be accounted for from the get-go. I try to have anything important have loads of on-ramps but still hold real possibility of failure. I also cheat in the players favor, from time to time, when it keeps things on track.

    Don't always wait for players: Some plots need to progress whether or not the players have interacted. Delays break the whole point, so don't delay. If they are in a race for something and take so much time that they are going to miss out on winning, they lose. The moment such a scenario pops into your mind, come up with success and failure outcomes. That's just a gimmie.

    Let the players play: The players don't know everything and they make weird choices. Roll with that. Minimize forced plots. Hook-in-mouth gaming is tedious in a CPRG, so avoid it. You want contingency plot? Give them reasons that fit their characters. They miss out? Well, run the logical consequences or adjust plot to guide them to the next alternate on-ramp. Use the player's character to set player motivation. It's their sister that went missing. etc.

    Rules are your domain: The published rules of a setting are there to provide consistency. Hence, a consistently-applied house-rule is generally just as good as the book, as long as everyone knows this. You want to change setting details? Consistency is key, but go for it.

    If a player wants to do something that their character should at least think that they could accomplish, help them sort it out. A scientist might well try to see if they can improvise and explosive using the parts around wjhile the player might have no clue how those systems work in your game. Help them.
    Player wants advice based on setting details? Be free with them based on that character's knowledge. Limit what you say to what the character would know, and feel free to require rolls to have them think something up that's rather obscure. All that fits in together with letting them play.

        I don't know about the winger/booker distinction there. In my opinion it's more of a sliding scale. It's impossible to plan for every possible eventuality, so no matter what a prep-heavy DM will eventually have to improvise. And (good) improvisation is really just planning and executing in a single step, so wingers that are truly good at winging it are the ones who have an intuitive enough grasp of narrative pacing that they can create a plan in the moment. No matter what you're going to need both skills to succeed, it's just a matter of finding the right balance of pre-planning and improvising for you.

                        Make sure that significant events unfold even when the player isn't present. Elections; addictions; assassinations; trouble in school; somebody has trouble finding work eventually gives up and starts delivering pizzas for a while... eventually getting his strange app off the ground and becomes a minor celebrity in certain circles.

                        Basically: Stuff happens off-screen and some of it is truly important. Some parts of the investigation the PC is interested in are done by NPCs in parallel, for example. Interesting plots run and the player has to push to become involved.

                        Similarly, have the relationships make sense. Busy big-wigs don't stop to help the character because of who it is, though they still might because of who they are.

                        You have things make sense while the NPCs treat them like a normal person, that's the vibe you end up with.

                            The cool thing about one-on-one games is there's no need to manage spotlight time between different PCs. This gives you the opportunity to tell a much more personal story than you could in a group game. Normally, you've got a limited budget of player attention span to spread around between personal scenes for each character. In one-on-one, every scene can be a personal scene, so you can play a much more "zoomed in" game with a lot less montaging. And since you can focus so much on personal details, it doesn't need to use grand events to get the player invested.
