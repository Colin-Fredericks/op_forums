                Wyrd the point is they didn't do that. It wasn't a instantenous process, cultural assimilation is a slow process that takes a lot of time and happens in real life. And it doesn't have to involve conquest.

                Just took at what happened with Ancient Rome. They conquered ancient greece but in the end they ended up adopting a lot of the culture, mythology and philosophy of the conquered nation. Heracles, son of Zeus became Hercules, son of Jupiter. During the spread of christianity is also assimilated various local myths into its own mythology, X-mass takes place on the day of the winter solitice in the Roman calendar. Old and new beliefs and traditions become synthetized, this has happened thorough history to many times to count. It's a natural process. We're seeing it even now with the advent of globalization, symbols and holidays that have been strictly national have now become more global. Saint Patricks day is celebrated outside Ireland despite it previously being a local thing, same with Halloween. It just happens.

                                    One thing that relates to all of this is how the Norse (along with a lot of people in Europa around the time discussed) handled the sort of ideas we're talking about. Namely, they just kind of added it in, and over time these things took root.

                                    The "Atlantean Dogma" is nowhere near as insistent as the Christian Dogma, for example, but as a point of comparison, a lot of the Norse just did not give a shit about adding Jesus and Elohim to their pantheon, accepting it as a substrata of what they believed. Over time, as the grindstone was steadily applied, Christianity became more and more of a thing, and most people just didn't give a shit about whether or not who they were getting their blessings from, so long as they got them and could blame/attribute things to their name. Really, if Christianity had been more invasive than it was, it probably would have been rejected.

                                    The model of the Diamond Orders is less insistent than that, since pretty much every mage since the era of To The Strongest has realized that it is a description of reality that's about as actually real as any of the Great Cults that started the entire shindig off in it's actuality-but the shape of it fits better and even fills the holes they own worldviews leave empty. That said, regional versions do exist. At any point in the slide from the Aesir worship to the full Christianity worship, Mages would probably be disillusioned by their own world religion (because the nature of Awakening means that even the most devout people in the age are going to recognize the Lie inherent in worship), however it's mixed, and probably describe the Atlantean model in whatever the socio-religious-occult context of the time was because that's what's easiest to talk about.

                                    THis might be one of the most important things to realize, actually-upon Awakening, religions lose their reverence. Religions become, not a way to worship, but a way to describe-and as anyone who has had someone pick apart any metaphor they use know, descriptions have limits. Very early mage cults and cabals might lean heavily on their religion, but that's because alternative models do not yet exist. THey don't even need to be subjected to other modes of thoughts to realize things fall apart, but when they are confronted with people whose own understanding fill out the puzzle that much better, mages (being drawn to truth) are more likely accept what describes a thing truly over their own cultural upbringing than not.

                                    The Atlantean model of the Diamond works because, while it has the trappings of a religion, it is really more just of an empirical observation of reality with an easy to digest narrative layer on top of it-and that narrative layer can flex and flux to work with people because the story it portrays is not as important as the wholeness of reality that it describes.

                                    Or, put another way, the "Atlantean dogma" works as well as it does because everyone knows it's only a model.

                                            In my usual attempt of getting lurking instincts and social anxiety out my system, I looked away from my comfy little corner of world and, through what I can only describe as the opposite of looking for a needle in a haystack, had a sort of moment of clarity about these threads.

                                            I'm late, I know. But I always miss out about what's going on around me and I learned the hard way that I need to chill, read and meditate before expressing rushed opinions.

                                            *takes a deep breath*

                                            Since Kieron Gillen taught me that I should try to be nicer:

                                            Ok Wyrd, I don't know if this is pointless or not, but let's try to tackle the issue in another way, just for the sake of trying to solve the questions this topic asks.

                                            It's clear that the idea of the supernatural societies of the various places being strongly tied with the "flavor" of the local human culture is something that enjoy a lot, to the point where you like to ramp it up more than the standard CoD usually does. Direct connection to clear-cut ideas of what impact myths and traditions should have on the setting. Sometimes things get more stylized than usual in the process, but you'd rather have a strong structure with blunt answers where the elements you want to use are clearly defined and kept apart from others, if simplified a bit. Modern Scandinavian Wolves have a Shadow, traditions and enemies to deal with that all owe to Norse mythology in a rather literal way and, Viking Mages never mention Atlantis or the Diamond and so on.

                                            It's fine. Not how I roll, in the CoD I like my connections to human religions to be murky, vague and more of a guideline, at best, but it's fine. I don't think you're alone on that.

                                            What I want to ask so that we can discuss it and see if we get to a place that solves your doubts is: what really says that your perspective loses legitimacy if foreign cultures come into contact? Let's put aside the how and when for a second, even though that's a question this thread already answered. I'm asking you if human cultures shape your setting so much, should not they be able to endure contact with others with ease? What breaks if a Viking Mage meets a Greek one? Who says they should stab each other the moment they notice their cultural background differs? Can we get over the fact that there's no Atlantean Nation, Euro-Roman or not, waging a cultural war against the world? Does it really matter to your game? And I say "your game", because let's be real: that's what these threads are actually about, neutral title or not.

                                            I'm not gonna tackle other issues or criticism the whole meta-discussion brings up because...no, I just won't. Having said that, I'm trying to at least provide a contribution to the topic at hand.
