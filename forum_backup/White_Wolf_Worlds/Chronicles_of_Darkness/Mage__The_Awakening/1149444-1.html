        Originally posted by Johnny Awesome View Post
        Thoughts?
        The way i learn Mage and also the way i run it is as such.

        First i assume player wont ever read the manual and accommodate to that assumption. Players sit on the table and expect a game to happen with a minimal effort on their part. Are there people who do read it? Sure but those people arent a problem. Second assumption, all players are looking to "win", by this i mean they always looking for the maximum efficiency (to whatever capacity they have) in everything they do, so things like "not using all their reach" wont fly. If i suggest that they will reply "no no no wait wait, just let me think" and grind the game to a halt while they are looking for that one use of free reach and insisting to let it go just to continue the game results in sours looks and resentment.

        So on how i do it:
        PCs start with no order and a cap on an arcanum at 2. Then they cant spend XP on raising Arcanums until a time skip happens (about 3 month of actual play) and then they can raise it to a 3. And so on.

        Why i do this? Because mage (all mage lines) spoils you with choices to the point that to that kind of player is paralyzing. So by starting in arcanum 2 i cut down the choices available to the PCs, they get less things to worry about and less capabilities. And the 3 months of play with trapped in a level of arcanum lets them get acclimate to both the system and the arcanum.

        When i started with ascension i read the manual and had knowledge of the mechanics enough but being trapped in sphere 2, made really internalize the rules. And the rest of the table who never ever touched the manual were able to play without ever reading. By rote (lol) they learned what they needed to do to get the spells they wanted. It gave me a better appreciation of each level of spheres with some spells being actual improved versions of their 2 sphere 2 versions. The progressions felt both more natural and earned than the basic assumption of starting.

        As DM, this also allows me to regulate the power level of the game. Managing a semblance of street level while building up to more big stuff over time. It also allows some credibility to the setting by avoiding 19 year old masters but thats a matter of personal taste.

        So thats my advice, but beyond reframing the way you present the game/rules there is little you can do to fix your issue without houseruling.

            Originally posted by Biston View Post
            I don't know if it's right to say spellcrafting in one edition or another is more or less complex, but what Mage 2E's system has different is that it's more involved. Now instead of just determining factors and firing to see how effective your spell is (which varied to annoying degrees even as a Master sometimes), now you craft the whole thing from the ground up, weighing the risks of how far down you reduce your dice pool, taking into account Reach and therefore Paradox and how much you're capable of reducing that.
            See, I love that. Now I'm in control of exactly what my spell does, and the only limiting factor is how many dice and Reach I'm willing to risk. It's much easier for me to start from what exactly I want the spell to do, and then work backwards to figure out how many dice and Reach I need to make it happen. Plus the spell factor tables are so much more streamlined now. No more separate factor tables for instant cast vs. ritual cast, all the various scale factor tables got consolodated into a single table, and everything is always -2 dice to go up a step. Much easier for me personally to grasp.

            Originally posted by Biston View Post
            There are more considerations that go into spellcasting than there were in 1E. It was both difficult to wrap my head around it, and it's been the trend to the friends I've introduced it to; spellcasting is always the thing they take the longest time internalizing. Worse, attempting to remove or significantly change parts of it for simplicity's sake can often de-emphasize certain important elements to spellcasting which define Mage's themes and aesthetics.
            See, spellcasting was what I couldn't wrap my head around in 1e. I got that in theory you could do pretty much anything with the right Arcanum, but it was really hard for me to figure out what Arcanum were required for what effects, and then on top of that you had to weigh the dice penalties for your spell's factors against your chances of getting enough successes on the roll to do what you wanted. In 2e all the weird corner cases with Arcanum got lumped into Reach effects, and the probability math is so much easier when all spells are just pass/fail. No need to worry about how many dice you'll need to reliably roll X successes, because you only ever need 1 success.

            Originally posted by Biston View Post
            The free Reach you get when casting at a spell's level is a good sort of all this; it exists so that a player can elect to cast a spell instantly (since what's even the goddamn point of being able to cast Magic Missile if it takes three hours to go off?) or to instead augment a spell for broader purposes through ritual casting. The ability to sacrifice instant casting to be able to achieve a more useful effect with prep time is a good introduction to the idea of Ritual Casting and its usefulness--but the natural assumption, since a given session tends to take place in the immediate with the need for immediate action instead of over extended periods, people tend to think of immediate casting as the default, and breaking them of that very natural assumption is vital to evoking the ritualistic and symbolic trappings that make up a massive chunk of Mage's aesthetics.
            See, to me the nice thing about ritual casting in 2e is that it doesn't much matter if you think about it as ritual being the default and instant costing you 1 reach or if you think about it as instant being the default and ritual giving you +1 free reach. It's functionally the same, so which ever is easier for you to grok, just treat it that way. More importantly, the spellcasting mechanics are exactly the same whether you are ritual casting or instant casting. This goes such a long way towards making the system more approachable for me because I don't have to think about two different ways to produce every effect and figure out which is going to be more effective. No more calculating dive penalties on an Instabt action vs. target number of successes on an extended action and having to do even more probability math to figure out which is the better one to use. A spell is always just a spell, the same effect is produced the same way whether you need it right now or have the luxury of taking your time. The only difference is, if you can't take your time, you need 1 more Reach than if you can. Simple.

                I genuinely think its something of a toss up as to whether 1E or 2E have a more complicated system. I will happily argue that 2E's is better, more consistent, and fun to manipulate once you master it though. Its attaining that system mastery that can be the problem.

                People are free (and correct) to throw criticisms of anecdotal evidence at me, but here's what I have picked up from my current game. I am currently running for 5 players. 2 have played a little 1e, the others are new to NWOD entirely. Both of my old hands have adapted pretty rapidly, but I had to be on the ball a lot to point out misconceptions and mistakes (lots of cases where key words have survivied the edition change, but actually mean different things, such as Potency not being linked to successes, not rolling against Withstood effects etc).

                For the sake of the new players I made a point in the first 3 sessions to model how we were putting spells together whenever the more savvy guys did it, verbally working through how much free reach was in play, how it was being spent etc. When the new players cast their spells (none of whom had done more than glance at the spellcasting section), I always had them suggest what effect they wanted to pull off, then offered a suggestion on how reach/dice pools/yantra's could be arranged, (this could be used to showcase any options they might not have been aware existed IE"For one more reach you can target all 5 of them!"). After showing them how I was doing this, it wasn't long at all before they were making their own suggestions, and then finally just taking back control of how the spells were constructed.

                So something like 10 plus sessions in, 4 players happily slap there own effects together, 1 is a bit newer (we had one drop, another come in) and is still at the 'here's how I would do it' stage, but I have high hopes that won't last much longer.

                A couple of things I would recommend.
                • Print out the quick spellcasting chart from the back of the book. Laminate it. Slap it in the middle of the table at the start of the session.
                • If you have players who don't have the time/inclination to the read the book, heavily suggest they invest in a NPC Mentor and come up with a concept that involves Awakening recently. Then you can turn learning about the setting (which can be just as much of a complexity barrier as the rules) can be made into a fun role-play experience, and the Mentor can point out magical options/tactics that the player might not know was available to them.
                • Make the Mundane relevant. Tied in with the complex magic rules, is a pretty simple and flexible core dice rolling system. Try and ensure that mundane rolls occur, and that mundane strategies and approaches have a reasonable chance of success. This doesn't necessarily help deal with the complex casting factor, but it helps ensure less confident players still have a strong sense of agency, keeping them invested in your game long enough for them to learn by osmosis.
                • Encourage creative thaumaturgy: Welcome players throwing out their idea's for effects they want, then work backwards with them to achieve it or something similar.
                • Don't throw too many antogonistic Adepts at your new players, but it is worth having the occasional NPC Mage pull out some weird, dramatic effect on them. Players will want to work out how to do it themselves! I nearly drowned my players last session when the necromancer they were hunting transmuted every solid object in the house they were investigating (apart from the walls and roof) into a liquid. The looks on peoples faces when he *dismissed* the spell on the next turn was priceless. My Moros player is now much more excited about the possibilities of State Change.
                • Love and praise those guys who actually read the book. Use them shamelessly to assist other players in hectic situations like combat, where your focus is being spent on NPC's actions and who-evers turn it currently is.
                Thats my 2 cents, anyway!
