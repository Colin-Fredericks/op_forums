            maryshelly For toning down Mages, you could as others have brought up, use one of the less potent versions, like Witches (from Hunter), or Proximi (you can give them various Mage-like features to power them back up).

            Other options are to add Mage-only threats (not threats only Mages can deal with, but threats than only impact Mages). Like Abyssal intrusions that only hunt down Supernal magic (you could even tie the Atrocity dice from Danse Macabre into Paradox rolls - triggering Paradox may not only add to the Scene, but the entire area). Or setting additions like a far stronger Mage-Police, or even supernatural Rules regarding magic use (No using certain forms of mind control without prior justification or you get punished, either by other Mages, or reality itself, for example). Or add in natural counter-measures against Supernal magic - like how some metals or symbols resonate with certain Paths for magical tools, maybe some materials or actions counteract some Arcana , such as blocking certain effects, or reducing potency or something (which would also help mortals against Mages).

            You might make Instant casting or Creative Thaumaturgy less effective. Mages can still sling their Praxis and any Rotes they know, but ad-libbing magic might require time-consuming Yantras, or not automatically get Arcana - 1 in Potency (Mages are still strong, and adaptable, but lose much of their power when they have to come up with spells on the fly, or those spells take precious Turns).

                Originally posted by proindrakenzol View Post
                The gameline isn't made to be balanced against other splats. They're balanced against themselves which is all that matters.
                Not entirely true. Mages study mysteries of the world. And mages are pretty much designed to trivialize any non-Awakened threat, which includes designing spirit and ghostly antagonists, which directly impacts a pure mage game as well as the kinds of stories you can run.



                Anwyays, In my experience, there are four major kinds of crossovers:
                1) supernatural antagonists (aka the war)
                2) social and mental mysteries to investigate
                3) casual interaction but no direct impact on plot
                4) mixed party

                In the case of 1? Mages are generally going to win for several reasons - the big two being a better pool of powers and the fact that mages are king at gathering information, and information is the linchpin of modern wars. Case 2 heavily favors mages as well, both in terms of avoiding notice, or digging up the information trivially. In case 3, there's really no problems unless a PC starts asking an NPC mage to do things for them, in which case things go off the rail again. Case 4, we have a strong tendacy for Awakened to trivialize any non-Supernal/Awkaned/Abyssal threat, and for no one else to be able to handle a the mage-based threats.

                if you really want to have a less rampant witch/wizard-splat, there's really no good way to do it than to redesign the powers of the Awakened from the ground up. Even if you correct the imbalance of the Awakened being able to affect even high Potency others, that's still not going to correct the manner in which Awakened magic can be used to empower other supers to the point of putting their natural powers to shame or to trivially deal with any threat.

                The sheer variety of techniques per Arcana combined with the shielding and other buffing abilities is a major issue in any crossover game (other than type 3).

                While there are times when crossover with mages hasn't been a bad thing, that's usually from a specific attitude on part of the mage player. So, its not an absolute. In general, though? Its usually a pain in the butt.

                And that's not touching on a rather important issue related to the balance of Awakened mages in the first place - spell limits. Nomrally, you can only have a few spells active at once. In a crossover game, especially type 4, you have easy access to a number of sleepwalker types that can hold your spells,a cting as major force multipilers.

                This is why I said you really need to redesign things from the ground up, because the simple fact of the matter is that there's simply too many things that can cause issues

                                                        I mean, to keep things in perspective to the thread, people were saying that Dr. Strange felt like a Mage movie. And rightly so! But Dr. Strange is a superhero film. The reason Mage can't crossover with, say, Vampire, is that Vampire is a horror movie and Mage is a superhero movie with horror elements. It's not so much about power level as it is about design attitude; Mage has higher scale opponents but it's not an intrinsically "personal horror" state to be in, unlike, say, being a vampire.

                                                        So the real reason this discussion keeps cropping up is people go into the CofD expecting horror games, and Mage is kind of an outlier where it really just isn't intended to be one down to its bones. Power level is just a proxy for genre, in this instance, I think.

                                                        I mean, look at the magic system itself - it's a fantasy magic system. It's a direct line from Ars Magica to Mage: The Ascension to Mage: The Awakening. It's an urban fantasy game with relatively high powered protagonists. This is not to say there isn't horror in Mage, or horror you can do with Mage, but crossover is going to inevitably run into genre contradictions.

                                                        EDIT: I mean, the bottom line is, there's real truth to Mage being kind of an odd man out in the CofD generally. This sort of boils down to "they should have made a different game than they made" as a criticism, which is completely worthless, obviously. But you could easily envision Mage as its own standalone game a la Scion and a different game about horror-genre magic specifically in the "Mage" slot fitting in better with the CofD, tonally.

                                                            Yeah, it is pretty central to Mage as a game line that they be powerful. The horror you find in Mage is rarely in the monsters you face. Yeah, those things are grotesque quite often, but the horror isn't caused by them; it's caused by you, and people like you. This is a game line that names Hellblazer as one of its inspirations, and every issue of that is a question of just how many twists John plans to put into the noose he's going to hang himself with this week.

                                                            The powerlessness in Mage comes from feeling like you're a god and then realizing you're human all along; from opening the ancient chest covered with warnings because you're curious what's inside and being terrified when it all goes pear-shaped; from causing everyone you love and care about to look at you like a monster. It's from taking off the hood of your executioner and realizing you're looking at yourself.

                                                            To get yourself into deep shit, as it were, you need a really big shovel. A huge part of Mages as people is that, once they've dug themselves into that hole, thinking "Y'know, if I made this shovel bigger, I could dig myself out of it".

                                                            My most memorable Mage moments aren't from facing off against some unspeakable horror from beyond the veil of time and space; they're where absolute certainty and conviction made a young Thyrsus nearly put an axe to her uncle's neck in a fit of rage and grief. They're from when that same Thyrsus, suddenly so full of the zeal of a thearch, realized that a young man whom she so desperately wants to help is beyond her ability to do so; not because she can't pacify his abusive father or offer financial stability or even answers to his questions, but because those things are what are driving him closer to Awakening (which she believes is worth the risk of his emotional health).

                                                            If you want to be spooked by monsters, you can do that too. Been plenty of that. Rising power means the ability to seek out greater threats. It's just that, for Mage, the horror isn't that something with too many consonants and too few vowels in its name exists; it's that you can't help yourself in wanting to go poke it in the eye and ask it for a favour.
