                I think this thread needs a sample spell to illustrate the issue. I've got just the spell: Present as Past.

                Present as Past allows the mage to witness incoming futures and react to them in real time. It expresses this in two ways:
                1. It reduces the Doors you face in Social Maneuvering.
                2. It causes everyone in combat to have to declare their actions before yours, and allows you to select when to act with perfect knowledge of your foes intentions.

                The first application is reliant on Potency. The second clearly favors duration. Both are entirely valid uses of the spell. However, as Present as Past has Potency locked as it's primary factor the second option requires additional reach or duration penalties to make use of. It's hard to argue that one function of the spell is better then the other, both are amazing in their respective fields. Different characters might buy this spell on Rote for different reasons, a socialite would find the Door reduction highly attractive, while a fighty type would love the confidence provided by the predictive initiative. However, the fighter has to sink additional reach into every casting, while the socialite is free to use that Reach elsewhere.

                This, in my eyes, is nonsense. Nothing but a speed bump in casting and a needless frustration to the Mage's player.

                                My assumption was spells in the book were recipes illustrating what can be done with the magic system. Meaning, in a meta-game sense, you can draw inspiration AND ideas from published spells, then tweak stuff as you see fit.

                                From a purely OUT-OF-GAME context, the spells in the book help the ST and PCs by providing a litany of rotes/spells to drive mysteries forward. If you didn't have the spells in the book, and only had the rules to make them, STs would have an immensely more difficult job.

                                ---

                                For inexperienced players, the spells in the book work as a road map or direct applications of spells holding their hand to a desired end (Present as Past).

                                The main difference being that a player does not gain the benefits of rote casting if they're improvising a published effect. The rote version of the spell would thus be far more desirable.

                                I don't understand why improvising a published spell with a changed default Primary factor is frowned upon other than getting away with not spending a Reach to change it.

                                If you cast every published spell as an improvised spell just to avoid spending 1 Reach to switch, then you're not casting the spell as a rote. If you're casting the spell as a rote, you start from the get-go with a set Primary, but you also get rote benefits.

                                ---

                                I also don't completely understand the "Primary factor is whatever factor you think of when you imagine a stronger version." It just seems like deciding Primary factor should be up to whatever the player decides they want at the time. A stronger Mind Control spell? Or a longer lasting Mind Control spell? A hotter/more forceful flamethrower, or a weaker but longer burning flamethrower? (Maybe damage is overkill and you just need the narrative effect of a flamethrower to keep the shadow demons at bay.)

                                If it doesn't matter and only serves to make the player happy because they get their way, why not let them choose?

                                        I think the game will inherently endure some meta-gaming since characters will enjoy the benefits of taking actions that they may not be able to contextualize within the experience of their character. An example of this is when a character does something, or knows about something, that the player didn't know the character knew about. Not until successes are tallied does the basis of this knowledge materialize.

                                        The issue here is whether or not it can be explained within the context of the characters and the natural progress of their knowledge about things.

                                        If you're coming up with improvised spells that are only slight tweaks of published spells, you could reason that these spells are nothing but the natural evolution of playing around with the arcane practices. The fact that you do not cast a spell exactly as it is written would show that your character is not using the version of the spell that was codified by a master. You are naturally progressing through practices and arcana you can play with at your level. It isn't as though you have 1000 practices to delve through.

                                        "Yes, typically when people use Death Knowing spells they tend to bell-curve around a very common set of factors."

                                        "Why do people use rotes, then?"

                                        "Because they're awesome, even if they require a bit of effort to wrangle into doing something removed from the primary intent of the rote when initially crafted."

                                        "Why aren't there hundreds of different versions of 'Spell X' rotes?"

                                        "Who says there aren't? Anyway, primarily because creating rotes is not a willy-nilly act and because 'approximate' copies already exist; however, if you're so inclined, you can make your own improvised version of 'Spell X' into a rote."

                                        ---

                                        It would be like playing the piano and happening across scales and chords by happenstance, which happens all the time. You just don't know what their official names are, or that they might be coded and sought after with particular zeal.

                                        The problem with denying the casting of spells that are too similar to rotes is that you've immediately defined a set of spells the mage cannot mold to his liking for some meta reason. Have they been platonically reinforced by the Supernal Realms? If this is the reason it make more sense, but it does certainly fly in the face of everything mage is setup to do.
