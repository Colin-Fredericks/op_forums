            When I start losing my way when it comes to Wisdom and Acts of Hubris, I go directly to the book for clarity. Page 88 defines what Wisdom is and the trait represents. Those are two different things. Furthermore, that same section says that it won't even begin to categorize what is "right vs wrong." On page 89, before they go over the various levels of Wisdom and the Acts of Hubris that risk degeneration, they tell you that these acts are merely examples, this list is not exhaustive, and for you to add or remove anything you want in order to suit your tastes.

            In light of all that, I think criticisms of Wisdom at this point, especially compared to the first edition of the game, are a moot point. If you don't like it, change it. That's literally the Rules as Written.

            Axelgear -- as for Wisdom being clunky: to your first point, it's not an ethical system and doesn't pretend to be. In fact, it is explicit that it is not. Your criticism assigns ethics to Acts of Hubris. Would you be willing to elaborate on why you think it's an ethical system contrary to what the game states?

            To your second point: That's a good point... that's character growth, maybe, or it's just getting into character. It takes a few spins around the block to break the character in. In those situations I would say use this time to help define the Acts of Hubris and Wisdom levels of your PCs. Put a little meat on the skeleton given the core book. I think that's the reason Mages being at Wisdom 7, the highest point of middling level Wisdom. Wiggle room to figure things out.

            After all is said and done, I think the subtext or theme of Wisdom and Acts of Hubris is for mages to realize that magic is about changing yourself... not the world. You can Awaken. You can do magic. You can become an archmage. You can ascend. You can't do it for anyone else. That's why there are Orders that have sprung up trying to guide people to that stuff... The Silver Ladder, the Free Council, the Guardians of the Veil... And then there are Orders that have sprung up due to the implications of magic... the Arrow, the Seers, the Mysterium.

            You can be Wisdom 1, like Darth Vader, and attempt to change the whole galaxy.
            You can be Wisdom 10, like Yoda, and live alone on a swamp world until you ascend.

            The choice is yours and yours alone.

                            I'm Exarchial ST - there aren't any 'valid reason to not be a AoH'. There could be one to move one AoH higher or lower in Wisdom Tiers, but you must assume that players need always to roll on those actions, with more or less dices involved. Exarchs made Fallen World this way.

                            Here are holy guidlines on Wisdom from WHW.

                            Originally posted by WHW View Post
                            Basic guideline:

                            - Is the act about something that could be done without Magic safely and without posing risk to the Mage? If yes, it's Enlightened Hubris.

                            - Is the act planned and Mage put at least minimum effort into understanding the short and long term consequences of their actions? If yes, bump it up by one category

                            - Is the act done on impulse, and/or Mage is giving into their urges? Acting on anger, lust and fear all fall here; if yes, bump it down by one category.
                            Originally posted by WHW View Post
                            More guidelines:

                            - Is the act hurtful on physical, mental or spiritual level in potentially long-lasting capacity? If yes, then it's base level is Understanding.
                            - Is the act leading to permanent destruction on physical, mental or spiritual level? If yes, then it's base level is Falling.

                                            Originally posted by Axelgear View Post
                                            It's clunky for two reasons. First being that ethical systems are rarely simplistic. It can take a lot of questions to hammer out and keeping track of that for every player in your game can be hell. Works fine for solo games, though. The second is that, quite often, players don't quite know their character at first either. What feels like it works and doesn't can take time to hash out and may change with time. A character may gain a moral stricture they didn't previously believe in during play, or they may find that a previous one doesn't fit
                                            I haven't seen anything in Wisdom that implies that its approach to ethics is simplistic. In fact, I'd say that the open ended nature of determining AoH means they're acknowledging the fact that it isn't.

                                            I'd hash out an overall ethical code that I want the game to enforce, for instance thoughtless healing can be just as bad as thoughtless harming. Then I'd use the ethical systems of the Orders described in their Hubris section to provide a slightly more individualized system for each of my players. From there I'd just do an ethics checkup when a player buys a dot of Gnosis or Wisdom where I could review the character's history (Preferably with the player) for actions that seemed able to significantly effect their ethical conduct and decide if any changes need to be made to their AoH list as a result. I'd also let the players know that I expect them to be aware of and responsible for their own Wisdom, pointing out potential AoH if I miss them because risking Wisdom is a good way to earn Beats. If they don't, it's not the end of the world and no Beat for them. I think this would make figuring out Wisdom for a player 1) A thing that happens only at certain intervals instead of something you need to track constantly, and 2) Allow the player to develop their Wisdom as they play and get to know the character while also providing a baseline framework for them to interact with.

                                            Personally, I mostly stick to the idea that Wisdom is about ignoring possible consequences to get what you want. Action matters more than intent.
