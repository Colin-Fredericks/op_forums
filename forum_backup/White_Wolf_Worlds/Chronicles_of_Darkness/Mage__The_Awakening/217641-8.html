        Some of the suggestions you’re making strike me as being more of a Banisher thing than a Sleeper thing; but really, the only part of your suggestion that I disagree with is the insistence that if you’ve taken up the Vigil, your memories remain intact. If that works for you, fine; but I find it more interesting if the default assumption is that hunters are, by default, just like everyone else when it comes to the Vigil, and that any special resistance or vulnerability an individual hunter may have takes the form of a Tactic, a Merit, or a Persistent Condition.

        Bear in mind, though, that my own opinion on the Vigil is that there are essentially three playstyles that Hunter: the Vigil should support:

        • an “ordinary people” playstyle, where the only difference between a hunter and a mortal is setting elements such as membership in a hunter org (specifically, no rules changes — not even Risking Willpower or Practical Experience — are assumed to be in play in an “ordinary people” Hunter Chronicle unless the Storyteller explicitly allows them).
        • a “hunters are special” level where all hunters get access to a few special benefits such as Practical Experience or Risking Willpower unless the Storyteller explicitly forbids them, and potentially have access to a number of additional Merits and special rules that don’t cross the line into the supernatural. This playstyle is specifically geared to groups who prefer their protagonists to be a cut above the regular mortal, but don’t want to venture into supernaturally empowered mortals.
        • “are they human, or monsters?”, where the hunters are assumed to have access to some sort of Supernatural Merits, and the line between hunter and prey is a blurry one.

        This, rather than “cell, compact, or conspiracy?” is how I prefer to think about Hunter tiers; and “hunters get to keep their memories” strikes me as being a “tier two, but bordering on tier three” thing.

        My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

                I suppose it depends on how supernatural you see resistance to the passive Lie effects. I don't see it as particularly unnatural, since the Lie is itself a supernatural condition and it's already supposed to have a subtle effect on humanity as a whole causing broad - but not universal - avoidance of curiosity into the strange. Since Hunters are, presumably, the kinds of people who have had the blinders ripped off in the first place, going one step further and saying they get to remember magic or werewolves strikes me as reasonable; those effects have to work within the sheltered psychological terrain of the general population and, well, hunters are outside of that. I don't see distinguishing between "normal person" and "person who knows the supernatural exists and may even know specifically that magical spells and werewolves exist" in terms of the effects the passive Lie/Lunacy effects have on them offers a superpower to the latter category. If you're a fifth generation Heritage House hunter trained and taught from birth that magic exists, or if you're the victim of a murderous spell and you've spent years undergoing memory regression therapy to regain your memories of that night...it doesn't strike me as damaging to the themes of Hunter if you'e able to remember magical spells or werewolves.

                Heck, if you want to make it really cruel, you could say that, since the memories don't fade, the Integrity loss is worse for Hunters than it is for regular Sleepers...

                But, really, this is just a metagame thing. It's a pain in the ass to turn every werewolf hunting or witch hunting storyline into a repressed memory memento investigation, and I just think amnesiac hunters are silly unless they're hunting a monster whose whole theme is memory eating.

You can be utterly convinced that magic is real and still succumb to Quiescence’s memory-clouding effect, just as you can be a Sleepwalker who doesn’t believe in magic. To the extent that they correlate at all, Belief follows from being a Sleepwalker, not the other way around. That is, a Sleepwalker will have an easier time believing in the supernatural because it isn’t hidden from him; but even assuming that he does believe (which is not guaranteed), that’s not the cause of him being a Sleepwalker. Likewise, the Sleeping Curse isn’t caused by not believing in magic; instead, you’re less likely to believe in magic if you have no recollections of ever having seen it.                         I didn't mean to insist there was anything wrong with the other way of doing things! I think Hunter, as written, distinguished hunters from the mortals of the core book in an analogous way to how all the supernatural templates work. I like this approach because I like the idea that hunters are out of step with regular people in certain important ways due to their exposure to the supernatural. If you're using Hunter as an expansion to the blue book line, then that kind of "A Hunter has entered the secret world and cannot go back" attitude doesn't fit.

                        I'm hoping the actual rules when we see them have a more elegant way of dealing with this, or maybe when Hunter rolls around we'll see a good workaround. Since "Sleepwalker" is a one-dot merit and presumably can be acquired through experience normally, it also just makes sense for a lot of hunters to be sleepwalkers if they deal with witch-hunting on a regular basis. Which would also imply that such hunters are more likely to awaken, which has interesting consequences for mage-focused conspiracies. Likewise, there could be a tactic that handles this adequately.

                        ​Ironically, I'm not as convinced all active supernatural powers should constitute immunity - just ones of supernal origin. Joe Blow Psychic whose power comes from the ancient occult genetic engineering of the God-Machine doesn't have any particular in-setting justification to not be under the Sleeping Curse, after all. Vampires and even Ghouls are immune, without really any reason given there's no supernal origin to vampirism - it's for crossover support purposes between the major templates and their key servants. And I see the logic to that and how it could extend to Hunters.

                        But, well, it's a Curse, not a matter of belief, so conceivably only in-setting revocations of the Curse should apply.

                        Perhaps the easiest compromise is to borrow Supernatural Tolerance from Skinchangers, turn it into a merit, and say it applies to the Sleeping Curse, Lunacy, etc. Anyone with adequate justification for being hardened to the supernatural might pick it up - psychics, ghouls, hunters, etc.

                                        Originally posted by Lord Jub-Jub View Post
                                        I'm curious as to why there was such emphasis on hiding magic and werewolves from mortals, to my knowledge Vampires don't have anything that naturally does this other than traditions and guidelines.
                                        Werewolf and Mage both deal with magic that operates on much more global forms of contagion, whereas Vampire occupies the awkward split between "'feed responsibly' covers most of the problems a personlike vector for the Curse can cause without deliberately fucking about" and "most of the non-personlike vectors for the Curse are either ignorant of the concept of limiting exposure or actively derisive of it" — there's no consistent secret world of vampirism that threatens to infect the mortal population besides the Kindred and a whole lot of weird bullshit that can't or won't keep the lid on on its own because it's a cursed letter opener or a tainted plot of land or a Strix, so the bulk of the hush-up operations are dealt with on the Kindred end of things through cultural taboos like the Traditions or dedicated conspiracies like the Invictus. Things still leak through the gaps between the boards, because that's the nature of vampirism and the supernatural at large, but needing to secure enough blood to reanimate your corpse until dawn for seven nights a week is generally harm enough and reason enough to try and keep a lid on your unfortunate diet and allergies as one of the Kindred.

                                        Werewolf has some of the same problems as Vampire does, with the added wrinkle of their lunar heritage actively degrading people's ability to resist spiritual influence with repeated exposure and a substantial chunk of the Shadow being sentient, mobile, and disinclined toward their entire species. Add to this the Doylist angle of "werewolf society diverges quite strongly from mortal common knowledge about werewolves and the warping of memories via the primal terror of the void-watcher's children serves as a workable explanation for the lack of useful information." (It's difficult for me to guess at how much this feeds back into Vampire's thing, because I have not done stringent research on where the emergence of the vampire as a gregarious analogue for exploitative nobility as a concept sits relative to the publication of Masquerade, but on an in-universe level globalization and the development of the internet probably work as a reasonable explanation for the greater number of social vampires in modern vampire fiction in a setting where the Kindred exist.)

                                        As for Mage, there's a couple of things, some of them to do with the framework of the game and some of them to do with the way magic is within the setting — the fact that this is a game set in a world that can at least superficially pass for our own has to address the fact that this is also a game whose mages can create a lot of highly visible effects, and this is also a game where magic is generally treated as a sacred thing of a kind with the secret rites of a lot of the cults of antiquity and their modern imitators. Add in the fact that Awakened magic is almost exclusively man-made with minimal vectors for direct Supernal interference with reality, then look at the one big example of direct Supernal interference with reality and note that its conceptual basis comes from a religious format that takes the "sacred things hidden from society at large" in the other direction, add in the force that turns unity with the divine into a supernatural gambling addiction, and you have a pretty decent grounding for Why Magic Is Hidden.

                                        A lot of these notably stem from the common points of contagion and paranoia — "supernatural forces are virulent and antithetical to humanity" and "most humanlike supernatural beings that are designed to be playable have some investment in humanity continuing to exist" go a long way toward not wanting a parliament of shadow-owls to engage in wanton mass-murder in the town square.
