                                  I think any such item would have to have a number of spells with individual on-off triggers like WHW said. A spell that is truly variable, especially with a dial rather than a specific trigger for each spell, is the very definition of the Practice of Dynamics. A Time Machine that flexible would have to be a) a single but likely large object with individual spell functions tied specifically to gears, levers, and knobs (i would say it cant change potency, but CAN, say, decide what moment within the spell's range that you end up in), or b) a Dynamic spell-creation of an archmage at least, with a flexible, changing, reactive, almost self-aware spell inside that can adjust its own spell factors and such. Archmastery's first spell practice is one that is flexible compared to regular magic. With standard magic, each spell is exactly as it says it is. You can add Conditions, adjust factors, imbue it into an object, tickle it with Attainments, but ultijately it is just a single, standard spell. You get what you put into it--conditions and triggers, duration, potency. Each effect, trigger, condition etc has to be a separate spell.

                                  Dynamism points out a very big barrier in terms of magic. In 1e, archmastery was also defined by bringing folks back to life or travelling back in time. In 2e, those barriers no longer exist. But standard magic is really uniform. Your successes don't provide a gradient of effect--that's potency, which is set when you cast the spell. I would suspect in 2e, the real gateway drug of archmagic is reactive, dynamic spells--a spell that adapts to the user and situation, like the time dial on a time machine (complete with selective temporal barrier to keep out weather and environmental changes and such, but allow oxygen yet slow metabolism so you can breathe and not starve to death etc). A really kewl flexible and awe inspiring object usable by Sleepers should take a lot of time, dedication, planning, and clever use of spell control, that'd be at least 2-3 stories figuring out, designing, and implementing for a standard mage. Easy-made flexible time machines with one spell are Archmastery.

                                      Corridors of Time can be Imbued into an object the user can then target himself and send himself back anywhere on his own timeline that he wants to go provided he has the necessary Temporal Sympathy. The things that are set are the Reach (I assume) for how the spell will operate and the Duration for which he'll remain in his own past. You could probably set it up so it only takes its target back to when they're in 5th grade, but the way the spell is written you only require Temporal Sympathy to access an entire timeline. Imbued Items that allow a person to teleport work in a similar fashion. You don't really -need- an interface that changes which locations you go to except as it applies Sympathy to the item's spell.

                                      As for the other examples from the OP, it's obviously just two different spells imbued into one item with settings being used as part of the activation of the item. When the dial on the gun is set one way it meets part of the activation requirements for a death ray and when its set the other way it meets the activation requirements for a stun ray instead.

                                                  Remember that Patterning spells do not merely perform actions but actually grant functions to patterns. If you create a magical prosthetic limb, Potency simply determines the limits of the limb, its maximum Strength and Dexterity for example. However, the user of such limb can perform any number of actions that would be "natural" for it, it doesn't require a separate spell for each possible action or motion the limb can perform, unless the creator of the spell imposed such limitations when designing their Imago. If you turn into another animal with Life, you know how to use all of your limbs and have all the instincts and senses of that animal at your disposal, you don't need one spell per benefit. The same logic can be applied to any other number of spells.
