    Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
    I think he was referring to the line about government out of the need to limit violence between groups that may not have common objectives, rather than Boston specifically.
    2e shifts the emphasis to Convocations. Consilii still perform the role of settling disputes and delivering collective justice, but Convocations deal with larger issues. You might go to one if a Consilium proves to be totally useless in dealing with your magic zoning dispute, but the Convocation would probably deal with it as a structural problem with that Consilium, leaving your actual problem to be settled by any directive. It might not go down this way, since much depends on the actual scenario.

    It should be noted that it takes a lot for a Consilium to be thought of as a failure. Remember that Awakened are capable of governing themselves through oaths, duels and exercising the Great Rights. Failure happens when mages don't do these things and the Consilium doesn't fix this, or the Consilium doesn't effectively respond to threats to the whole. In Boston, the Consilium under the Nemean was effective at making it a very bad idea to break rules, because the punishment for doing that was having to deal with the Consilium. Mages there mostly governed themselves. It was ineffective at dealing with collective threats because of overfed self-interest, the Consilium's lack of proactivity and its focus on one big problem in the form of the Secret Concord. But even the Nemean knew that maybe somebody should do something about rampaging cannibals and such.

            I ran a single player Boston chronicle, taking an established character from another game and inserting them into the Ebon Noose. It didn’t feel like there was any better way to touch on the Secret Concord. The book felt unclear on ways to explore that mystery — just a few side bars here and there. My twist was that the third signatory was a cruel Ananke created by an archmaster who survived his time in Epona’s Chariot, seeking to undo the dread summoning of the Blasphemous Scribe.

            It is a very interesting setting, but it does not seem easy to run at first glance. I felt like I had to do a lot of digging to get a good feel for how everything works together. More than a few NPCs are compelling and can carry stories if fleshed out. Ferrum, for instance, has a lot of potential. He is already an Adamant Sage at a relatively young age. There is some natural conflict with the death of his brother and a growing rift between his cabal and the Brotherhood. In my chronicle, the player mediated a release of the Knights from their vows of service, so that Ferrum would become a Councilor. If the Nemean is waiting for a Sage to defeat him, I think Ferrum would be the most likely NPC to be able to do so, though he might need to be nudged by a PC out of his ennui.

            The Grey Pope and his Pylon makes a terrifying antagonist. His write-up was done before the Seers were fleshed out, which makes it difficult to get a good grasp on how all the Seers are operating. The Consilium’s leaders are based more in Northern Massachusetts than Boston, and the Asylum is a likely obstacle for cabals looking to move the power base south.

            The Leon family would be nice to see more of, as it hints at a large group of sleepwalkers and Proximi, as well as an entire spirit court under their control, judging by Numa’s rotes. My player took an immediate liking to the family, and their creepy antiquated notions of arranged marriages, so I had that tied to an ongoing plot about the Guardians of the Veil’s past dabbling in eugenics.

            Overall, the setting has a nice libertarian feel with regards to the consilium structure. Other mages are not going to snoop into your business too much, but it will be hard to get help for your problems if you don’t have something to offer. Even tackling a problem by having an NPC do something for you will lead to another conflict to enrich the story.

            I’d be interested to see this setting updated for Mage 2e, given that there is a focus more on the various order caucuses. Some caucuses seem lacking compared to how the cabals are described. There are only three Guardians of the Veil in the consilium (Culsu, Februs, and Hades) but there seems to be enough unnamed solitaries in the area to call a caucus to order. It would be great to see these communities fleshed out, and have some brief descriptions on their interactions with neighboring out-of-state cities like Providence, Rhode Island.

                (Forgive my quick rambling that I can't spend overly much time on b/c of more pressing obligations, but I felt compelled to opine.)

                Originally posted by Malcolm View Post
                I think the reaction to the setup in Boston really shows the difference between readers and players. I designed Boston for play. When people approach Boston that way they get a great deal out of it. When they want the book to be something to read about an archetypal/typical city, it's not as useful. Fact is, none of the three original signature cities are really typical.
                This statement is a little (unintentionally) disingenuous as it sets up a false dichotomy between those who read vs. those who play. Many of us read with the intent of integrating those disparate pieces into a framework with which we can visualize and determine the appropriate reactions in play. After all, it's incredibly awkward to have a player highlight an inconsistency or point of order that conflicts with or possibly derails the "narrative-in-process". I've seen it happen to others, and definitely wouldn't want to be the subject of it! Thus, any anomalies that intersects with our gaming plans/planning can become sources of grating consternation that, if unresolvable via heuristics (or worse, complicated by) can lead to the collapse of a game/gaming opportunity, stress, etc.

                Granted, this player vs. reader idea is also entwined with the idea of the Boston being an atypical consilium within the super-setting of Awakening. (And being ditto for other lines' settings.) Yet again, many people are looking for an example to build their own games from - so of course they're going to look at the one official example given for ideas, inspiration and/or baselines... in hopes of playing.
                ---

                Instead of a division b/t gamers and readers, perhaps the split that Malcolm is referring to is a spectrum of analytical vs. ... archetypical? At the extremes you have those who OCDly require internal consistency vs. those who just don't care. Or, close to something that could be of use in reasonable discourse (or at least its casual cousin), people who find enough internal consistency in archetypes, conflicts and motion (and are fine with hand waving away in-setting paradoxes for a variety of reasons, not exclusive of: It's just a game, "cinematographical" or short story influence vs. novels or technical documents, not having cringe reflexes to deus ex machina or 'just cause' reasoning for occurrences, reactions, etc., being prone to improv vs. structured plotting, and more.)

                [Note: I am not deriding the latter pole, especially since they're probably the happier of the two lots. I wouldn't doubt that anxiety, perfectionism and other neuroses drive folks like myself towards the first notion, thus forcing us to waste a lot of time and heartache over minutia, instead of just having fun. At least to a certain extent. YMMV,. Plus, it's just an unvetted brainfart... err, theory.]

                ---

                Of course, we who regularly over-think things often fail at recognizing that (in this specific case) a privileged perch produced our pedantic proscriptions - hindsight. Not all inconsistencies within the supplement were born of reticence, carelessness, or worse - intent. Nor could the authors have Guardians of the Veil or Summoners (etc.) on hand to pull from/check against. Awakening as a platform was still inchoate. Facts are provided from a set of assumptions that either didn't have design-consensus or were still evolving within the creative crucible. "Water colors" are employed where the material touches on these aspects. Loose, blurry but enough to carry a definitive sense. (ex: Orders & Titles)

                Other causes of consternation are rooted in conflicts or concepts that lost prominence or changed by degrees. One example is the whole "Boston Consilium ruled from Salem" thing, but the Consilium comprises member cabals in Gloucester, Cambridge, and possibly more. Or the weird Chain Parris choosing to be a mystagogue being taken as a sign of some sort of acquiesence to the Nemean's control (as opposed to rebellion) and w/little other discourse as to the qualities he or the order possessed which brought the two together. And yet his *thearch* cabalmates think he's upholding the status quo, but want to leave the consilium. Or how the Putnams reconcile religion/Christianity's mandate for humility w/Thearch ideology. (Heck, I'm willing to bet that the only reason the Nemean is a thearch instead of an Arrow is b/c 'ladders lead' - since the Arrows in his cabal have no qualms with emulating him.) Or why the Gravediggers are oathsworn assassins to the Hiearch/Noose(iirc) or whatever. Or the dearth of discourse on the Wampanoag/Algonquin/etc. expression of Atlantis/Diamond/Seers, compared to the myriad material hinting at sleeper-culture/truths-rooted divisions trumping Pentacle. Or the Lex & Gold Laws. Or all the proximi/-like running around. The minimal/implied Seer pyramid of pylons being regional (if not municipal) vs. regional (and the sole ministry represented being (the only ministry mentioned?) in the core book.

                Exacerbating the kinks induced by by being on the wrong side of Awakening's maturation is the simple fact that, let's face it, some details/decisions just needed to be made and numbers needed to be rounded out- and coming up with a cast of cabals is much tougher than, say, kindred luminaries. . Like the Chaplain confusion, the Noose's weird lack of a potent acanthus; the lackluster libertine lineup; the Shadow Chorus having two mystagogue members for whatever reason. Or how Arathnos and/or Thanatos ever got into the Silver Ladder. Hades and his Wisdom 8. Hydra. Thanatos. Silentium. Or the lack of historical cabal rosters despite the supposed big deal of certain cabal's longevity. Like to the Nemean's predecessor, who is likely a thearch magister - or better yet, the Ebon Noose's lack of any one belonging to the cabal prior to the Nemean's rise to leadership. (Tiamat and Ahriman are refugees from other cabals circa the 90s? Then the Ax. The rest are basically newbs. That's ~ 30 years of the Nemean, the guy he replaced and ... ??? The Putnams have a similar quirk revolving around .. Jonas(?) but not mentioning Chain's predecessor, or etc.)

                But I digress... and I'm sure I'm missing/misremembering a lot, too. The important point is, the root of a lot of the confusion is the natural disadvantage of coming out at the front end of a platform doesn't nail its essential non-mechanical particulars down prior to release. With the exception of certain decisions whose wisdom eludes me/I respectfully disagree with, I'm willing to bet that if the setting was being fleshed out for the first time at the back end of Awakening 1.0, a lot of those prickly particulars arising from the details would never have been. (I'm seeing the Putnams as a GoV/Hieromagus-leaning aparatus, an Ebon Noose whose initial protectorate dictate leads to its sublimation from community oriented / atoning SLs to rugged frontiersmen AAs who think true strength comes from independence or somesuch (or alternatively, pulling together & emphasizing two bits the Ebon Noose's background flavor text: namely it being a loose confederation of cabals and its being whispered by some of Salem's occultists - make it a Cryptopoly under the management of a pseudo-lineage succession cabal(s)/alliance; Native American themed elements like lost temples/outposts/rando gold laws, Raptor-pylon-cults; maybe even aligning or preluding the Nameless Wars with 1776-1812 somehow - could maybe make a better tale for a more thematic uprooting of the Stone Assembly; and a lot more Paternoster. Reworking the Pentad's ingress into Boston involing a proximi-bloodline-maintenance courting-like-b/t Khumeia & Numa's broods. Certainly, at some point there could be some toying with that weird alternate Scales of whatnot governing option in either the ancient, pre-colonial or post-revolution eras. Maybe adjusting the red word away from the time-stuff and more towards the idea of the bounds of a sleeping hunger being loosened when the Tri-Hill or whatever area was filled in and disrupted the ley lines.

                ... ugh, must stop digressing. NOT ENOUGH TIME!

                                                While this is true, I think Michael's broader point still stands - and is an important one to keep in mind when developing a 'setting supplement' (regardless of system):
                                                • The general audience for such books tends to be STs (GMs, etc.) (Although some players may just want to see the 'stories', etc.)
                                                • STs (etc.) who are confident in their setting-building-fu aren't going to feel the need to purchase a setting (except to mine for ideas, save time, or to fulfill a semi-plottish interest)

                                                This means that the primary purchasing audiences for such a book are (typically) looking to minimize the time investment and/or have their hand held on the setting front OR are curious about things from some sort of plot perspective.

                                                The value of variation and fluidity in a pre-assembled setting is seen as a way for the ST to put their own spin on things, as well as preserving certain 'mysteries' for instances where a potential player has digested the published setting material. But, by the above standards, purchasing a setting book that says, "Do it yourself" for the linchpin conceits can feel like a slap in the face (or, more likely, frustration & a waste of $). This is why I like the VII approach - it maintained the toolbox utility while providing ready-to-use inserts for the antagonists. Of course, the problem with the VII approach is that one only has three options to choose from. Striking a balance is probably the way to go for future endeavors.

                                                Just because someone can come up with something great on their own, doesn't mean they want to - or even have the time to do so.

                                                            OK, I'm at work, so I'll toss my ideas around in no particular order:

                                                            * Regarding the Devourers of the Flesh, there's a cabal of cannibal arrows in my planned chronicle. The two founding members of this cabal were senior Noose arrows that came too close to the truth about the Secret Concord and too fond of human flesh to keep it up with the witches' farce. (What happened with the other senior Noose members, asked Phil? Here's my answer )

                                                            * I'll go check both the Sublime for the Boston Marathon reference and the Ancient because I think Culsu could be a fine Thread-cutter (Her and Hydra, even implying a secret relationship between the two).

                                                            * Apollonia went Vidanti after the death of Ferrum's brother ('cause they were lovers in my mind, and her being devastated and heartstruck is the only explanation I could conceive for staying behind in the order).

                                                            * The Subtle Ones have operated in the Massachusetts Bay colony almost since its inception, attracted to the promised 'Shining city on the Hill' and manipulating puritan culture to better accept the sacred work they were intending for the region. However, Epona's Chariot curse on Salem and Danvers and an untimely defection of a Subtle One to the Seers of the Throne meant their ambitious plans were torn apart. The Antinomian Controversy (http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antinomian_Controversy ) is seen by the legacy as evidence of that failure. Nowadays the Seers Subtle Ones outnumber the original legacy. Jeremiah and now Simon belong to the original Ahl-i-batin line, but there are at least half a dozen members spread along New England, New York state and Montreal still thwarting the efforts of their wayward brothers and sisters.

                                                            * Did you notice the Order leaderships are absent or seriously downplayed in the book? With the exception of Potestas with Order Status 5, no other mage have order status that high. Ferrum has Arrow status 4, Culsu has Guardian status 4, and both The Nemean and Davy Jones have Order status 3 in their respective orders. That could mean that either there is a weak leadership all around the region, or that the setting could be retconned by placing some caucus leaders to fill in the upper echelons and bring the setting in line with the 2nd edition changes. What do you people think about this?

                                                            * I'm pondering about the local Seers hierarchy and the overarching Iron Pyramid of the New England Tetrarchy. I've been following the Iron Pyramid thread postings ( http://forum.theonyxpath.com/forum/m...r-iron-pyramid), and I think I'll go for masonic titles with a dash of neocapitalist, governmental red-tape trappings. Still a work in progress, though.

                                                            * One interesting avenue of exploration, setting-wise, is the relationship between the American Independence War, the American Civil War and the birth of the Nameless orders that would eventually coalesce into the Free Council at the turn of the 20th century. In particular I'm thinking about the role the Ebon Noose covenant played in all of these, since it was a loose confederation of disenfranchised witches that rejected the Stone Assembly consilium yet didn't care much for the grander libertarians ideals of the time. It could even be possible at one time that the core Ebon Noose cabal didn't have any Diamond mages at all in their roster.

                                                            * For the Mysterious Dynasty of Proximi that Numa belongs to, I'm thinking about a Spirit-worshipping cult in Ipswich tended by these Proximi, with the heritage cabal leader at the top of the cult ritually assuming the mantle of head of the spirit court (that's why Numa has the rote, BTW, he has to continue the legacy). The spirits Numa rules over are ancestor spirits and knowledge spirits of ancient Atlantis, or at least some the earlier refugee bloodlines that survived the cataclism of the Time Before. So, while he's in Ipswich he's found that the spirit wilds are behaving strange, and the Wood of Empty Houses could be a powerful lure for him that could allow him to contact more spirits from ancient Atlantis.

                                                            * Khumeia's Proximus family has a long and bloody feud with the Seers of the Throne from France, particularly against the Ministry of Paternoster. I'm tempted to use this Silver Dynasty as a Dan Brown homage of occult caretakers of the royal blood of Atlantis, hoping to reinstate the "Blood of the Dragon" heir in her rightful throne once Hieraconis finally comes. However, I'm not yet sold on that particular idea. Besides, I'm not sure what could they be doing in Boston anyway. Any suggestions?
