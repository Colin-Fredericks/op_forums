    Originally posted by Falcon777 View Post
    I think people who have a problem with mages being strong are missing a CORE theme of Mage: Hubris.
    How is that anytime that one of this thread happen, people come and start throwing the old "you must not be getting it" as if the option were "getting it and loving it" and "not getting it". I think some people here forget that one can get the theme of hubris of the game and either think is not well done (me, for example) or just not subscribe to it.

    Originally posted by Falcon777 View Post
    Honestly, if you want to do something that goes specifically against game design you probably shouldn't go to that game's forums to seek the creativity to change the game. Sure, some people will help you, but if you don't know how to change the game on your own then you might not know the game well enough to appreciate why it was made the way it was made.
    What place for example is there to discuss hacks to Mage with people that play mage?

    Originally posted by maryshelly View Post
    I see that a common opinion is that Mages are overpowered compared to the other splats. Is this ok or will it seriously unbalance the game if mages are allowed to stomp around the World of Darkness as the most powerful beings around. Should there be some ways to bring them in line with everyone else? Here are some ideas that I just brainstormed

    1/ Don't allow rotes so easily with some really powerful spells, psychic domination comes to mind. Maybe allow the rote later on with learning, but not readily available at character creation
    2/ Don't allow 3 dice with a willpower point when casting a spell
    3/ Allow other supernaturals to use supernatural tolerance to resist spells
    4/ Don't allow 1 free potency if potency is the nature of the spell ( I recall a rule about that)

    Just some ideas, what are your thoughts?
    I think that the problem you are having is that your goal is too broad. "Balance mage", thats to aimlessly of a goal to properly put into practice. Option 3 and 1 are fine but the rest are meh as balance goes. Instead start small adressing small issues.

    For example when i houserule mage my main issue is "I wanna run urban fantasy and that mages and beyond arent the only thing threatening to mages" similar to your objective that is too broad of a objective so instead i broke it into small concern and i adress them each and everyone. "the difference between arcanum 2 and 3 and so on is too big of a gap", "time magic being able to rewind a whole session worth of scenes without issue", "the game favoring casting from your sanctum instead of face to face".

    So on the difference on arcanum i went with not allowing players to freely raise arcanum past a certain point in each chapter. On the time magic thing i am trying to port permanent paradox do as thing to say to the player "oh sure you can go back 1 scene.....but it gonna cost you A LOT so use it wisely" and for the face to face issue i make that casting symphatetic doesnt give you the exceptional successes unlike face to face but that the aiming roll can add bonus to the roll (which balances itself with the fact that the player will have small gnosis + arcanum pools). And so on.

    Point being, segregate your issues with the power of mage in small issues to be adressed individually. Is a longer approach than bigger all emcompassing houserules but it allows you to think them over and carefully consider each and every single one.

                                                    Originally posted by Wormwood View Post
                                                    I agree with Mage being different, but I wouldn't say it has no actual horror. The things you can, and are liable to, do can be pretty horrific in and off themselves, and unlike some other splats, you cannot claim "my inner Beast made me do that" or "I have no choice, I need to do this to survive". The horrible things you do? "I wanted to see if I can do that". Some Rick Sanchez level of justification, there.
                                                    Hypothetically, sure, but the game does a terrible job highlighting and emphasizing those things. I don't dispute "Mages can abuse people! Abuse is scary! Therefore, Mage is scary!" is at least a coherent position to take, but we are talking about a game where a fifth of all player characters can start with Kilgrave-levels of mind control that never talks about rape. We're talking about a game that declares every character is obsessed with one or more occult mysteries, but never goes into any discussion of how obsessions like that might interfere with day-to-day activities, health or sanity. A mage in Unknown Armies is obsessed; a mage in Mage is just really interested in something. It might be dangerous, sure, but so is parkour.

                                                    We're talking about a game that spends most of its attention on whiz-bang magical adventures against fantastic adversaries. We're talking about a game that never once declares itself a horror game. So, yeah, you can talk about hubris and the self-destructive temptation of power, but there's no teeth to any of that. Look at the Wisdom mechanics, for example - it's actually incredibly easy to avoid the major wisdom sins and still get what you want, because the magic system is so low-cost, powerful, and flexible. Vampire made it genuinely difficult to both survive and keep your humanity; the whole axis of the game revolves around that dilemma. Mage claims something similar in its trichotomic dilemma of "Engage the Mysteries, Survive, Preserve Wisdom" but it doesn't really make it that hard.

                                                    This is coming from personal experience, by the by, when I ran a Mage game that I deemed largely successful, I felt I had to be unusually punitive as a storyteller in emphasizing the darkness, danger, and occult consequences of the player's actions. "Unusually" in that I felt I was working against the game in introducing horror elements, not following its lead. In actual play, it felt a lot more like an Ars Magica game than it did Unknown Armies. That is, it felt more like a fantasy game than a horror game.

                                                    Heck, Ars Magica has more baked-in with the Warping mechanic than Mage does and Ars Magica isn't remotely a horror game. There's a reason the Abyss took such a huge backseat in 2E (well, wordcount) - because it's not trying to be a horror game the way, say, Vampire is.

                                                    If you look at the genre tropes, Mage fits urban fantasy. You're playing as Harry Dresden, not John Constantine. Constantine routinely pays horrific prices and dances with dangerous forces; Dresden just occasionally burns down a building. Both can be described as "hubris" but only one is horror.

                                                    I'll say that arguably Mage tries for horror and lands on urban fantasy, but I'm not sure it's really aiming at horror to begin with. I feel like some people are seeing this and thinking I'm shitting on Mage; I'm just explaining my theory as to why it doesn't fit with the CofD in terms of crossover. It's a perfectly cromulent urban fantasy game.

If you want no holds barred chronicles, the ST can come at you with a veritable shit-storm of entities you've pissed off during the course of your frolicking. Just because you're a mage doesn't mean you're equipped for any and all threats you could attract. There are tons of stories of careless, short-sighted, cocky, cavalier mages that became the impetus for an investigation.

                                                        Mages have the tools to know stuff about stuff, but they don't have it all figured out. You play a game where you're obsessed by Mysteries - things that aren't solved and have the potential to be exceedingly dangerous if not approached tactfully.

                                                        The ST has license to run mage like a hyper-vindictive DM, where an insta-gib trap or condition lies in wait at every turn.

                                                        Horror in mage is also about knowing how limited you are. Knowing how powerful other things are or could potentially be.

                                                        You just figured out you have enormous freedom to do whatever you want! But you also found out you're not the only one! You also found out you're already outclassed when you Awaken! You also found out the people who outclass you are still outclassed by others!


                                                        Just as easily as you can manipulate and play with things, other entities can do the same to you.

                                                        Some of the other splats work in vast networks, too. Oh, look, a vampire is no threat to you - you must have it all figured out - so you cream them and go on about your business. That couldn't possibly end up bad for you and your loved ones.



                                                        A common misconception about power and versatility is thinking you don't have anything to lose. You likely will have things to lose. The classic example is thinking you're unstoppable, until someone reminds you that they can wear your mother's skin or turn your dear old dad into a sex-slave.

                                                        You have options, but so does everything else. Except they're not necessarily tied to resources the same way you are.

                                                        In mage, because you solve Mysteries, you're also acting as vanguard or frontiersman. You're trying to figure it out where either a) others couldn't or b) no one has tried before. Spoiler alert: there is always room to fuck it up.


                                                        There is a whole system for descending into madness as a consequence of being careless or unlucky - there is also a system that acknowledges breaking points, or the fact that you can be bothered by specific things even though you're "all-powerful."


                                                        You're free to believe it isn't something you find personally scary, but there are plenty of reasons people do find it unsettling, terrifying, unnerving, etc.



                                                        I know if mages existed, and I met one, I'd be both hyper curious and simultaneously scared of what they could do to me intentionally or otherwise. People don't always mean what they say, or mean for the consequences of their actions. Things don't always play out in your favor.

                                                        The same is true for playing mage.

                                                        It is your prerogative to play Mage like it is a superhero game, but that doesn't mean it works that way.


                                                        Music can also go a long way to make you unsettled, even as a mage.


                                                        You do the work and mage can be scary, but if you just hand every piece of info away or make every conflict play out in a predictable or lackluster way, then of course - mage becomes "not scary."

                                                        That isn't the game's fault, though.

                                                            Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                                            Pff. Yeah, sure. The best and worst of humanity exploded into hyperfocus, all of the petty travesties and grotesque mis-decisions writ cosmic and "There's little to no actual horror in Mage."

                                                            Mages are terrifying-terrifying to be around and terrifying to be. And a significant part of that horror is the amount of people who don't catch what makes Mage horrifying.

                                                            This. This is the point, and it's one of the reasons why Mage plays so badly with (or next to) other splats. Their players are off worrying about problems that are truly trivial to mages, while mage players are trying to hammer their minds into the box of "how utilitarian a god will I be today?" All while dicing (literally) with a reality that would very much like to have a word with them...!

                                                            The tonal disconnect, from splats about body horror and loss of control and the like--fairly mundane acting-out of our terrors, to one of a higher plain of awareness of humanity and how you can break it, is why Mage is both a very good game and very hard to pull off right. And there is a "right" to mage, as otherwise you are playing superpower sub-genres, Cthulu investigators with powers, or Kult-lite (for a few examples).

                                                            The point of the game is asking "you can now solve that unsolvable problem" answered by "well, poop" after doing so, interspersed with periods of banal academic effort and making a damn fine tea with your mind.

                                                            So, Mage is really weird horror, and I'll admit after 20 years to still having a hard time playing, let alone storytelling, in it.
