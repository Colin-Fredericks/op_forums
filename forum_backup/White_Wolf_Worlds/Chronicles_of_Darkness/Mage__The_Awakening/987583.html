                  Hello there, I'm your friendly neighbourhood Anthropology student! As it just so happens, I'm currently taking a course called "Myth, Magic, and Shamanism" which focuses on different approaches in various cultures to the sacred supernatural, societal rituals, and how those things intersect. Conveniently, it's turning me into a significantly better Mage player because when you sit in this mindset for a while you can tease apart symbols in ritual with absolutely laughable ease. One of our in-class assignments was to come up with a myth and a ritual based on said myth, and our prof plucked at least half a dozen of what Mage would term Yantras out of the invented rituals with ease.

                  The Awakened are, by and large, what anthropological terminology would classify as a Shaman. Technically speaking, a Shaman is a part-time religious specialist whose position and authority in a society is predicated on personal contact and relationship with the sacred supernatural, demonstrated through effective rituals. Some seek out the calling of Shaman, but most are chosen by different spiritual beings, or called in a dream. Usually these new Shamans are thoroughly unhappy about this, as they know from cultural immersion in this topic that being a Shaman is dangerous work that could potentially involve death and/or having their soul eaten if things go badly. Nonetheless, they eventually find themselves a teacher who trains them in the ways of their art, these relationships are often volatile, as the student typically doesn't want to be there, and the master usually does't like the student, but they persist and eventually the Shaman is trained and initiated in a ritual that involves symbolic death and resurrection, making the Shaman a new person. Shamans are 'on call' in the sense that they're basically a normal member of their social group, doing normal person things, and then the supernatural intrudes on everyday life and somebody needs a divination, or a healing, or a soul needs to be stolen back from a spirit or angry ghost, and then the Shaman hat comes on. Generally, to help separate their 'work' life from their 'civilian' life, these Shamans have ritual personas, letting the spirits and the people know when they're on the clock.

                  So, from a cultural stand-point, Shadow Names are hard-baked into the idea of 'Magic Practitioner as Member of Society' (Note Important Capital Letters, Wizards Like Important Capital Letters) and in Mage: The Awakening this is additionally good practice due to the metaphysical weirdness of the long-term Nimbus, all the awful things Sympathetic magic can do to you (a real-world concern for serious practitioners as well, and an equally solid motivation behind the person), and a way of letting others- Awakened or otherwise -know you're on the clock, as it were.

                  TLDR- Even in the real world, Shadow Names are a thing for those who practice magic in their communities. The idea and the mantle of Practitioner of the Art as a separate person from Bob-who-knows-some-Magic go hand-in-hand as far back as anyone can tell, even to shamanistic cave drawings.

                                      Calling yourself Thrice-Great Hermes would seem a bit impious to the Ancients---even from another land, if they knew what you were claiming.


                                      There's a rather large difference between calling yourself "The Great Thunderhand" when your hero who held that title dies and calling yourself "Liu Bu". The attitude of the ancients to those stealing names was pretty negative... and I mean in most cultures I've studied, not just western ones. Sure, the Mastigos are all about picking that sort of fight, but it seems pretty weird for an Obrimos to violate honesty norms, of all things.

                                      Taking noble or ruling names, though, has a very time honored tradition, especially when stepping into ceremonially significant roles. (say, becoming a noble, stepping up to rule a house, or even becoming a general) Changing one's name when adopted, as well, and I don't just mean just the patronymic.

                                      I'd suggest that taking Shadow names is ancient, but the sorts of names taken has shifted wildly and claiming the names of ancient figures of legend has increased over time, while choosing meaningful (for various mystical reasons) names that are more original or are descriptive titles has decreased. (See Werewolf: The Forsaken (or Apocalypse) and Fullmetal Alchemist for examples of what I'm saying.)

                                                      Imhotep actually means, in Ancient Egyptian, "one who comes in peace". But, if you want to go with what he is known for (and became god of) then Ptah is a good choice, especially since said god was rumoured to be Imhotep's father. There's also Kothar, the Levantine god who is somewhat analogous to Ptah. Nuwa is a Chinese goddess who was known for creating mankind, and repairing the Pillar of Heaven with five coloured stones representing the five base elements of the world, and she is occasionally depicted as a dragon.
