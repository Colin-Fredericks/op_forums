    Originally posted by Axelgear View Post
    Hence, if it is a moral judgement, it requires a unified moral system on which to base its judgements. One cannot declare something right or wrong without a basis for what is right or wrong.

    Since Mage has largely eschewed any sense of objective morality and neglected deliberately to provide such a code - indeed, there is an explicit statement that Wisdom is at least somewhat subjective - that leaves us with an incomplete system.

    When is it right to use magic? When is it wrong? How do we adjudicate? Is it subjective? If so, why is any act ever unWise? If it's objective, what determines it?

    That is why I can't accept Wisdom at present: It is incomplete. It tells you to make judgements but not how or why to make them and that is ultimately detrimental; it is a gateway to pointless arguments at the table and to watering down a game of grand philosophical questions into bland, conventional answers.

    Incidentally, I'm a hard determinist and shun the notion of free will. Not relevant but there you go.
    I just here quote sidebar in Silver Ladder book - it's 1E, but I think it's really relevant to 2E Wisdom idea.

    Originally posted by Silver Ladder, p. 39
    Morality versus Wisdom
    When you read about the Silver Ladder’s beliefs — Thunder in particular — you might ask yourself how they stack up to the cold realities of the Mage setting, especially when it comes to Wisdom. At first glance, it looks like any orthodox théarch would lose Wisdom at an alarming rate by refusing to moderate magic with humility. Silver Ladder characters can’t get a look at their own sheets but they’d still notice that Paradoxes linger a little longer when they repeatedly throw caution to the wind.
    The Ladder does in fact have a very rough idea of Wisdom’s laws and consequences but it’s no evidence they’re wrong. It’s just a symptom of the Fallen World, inseparable from Paradoxes and Quiescence. Thus, magical acts that risk Wisdom loss aren’t necessarily immoral — but then again, they aren’t automatically moral, either. To put it in concrete game terms, a théarch blames Wisdom risks that result entirely from magic on the Lie. Anything that would provoke a Morality check in a human is still a personal failing, whether or not magic had a role in it.
    In Mage, Morality ( and morality ) is Fallen World construct - there are not 'good' and 'bad' actions - there are only things that are permitted and forbidden by Exarchs. Want to know what actions would be Acts of Hubris? Think on each 'Would Exarchs be pissed off on me for it?' - Chances are high that those with unforeseen consequences or letting magic sling right and left will be the 'worst' from Exarchial point of view. Here is your 'morality' of magic in CoD universe.

    Imagine that Exarchs want's everyone mage on planet to be Seer of Throne - if you are not playing by their rules, you break Wisdom, i.e. make Acts of Hubris.

                        Originally posted by Pale_Crusader View Post

                        Because people who are "in control" don't spend weeks planning to kill 25 people? If that doesn't work try this paraphrase: People who are "in control" don't kill 25 people. Something has gone off the rails somewhere, most likely in their own brain. And 25 people dying is going to cause a lot of chaos and chaos precludes true control.                         So a military officer who carefully plans a strike on a bunker isn't in control? They're off the rails in their own heads?

                        Can you really think of no circumstances in which killing 25 people may not be a just and appropriate moral choice, either? Just because we, in our somewhat safe and comfortable corner of the world, never have to consider such a thing doesn't mean that all possible situations in all possible worlds are the same. Moral judgments like "it's always wrong to kill" are just as subject to disagreement as any other.

                        Let's take a hypothetical -
                        A slave in 19th century America Awakens and plots the deaths of 25 slave owners in a given area.
                        At what level is this not hubristic?

                        One might argue that simply killing them without planning is hubris, because there could be a massive backlash that leads to great harm against the populace (though I would point out that this requires certain axioms to be in place. You have to assume a basic level of morality in order to consider harm negative.)

                        What if he extensively planned his act, though? He accounts for the actions of their families, he casts ambiguity over whether or not the deaths were accidental, he times things so that the maximal number of slaves can escape as a result or even personally selects their replacements so that they're kinder masters who he subtly compels to manumit their slaves and adopt different practices, he sticks around to make sure neighbors don't retaliate or make examples of their own slaves.

                        A lot of good can come from killing the right kind of person.

                        On the flip side, "morally positive" actions (which @Axel rightly points out is a fraught term) could easily result in acts of hubris. And what about inaction? Is it always Wise not to act? Or is it hubris to withhold your greater power for others in fear that you might cause unintended consequences? Believe me, not acting can also lead to unintended consequences.

                        We don't allow mages to cast spells, since this is the most unbalancing rule of all.

                                        Again - what's inherently more moral about not killing? Saying that there's always a nonviolent option is cute, but nonviolent solutIons require a different sort of follow-up and aren't always suitable for all situations.

                                        We don't know the capabilities of this mage or their training, we don't know the exact circumstances of what their resources are, what their support is like, what their needs are, what time constraints they're under, so saying "well, they should do this nonviolently!" borders on childish. Terminal violence against an oppressor can be both moral and justified if it is the least harm needed to produce the needed result... but sometimes that still involves violence, and not everyone agrees that killing is the worst thing ever.

                                        What about the Awakened Wolf-Blooded, who comes from a family of Iron Masters, who has watched her family hunt and kill human beings for the good of the greater organism? To them, death and life are intertwined, and they wouldn't see the value in unnecessarily prolonging a life that is spent harming the public good. They may even believe in reincarnation and that starting life anew is better than attempting to correct bad growth, and that there is no great difference between being living and dead at all. Can that be hubris, forcing your will over another person? Sure, totally, but any action to forcibly alter circumstances for other people involves that on some level. The only meaningful distinction between slamming a drug lord in a Space prison and killing him for the boarder public is essentially non-existent if he doesn't return on an immediate timescale.
                                        Keep in mind that transformation and binding have costs - you need to keep an eye on a bound person, make sure no one ever finds your transformed person. If you lock a Seer up instead of killing him, you run the risk of him getting free and wreaking more havoc than if you'd killed him. You or someone else needs to invest time and energy in protecting him, but is that really a wise use of your resources?
                                        Efficiency has a value in morality when not having to devote resources to a thing means that you can engage in other acts, and killing someone is an extremely efficient method of keeping them from coming back. If you can have magical certainty of their guilt and there's no real way to rehabilitate them especially. Their culling mentality is perfectly moral from their perspective because they have limited resources and so must choose options that produce better results. This needn't mean that they're random, either - they can take just as much care in consequence, managing the aftermath.
                                        That's an alien wisdom, but there it is.

                                        In the real world, we find absolute certainty hard to come by and so execution is rarely moral, but even in the real world we sometimes find violence morally justifiable and necessarily.

                                                            Integrity, Humanity, Harmony & Wisdom, in 2ed none of these are actually about morality or questions of right or wrong, they are about actions and events that move the character in question further away from the person they used to be and they are not always seen as negative, just look at Mummy were Memory moves them away from unquestioning servants towards self determination.
                                                            ​Axelgear had it right when he said murder is on the list because for that individual it pushes them outside their safe little bubble of who they see themselves to be (or what the developers believe to be human nature). In the God Machine adaptation rules it makes a point that for a hardened police officer the sight of brutal murder has little effect, while a suburban dweller has a breakdown after accidentally running over a squirrel with their car.
                                                            ​A person with low Integrity/Humanity/Harmony or Wisdom isn't actually insanely dysfunctional, they have just become so removed from what we think of as humanity that they no longer see things the way we do.
                                                            ​For a Mage Wisdom loss isn't about evil, it is about changing from being human to honestly seeing yourself as godlike. It is little different from those in positions of power who stop seeing people and only see statistics.
