    What is your playstyle for Mage?

    05-26-2022, 09:29 AM
    I've noticed there seems to be 2 different play-styles in the mage community and I want to see how common each is. For lack of better terms I'll call it "wide" and "narrow" play.

    "Wide" play I'm defining for this discussion as when the players and STs treat the sphere system as more open ended with a lot of overlap. For example: A wide ST could allow the Life to manipulate a person's emotions and sanity via manipulating hormones, but it would be objectively worse at it than mind(it takes life 5 to not mentally devolve into an animal if you shape-ship into one and Life 6+ to prevent the same when shapeshifting others, but the same thing can be pulled of with a rote using life 4 and the mind sphere).
    • Pros: players have more freedom to come up with interesting effects and this can lead to more interesting stories.
    • cons: Obviously this can get out of hand because player can easily become Much more powerful at lower sphere levels. I've played games like this where the Original end boss was killed way too early because the ST underestimated how creative his players could get.

    "Narrow" play I'm defining for this discussion as when players and STs remove any overlap between sphere and require multi-sphere effects for any complex effect. For example: A "narrow" play ST may require the time sphere as well as the life sphere for any effect that makes the mage younger, even if the effect was for creating a new body rather than reversing their own age).
    • Pro: This makes it Much easier to pace and balance a story, making it possible to plan out more of the campaign.
    • Cons: This can get very Rail-Roady and annoying, Especially if the ST in question thinks their interpretation is the "one true canon". I've spoken with STs who've argued until they were blue in the face that the Forces sphere was required for any kind of telekinesis style effect, until I showed them the page number and references in Every mage core book from 2nd to 20th that specifically said Forces, Matter, and Correspondence could do that(Most of them then doubled down and said Their table require forces and continued to say by that theirs was "the right way" to rule on that).
    There are likely a thousand other ways to look at this(some hermetic in the distance is likely ranting about paradigms as I type this), so If anyone has any other playstyle trends they've noticed feel free to bring them up.

      Wide. I'm a big believer that the purple Paradigm is just a way to represent the system of the game. Not the valid way all things have to break down into.

      To summarize, I read on SV from earthscorpion,that the purple paradigm is basically treating the rules of the game as the way the setting "really" works. M20 is very much written from this perspective - the version I prefer to run, which is Revised-era, very much doesn't.

      Under the purple paradigm, when you throw a fireball at someone, you're using Forces 3 to make a fireball and justifying it with invocations of the angel Gabriel. Under the version I uses, there is a person who can invoke the angel Gabriel to call heavenly fire down on someone, and this is represented in the game mechanics by them having "Forces 3" on their character sheet. Mostly only Hermetics talk about the Nine Great Spheres (and people trying to use the Traditions' magical Esperanto to work out how to get their ritual working and get their paradigms working together).

      Hence, under the purple paradigm, Traditionalists basically know all along that they're using their Avatar to warp reality and then justifying it with whatever they believe in. This has the major problem that it means that any historical figure who was a mage didn't really believe in their ideas, and just hid the truth from the Sleepers.

      Rejecting the purple paradigm, however, means that Newton could do various alchemical acts, forms of advanced mathematics which let him predict things, and magical things involving light using lenses and crystals. This is represented by him having Matter 4, Forces 3, Time 2, etc on his character sheet, but he probably didn't think about it this way. He could just do these things because he'd studied them. His mathematics and optics would be accepted into the later Consensus and so he's respected for them, but his belief in alchemy is laughed at and used as an example of how even figures as influential as he was can be wrong about things.

          I prefer the wide approach. My go-to approach for reining in abuse is to insist on more successes if you don't have the “right” Spheres for an Effect; though that's more of a guideline than a rule. Basically, if the player's explanation for why his choice of Spheres makes sense to me (whether or not it matches the rules as written), I assign the usual number of required successes for the task (two, by default); if it feels a bit off, I might allow it but call for twice as many successes. That usually means that the Effect is going to require a ritual, as there aren't many mages with sufficient Arete to reliably achieve 4+ successes on a single roll; so in practice, this leans a bit into the “narrow play” in that there's some disincentive to use non-standard sphere choices.

          My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

              Middle?

              I mean, I would generally approach thing as "wide," but not beyond the scope laid out in the books. You can use Life to mess with people's hormones... the books even tell you what that does. I'm not going to let Life go beyond that; the edges between the Spheres should be plenty blurry, but there should also be some clear areas where something is under a given Sphere and not in the hazy middle between it and the others. I've also always been a strong proponent of the PBD approach, which means multi-Sphere requirements is a natural to my playstyle.

              Getting too "wide" makes the game impossible to run, and too "narrow" makes the game too restrictive to enjoy.

              Maybe I should just call my style the "Awakening" style, because I think Awakening 2e hit it out of the park with finally managing to make Mage magic work as a game tool in a fashion Ascension has so far never really done, even if Ascension will continue to be one of my favorite lore/setting things of all time. It makes sure each Arcanum is good enough as a self-contained power to be fun for "wide" magic, but the general rules ensure that there's far greater consistency in what does what that appeals more to the "narrow" approach's advantages.

                              For what it's worth, my understanding is that the reason why Forces are the way they are in M20 has to do with a quirk in the magic system: the higher the Sphere requirement for the Effect, the fewer successes you're likely to get. That means that if you try to create a hurricane, you'll be better off using Forces 3 to do it than Forces 5: Forces 3 is Difficulty 8, while Forces 5 is Difficulty 10. And because the final Difficulty can't be reduced more than -3, the best you can do is Difficulty 5 vs. Difficulty 7.

                              Personally, I ignore that cap; I want my players loading up on the negative Magical Modifiers, to a minimum of Difficulty 3; so that means that the higher Difficulty of Forces 5 translates to more effort put into making the Effect… effective, rather than on the dice pool being less effective
