    Originally posted by Aleph View Post
    However, some Premiers aren't coherent with such trait as described in WoD.

    Like, say, the Premier that's described as a scroll of martial arts seems to imply that you need to do the forms perfectly in order to Awaken. Muscle memory and body intelligence seems to have more to do with that than being a good intelectual... and, really, WoD Intelligence isn't good to represent "non conventional" forms of Int, like the one involved in learning MA. WoD Int seems to be the intelectual kind of Int, that's not really that important for many Paradigms.

    The restriction, if any, should be Paradigmatical. I can see Hermetic grimoires needing Int. Others could require other Traits, like Dex or Skills like Emphaty or Science...

    Probably having a skillset as a gate would represent better these limitations than Atributes. I can't see someone understanding a typical Hermetic Grimoire regardless of their Int 5, if they don't have also some Knowledge to understand the simbology used. Science would make sense for a Premier that's trying to teach you the secrets of basic enligthened science. Skills tend to be more "paradigmatical" than Atributes. Of course, there's still a relation with Atributes: Like, how did you got Science 4 with Int 1? - you technically can do that, but most likely you have some int.
    I'm going to argue here that a certain degree of Intelligence is nevertheless indispensable in all cases, even though I would agree that there are some Primers that should probably require more than simply Intelligence, if only by dint of the fact that properly executing the practices set forth in the some Primers probably require some basic understanding or capacity with another skill or knowledge.

    For a martial arts scroll that can confer enlightenment, I would agree than an inability to actually perform the katas (or whatever) within the scroll would be dispositive of fulfilling one of the basic requirements of benefiting from the Primer: affirmative incorporation and performance of the teachings contained within (to paraphrase my earlier post above). I won't belabor this because I don't think I'm disagreeing with you on this point. And while I think that's a valid gate, I think the "minimum Intelligence 4" -- or really, some minimum Intelligence (because I don't want to get caught up on the number necessarily, so much as I'm interested in discussing why a relatively above-average intelligence is necessary in all cases) -- is also perfectly valid.

    Being "Awake" is matter of knowledge and understanding--not "Knowledge" as contemplated by the character sheet (i.e., a bunch of facts about a particular subject that you have at your fingertips)--but more broadly knowing; you know and understand/comprehend something about the fundamental nature, and functional operation, of reality far beyond that of other mortal people. While the word "Arete" is subject to various interpretations (it's most basic and direct translation is "excellence"), it is broadly understood to be directly tied to "knowledge", and is often used by classical philosophers as being interchangeable with "knowledge". Wikipedia has a good article on the subject, and it's pretty well cited (just to show my sources). In furtherance of this interpretation of "Arete" (as used by the game), we see that the Technocracy refers to the same stat as "Enlightenment" (which I think is less controversially tied to knowing/understanding). As additional support, I'll point out that when the game was remixed into Mage: The Awakening, Arete became "Gnosis", which does literally translate to "knowledge".

    In my view, even if a martial arts scroll requires that you have some other attributes or skills to execute the lessons contained in a Primer, those practices are ultimately just vectors for giving rise to a state of active comprehending or knowing. The marital arts scroll may require that you go through certain movements, and build some technique, but there has to be something else in that scroll that explains how those movements or techniques relate more broadly to some theory of the cosmos/the truth of reality/etc, or some koan or puzzle. Otherwise, you'd have to believe that simply performing these particular movements a number of ways, a given number of times, would on its own confer Awakening--in which case, you wouldn't even need the scroll... someone could use Mind and Life to transfer the muscle memories and whatever else necessary to just go through the movements X number of times, and boom, you're awake--but it just doesn't make sense that you do whatever kata perefectly X number of times purely by rote mimicry, and all of a sudden, you have a new theory of reality.

        I'll have to disagree with this. If we're going to be pulling in other gamelines to talk about what Arete represents, I think that Dark Ages: Mage deserves a hearing as well. It's far more relevant than a separate gameline that, while inspired by Mage: the Ascension, is its own thing. By contrast, Dark Ages: Mage is actually part of the same world.

        In it, you've got the Foundation and Pillars system, where each style of magic has its own Foundation (the system's counterpoint to Arete) and Pillars (analogous to Spheres). I'll focus on the Foundations here, as that's most relevant to our current discussion. To the Ahl-i-Batin, the Foundation is Al-Ikhlas, or awareness of unity. To the Messianic Voices, the Foundation is Divinity, the relationship to the divine. To the Old Faith, the Foundation is Spontaneity, sympathy with life's pulse. To the Order of Hermes, it is Modus, formidable discipline. To the Spirit-Talkers, it is Sensitivity, the call of the Invisible World. And to the Valdaermen, it is Blôt, the depth of sacrifice.

        What Arete is, beyond the generic “the innate quality that lets you perform magic”, is intentionally not pinned down. It's left as an aspect of a mage's Focus. Since the Union strives for a more or less uniform approach to what they do, it's not surprising that they have a more specific definition for their analog of Arete. But in the big picture, Enlightenment isn't a different name for Arete; it is the Union's Foundation. It is but one take on what Arete could be.

        My premise here is that a Foundation is analogous to a take on what Arete might be. And different Foundations place importance on different traits, or sometimes none at all. While Enlightenment would seem to favor Intelligence, Spontaneity feels more like a Wits thing, and Sensitivity seems to be more about Perception. And I have no idea what Attribute Divinity would favor; the personal qualities that it places value on are ones that simply don't appear on a standard Mage character sheet. Essentially, it would be something closer to what Vampire calls Conscience. Modus is more of a Self-Control thing, while Blôt could be seen to favor a kind of Courage, a willingness to suffer in order to get what you're after.

        All of these are valid takes in what Arete could be, and none of them should be set up on a pedestal as being more essential than the others.

            Also calling officially, the most important characteristic of a mage isn't understanding. It's drive. There's a reason why majors automatically start with a willpower rating of 5, and why Willpower caps Arete. Where Primers are concerned, they aren't really dispensers of knowledge. I mean, they do that; but that's not what makes them Primers. What makes them Primers is that they serve as a call to action to the mage's Avatar, challenging it to Awaken. That's why it's not enough to just read what the Primer has to say; you need to do something about it.

            I have a house rule that lets you incorporate something more along the lines of Awakening's Gnosis, where Magick isn't necessarily so much about employing an innate quality of the mage to bend reality to his will, but can instead be about uncovering hidden secrets about reality. But three things about it: first, I fully and readily acknowledge that it's a house rule. Second, I don't replace Arete-style magick with Gnosis-style magick; I suggest the latter as an alternative to the former, for those character concepts that make more sense in terms of the mage tapping into innate magick in the world around him rather than being innately magickal himself. And third, even then it's not necessarily a matter of cerebral understanding: depending on the nature of the mage, it may be a more visceral thing; or if the idea is that the magick in the world is somehow alive and has a will of its own, the kind of understanding needed might be more of an empathetic bond that would be better represented by social traits than mental ones.

            Bottom line: even if you end up deciding to make understanding more important than motivation, Intelligence isn't necessarily the best way to represent the kinds of understanding needed.

            My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

                My argument here would be that all of the Foundations and Pillars were ultimately able to be distilled down into a singular model (i.e., Arete and the Spheres)--and while the Hermetics may have ultimately had more say in what that model was (and thus why its a Greek word that featured prominently in classical Western philosophy, as opposed to a word from Sanskrit, Yoruba, Navajo, or Proto-Indo-European), it clearly functioned well enough that everyone was able to hold on to their own paradigms, theories, styles, and trappings in the transition. In other words, Arete appears to function well enough as a common denominator for Al-Ikhlas, Divinity, Spontaneity, etc. If we're going to take as highly instructive the meaning of the individual words used to name what each tradition was calling that component of the magic system before it was called "Arete", I think you have to also give due weight and consideration to the meaning and functional historical usage of the word "Arete".

                With regard to how instructive Mage: Awakening is, I agree that it's not as persuasive as Dark Ages: Mage, when I addressed that above. Having said that, I will note that the person who wrote the section of Forged by Dragon's Fire dealing with Grimoires (and therefore Primers), Kraig Blackwelder, is also listed as a contributor to the revised Core Book, and is listed as one of the handful of authors on the Awakening core rulebook, alongside Bill Bridges. Yes, it's a different game with a different mythos and nominally different metaphysics, but nevertheless there's something to be said for the fact that of all the games that were re-imagined, Mage and its magic system changed the least, by an incomparable degree frankly. What I'm about to say is highly speculative, but I would not be surprised to find out that when they were cobbling together the first edition of Mage (on which Bridges was one of the original designers), that they sort of kicked themselves by having already used Gnosis for Werewolf (Bridges again), and went with the next closest thing.

In other words, if read a rule that doesn't jibe with my already formed notion of how the game is, I'm more inclined to change my notion to include and hold as true the new rule I read, than I am to excise the rule entirely. Mind you, I try to change my theory of the game in the most narrow way possible that allows it to work with whatever new rule I found, but my goal is still to make it work before I assume they made an outright mistake.

                    A few points: first, I fully agree with your philosophy as outlined in your last paragraph. I tend to do the same thing. That said, you have to keep in mind that Mage is built on at least two not entirely aligned visions: there's the 1e*/2e/SC/M20 vision of Phil Brucato, and there's the MRev visions of Jess Heinig (Guide to the Technocracy through Storyteller's Handbook), Bill Bridges (Storyteller's Handbook through Ascension), and Ryan Macklin (the revised Convention Books that came out a year before M20 was announced). A big part of the reason why M20 needed to take steps to heal a broken fanbase was because MRev broke the fanbase. Which is not to pass judgment on anyone; just to illustrate the distinctions that exist. So when MRev says one thing and M20 says something different, you need to ask why, and then decide whether you'll go with what MRev says, what M20 says, or some alternative of your own.

                    MRev says that Primers require the reader to have Intelligence 4; M20 doesn't. Did M20 just forget to include that requirement, or was its absence deliberate? If it was deliberate, was it because Brucato didn't understand what Bridges was going for, or was it because Brucato disagreed with what Bridges did? And if Brucato disagreed with Bridges, was he right?

                    In my view, the change was deliberate; it was done because Brucato saw the MRev version as needlessly burdonsome; and he was right.

                    * I include 1e provisionally, as Brucato didn't actually get involved with Mage until after the core book was written; as such, there are a number of things in 1e core, and a handful in the earliest supplements, that don't mesh with Brucato's take on what Mage is. But such is what happens in the earliest days of a gameline.


                    With regard to Ascension's Arete vs. Awakening's Gnosis: the Mage Translation Guide (which was comparing MRev to the first edition of Awakening) points or that the conceptual difference between the two is that Arete is about excellence and virtue while Gnosis is about uncovering arcane secrets. It's pointed out elsewhere in the Translation Guide that the whole of the metaphysics for Awakening align better with the beliefs of the Order of Hermes than with any other group in Ascension; in effect, Awakening is an example of what Ascension might have ended up looking like if the Order of Hermes had gotten its way back when the Council of Nine was founded, and the other proto-Traditions had been absorbed as Houses. So the fact that Awakening's Gnosis is conceptually similar to what you'd expect of the Hermetic notion of excellence and virtue (investigating arcane mysteries and building up a body of arcane lore) doesn't surprise me.

                    I would add that a strong case could be made that Awakening represents what the MRev crew wanted Ascension to be; so it does provide insights into the design decisions made during the MRev era. But as I said at the start of this post, the vision(s) of MRev aren't quite the same as the vision behind every other edition of Mage.


                    Finally: concerning Foundations vs. Arete, my argument is that the genius of what the Traditions came up with in Arete was that they divorced matters of Focus from Arete. That's the main difference between Arete and a Foundation: a Foundation is deeply tied to one specific view of what Magick is, while Arete tries to be agnostic on the subject by sticking to generalities: Arete is about excellence and virtue. What does that mean, precisely? That depends on which Tradition you ask (or in M20, which mage). The reason why the Traditions were able to buy into Arete was because the Verbena could still say that excellence and virtue is Spontaneity and sympathy with life's pulse while the Choristers could still say that excellence and virtue is Divinity and a relationship to the divine, etc.: they didn't have to compromise on the essentials, they just had to accept that there was a broader principle that as different and often contradictory as their beliefs seemed to be, they were all in some abstract sense doing the same thing.

                    But again, that only worked because Arete doesn't insist on defining exactly what excellence and virtue is, certainly not to the extent of requiring it to prominently feature the specific kind of understanding that Intelligence represents.
