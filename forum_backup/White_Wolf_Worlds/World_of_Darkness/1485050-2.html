                If I would storyteller this way, I would not even be motivated to be a Storyteller. Just reading oversimplification makes me want to not think about the game, even when I started, more than 20 years ago, I always gave unique traits to my characters, they're not there simply to &quot;be a challenge&quot; or to interact as a nameless video-game rpg NPC. But then again, I'm also a writer, writing about my characters makes me feel good...
                In the end of the day, the super awesome and super interesting, complex and absolutely extraordinary villain that you spent weeks concocting may be done in a literal stroke of a dice.

                In other times, it can happen that the insignificant mook which you just named "Bob" so that it could even have a name for that moment and that wasnt supposed to be anything more than part of the furniture, grows out to become truly memorable (and yes, this has happened to me).

                The ST has to understand that this is not like writting, we are indeed creating a story, but that aint a solo creation. Your best plans will come to dust in face of player creativity - and you know what, I love it when that happens, I love it when my players surprise me. Which is why I tend to be VERY lenient to them - you'll never see me trying to restrict my players because they managed to explore a loophole I hadnt thought about before.

                Therefore I dont set my plans in stone - ie I dont create a story that is supposed to have a middle and an end, and rather what I usually do is create worlds that feel alive, and THAT"S what I invest a lot of energy into, which is why Im always trying to simulate the biggest amount of realism to the scenery in my head; so for example, I do the math of the expected vampiric population, the hierarquical positions, the connections, the important people relevant to the setting (if a town, the Xeriff, the Mayor, major companies, jornalists etc) and yes some NPCs will be a little generic, but tell me, in a movie how many backgrounds of the characters in play to we get? In horror movies we get a history of the main players, but the exorcist priest is always a "generic priest". The cop is always generic too.

                You get a bit more info on the main stars/villains, but that's it. We dont need info on the clerk of the hotel - unless the story being told is about him.

                And you know what? This creates AMAZING stories.

                Game of Thrones was like that (when it ressembled anything marginally good.).

                The problem of game of Thrones is that it is a book - and making open ended stories for books/series is absolutely terrible, because those are supposed to end. You end up with the shit show that was GoT or Lost.

                RPG however is not supposed to end - you are after all telling the daily life of someone, and that only ends with death. In the movies it ends with "and they lived happily ever after", but on RPGs it doesnt end there.

                You should give it a try one day, you would surprise yourself with how good the results can be and how many awesome stories simply naturally come to existance out of the blue this way

                    Originally posted by Black Fox View Post

                    At one point in time, I would have been horrified by this mentality, but it's one I try to adopt as much as possible now.

                    The terrible thing for many STs to admit is that 90% of our preparation work is irrelevant to actually running the game. We provide more details in our game notes than is needed at the gaming table. Writing complete PC style character sheets for NPCs falls into that category.

                    The reason most of us do that (and other superfluous things about the game) is because it's fun. (And because it's what see in sourcebooks for NPCs.) We like world building. We like creating characters and filling in dots on a character sheet. But at the end of the day, all we need to determine is &quot;What size dice pool should I be using for this character for this type of action?&quot; The Storyteller system actually makes this much easier than other game systems because its dice pool system makes a lot stats inherently generic.

                    In terms of productive use of time, most STs only need to write up some very brief generic stats for major character types. Those will work at least 80% of the time. For that other 20%, you can go into more detail, at whatever level may be needed for what you want to do with them. STs shouldn't think that if they don't have completely statted NPCs that they are somehow &quot;cheating&quot;. It's not.

                    I'm actually a GM who massively overprepares when I run a game. The reason I do is I don't do &quot;on the fly&quot; very well. Some GMs are great at it, and their games never suffer. Others do it, but fail miserably because their &quot;on the fly&quot; decisions don't make sense and undermine the campaign. In order for me to do things &quot;on the fly&quot; well, I need to do a lot of prep work beforehand by working out the most likely things that will come up during game myself. But that generally is about major things about the setting. &quot;PCs do stupid things, I need to figure out ahead of time what happens when they do the ones I've done myself or seen other players do at the table.&quot; &quot;This thing is bound to come up, so I need to be ready how the NPCs will handle it.&quot; And so on. It's not about determining individual stats for individual characters.

                    It's not a problem if STs have fun creating NPC stats, and want to do that. Or if more detailed mechanics actually help them. But what I discovered over the years is that very often a GM can get away with a lot less prep work than they might expect especially when it comes to grindy things like statting out individual characters.
                    I spend a lot of time trying to build worlds that feel alive. I play WoD but I always convert all mechanics to Gurps, so for example, I did use a small python program to calculate for me the percentage distribution of each attributes among the population, just so I could have a ratio. Im creating an entire mini game inside the game of "Realm Management" for my fantasy games, that breeds life to nation-states, countries and Realms, taking into consideration any number of factors that you would like to factor in - production of sugar, tobbaco, coffee, wheat, cattle, water needs, fuel (charcoal, oil, wood, magic alchemical fuel), workforce by type, loyalty and conformness of the population, infraestructure, anyway, anything that you can imagine, levels of corruption and effectiveness of the bureocrats, administrators and higher ups, and turning all of that into a system that you role month to month, executing "maneuvers" to extract resources, rally armies and negogiate with neighbots. Now my next step is to turn this into an app or sheet to make all math automatic for me, just giving end results (after dice rolls). I want to use this system even without player imput, so my fantasy settings for example would have "oh, a plague sets in into the realm", "the realm is experiencing a drough" - or when they go to the steriotypical "quest giver tavern" they may hear "did you hear? It's been said that a vein of gold has been found in the dragons valley... People have gone nuts, selling all they have to try their luck there". So, rather than having the background being just background, I have it be alive, I have it affect the lives of everybody (including the players) and it gives a sense that somethings are out of their control; sometimes the random events can even bring hooks for advemtures! (the plague is a common one for fantasy for example).

                    So, I spend most of the time thinking on history and geography, rather on who were my Bad Guy's school teachers. I think on what would happen to the local economy if there were a gold mine found in the edge of the city, or oil fund underground; why things in a given community are the way they are; instead of thinking about the entire 353 years of the city's prince (un)life story, I try to think what sort of vampiric Prince would arise from the city X. What sort of vampiric community or mage or were or leprechau community would blossom in this place. Then I think about history. Are the guys here the same that came with the city's first founders? What sorts of troubles did this city went through in the past? Plagues, war, famine? Has the population being dislodged?

                    Those questions could, for example, mean a Prince that has (un)lived his entire existance in the same place, and thus he has a very firm and stablished control, with a very solid hierarchy for the local vampires and very few power grabs. This also means that while vampires may gossip and try to climb over rivals, the local population is much more traditional and everybody "knows their place", if it's a city that hasnt been shaken all too much. A place like Warsow in Poland however, who has been devasted so many times over the centuries that is almost impossible to tell, would have a totally different culture, perhaps with a bunch of Anarchists and very unstable, with a weak prince and weak elders constantly trying to backstab each other.

                    Once you start building the world, the personalities of said world present themselves to you without needing to create them

                                                Originally posted by Herr Meister View Post
                                                As I believe most good stories tend to be character-driven
                                                That in my personal experience and personal opinion, is the case for RPGs, because that's an "open experience" type of story; for book, TV series etc, I believe the reverse to be true. If we take all the good stories - Lord of the Rings, Avatar (the Legend of Aang, not that crap remake of Pocahontas in Spaaaaaace), Dune (ie Star Wars for adults), Star Wars (excluding the corporate fanfic garbage from Disney) - they are all story driven.

                                                The greatest example of character driven stories for books and TV are Lost and Game of Thrones - both of which started great, but as expected, ended in an unspeakable shitshow of the bad, the worse and the ugly.

                                                When you're telling a story with beggining, middle and end, you need the damn begin, middle and end. Otherwise, if you just let things "flow", when it's time to end the story you have no idea on how to do it, because your story never had a planned end to begin with, and even worse, you simply let it all drift so far all that it reaches an unsalvageable point.

                                                That's why for example I told from Season 1 of GoT that the show would never have an ending (a proper one), and that G.R.R.M, the author, will NEVER finish the books. Seriously, he has no intention of EVER finishing the books - because he knows that there's no possible satisfactory end to it, since he never thought about one. You can bet that he plans on dying without ever doing it.

                                                For RPGs however it's different. I mean, it would be good if you had planned beforehand at least how the Big Baddie Macguffin device of death can be disabled/destroyed. If you didnt, that's ok, because the players CAN flee for their lives and try their luck on a "safer neighborhood", and thus begin a new arc even thou having failed the previous one.

                                                So yes, I'd say that for RPGs char driven stories work like a charm - but I wouldnt use that method for writting a book
