    Originally posted by Obyrne View Post
    It's much like the teethclench Masquerade fans have at people saying "but it's vampire superheroes with katanas in trenchcoats".
    Yes, for some reason Changeling in particular seems to bring out those sort of clueless, derisive comments. It feels like there's at least one just about every time Changeling comes up. I usually try to be patient, but having been into the game since the 90's, I'm beyond sick of it.


    Originally posted by killersquirrel59
    My biggest problem with Changeling was always that it was far too easy to run away from your problems.
    ​The point of Dreaming is that your character walks a razor's edge between the mundane and the fantastic. If you stray too close to the mundane, you fall to banality. If you spend too much time with the fantastic, you become overcome with bedlam. No one can keep up that balancing act forever. The fate of every Changeling is to either forget their whole existence - their hopes, dreams, and everything they hold dear or be consumed by absolute madness. Spending too much time in the Dreaming running away from their problems is well past the first steps on their way to bedlam.

    Changeling does have its issues. The levels of the various Arts could use some tweaking, and the Realms are a complete mess. I love the flexibility of Changeling's noun + verb magic though. I don't want to get rid of it, just fix it. Banality needs a clear and consistent definition. Currently, what constitutes Banality changes every time the author does. It also needs hard rules for gaining it. As it is now, it feels like an arbitrary punishment from the ST. I'd like to see it have the mechanical rigor of Vampire's Humanity, Mummy's Balance, or Demon's Torment. With the rules as written, the glamour economy is unsustainable for even a single game session. Changelings are lucky if they can manage to gain even a single point of glamour per month. Meanwhile, just about every non-mundane action they can take requires a glamour expenditure. The rates glamour is gained and lost needs to be brought closer to parity - either glamour needs to be easier to obtain or it needs to be spent less often. I'd like to see some combination of both. I'd also like to see the artwork be a bit more consistent with the tone of the text. The second edition core is either teddy bears and balloons or all ren faire all the time. The first edition did a much better job of conveying the juxtaposition of the fantastic with the mundane.

                                                        I really don't know where you're getting the idea that the game encourages players to jump headlong into bedlam. I reread the section on bedlam to make sure I wasn't forgetting anything. There are no bonuses for descending into bedlam. The book gives a detailed list of bedlam warning signs. The three stages of bedlam are increasingly horrific and debilitating, and in the end, the character vanishes from earth, consumed entirely by the Dreaming never to be seen again. Changeling does have issues, but bedlam isn't really one of them.

                                                        (Bedlam does have the unfortunate inclusion of autism as Laughing Hyena pointed out. It's the same sort of early White Wolf-ism that also gave us early Fianna, World of Darkness: Gypsies, and other unintentional bad stereotypes. I'd hope that if there's a C20, it won't be called autism.)

                                                        Now, for Big Brother's question about how I would fix banality and the glamour economy....

                                                        A solid definition of banality is seriously difficulty. So many writers over the years have had very different views on what banality is, and so many gaming groups have interpreted it in their own way. At this point, clearly defining banality is almost certainly going to contradict someone's chronicle. That being said, it really needs to happen anyway. I'd start with a great post from reddit:

                                                        Banality isn't about the division between science and magic, which the resurgence of the Dreaming at the time of the moon landing should show.

                                                        Banality is the slow soul-deadening creep of conformity and the death of hope, the bleak granite wind that finally blows out the candle flame of childlike wonder. It is not the rise of glass and steel and circuit that chokes off the Dreaming, not the spread of pavement over grass that brings the ruin of Arcadia, but the 'rat race' of blind consumerism and the constant struggle against poverty. Every dreamer, every artist, every would-be bright spot in the bland nightmare of modern life who finally puts on a tie and goes to work in a cube is another drop of Fae blood spilled.

                                                        If you don't think technology can be magical then you've never been to Disneyland, you've never seen Doc Brown's time machine, never watched Explorers or heard a young child declare they are going to be an astronaut with the kind of wide-eyed certainty reserved for playing at being Superman.

                                                        If you want to hear what the struggle sounds like over the years, listen to this: http://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zxazWMlxQLM (Fair warning, it makes me cry 9/10 times.)

                                                        ...The game isn't set up to be straight horror as much as it is a sort of appreciation of the last few flowers on a rose bush that is being slowly buldozed under. Thematically, the game is set up to be about the last days of a once-great empire of dreams and the fear every morning that this will be the day you are too tired to fight the coming winter any longer. You aren't meant to fear for your life, but for your hope.
                                                        From there, I would look at retooling banality gain to follow a hierarchy of sins in similar way Vampire, Mummy, and Demon do. Instead of charting a descent into becoming a monster, this path would be the slow, inevitable death of hope and wonder. This would require a major reworking of the way Autumn Person and Dauntain powers work, but they're in desperate need of an overhaul anyway.

                                                        For the glamour economy, I think I would keep musing (reverie and rapture) the long, personal process that it is now. It should be difficult, and it should be intimate. I would make the pay off much greater though to reflect the amount of effort put into it. I'd look at it resulting in either a permanent glamour or a reduction in banality. I would keep ravaging and rhapsody pretty much as they are. They're both a quick fix of glamour and extremely thematic. If banality was changed to a hierarchy of sins, they would both definitely be sins on the chart.

                                                        I would add a method to gain glamour that is something like spontaneous inspiration. It would be a bit more difficult than ravaging, but not punishing and doable within a scene of a game session. The changeling would do something to inspire a mortal (individually or an audience). It could be a performance, a motivational speech, showing them how to do something or how something works - anything that could fit a musing threshold. It would produce a little bit of spontaneous glamour - one or two points, more if the target is one of the changeling's dreamers. It wouldn't be a victory against banality like musing is, but it's enough to keep the cold winds of winter at bay temporarily.

                                                        To reduce the constant expenditure of glamour, I would default all cantrips to chimerical. If they want a cantrip to have a wyrd effect, then they spend a glamour point while they're casting it. Alternately, they could call upon the wyrd for a slightly increased cost, but then everything for the rest of the scene is wyrd without having to pay the cost per action.

                                                            I really don't know where you're getting the idea that the game encourages players to jump headlong into bedlam. I reread the section on bedlam to make sure I wasn't forgetting anything. There are no bonuses for descending into bedlam. The book gives a detailed list of bedlam warning signs. The three stages of bedlam are increasingly horrific and debilitating, and in the end, the character vanishes from earth, consumed entirely by the Dreaming never to be seen again. Changeling does have issues, but bedlam isn't really one of them
                                                            It's not that there are bonuses for bedlam. It's that there are serious drawbacks to banality, and no drawbacks on the road to bedlam. Banality affects you constantly as a detriment and cautionary tale of falling back asleep. Bedlam doesn't have warning signs that hit you mechanically at all. You get higher Glamour and get more powerful. It's all better. Bedlam is something with no mechanical support. The ST can threaten it, but the threat is always arbitrary because there is no mechanical way to "fall" to bedlam other than ST fiat. Therefore it isn't really a threat. Therefore the razor's edge doesn't work.

                                                            What is really needed is to redo the Permanent Glamor score to actually mirror Permanent Banality, independent of their temporary values. There need to be different advantages and penalties for getting either too high above the other and force the player to play the middle ground or go careening off into madness or slumber. Hell make the power of doing either look really damn tempting, but seriously risky. But there needs to be a mechanical enforcement of this balance. If you want to encourage a balancing act, both sides of the tightrope have to look equally dangerous, both in theme and in mechanics. Bedlam is not a risk, because right now there is no hard and fast way to get there that a player can point to on his sheet and worry about how close he's getting.

                                                            Personally I'd run it like KOE Yin and Yang virtues. Make them opposed virtues that can't exceed a certain number (I'll say 10 for now for the sake of argument, but the number is arbitrary). Then you have different actions that can increase one or the other (personally I'd take xp out of the equation entirely and move this closer to a morality scale with a hierarchy of sins and virtues). Then you establish that if you gain a dot of Glamour that would take you above the allowed max, you lose a dot of Banality and come that one more step closer to Bedlam and vice versa. Say when either reaches 10, you're screwed, falling to either Slumber or Bedlam. That way you really have to balance your actions. The other side to this is make Banality not entirely bad. Make it feel familiar and comfortable. Have a higher Banality score give bonuses to dealing with the mundane in some form. Make it easy to just slip back off to sleep, because it's easier than fighting.

                                                            With a system like this, Changeling really becomes about walking that delicate tightrope between being a Fae and being human, between creativity and dolor, with either side looking just as nasty.

                                                            THAT would be a World of Darkness game. I may just go write this system up and try it out. Funny, I've never really been inspired to actually run Changeling, but this is a hack I could see making it actually work.

                                                            On second thought, don't use Glamour for the opposed stat to Banality. Glamour is a spent resource and has its own mechanics that need to stay constant. Leave Glamour out of it. It needs a new stat, let's call it Inspiration (at least for now). You set up Inspiration vs. Banality, each with their own hierarchy of sins and virtues. Make the sins and virtues for one exactly diametrically opposed so that a sin for one is always a virtue for the other. When you commit an over act that would put them into conflict, roll your Inspiration vs. your Banality at an arbitrary but equal difficulty. The number of net successes gained is the number of temporary points gained in the victor's side. 10 temporary points equal a permanent point. When you make your character divide 10 points among your Inspiration and Banality depending on the kind of character you want to play (Seeming can still set a minimum Banality rating). When a new Permanent point is gained in either one, the other loses one. This makes it a vicious slope that once you start falling one way you are more likely to pick up speed, continuing to fall down the cliff until it's too late.
