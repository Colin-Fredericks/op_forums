            1) You have to be careful with the Ferrymen (and to a lesser extent the Pasiphae) as they are pretty damn powerful and can easily hog the spotlight from the PCs. Now I've never played WtO, trying to run a PbP WtGW game here on the forums at the moment, but I have read some of the pre-made chronicles that do feature a Ferrymen.

            My suggestions are the following based on what I've read and my own thoughts:

            -Less is better. Both the Ferrymen and the Pasiphae are very mysterious beings, seldom seen by the common wraith. Plus the Pasiphae rarely leave the Labyrinth. So use them sparely, showing up every once and awhile but not so much that they start to lose their enigmatic nature.

            -A savior. Some times the PCs end up biting off more than they can chew it looks like the whole Circle's going to be wiped out. Having a Ferryman showing up at the least second to save them all and drive back whatever is trying to kill them.

            -A fond of knowledge. Some Ferrymen are old, quite old. They know things that most wraiths haven't even heard of. Naturally they can be very useful for providing PCs with knowledge they can't find elsewhere. Hell simply finding them could be a mini-quest of itself.

            -Transportation. The Ferrymen are...well Ferrymen. They control the Midnight Express, perhaps the most reliable means of transportation in the Underworld. Some times your PCs need to get somewhere, somewhere far or hard to get to. Getting onto the Midnight Express or finding and convincing a Ferrymen to help out, especially if they're attempting to pursuit Transcendence.

            -A potential recruit. Perhaps one of the PCs is unknowingly being considered as a potential Ferrymen and is being watched from afar by one of them. Maybe every once and awhile appearing to lend some help for seemingly no reason whatsoever. Don't tell anything to your players and let them stew on why a Ferrymen seems to be showing up periodically.

            2) I've never really touched the Far Shores. Honestly they've always felt a bit like a distraction in the modern setting. If they ever did a Dark Ages: Wraith than I could see the appear of them but as it stands they just don't interest me.

            Homo sapiens. What an inventive, invincible species. It's only a few million years since they crawled up out of the mud and learned to walk. Puny, defenceless bipeds. They've survived flood, famine and plague. They've survived cosmic wars and holocausts. And now, here they are, out among the stars, waiting to begin a new life. Ready to outsit eternity. They're indomitable. Indomitable.

                Ferrymen and Pasiphae
                A long time ago, there was this delightfully brilliant RPG called H.o.L. When I discovered the game, it was being published under the Black Dog label. In this game, there was a badass gun-slinging NPC named “Edgar Sparingly.” His purpose was to give Storytellers (Holmeisters) a tool to bail out characters if they did something stupid. The final advice given on this character, iirc, was to use him, “…sparingly.”

                That was my take on Ferrymen. During the times I found myself as ST, I had a Ferryman NPC on stand-by, written in the same spirit as Edgar. Just in case the troupe did something stupid (or – and I’ll never admit this to them – in case I inadvertently set them up for failure).

                I never personally used the Pasiphae, but our regular ST used one for a story arc involving my character and his shadow. It added a little je ne sais quoi to the story; more so than just a Nephwrack would.

                [My observation: Pasiphae can be played outside the norm of most Nephwracks, but below the power curve of Never/Once-born. They work well for when you need an *evil* antagonist without all the baggage of the culty Nephwracks and their hordes (even Coldheart had his cadre).]

                In either case, give them the Arcanoi Plot Device OOOOO.

                Insofar as the Mummy stuff and the Ritual of Severance (? – been awhile since EoE), it doesn’t come up unless you want it to. If you don’t like the idea of it being dependent on some Mummy fluff, make up an uber-Castigate Art.


                The Far Shores
                These are McGuffins just waiting to happen. Need a backdrop that doesn’t really fit anywhere else in the Underworld? Make it a Far Shore.

                A cabal of Knights Templars that created a Valhalla-esque “afterlife” devoted to accumulating Relics of war in order to achieve Transcendence by becoming [martially] “without equal”?

                Far Shore.

                A hub for aerial pirates (a la Talespin) that work with the Renegades to accrue wealth, thereby achieving the necessary funds to enter Heaven?

                Far Shore.

                I took the fact that there’s so little written on them as a license to do whatever I needed them to do. YMMV.

                    Hmm . . . I think a quick explanation of Wraith's cosmology would help a lot here. You're familiar with Werewolf and Mage, right? If so, that'll help a lot.

                    Wraiths exist in the Underworld, what mages know as the Low Umbra - the dark twin of the Umbra that werewolves visit. The Kuei-jin know it as the Yin World. Playing off the usual metaphor, you can think of the Underworld as multiple sub-floors of a building.

                    The Shadowlands are the ground floor. Separated from the Skinlands (the mundane/living world) by a metaphysical barrier known as the Shroud (the Underworld's equivalent to the Wall), it is very, very similar to the Skinlands . . . but on their side of the Shroud, the buildings are rotting and decaying, a grim parody of what the living world looks like. But the landmarks are the same, and a map of the living world applies to the Shadowlands, too.

                    But the Shroud is so very much more porous than the Wall - there's a reason they use a cloth metaphor. Even a newly dead wraith can look through the Shroud to see their loved ones, and even many completely mundane humans can glimpse wraiths out of the corner of their eye, or just barely sense their icy touch ("feels like someone walked over my grave").

                    However, not every wraith can reach the Shadowlands - they have to use their fetters (emotionally important people/objects/places) to pull themselves that close to the realm of the living. That's rarely a problem for recently dead wraiths, but older ones . . .well, things get destroyed, people die, etc. In fact, destroying a wraith's fetters is a popular battle strategy, because it cuts them off from the Shadowlands and many of their passions (unfinished business).

                    But there are "basements" below the Shadowlands. Places where the rules are different. Some wraiths can travel freely between "levels", others have to rely on Nihils - strange tears and holes that occasionally appear in the fabric of the Underworld. Some are as regular as Old Faithful in Yellowstone, others are more random.

                    Below the Shadowlands lie the Sunless Sea - a storm wracked sea that contains the Tempest, the Dark Kingdoms (such as Stygia), and the Far Shores. Most newly dead wraiths can't explore this level much - the same fetters that bind them to existence chain them to the nearest Dark Kingdom and the surrounding Tempest. For a Western (American, European, etc.) wraith, that would be Stygia.

                    A Dark Kingdom is built of the "ghosts" of what the living have treasured and valued. Churches, palaces . . . Stygia, the Dark Kingdom of Iron, even contains an intact version of the Great Library of Alexandria. Which makes sense - Stygia was built by Western wraiths, for Western wraiths.

                    A massive megalopolis, built across seven "islands" in the Sunless Sea, some parts of Stygia are gorgeous. A wraith who's into history can spend months just touring the nicer parts, marveling at architectural wonders long lost to the living world. All the lost treasures of antiquity are there.

                    Other parts of the city are . . .less pleasant. Stygia was never supposed to be this big - it was just supposed to be a stopping point on the quest for Transcendence (which the mages call Ascension and the kuei-jin call "dah"). However, many wraiths have lost faith in the possibility of Transcendence, and are content to stay in Stygia. And the living can only build (and destroy) wondrous palaces so fast. . .

                    The result has been the explosive growth of massive slums . . . and the unending screams from the soul forges. If the living won't provide building materials, well . . . there's only one other source of raw material. And that's other wraiths.

                    Technically, only slaves and convicted felons are supposed to be melted down into soulsteel. But its an open secret that people do go "missing" in the slums, and only a fool walks around the bad parts of town unarmed . . .

                    As for the other Dark Kingdoms or the Far Shores . . .well, most wraiths know they exist. But getting there is difficult, even under ideal circumstances . . .and of course, most wraiths have fetters to worry about. So, unless you deliberately want to include them in your campaign, you can safely ignore them.

                    But basically, the Far Shores are an "afterlife" for the wraiths. Sort of counterparts of the Dark Kingdoms, each place in the Far Shore is themed after a different afterlife. No one remembers who built them (or if they're the "real" thing), but a wraith can visit Heaven, Valhalla, the Elysian Fields . . . or Hell, Tartereus, or worse. According to legend, the Far Shores exist to help wraiths pursue Transcendence, but few have been there and returned . . . and fewer still are willing to talk about what they saw there.

                    Below the Sunless Sea, accessible only via the Veinous Stair, is the Labyrinth. No one knows what the Labyrinth is made of . . . and few want to. If a Dark Kingdom is made of memories that the living treasure, then the Labyrinth is crafted of things they desperately want to forget. Overflowing sewers, the gas chambers of death camps, the basement where a man did unspeakable things to children . . . The Labyrinth knows what scares you, and constantly shifts between horrors, with all the consistency of a nightmare.

                    And at the bottom/heart of the Labyrinth are the Malfeans. Some wraiths whisper that the Neverborn are fallen gods, slain at the dawn of creation. Others speculate that they are the children of Oblivion itself. Either way, the Malfeans scheme endlessly to drag all Creation down, and into Oblivion . . .

                            Something I haven't seen mentioned in here yet as to why someone might want to play Wraith...

                            It is the only game I have ever seen that could handle The Shoah.

                            Charnal Houses of Europe: The Shoah is a book that could not have happened anywhere else. There are certainly game worlds that could make room for a holocaust, or even The Holocaust. There are also game rules systems that could model various parts of what happened during that time. However I do not think that there has ever been any other game world and system that could handle that particular topic with even a fraction of the dignity and respect that Wraith did, while still making it a playable game.

                            There have been very, VERY few games that try to tackle topics this serious, and for good reason. And yet despite all of the possible pitfalls which are too numerous to even try to list here, Wraith as a game was (with the help of some amazing writers) able to actually deliver a setting smack in the middle of one of the most horrible tragedies that has ever befallen mankind.

                            As a book Charnal Houses of Europe managed not only to avoid raising the ire of Holocaust survivors everywhere for making light of their suffering and loss, but actually was applauded its efforts for continuing to tell their story which must never be forgotten. A book that makes a game out of the death and suffering actually won awards from the community of people that it spoke about.

                            Think about that for a moment. People who actually lived through the Holocaust are still alive, and by most accounts their experiences are sacrosanct. Yet this "game" managed to do what museums and documentaries had been struggling to do for years. It actually puts the players right up against the true horrors of World War II and makes them confront those events from the inside out. If the writers had slipped up by even the slightest margin they and Wraith: The Oblivion would both have been torn apart by the combined outrage of people spread across the entire world. Yet despite all of that it succeeded where no other game could have. No other game that I know of has the maturity to pull off something like that, or at least not half as well as Wraith did.

                            Not every game of Wraith has to be that serious, and I have seen some very good but very lighthearted role play come out of Wraith. But it's capacity for handling those serious moments is unmatched by anything else out there.

                            Wraith is a game about the deepest of wounds, yet it can heal.
                            Wraith is a game about the most traumatic memories, yet it can turn us towards the deepest introspection.
                            Wraith is a game about the darkest of nightmares, yet it can comfort the frightened.
                            Wraith is a game about the most frightening terrors mankind can produce, yet it can calm the soul.

                            I have watched people confront their own demons in a wraith game by putting aspects of themselves into their characters that they could not otherwise confront, and in most cases they did it without even realizing it until after the had already grappled with the parts of themselves that they were terrified to admit even existed.

                            Games in general are a powerful tool for teaching, and healing, at least when everything lines up right with the game, the players, and the person running the game. But none of them are more powerful of a tool than Wraith.

                            These are the stories we tell. They are our own stories even when we do not realize it when we start the game. Wraith is the game that lets us tell them.

                                    Originally posted by Hawthorn View Post
                                    So as another couple of questions that spring up from my casual reading of on-line sources about Wraith.

                                    1) If you are a Wraith that finds itself in the Skinlands in an immaterial state, how easy is it for you to interact with the material world?

                                    To expand on this a bit, is it made easier by the intervention/involvement of other supernaturals? Can Mages with Spirit and Vampires with Necromancy maybe help you out? Just thinking of possible points of "cross-over" that might facilitate both PC and NPC actions and plots.
                                    Without using Arcanoi (the wraith equivalent of spheres or disciplines), a wraith can't do anything in the Skinlands (world of the living). They are ghosts, after all. All they can do is look. They can't hug their grieving wife, talk to their child, strangle their murderer . . . if they try, they just pass harmlessly through the living.

                                    On the other hand, a wraith can go almost anywhere. A wraith interested in the occult probably knows who all the other supernaturals are in the area, what they are, who they're scheming with . . . You can easily ward against wraiths, but in the modern day, almost no one does. So they've got an all-access pass to mage chantries, changeling freeholds, vampire safe houses . . .

                                    But with the right Arcanoi . . . ever see Ghost? Poltergeist? Beetlejuice? How about Amityville Horror? A wraith with the right skills can rip souls from bodies, hurl butcher knives across the room, even reanimate their old corpse and claw their way out of the grave

                                    4) So Wraiths from other Dark Kingdoms have different souls based on cultural beliefs basically?

                                    Does this also tie into the fact they get different ghostly powers? I noticed the 185K stretch goal talks about the Jade kingdom getting different powers. Are they alternate levels of the same powers or are they brand new power types?
                                    Picking up on EisenKreutzer explanation:

                                    A wraith is a wraith, regardless of where they're from. Terminology differs, and there might be some slight practical differences, but from a practical perspective, you can consider them the same.

                                    However, a wraith is also little more than a bundle of memories & emotions, and their Arcanoi are an expression of that. So there are substantial differences between the Arcanoi practiced by the wraiths of the different Dark Kingdoms. There are some similarities due to circumstances - for example, travelling through the Tempest is a problem for any wraith regardless of origin.

                                    But other Arcanoi . . .well, each culture has its own ghost stories and traditions, stories about what ghosts are supposed to be able to do. And that's reflected in the Arcanoi the wraiths from each culture develop. Stygian wraiths group their arcanoi by general effect: unleashing their anger as telekinesis, or making the walls bleed, or hijacking a living person's body and going for a joyride . . .

                                    The wraiths of the Dark Kingdom of Jade (basically China), on the other hand, organize their Arcanoi by profession - the powers they'll need to pursue a particular career in the afterlife.

                                    Oh, and as for Dark Arcanoi? Those are the wraith equivalent of a kuei-jin's Demon Arts. Powers fueled by the Shadow (P'o, dark side of the soul), and not the healthiest skills to practice . . .
