                As far as I know, the only wraith-like creatures not to have the Psyche/Shadow division (regardless of what you call it) are mummies. And the mummies aren't exactly "naturally" occurring - even by supernatural standards. It takes ritual magic performed by a(n awakened) mage to create a mummy, so that explains any differences right there.

                (For those who don't have Mummy, a mummy's soul splinters upon death. The "ka" has full access to both Skinlands and Shadowlands, but can't leave the body. The "ba", meanwhile enters the Sunless Sea (Tempest/Dark Kingdom level), but can't enter the Shadowlands. . . until the body's repaired and ready to be reanimated. And the mummy's khabit ("shadow") is completely suppressed the entire time (so no Shadowguiding or Harrowings. Or at least that's how it was - I don't have the last release)

                Originally posted by Ramnesis View Post
                Which of course begs the question of why not? It's not like they weren't in an insidious decay of their own; insidious and inevitable decay is one of the key features of the underworld. Either the other Dark Kingdoms contributed to or at least experienced the Great Maelstroms, or they caused and experienced Maelstrom's of their own.
                Well, there are official causes, recognized by the wraiths of Stygia. But who says they're right? If you want to have the "real" cause of a Great Maelstrom be something that happened elsewhere, go for it. Its not like wraiths (or the living) could easily communicate for most of history, so they couldn't exactly compare notes.

                From a writing standpoint, though, doing a true multi-cultural apocalypse is . . . complicated. Each culture has its own landmarks, values, social weak points . . . Finding something you can break in each culture to bring about an apocalypse, which will resonate with other cultures, is tough. If you're going for an epic feel, its much easier to pick a single culture, and run with it. Take a look at Susan Cooper's Dark is Rising quintet, or Clamp's X. From an outside perspective, it makes no sense why the fate of all humanity would rest with the British (Cooper), or the Japanese (Clamp). But from within the narrative, it works - and you don't end up with Japanese oni trying to invoke Celtic magic.

                            (Lengthy Linguistics Note Follows)

                            Hun and P'o. Hun should not have an aspirant marker. P'o does, but English usually aspirates p to begin with, which is why Romanization of most Chinese languages moved away from adding in ' or h over the years to denote aspiration for initials. It's rather difficult to get most native English speakers to form an unaspirated t, p, or k, so use of d, b, and g, respectively, tended to replace them*, which is where you get Daoism instead of Taoism or Gong Fu instead of Kong Fu.

                            *Such as Yale or Pinyin.

                                Very much so, although we also have another example (the city of Swar) of a Malfean (sort of) in disguise running a Dark Kingdom that seems much more interested in its own power, security, and comfort than in advancing the cause of Oblivion. I'd assume in both cases their peculiar origin and circumstances has given them a more complex personality than the average Onceborn. Moreover, DR:S and D:itL both suggested Onceborn are often just as invested in their own benefit as in fighting for Oblivion whereas the Neverborn are more mindful of the latter and Orpheus seems to indicate even the Neverborn's loyalty to Oblivion may have limits if their own survival is at stake.

                                I think a good gauge may be the human parts of their personalities drive Lung Wang/Yu Huang and the city of Swar to care for their own comfort and security, and cause Yu Huang to show such quirks as an interest in ambitious infrastructure projects. In practical terms this becomes an interest to ensure the society of the Yellow Springs and Swar are built to stand in the long terms, are somewhat less instable and self-destructive than your average Amphiskiopolis, and can maintain a facade of being "normal" wraith societies. On the other hand, the Malphean parts of their personalities likely push them to make their Dark Kingdoms as brutal and oppressive for their own sake as they can get short of revealing their rulers' game. We also know of other examples of wraithly societies (e.g. several Far Shores) that came to be ruled by the Shadow-eaten and the same pattern tends to repeat.

                                We know Charon may have been influenced by his Shadow at times during his career but he never became a Shadow-eaten. Things may be more ambigous with at least part of the Deathlords, at least a few may have fallen to their Shadows. It may be interesting to contrast and compare Stygia with the other major Dark Kingdoms and the one example of an Underworld society that is entirely from Oblivion's taint (the Ferrymen) to try and tell what in their bad aspects came from "normal" corruption of absolute power or good intentions gone bad and what was the influence of Oblivion.
