                            That's because most sources, quite frankly, use the x-card wrong and fail to address the entire range of uses. It's basic use is as an indicator that 'yo, hold up, I need to take a moment', and most sources leave it there. That's because in some cases, it may not be a possibility for there to be a discussion about why a halt needed to be called due to the private nature of trauma, but treating it as a universal 'BAN THIS' indicator is misusing it in the same way people have misused trigger warnings.

                            Here are the ideal scenarios.
                            1. Something occurs and an x-card is dropped, and the reason why is not clear. The game pauses, and the ST takes a moment aside with the player. 'Are you alright?' 'Yes, I just need a minute, this is very rough because <x>, but then we can keep going now I know to expect it and I've had a chance to take a moment and get some distance'.
                            2. Something occurs and an x-card is dropped, and the reason why is not clear. The game pauses and the ST takes a moment aside with the player. 'You okay?' 'No, and I don't want to/cannot say why.' The ST accepts, moves the scene along away from the issue as much as possible, or if necessary calls a halt to the session. This is only an appropriate use of the card if the trauma involved is serious and personal or covered by a confidentiality requirement, but the reason it is treated as the default scenario is because you shouldn't pry into someone's private shit unless it's important, and a tabletop game is not. The appropriate response to this is 'okay, what do we need to do to make you okay right now?'
                            3. Something occurs and an x-card is dropped, and the reason why is not clear. The game pauses and the ST takes a moment aside with the player. 'You okay? What's wrong?' 'I can't handle <x> because it's way too close to <a thing that happened, in whatever level of detail the person is comfortable with> and I have some baggage there/i have a serious phobia of <whatever>. Can we adjust to avoid that in future?' 'Maybe, let's try and work it out and see where we go.'
                            4. Something occurs and an x-card is dropped. The ST knows why because it has been previously discussed (perhaps at an effective 'session zero' or privately or just through knowing the players well) or because the specific scene content is heavy even within the context of the game*, and smooths the scene on away from the cause. The game continues smoothly. This is the ideal outcome and usage scenario because it prevents the need to slow the game down, but it is far from the only one.

                            (*: what does that mean? In Wraith, it would mean not so much 'a suicide' as 'extremely detailed descriptions of the suicide'. This is because the context of the game is important in determining what is or is not expected and reasonable.)

                            Only in one of those usage scenarios is it a straight up 'whoah, no, never, not ever' veto. That one unfortunately is treated as the only one for simplicity by too many sources. It's also why more than a really brief sidebar is actually a good idea, since x cards are actually far more useful than that with more varied usage scenarios. Basically, in the same way that the other players and the ST have a responsibility to treat the emotional wellbeing of each other seriously, the person dropping the x-card has a obligation to use it responsibly. The individual circumstances of their life will determine which usage scenario is appropriate, and it very well may be number 2 - but it may also be 1, 3, or 4 just as easily and just as validly. Scenario 2 is especially applicable however to groups that don't know each other well.

                            So, why the emphasis elsewhere on usage scenario 2? It's because a hell of a lot of people with deep-seated and serious traumas need to have that as an option - an ability to just say 'no'. But advocates for that are not saying it should be employed willy nilly. It's about things like 'I can't talk about this without bringing up serious emotional baggage no one here wants to deal with, myself included'. It is an extremely limited usage scenario that fundamentally diminishes the actual utility of the x-card, but which is emphasized in order to make it clear to people that 'no, and I do not want to/cannot discuss why' is an acceptable response when dealing with serious trauma. It is not the only usage scenario, and the card concept is not designed to lump every usage scenario under that one response banner but rather to ideally be used in conjunction with prior 'heads up' notices regarding content where possible.

                            Basically, so far the discussions elsewhere have been inconsistent, choosing to emphasize different aspects without considering all usage scenarios. It's why you get some weird situations like 'this is just boring' being grounds for an x-card in some discussions of them (usually paired with the opposite 'o card' for 'more please') that cannot be questioned, because they aren't addressing different usage scenarios and the different natures of the tools and are trying to apply a serious tool to something it, quite frankly, should not be used for. X cards are appropriate for either serious traumas or touchy subjects generally like child molestation, and should be used and respected as such.

                            Think of it this way. They exist to signal discomfort. If you know what the discomfort is as an ST, you just move on over it and adapt accordingly. If you don't, then the appropriate response is a check in with the player - preferably out of earshot for privacy reasons, though this is not always the case - and they may or may not disclose why. The appropriate contexts for their use will be decided by the game and the players, by reference to the maturity of the players and of the game, and it is entirely possible that some people might use them inappropriately, just like some STs act inappropriately towards their players. Just like that latter scenario, it does not render them any less useful a tool.

                                            I've been discussing the X-card elsewhere, especially the ambiguous reactions towards it, and here's some observations I've made so far about the current clash:

                                            First of all, the card is a tool. And tools can be used towards good or bad ends, using good or bad methods. Something to keep in mind - something that seems to occasionally be forgotten, with people attributing virtue or vice to the tool itself. This flexibility of usage means that different people will have both different ideas and different actual experience with usage. With that in mind:

                                            World of Darkness is generally a game where, as others have stated, discomfort, anxiety and a state of fear are expected; they're why the Personal Horror thing is brought up. So the game line caters to people who want to push their limits. And when pushing limits, some sort of ejection lever is highly recommended for emergencies, and most risk-acknowledging, willingly-playing participants will nod in agreement to that. But there's a nuance to that agreement.

                                            The nuance lies primarily in the weight of the decision to use it. So let's say I decided that the plane is about to crash, or the building is on fire, or the train needs to be stopped. I look at the ejection switch / fire alarm / train's emergency brake lever. "In case of emergency, break glass and pull the lever", it says. I acknowledge the cost of pulling the lever. Should I do it, the plane will crash without the pilot / the people will drop what they're doing and exit the building / the train will grind to a half and nobody will commute on time. Should I pull it, I'll break the immersion for me and my co-players, let the narrative lose its momentum and so on. Both for me and for others. If I crash a plane / scare a whole building of employees / stop a train, and after the fact it turns out I did so without justification, I'll carry the consequences for misusing such a necessary tool. So both the presentation of the tool and the rules of usage thereof encourage treating it responsibly.

                                            However, the original wording of the X-Card seems very easy to read in a way that erases the above nuance and responsibility:
                                            Originally posted by John Stavropoulos
                                            “I’d like your help. Your help to make this game fun for everyone. If anything makes anyone uncomfortable in any way… [ draw X on an index card ] …just lift this card up, or simply tap it [ place card at the center of the table ]. You don’t have to explain why. It doesn't matter why. When we lift or tap this card, we simply edit out anything X-Carded. And if there is ever an issue, anyone can call for a break and we can talk privately. I know it sounds funny but it will help us play amazing games together and usually I’m the one who uses the X card to protect myself from all of you! Please help make this game fun for everyone. Thank you!"
                                            Statements such as "uncomfortable in any way" and "doesn't matter why" are very easy to read as meaning that (a) the card is to be used lightly - not as an emergency break behind glass, but as a button one pushes with as little regard as when spending WP or the like and (b) that there is no place for evaluating whether the use is appropriate or not. Hopefully it's understandable why immersion-oriented, thrill-seeking people may have objections to both points a and b.

                                            Now, emphasise again that the existence of an emergency red lever behind a glass is a good thing on its own, as anyone may end up in a situation where it's needed. But the way the card is introduced makes it look like a thing that's less about needs and more about wants, and thus runs into the topic of whether [mis]use runs the risk of ruining the fun for everyone present.

                                            On a matter of personal opinion, of the safety tools I can recall offhand, other ones seem better designed and/or more nuanced than the card. Discussion of/agreement about things to avoid, red/yellow/green three-card system, veils and lines - they all seem more nuanced and more well-designed by comparison. Instead, the more frivolous interpretation of the X-card (but one that seems to match the letter of the card-rule) seems to be attracting enough negative attention to cast a shadow on the whole group of tools.

                                                    No one is going to take away your fun because a few pages worth of "here's how to deal with potentially stressful content and situations that may arise" get added to a book.

                                                    I've been running Vampire with someone who played for years before and for years after. He loves the game. He is a huge fan of horror movies, horror novels, etc. But he decided to go hunting one game session, on a dark and mostly-empty road. Succeeded, and decided he really needed to top off his Blood Pool. I told him he found a woman alone in a minivan that had broken down on the shoulder, trying to call a tow truck. He stopped, offered to help, then ambushed her and drained 8BP, which killed her. He staged it to look like an accident with the car. After all that, I made him roll Conscience and he got a bunch of successes. His character felt awful about what had happened, but kept his Humanity rating (it was kinda low, too, like 5?). So I described how he couldn't get the image out of his mind of her kids waiting at home, and how their mother would never get home. They'd just be waiting, and waiting, and she'd never come home. They'd never see her again. I said that as he'd arranged the "accident" scene, he'd found a photo of the kids in the minivan, and some of their toys.

                                                    He had to stop playing that night. It was too much, and it stressed him out, so we called a stop to the session. The game continued next week, no real problems. And he's played VtM for years since.

                                                    A lot of WoD Storytellers like to get graphic, and like to get emotional. They want to really stick the horror to you, and make you uncomfortable. That can easily go too far, even for people who love horror as a genre. Because it can very quickly get personal, especially in a group that knows each other. You may not be intending to make someone upset, but if you know a lot about their past and what scares them, or makes them worry, you can go too far without meaning to, just because you want to make it really creepy.

                                                    It's not asking too much for a horror game to include information on how to manage horror properly. And properly managed horror games are still about having fun. There's a huge difference between watching a well-made horror film, and just watching footage of atrocities. One is enjoyable. The other makes you sick. We want to stick to the enjoyable part. If we're making our friends sick, we have a problem. And there is plenty of material that can easily be included in the game to help players avoid that as they learn how to manage horror.
