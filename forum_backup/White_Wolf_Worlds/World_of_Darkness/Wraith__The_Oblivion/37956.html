    [Wraith] House Rules

    12-27-2013, 03:25 PM
    I was thinking Mr. D might find it useful to see what house-rules troupes have created over the years and why.

    The purpose of this thread is not “suggestion.” We’re just here to offer some of our own best practices and give the dev team insight into our psyches (or Psyches, in this case).

    Below are some House Rules that my troupe used. Like I said, these are not suggestions; just rulings that we made that gave our games more zest. Don’t judge us .

    Shadowguides

    For the most part, my troupe used Shadowguides as written with some slight caveats.

    House Rule
    The “whisper pool” (named so because we used creepy whisper voices to represent the Shadows talking to their Psyches).
    The Shadowguide’s Whisper Pool is equal to his permanent Angst rating. This is the number of times in a session that the Shadowguide can “whisper” to his PC counterpart.

    Why
    Sometimes players would derail themselves by paying too much attention to Shadowguiding. We found that the Whisper Pool encouraged Shadowguides to “pick their shots.”

    Depending on the session, Thorns may or may not count towards Whisper use. RL time constraints, where the story was at, gaining/losing story momentum, and such; I didn’t want players to get bogged down in tormenting the PCs.

    Sessions usually began with a quick call-out of Shadowguide expectations. A few of the ones we used were:

    Quiet Time
    The story needed to get moving along (or ST didn’t want to deal with it at the moment) and ruled Shadowguides inert – we just assumed a snarky internal dialogue between the Psyche and Shadow. [I think we only used this for one session]

    Whispers/No Thorns
    Whisper Pool was in effect, but Thorn use didn’t count towards the Whisper points.

    Whispers/Thorns Included
    Whisper Pool, but each Thorn-use was included as a “whisper.” [Rarely used, but still more than Quiet Time].

    Full Chatter
    No whisper pool, Shadowguides operated nearly as-written. [Often used in the beginning of new stories and allowed for some internal psychological drama. I’d use it often at the beginning of new stories just to buy myself some more time to flesh out later chapters.]

    Destructive Harrowings and Becoming a Spectre

    Destructive Harrowings (187-188): [paraphrased] botches result in the character becoming a Spectre.

    House Rule
    Instead, botches result in Oblivion while failure results in a Spectre. Afterwards, we’d sit down with Dark Reflections and draft the new character as a Spectre of an appropriate Caste.

    Why
    Translating the new Spectre into a playable character was fun and cathartic (and Cathartic). Afterwards, we filed the new Spectre away. The plan was if we had enough ex-PCs in the folder, we’d run a few Spectre-oriented stories. [This only happened to one PC, but I’m convinced the theory is sound.]

    The Necrogeography of the Underworld

    Void, Labyrinth, Tempest, and Shadowlands. My troupe understood (more or less) the as-written stratification of the Underworld layers, we just didn’t like the way the Shadowlands operated mechanically. We fixed that by adding another layer - The Deadlands – between the Tempest and the Shadowlands.

    Everything operates as-written until you get out of the Tempest and move towards the Shadowlands. Before you get to the Shadowlands, in our games, you enter the Deadlands.

    The Deadlands

    Very similar to the canon Shadowlands, except there is no interaction with the Quick. It’s the dead reflection of the Skinlands, absent even the fuzzy images of the living. In this layer, if you listen you can hear faint echoes of the Quick on the other side, but only as fleshy meatbag static. Like the canon Shadowlands, Wraiths need Fetters to hang out in the Deadlands.

    Getting from the Deadlands to the Shadowlands is relatively easy. To cross over to the Shadowlands is a metaphysical/psychic effort on par with getting up from a chair or opening a door. Any Wraith can do this in a place the Shroud is less than his Willpower. In the Shadowlands, Wraiths can see and hear the Quick (as-written).

    Why
    We liked the idea of the Shadowlands, but we really liked the idea of a “bustling” Necropolis sitting parallel with a bustling Metropolis. This extra layer helped us have that parallel in a way that didn’t make us think of Bill Cosby getting run over by a car.


    Other Notes

    In my copy of Wraith (Second) on page 231, first column, last paragraph, “When in Stygia itself…” it looks like I wrote in the bottom margin (in my cuneiform-esque chicken scratch) “this is dumb.” I can’t remember if I was adding my inelegant 20-something commentary, or noting that we ignored that rule…

      Okay, I know you said 'not suggestions,' but my biggest 'house rule' is to fill an omission from Wraith: the Oblivion Second Edition, and that's how the dead can use Willpower... Sadly I don't have my notes with me since I'm at work, and those notes are tucking into the pages of that book. The following are personal paraphrases of the ones that I remember being most useful in games.

      Spend a point to add a success to a roll: As per other lines.

      Spend a point to resist a Harrowing: This is only for a single turn, a-la using Willpower by a Vampire to resist frenzy for a single turn, only for dramatic purposes really. This gives the character a chance to make one last plea or statement, usually along the lines of saying, 'I knew this was coming,' or 'Don't leave me behind!' This is something that came up in BOTH of the Wraith games I ran, and it really added to the pathos (but not Pathos) of the experience for the player involved as well as for the rest of the troupe.

      Spend a point to spend one extra Pathos: It's just 'coz sometimes you need to make a BIG thing happen, right?

      ...I know I had some others, many of which made perfect sense, some of which didn't at all. Of course, the above are all limited by the same 'only one point spent per turn' caveat from other games. Of course, the above IS also a suggestion to make sure we get rules on how to use Willpower in Wr20, but MOSTLY it's just me adding to the discussion. Honest!

              I'm planning to run a Wraith game in the not-too-distant future if I can find players. The biggest house rule I'll be adding isn't mechanical, but setting-based. I'll be reinstating the Guilds and divorcing the Tempest, Stygia and the Legions from the setting. In a Shadowlands-focused game, I like Stygia and the Tempest as a mostly-unknown "next world" that 'rules' over the Shadowlands but mostly in-absentia. Legionnaries are feared ghost-armies that occasionally storm through to remind everyone whose "in charge". Slavers and forgers also come from the Tempest, making it a source of fear and boogeymen.

                      [Found some more notes, these ones buried in my copy of Doomslayers]

                      Shadows and the Internal Dialogue

                      Background
                      Our troupe went on an amateur Doomslaying stint. Before the characters went off to flirt with Oblivion for fun and profit, the players wanted to know how much information their Shadows knew about them. In short, “Did their Shadows know what they were thinking?”

                      Based on some interactions between Erik and his Shadow (when he kills his boss, I think), I made the following:

                      Ruling
                      In Life, the Shadow and the Psyche were one in the same. The Shadow has the same memories that the Wraith possessed in life (perhaps slightly skewed from Dark Passions). However, after the split of their metaphysical corpus collosum [think there might be a pun in there], the internal dialogue that the living take for granted changes.

                      Shadows have access to the same sensory data that their in-control counterparts do. If someone tells the character a secret, his Shadow knows the secret too.

                      Having spent a fair amount of time as the same entity (in life), Shadows can gauge what the character is thinking. If the Shadow catches his Wraith making googley eyes at the busty Anacreon, his Shadow will offer lewd commentary because he knows (based on all that time they spent together) that – when they were alive - he would have made googley eyes at an equally busty meatbag.

                      This does not mean that a Shadow has direct access to what the character is thinking. If a character is planning a trap, his Shadow won’t know the details unless the character “says” something. The Shadow will know that the character is up something (again, based on all that time they spent together), but won’t know enough to warn his buddies on the other side of the Nihil (unless the Shadow saw Return of the Jedi – It’s a Trap!).

                      Relics and Their Usefulness

                      Background
                      A player wanted his character to have a nifty Dr. Who-esque scarf. He wanted to know if he really had to spend Background points to have a scarf. After some debate, we came up with two categories of Relics.

                      Rulings
                      It began with the question, “Does it [the Relic in question] need stats?” If yes, it was deemed a Relic. If no, it was deemed a Curio.

                      At the time, we tried to differentiate between utility and pure-aesthetics. We created Curios as a way for players to have some knickknacks without diluting the theme of scarcity in the Underworld.

                      Curio
                      Each dot of Curio gave a player five points worth of “non-stat’d” Relics. After this ruling, the player took a dot in Curio and gave his character:
                      • Aforementioned nifty Dr. Who-esque scarf
                      • A snazzy fedora
                      • An equally snazzy drover coat
                      • And a broken pocket watch. (I think we agreed that a working Relic pocket watch would be a proper Relic)

                      Retrospection
                      At the time, we were trying to cement abstractions that we really didn’t have words to describe. Being slightly older, wiser, and cripplier [sic], I think I’d update my previous ruling slightly.

                      I would approach this subject as a matter of mis-en-scene versus narrative. Rather than asking if an item needs stats, I’d consider what kind of impact it would have on the story – the hitherto unwritten script that is a Chronicle. Is the Relic part of the Set, or is it a crucial part of the Plot?

                      Example One: A Knife
                      A knife can alter the narrative. It can (violently) resolve conflict or present a source of conflict, depending who’s brandishing it and why.

                      “You call that a knife? THIS is a knife!”

                      “That's not a knife, that's a spoon.”

                      “Alright, alright you win. I see you've played knifey-spoony before.”

                      Example Two: A Spoon
                      Outside of very story-specific reasons (perhaps bribing the Wraith of Bill Cosby or a Soundgarden fan), a spoon has no impact on the Chronicles’ narrative.

                      Overall, I’m not sure I agree with my past self for the reasons why, but I think we would have come up with a similar conclusion.

                              I really like all your house rules, Nazfool. This one in particular I also came up with independently:

                              Originally posted by Nazfool View Post
                              It began with the question, “Does it [the Relic in question] need stats?” If yes, it was deemed a Relic. If no, it was deemed a Curio.
                              I called them "corpus relics", although I like "curio" better. I didn't charge for them since by definition they aren't apparently useful. I also said:

                              * If one gets separated from you, it will disappear/disintegrate and reappear near/on you by the next scene. While it is separated from you, there will be a wispy thread of light connecting it to you. Watch closely for this when strangers try to sell you things.

                              * They can be consumable, like a bottomless flask or pack of cigarettes -- but technically, consuming them costs corpus over time (about 1C for every 2 packs or flasks-full). So, you wouldn't want to routinely dump them out or treat the whole bar; and it is possible (though not recommended) for a depressed wraith to smoke/drink himself into a harrowing.

                              I also encouraged "corpus shifting", so people could -- if they're into that -- narrate their corpus altering itself to befit (or clash with) the situation, or to match their character's mood or evolving self-image. Same rule of thumb -- any items shouldn't be too useful. Don't be cheesy or the GM will veto it. It was never much of a problem, but obviously YMMV.

                                  Altering Appearance Score with Moliate

                                  Appropriate uses of Moliate can be used as cosmetic surgery to alter the subject's Appearance score. Uglifying is easy; it can subtract from Appearance by 1 point for every success, to a minimum score of 1. (Inanimate objects with no anthropomorphic qualities effectively don't have an Appearance score.) Appearance is partly behavioral, though; Moliate can only raise it above its natural rating by 1 point for every 3 successes on the roll. It helps to have experience with realistic sculpting (Dex + Crafting) or actual cosmetic surgery (Int + Medicine specialty). Assuming the Moliate roll is minimally successful, a second roll can be made on one of these, using the better outcome of the two to determine how many Appearance dots were added or removed.

                                      I really like the concept of Curios as super-duper minor Relics.

                                      I had a similar but less formatted idea in that I had a sort of default Relic 0 that every character could have that represented something they possessed with a sort of invisible level of invested emotion. The most usual example was cloths. I didn't think of clothing as a part of self-image to the point that a wraith would come across the Shroud with their Corpus shaped as clothing. (They could do that later with Moliate, if they wanted. Or they could just wear Relic clothing, Moliated or soul-forged wraith as couture... and it was couture because it was a sign of wealth and status). But, people do have an emotional investment in not being naked, so a wraith would come across with a few Relic items of clothing. Usually the clothes that you really liked but had worn out and been thrown away. (Lots of comfy socks among wraiths, I'm sure). If you were cremated, the clothing you went into the oven with. If you had died in some horrible accident that destroyed your body and clothes, you had a full outfit. It also gave the characters some immediate barter items... They could trade off their sub-Relics for other things. (As wraiths don't NEED clothes).

                                      But Curios accomplishes the same ideas much better. Buy a dot of Relic and get 5 Curios. If your character really doesn't have any emotional attachment to the concept of being clothed and/or had no emotional attachment to anything sartorial, he or she DOES come across the Shroud naked as a jaybird in my games from now on. And Curios encompasses the idea of having some apparently really useless crap as a Relic (see spoon vs. knife above).

                                          I'm still going through my copious notes from back in the day looking for stuff worth posting. It's a process.

                                          Advantages of Masks

                                          House Rule:                                           If your target is wearing a mask, you're at +1 difficulty to                                           analyze her using Castigate or Lifeweb.

                                          Why: Much is made of the popularity of masks in the fiction and setting info, but not much explanation as to why any powerful wraith in her right mind would constantly wear one, making it easy to steal or replicate it to assume her identity. The setting also makes more sense if they generally remove the masks upon greeting other important individuals. The mask still limits the number of people who have seen, and could therefore replicate, their faces, and removing it momentarily provides strong evidence the mask itself isn't a Moliated fake.


                                          These next two are really more setting embellishments than house rules. They operate on the principle: just because your mind managed to project you into the underworld doesn't mean it arrived intact.                                           I'm not 100% sure I really came up with these so much as inferred them from the published material.

                                          Partial Amnesia

                                          House Rule: Wraiths, especially newer ones, tend to have partial amnesia concerning events prior to their deaths.

                                          Why: It can make for fun plot devices, and a more gradual lifting of the caul's confusion; but mostly it's a liberating roleplaying convention encouraging players to have their characters remember interesting things about their past, or skills that would be hard to learn once you're dead.

                                          Drone Thralls

                                          House Rule: Drones are somewhat common, and can have varying degrees of intelligence and memory. Reaped drones the Hierarchy determines are stable and no threat to the Dictum Mortuum are commonly branded as thralls of the appropriate Legion and left mostly to their own devices like mental patients deemed self-sufficient, perhaps even taught to defend themselves so they're less likely to feed Oblivion. The case for releasing them this way instead of forging them is stronger if they do useful menial labor -- probably similar to their job in life, e.g., janitor or low-level security guard. It's illegal, and considered cruel, to Moliate or detain drone thralls in any way. For would-be slavers to profit from one, the brand must be tampered with and the drone probably trafficked to another jurisdiction where the intake staff at the Citadel are less likely to recognize her.

                                          Why: It makes things more eery when the PCs commonly encounter somewhat-functional lunatics who may not fully realize they're dead. Together with partial amnesia (above) and the usual Shadow-induced insanity, the line between drone and wraith can become an unsettlingly grey continuum. Roleplaying notes: anything you see or hear that doesn't fit into your world view is either not there, or perceived as something slightly or completely different.

                                              Background: Consort (1-3 points): You are attuned to someone or something with regard to all Arcanoi you command. Costs 1 point for a Fetter, 2 for a close friend/lover, or 3 otherwise.

                                              Merit/Flaw: Affected by Arcanos (1-5 points): You have been Moliated in some way, connected to a living person via Soul Pact (Lifeweb 5), or otherwise permanently affected by some combination of the arts within one Arcanos. This can be a Merit or a Flaw, and the point value is equal to the level of the highest-level art necessary to achieve the end effect (but can be lower, at the GM's discretion). Ignore all costs in Pathos, Corpus, Willpower, and Angst. If the wraith or one of his allies possesses all the necessary arts, this cannot be taken as a Flaw, but you can assume the maximum number of successes given the practitioner's current abilities (with no assistance from her Shadow). If not, assume successes equal to the highest level needed, plus two.
