    Why do you like simulationism?

    04-13-2017, 09:21 PM
    I'd like to discuss something. I have a slight feeling that the new editions of WoD games will move toward lighter rules and narrative style. I'm basing it on their background in nordic LARP and on the LARP games they organized so far. It's not a secret, that I'm not a big fan of narrative mechanics and I somewhat fear that it will turn out to be something like the 7th Sea 2nd edition in comparison to its first. But pondering on that is not really the goal here.

    WoD was and is always a more story-centered game. In a sense, I think it's approach paved the way to today's super-ruels-light, narrative games. At least I'm sure a lot of the creators of those games were influenced by it back in the day. WoD also has a fairly light ruleset in comparison to it's contemporary peers. Although, despite all that, it is a classic rpg in a sense that it has a fairly detailed ruleset and moderately simulationist mechanics, advancement tracks, characters defined by stats, not keywords and the lack of narrative rules, among other things.

    So, what I really want to talk about and hear your opinions, estimated dwellers in the dark:

    - What do you think, what are the merits of detailed rules and simulation? Not that it's better or not than the other side (I think it's a matter of personal taste), but what do you like in those kind of games, in comparison?

    - How can details enhance gameplay?

    - How simulation could add to the story?

    - How all these aspects are affecting the overall gaming experience?

    Disclaimer: I don't want it to derail to a "what is the true way of gaming" argument, I'm interested in a positive thread, to hear about other people's thoughts and experiences, please bear that in mind, thanks!

    If nothing worked, then let's think!

      The merits of detailed rules is that storytellers have the option to use or remove rules rather than having to make the rules up whole cloth (or to adjust the rules to fit their campaigns). Simulation games are also usually more balanced than narrative games because the designers of simulation games tend to test every aspect of their game (though there are always mistakes made because the designers are human). Details add options, which means that characters can be unique, while narrative games tend to make characters that tend to quite similar. The primary weakness of simulation games is that the storyteller must understand the system backwards-and-forwards to make sure that the games mover smoothly while narrative games can be run with only a basic understanding of the system.

          I emphasize narrative storytelling, actually, but suspension of disbelief is vital for a powerful narrative. To know that the NPC's have their own agendas and trajectories in the background, and will continue to follow them rather than just being there to support the PC's stories, is a beautiful thing. A well-defined environment is grounding and enriching. It will have a lot more surprises for the players. Narratives are often made from familiar tropes. A thoroughly-realized world can give rise to much more natural and unique scenarios--truth is stranger than fiction. You don't create truth in a roleplaying game, but you can simulate it.

                      I use ST and DM interchangeable since I play a lot of D&D in addition to WoD.

                      I don't like rails. I appreciate that an ST can write a good story, but if I have little to no impact on the story other than playing a predetermined part I very quickly lose interest. I like narratives, but I don't like narrativism, where the system is designed to serve the plot. More often than not I find that extensive fiat causes stories to flounder than succeed. In a narrativist or rules light system it's often been the case that, say a character has +20 to pick locks, the the DM decides he needs a 30 because a chance of failure keeps it "interesting", despite the character being a master lockpick and the lock being relatively simple. In a simulationist system you know approximately how good a character is in comparison to the world around them. How good does a character need to be to beat an Army private most of the time? How good do they have to be to hack into a personal computer? A corporate server? A military server? In many cases pure narrativism dictates that how good they are exactly does not matter, what matters is whether or not the events are "interesting", where in a simulationist system you can be generally comfortable in knowledge that your character is good at things they're good at, and bad at things they're bad at. So I often see silly contrivances where a master lockpick breaks his picks on a plywood door with a garbage lock because the ST didn't think it would be interesting if the characters managed to get in or out of whatever scenario the door was enforcing.

                      Some modern systems have taken this so far as to have "Story" or "Fate" points that a player can spend to summon plot points, which I am not a fan of. My preferred style has always been that the players control their characters, the ST controls the world around them and it's reactions. Simulationism fits that best. It's not the characters against the DM or the characters against the pamphlet of very linear notes the DM has written about the story, it's the characters against the world, and the consequences of their own actions.

                      I will say that this is obviously my preference. Play RPGs however you and your players enjoy. I just raise an eyebrow when an ST tells me that the chronicle being run is inspired by a book that s/he wrote.

                      And while I used the terms to respond to your post, I think GNS theory is garbage. I like "simulationist" systems, but I also value good roleplay and don't think it's something that can be mechanically compelled. And I'm "Gamist" in the sense that I think every character should be equally capable of contributing to the overall goals of the group.
