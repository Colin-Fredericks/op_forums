    The order of reason - for the man?

    02-22-2019, 12:21 AM
    Ok, I'm rusty. Last time I read mage, it was a year ago. And it was progenitor, M20, toybox, and a few others.

    Question. Can I say that the order of reason, who transformed into the technocracy later on, was a champion of the common man? That they were the first ones to try and give magic into the common man. To try and give magic and power to the masses in the form of 'technology'. And so that's why people rallied to them, and their dedication and unity and cooperation, caused the people to rally to them. And that's how they won the war. By putting magic and power into the hands of the common man.

    Is what I said, correct, or at least, a reasonable interpretation?

      I think it's somewhat misleading - but it's certainly what the Order believes.

      We know that mages were engaged with Sleeper communities for millennia before the Order's founding - the ancestors of modern Trads and Conventions were championing literacy, agriculture, weaving, animal husbandry, and metalwork, for instance, and ministering to and protecting their respective cultures. What is distinctive about the period immediately prior to the founding of the Order - the time frame roughly analogous to the Dark Ages Mage era - is mages banding together into Fellowships and pursuing mystic attainment, artifacts, and personal magical power instead of attending to their fellow man. The Order of Hermes is obviously a chief offender here, but to a greater or lesser extent all of the mages of that time period are heavily focused on Mage Stuff, to the detriment of Sleeper society. It became very easy to consider the pursuit of enlightenment more important than helping the people around them.

      (I think we might credibly argue that the mages of the High Mythic Age are more powerful than their predecessors, with more nodes and artifacts in their grasp, but that magic is less powerful as a whole, as the mages have neglected to maintain its role in human life and has let bygone and spirit allies fall by the wayside in the pursuit of Wizard Politics)

      The Craftmasons - with their focus on humble, down-to-earth, stable magic and their commitment to "the common man" - are an outlier among the Fellowships of that era, and it's their influence that pushes the Order - and, later, the Traditions - to reconsider the mage relationship with Sleepers. I'm more critical of the Craftmasons than others (I think their stereotype in the fandom is more democratic than is merited from the text), but their contribution is very important - the other mages needed the kick in the ass.

                                                  Is what I said, correct, or at least, a reasonable interpretation?
                                                  It's been a while since I've read that part, but as I recall it that's not exactly correct
                                                  The Order of Reason was indeed championing for the common man, but they didn't want to give magic to the common man.
                                                  Even with technology, they didn't want to "putting magic and power in the hands of the common man."
                                                  It was more like "stripping the overpowered 1% of magic and power" so that the common man wouldn't be a victim of those pesky mages anymore.
                                                  It wasn't about redistribution of power and magic, it was more about "no more magic for anyone"

                                                  Now, I'm sure in modern times there are softer ideals among the technocracy that want to "put the power in the hands of the common man"
                                                  But I think it's that blur niche where the Traditions fit in.
                                                  Otherwise the Technocracy would automatically be the good guys, no doubt. But things are a bit more... relative.

                                                  The point is that a flexible reality is a problem because just a handful of people are Awakened, and so the Sleepers are helpless.
                                                  While in a fixed reality (a completely static one) no one can alter reality, no one has power and so the playground is even for everyone.

                                                  The counterargument of the Traditions is basically "let's put the power in the hands of everyone for real", to Ascend humankind in an Awakened state.
                                                  Reality returns to be flexible (as in ancient times) and people, the common man, can truly and freely decide what to do with it, and stop living in a fixed reality chosen by someone else (the Order of Reason)
                                                  Which depending on personal trust towards humankind might be a good or bad idea. Optimism against pessimism, really.

                                                  I don't remember in which book I've read a passage like this but I'm pretty sure there was one. I think it's the core premise. You have to choose if it's better for the common man to be master of its own fate on a metaphysical level, or master of its own fate in a metaphysically artificial playground.

                                                  Funny thing yesterday I was explaining the setting to a friend and I said "if you think about it, the Order of Reason were the good guys".
                                                  But I'm not really sure about it. Which is interesting
