    Originally posted by Theodrim View Post
    Speaking to the topic and premises behind the topic, if you're looking for the Syndicate to expressly do things in "mage X does Y thing, and due to a direct causal relationship between X and Y, Z occurs" terms from a Doylist perspective, you're not getting very far. You're not getting very far with that perspective with any mage, tradition or technocrat. Let alone disparates, nephandi, or god help us, marauders.

    ...

    From a Doylist perspective, is the Syndicate mage filing paperwork that doesn't matter, sending it to a requisitions office that doesn't exist, and in return some silly thing is poofing into existence at some random predetermined location? Well, yeah. But it's Mage, Mage doesn't work from a Doylist perspective.
    This is broadly true, but it runs aground on the Syndicate Agent himself. He's going to need to know how the supply chain exists, how it works, and how it will manifest when it delivers. I think if we are at the point where the Syndicate has a fake department to mask that field agents aren't doing what they think they are doing then something has gone wrong. Nor do I think that problem goes away if said department or infrastructure doesn't exist but is just assumed.

    The Hermetic knows how his magic works and has spent quite a bit of time preparing his implements so they are ready to use when needed. His spell manifests the lightning, but it doesn't manifest the staff. That's already there. The supply network needs to be as well.

    That all being said. I think a Syndicate Agent would be very at home with the idea that there is much more connection between the numbers on the ledger and the actual shipping/recieving process than the typical sleeper thinks. Terms like Post Hoc Supply Chain Management and Just Before Time Inventory Systems come to mind. I could easily see him altering shipping routes and manifests so that an item which was in the warehouse was actually in a nearby truck. A kind of commerce based reality hacking.

    This is all, as Aleph said, for a PBD system. If we are running Results Based Determinism things get much more fluid.

        Not really. I mean first things first, doublethink and ignoring cognitive dissonance is kind of what the Technocracy does. That a Syndicate agent may be filing requisitions to a department they know doesn't actually exist, to justify acts like teleportation or creation ex nihilo, is totally fine when they're conditioned not to overthink it. What matters is they believe it works, and for them it would -- they're still mages however impenetrable their logic may be.

        This brings me back to my original point: any paradigm falls apart when put to the strain of undue scrutiny. The Syndicate isn't deserving of a higher degree of scrutiny than any other discrete group of mages.

        I ran into this issue in my most recent Technocrat game, in which someone who had only played TSC before decided to play a Syndicate agent and had a teeny bit of trouble wrapping her head around Ars Cupiditiae at first. Two-thirds of the people at my table are hardcore Trek fans, including her husband who's also one of my players, so the natural resolution to it was to direct her to the DS9 episode "Treachery, Faith, and the Great River". What's relevant on the deeper level, is the Ferengi concept of the Great Material Continuum: their metaphysical principle that everything is connected via value, supply, and demand; that goods and services flow from supplier to demander in a way akin to the laws of thermodynamics; and, the nature of good business is to facilitate that flow from supplier to demander.

        That's Primal Utility in a nutshell, really.

        What's superficially relevant, is the character Nog launches on a series of trade deals, that involve some extremely odd items and shenanigans, leaving the audience to wonder how Nog knew the incredibly obscure set of info he knew, how the pieces all fit together, and how he managed to pull off some of the "heists" he did to facilitate the trade deals, in the first place. One could point at the episode and scream "plot hole!"...or they could not think about how Nog stole Sisko's desk without anyone noticing and enjoy the episode, and think "how could I use this for inspiration for a Syndicate mage?".

        This is all, as Aleph said, for a PBD system. If we are running Results Based Determinism things get much more fluid.
        To a certain extent, this is the level of incrementalism I'm attacking. It's nice for discussing the game from a meta-narrative perspective, but highly dysfunctional when it comes to application in a game. That's actually why I dislike M20 to a certain extent, but particularly it's game supplements like HDYDT? which I personally straight-up reject at the table save sample rotes and examples.

                    Remember that M20 also uses the hypothetical average bystander. That means that you can summon an Uber (taxis are so last decade) without needing to know in advance whether there are any around. It's no different from pulling a coin out of your pocket: the HAB can't see into your pocket, and the HAB can't see what's around the block. So you don't have to determine whether or not an Uber actually is nearby before calling it; if you achieve the necessary successes in a “summon a ride” Effect, then there is one nearby. You didn't create it; it was already there.

                    Note that this isn't a Syndicate vs. Hermetic thing: both can summon a ride from “off-camera” as a Coincidental Effect, differing only in the Practice and Instruments used to do the summoning. HAB allows that for everyone. In particular, Coincidence isn't about whether there's a rational connection between what the mage did and what followed; it's about whether what followed makes any sense on its own (though there is the Domino Effect to consider, for repeated Coincidences).

                                                        Originally posted by Ramnesis View Post

                                                        The difference is that in this case the Convention is propping up a paradigm it knows is a lie. It is deliberately building in an inconsistency that doesn't have to be there...
                                                        If it's a lie but it works, then it wasn't a lie. All paradigms are lies, and all groups of mages engage in doublethink and prop up lies whether they explicitly acknowledge it or not. That's no more obvious in practice than mystics eschewing tools and foci as they grow more powerful, and come to the understanding paradigm isn't what makes their shit work in the first place. They do it because it's easier to externalize belief, even though that externalized belief is completely illusory.

                                                        Technocrats just happen to be mages who specialize in doublethink, to make their shit simpler and standardized. And the "doublethink" of this is the key, simply for how technocrats deliberately overlook the complete lack of logical consistency of their own work, on the micro scale, in order to ensure it works on the macro scale.

                                                        RBD and PBD are purely rules adjudication methods. They only take on any kind of meta-narrative quality if we selectively apply them to different paradigms. Some people do that, true, but I was not.
                                                        They're inherently metanarrative; Mage is a game about metanarrative and metanarrative cannot be divorced from any of its concepts how strongly divorced from the setting, metaplot, or even mechanics, it may seem on first glance. Mage PC's are, in effect, ST's from a Watsonian perspective; the rules you're attempting to adjudicate are how characters act as the editorial board for their own personal, and interconnected, narratives in the first place.

                                                        I'll put it another way: fuck "PBD", fuck "RBD", fuck "HAB", fuck "HOO". Imagine that instead of interactive storytelling, your average game of Mage was a television show or movie, and we're going with "Hopeless Average Viewer" (HAV) and "Habitual Asshole Neckbeard" (HAN). Anything that happens that'll cause HAN to run to their computer and make a 10:06-long YouTube ragebait video for dat ad revenue about how Mage: the Televisioning was full of plot holes and totally unwatchable, well that's just magic. Something that happens that's blatant enough to cause HAV to put down their popcorn and say, "huh?" is vulgar magic.

                                                        HAV checks out and barely pays any attention to what's going on screen at all, and doesn't really think about it too hard. So if a character pulls a gun out of a pocket in a scene when the film/movie didn't establish they had one in their pocket to begin with, they're not going to notice or care. HAN is a nitpicking shithead that doesn't really care about the circumstances or context of the scene, they just get pissy because there wasn't an establishing shot of the person having a gun in their pocket.

                                                        You're probably wondering right now what the effective difference between what I'm suggesting is between HAB and HOO. There isn't any, because it was about metanarrative in the first place.
