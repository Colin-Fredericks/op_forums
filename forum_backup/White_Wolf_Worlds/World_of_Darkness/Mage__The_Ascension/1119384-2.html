        21) Scepter of Simulacrum (5 pt. Talisman; 4 Arete):
        A bronze rod, topped with a tiny statue of a stately, faceless figure, wearing a tiny gold crown. The base of the statue, where it connects to the rod, is made from alternating rings of polished granite, clay, and marble. Each blend together, and have had relief images carved or cast into their combined surface, depicting tiny stick figures in various poses. Some mages claim the scepter to hail from the Roman era, but most scholars think it was a later creation. Texts in the libraries of the Order of Hermes reference the item as early as the Order's founding, though the "oldest" texts may be forgeries.

        Legend states that the creator of the Scepter was a Roman magus, dwelling somewhere in the Mediterranean. According to the myth, a gorgon terrorized an isolated fishing village, on an island or sequestered patch of beach. The magus, seeking to rid the populace of the threat, crafted the Scepter of Simulacrum, as well as a set of early spectacles that used tiny mirrors to allow the wearer to navigate by reflection. When he confronted the gorgon in her den, filled with stone humans, frozen in last moments of horror, he did not fight her directly. Instead, he raised the rod and commanded that the statues move. And lo, the statues moved. The gorgon shrieked in fear and alarm as her own petrified victims surrounded her, seized her limbs, and tore her to pieces. With the deed complete, the magus collected the monster's remains, and crafted powerful concoctions and further Wonders from them.

        Time passed, and the Scepter of Simulacrum found its way into the Order of Hermes' hands. It was last seen in the magical armory of Horizon - ready to rouse any of the realm's hundreds of strategically placed statuary - before it fell to Technocratic forces. Whereabouts currently unknown.

        System: Matter 3 animates statues, causing them to walk and act according to the user's command. Each success affects one statue, which the mage directs by using their action. Stela - carved forms set in background materials like walls - slip out of their base like a figure exiting water, complete with likely limbs and body parts made from like material. These statues can be small figures or large sculptures, though statues larger than the average human require two successes to animate. The figures will move and behave according to the type of creature they simulate - a man walks like a man, a horse trots like a horse, and a serpent slithers like a serpent. They do not act against this borrowed nature, nor bend their forms in angles impossible for the "real McCoy", if such a being exists. They move, at most, as fast as a human's walking speed, and have physical stats appropriate for their form (a foot-high figure will have no Strength, whereas a 10 foot statue will have a sufficiently great power). The user makes all relevant Ability rolls for the simulacrum. If they aren't following any commands, they freeze in place. The Effect lasts for one scene by default.

        Flaw: The Scepter of Simulacrum is limited to the kinds of statues its creator would know of. In short, it can only command statues constructed from natural materials, like rock, clay and ceramics, and metals. Modern, artificial creations like plastics do not move.

            22) Cloak of Many Colors (7 pt. Artifact):
            The prized clothing of an artist mage, this combination of flowing robe and accompanying cape sports a high collar, manifold pockets, and a dizzying array of colors and patterns. The fabric, however, seems unmarred by paint, despite it being the creation of a painter, who wore the cloak even when hard at work in his studio. Some claim the artist painted the cloak into being, ripping it from the canvas and into reality. A fitting origin, if true, given the cloak's powers.

            A vain magus, the painter saw himself as a work of art. He saw his path to Ascension as a relentless attempt to perfect himself, that he might transcend the canvas of reality. Because of his obsession with blending Art with Life, he created a Wonder that allowed him to hop between the 3D and 2D planes at will. The Cloak of Many Colors turns the wearer into a painted image, that could travel across walls, floors, ceilings, and other surfaces. When used on another image, its second power comes into play: by walking "off panel", he could travel freely to other images. It was said the painter would appear to his apprentices and consors through the images around them. An annoying and mildly disturbing habit that painted him (pun intended) as a hopeless eccentric, even by mage standards. It fueled the rumors that the artist was a long sufferer of Quiet, or had perhaps even slipped into Marauder-dom at some point. This he vehemently denied; his eccentricities were conscious decisions, made by a man who wished to live a singular, unique existence. Not a copy of any other man, but himself alone.

            System: Life 4 + Matter 3 changes the wearer into a painted image and affixes them to a surface, allowing them to crawl along it freely. This may also be used to enter physical images, such as photographs and paintings, and travel through their depicted environment as if physically present. Correspondence 3 allows the wearer to move from one painting to another, with no regard for intervening distance. Obviously, both Effects are quite Vulgar.

            Flaw: The Cloak of Many Colors carries a measure of its creator's eccentricity. While wearing the cloak, whenever someone addresses the wearer by name, they will respond with the phrase "That's me!" Even if it means interrupting the other speaker. The wearer cannot help themselves, mirroring the creator's insistence on reiterating his singular existence at every opportunity. There is also another strange Flaw - whenever the user crawls over a bloodstain, their passage over the surface is marked by bloody footprints. It is as if the blood is fresh and wet in the 2D space, and passing over it results in leaving a trail away from it. This trail does not go away after the Wonder's Effect ends, and the trail's length is proportional to how fresh the stain is when the wearer passes over it.

                23) Cross of Shame [4 pt. Artifact]:
                Crafted by a Southern preacher and Chorister, this silver cross necklace was rolled out during the height of the American civil rights marches of the 1950s. Taking to heart the ideals of nonviolent protest - as begun by Ghandi and implemented further by Martin Luther King Jr. - this Wonder aids the wearer in pursuit of that goal. When the user is attacked - whether by stick or bullet or fire hose - the attacker is made to feel great shame. Moreover, it makes those around the attacker feel disgust toward the latter.

                System: One Mind 2 effect causes the primary target to feel shame, while another instills disgust in others.

                Flaw: The Wonder is, first and foremost, a tool of nonviolent protest. It only works when the user has been harmed, and only if the user has not themselves attacked another. Only one willing to adhere to the path of nonviolence can benefit from the Wonder.

                    24) The Rock [2 pt Artifact]:
                    Not just any rock, but THE Rock. A polished, irregularly shaped gray stone, small enough to fit in the palm of the hand. It's magic is simple: when thrown, it hits harder. A strong arm can easily slay a pig with one hit from this projectile. Otherwise, there is nothing significant about the stone in and of itself, magically or aesthetically.

                    The real reason why The Rock is significant - relatively - is because of the rich, extensive LORE surrounding the stone. It is a legendary chunk of earth, with hundreds of tales told about it. Most serious mage scholars consider the bulk of these tales to be utter rubbish - or at the very least exaggerated - but less serious mages love to indulge in The Rock's mystique. It's a game, really, to invent or distort the tales surrounding the item. Many hours of speech and gallons of ink have been invested in espousing the supposed legendary origins and feats of The Rock (also known as The Rock of Gamigoroth, the Pebble of Pig Smiting, the Earth Mother's Fury, and the Giant's Gallstone, among many other affectionate titles).

                    There are tales of The Rock's origins. That it was cast in Hell in Lucifer's personal forge. That it was plucked from the center of the earth by the ancient Golo monks. That the first cave mage carved it from a chunk of meteorite. That it is all that remains of a legendary mage, turned to stone by the gods for his or her hubris. That it was the first stone coughed up out of the primordial chaos. That it was the stone Cain(e) used to murder Abel, and absorbed the fallen brother's blood. Etc, etc. And there are epic tales of its use down through the ages. That Hercules used it to slay the buzzards that tormented Prometheus. That it was the stone in David's sling that slew Goliath. That the stone was the first shot fired in the (current) Ascension War, and that it will be the weapon that will one day end it. That Heylel carried it while he betrayed the First Cabal. Etc, etc.

                    The number of tales pertaining to The Rock are numerous and ever-changing. If you ask one thousand mages who know of The Rock, they will offer one thousand different stories. Through Time checks, it's clear that some of the less outrageous stories may have occurred, though post-cognition is a notoriously unreliable art. Not helped by the fact that, because the enchantment on the Artifact is so basic (it performs an Effect any Forces novice can do), there are dozens of stones circulating around that are purported to be the real deal.

                    The stone considered the most likely genuine article currently sits on the desk of a Danish Hermetic wizard, bani House Quasitor, who collects stories about The Rock as a hobby. For him, The Rock serves partly as an emergency weapon, partly as a conversation starter, and partly as a paperweight.

                    System: Forces 2 focuses and enhances The Rock's motion in flight, making it more accurate and hit harder. Difficulty to hit a target is -3, and does one additional health level of damage. The damage it does is Lethal. It has also been proofed against the ravages of time and any damage, mundane or magical, short of fifth level magic (the product of Matter 5, signifying that someone cared a great deal about the item). Why this particular stone was given such treatment and power is a mystery, though it can be (and has been) easily replicated by a mage with enough power to create Wonders.

                    Flaw: The Rock seems to invite people to invent stories about it, and to give it greater value than it perhaps deserves. Even if only for ironic purposes, people simply can't help adding to its alleged "lore".

                        25) Seed Money [1 pt. Charm]:
                        Ancient legend tells of a wealthy man who lost his fortune, only for the gods of fortune to compensate him for his loss. In time his wealth returned to him twice over. In a perfectly expected tragic turn, he proceeded to squander his wealth again, confident he would be reimbursed twice over again...

                        For centuries and unto the present day, mages have understood a basic principle: spend money to make money. By specially preparing coins, magicians of old would destroy these coins, as an offering to the gods, in exchange for good luck. A slightly expensive way to change reality, but then magick has never exactly been cheap anyway; whether it costs effort, time, or rare resources, most every sort of mystical or technomantic power requires investment of some sort. Still, in recent times, the practice of using coins for this process has given way to using paper currency (often bills soaked in some sort of prepared tincture, holy water or oils, or marked with arcane words or sigils). Two reasons precipitated this change: coins becoming generally devalued in relation to paper (there are few coins minted in most modern countries that are made of valuable metals), and because it's easier to tear up or burn a slip of paper than it is to crush a metal disc.

                        Other forms of this Charm eschew actual destruction, instead involving the deliberate giving away of the coin or bill. The idea being that, so long as the mage doesn't have the money themselves, they have made sufficient sacrifice. Especially if it was given away charitably, which certain groups like the Celestial Chorus and Templar Knights consider more admirable than destroying the currency's value (one of the few points upon which they and the Syndicate agree). However, the spread of this idea - that wealth given away can result in return greater returns - has lead to a practice and philosophy that both groups abhor: the so-called "Prosperity Gospel". Choristers and Templars alike have watched in horror as certain corrupt televangelists (or, perhaps worse, televangelists who originally had their hearts in the right place) encouraged their followers to give money away (to their church, of course), in the hopes that the Lord would multiply their returns, or even work other miracles in their lives. A practice which has lead to many desperate, faithful people bankrupting themselves, or spending money on this rather than vitally needed medical procedures. It's a contentious topic among faithful mages, the merits of spreading faith being debated against the real human costs of so many people being swindled. The Syndicate, meanwhile, often has hands directly in those very megachurches that draw revenue by exploiting the Prosperity Gospel, as part of its general "if you can't beat it, exploit it" policy of handling superstition.

                        Still, the use of Seed Money by mages continues, despite the controversy.

                        System: A simple Entropy 2 Effect changes fortune in the mage's favor, usually towards a specific end. It might be used to allow the mage to obtain more money (whether by happenstance, legitimate earnings increasing, or gambling). It might be used to ensure a safer journey or find "by luck" the one door that happens to have been left unlocked or ajar. Or it might be done before a fight, in order to enhance the user's chances of survival. Simply destroy the currency or part with it, and the luck will flow.

                        Flaw: If the mage ever tries to reclaim a measure of the wealth - melting a broken coin into valuable raw metals, taping the torn up bill together, fishing the coin from the fountain, stealing it back from the bum on the street, etc. - the forces of Fate and Fortune will turn against the mage for their duplicity. If a dollar bill circulates through the money supply and ends up in the mage's hands by accident, it's fine; they never intended to see it again. Same if the money is eventually returned by whoever they gave it to. But if the mage takes the wealth back intentionally, they will suffer bad luck as recompense. Heaven does not enjoy having their sacrifices stolen form the altars at the last minute.

                            26) Thaumanomics - The Trading Card Game(TM) [2 pt. Primer; Arete 1, Forces 1, Matter 2]:
                            The 90s saw a boom in collectibles, which in turn encouraged aspiring mages to use this to their advantage in promoting their ideologies...and encouraging Awakenings. While some used the medium of comic books (or, as so many wanted to rebrand them, graphic novels), two magi from Hermetic House Thig took a different track. One a game designers, the other an artist/writer, they collaborated to create a Primer in a different collectible medium: trading cards. It was determined that a commercially distributed Primer would be harder to detect by the Technocracy if it were in the form of disparate cards, since no serious Technocrat would conceive of it. They were not wrong; it took two decades for the Union to realize what had happened.

                            Thauminomics - The Trading Card Game was released at the height of the trading card boom. For this reason, it had trouble reaching a mass audience, because it was competing against every other trading card game. It also had a low print run; the duo managed to get a Hermetic financier, bani Fortunae, to foot the printing costs, but not to a great extent. Still, it was out in the market, and that was all that mattered.

                            Thauminomics functioned like a regular card game - where players would take on the role of wizards in a combination of battle, monster summoning, and resource management. However, the game mechanics were only half the content - playing the game would only take the user so far towards Awakening (although playing WAS important). Each card also sported artwork with rich visual symbolism, an esoterically important card name, and a paragraph of lore. In cases with cards with complex rules, this resulted in busy ones with a lot of fine print. Half the production cost came from the manufacturer needing to invent a new printing system, just to get the text as small as the creators demanded, while also making it readable. (Indeed, this if nothing else left the game's mark on the trading card industry). Even the watermarks on the cards had lines, symbols, and faint text, that could only be symbolically deciphered when multiple cards were arranged in different shapes. The lore, too, was fragmentary, giving just enough information related to the cards they were printed on, but hinting or implying considerably more. These implications - in riddles, deliberate word choices, and obscure references - could also only be put into proper understanding when examining Thauminomics as a whole.

                            Indeed, in the 128 cards included in the core game, a user would need to study all of them in order to achieve an Awakening. Not only study, however, but use. The player had to run at least one game - and more likely several games - in order to grasp how the rules of play informed aspects of the lore, and vice versa. The flow of play involved positioning cards in various spreads (much like in Tarot reading), making it only possible to grasp core aspects of the creators's message by experiencing the game in action.

                            Each starter deck of Thauminomics also came with a rule book. The main rules told the player how to run a normal game versus one other player, an "Elemental Game" (4 players), and, strangely for a trading card game, a solitaire mode. In order to gain the full benefits from the Primer (not only the Arete increase, but the Sphere knowledge), the player must run each game mode at least once, and will probably need to do so several times each. And given the rich symbolism, the potential initiate into True Magick will need Intelligence 3+ to grasp it, and would benefit from dots in Enigmas.

                            Again, Thauminomics had a low print run, in a time of great competition. So naturally, few people could get their hands on all the cards (especially the four Ultra Rare cards), let alone find other players to run games with. Fewer still were smart enough - and obsessive enough - to dig deeply into the game, and derive the full value. As such, only a handful of people ever Awakened from it during its initial run. Still, a few more Awakenings are better than none, so the team responsible for the cards considered it a success (especially in light of its handicaps). They were so excited, in fact, that even though they took a loss on the original run, they convinced their Fotunae backer to fund an expansion (to be discussed another time). However, once the trading card bubble burst, it became more difficult to expand from there. Moreover, not long after, the Avatar Storm hit, killing the game designer and leaving the artist too emotionally broken to continue.

                            Up until a few years ago, assembling a full set of cards (plus instruction manual) was difficult. Even using copies uploaded to the internet was a non-option, until the advent of cheap scanners that could capture all the details (a black and white photocopy simply does not convey enough information to do the Primer justice). Only a few full sets were assembled by the creators themselves, and either owned by them or sent to various mages for safe keeping. One was sent to be stored in Doisteppe...and so is probably either destroyed or adrift in the Umbra. The artist has since moved on to perfecting their work, but works as a part time art teacher in their retirement. Where they will sometimes teach students how to play this weird old card game they have in their back office.

                            Other than that, the pieces of this Primer are scattered in the hundreds across the English-speaking world, waiting to be assembled. One cabal of Hermetics and Virtual Adepts are attempting to find a full set, so they can scan it and distribute it over the internet properly. Another mage is in talks with the artist about creating a new print run...

                                27) Thauminomics(TM) - As Above, So Below [4 pt. Grimoire; Arete 2, Correspondence 2, Forces 2, Spirit 1]:
                                The first and to date only expansion to Thauminomics - The Trading Card Game, before the line's folding and the eventual death of its lead designer. A Grimoire, it expands upon the foundation established by the original core set of cards, adding 88 new ones to the game. It is possible to gain most of the benefits of this "text" using only these 88 cards, as they have enough material and game pieces to run a successful game on their own. However, gaining the dot in Correspondence requires not only the study of the expansion, but its examination within the larger context of the original 128. The rich lore, symbolic artwork, and especially the arcane designs in the watermarks of the cards elucidate additional secrets when the new cards are mixed with the old.

                                It is this sort of interconnectedness that forms half the point of As Above, So Below. The other half is its introduction of themes pertaining to angelic and infernal powers (hence, the ability to learn Spirit from the Grimoire). The designers, wisely, did not give any actionable details about demons, choosing broad strokes in its lore and mere theory. Lest they give their customers rope to hang themselves with, infernally speaking.


                                Parts of a second expansion - this one supposedly covering introductions to Mind and Time - were designed, but never finished. Roughly half of a planned 100 cards' worth of artwork was painted, symbolism already planted. Such was never to be, of course, but it did not go unused. To recoup some of the losses incurred over the course of the project, the artwork was published in an art book. It and previous works were also incorporated into a different project: another group of Tradition mages, trying to get a tabletop role-playing game off the ground, were given leave to use the art in their source book, which ended up being a module for a different, popular RPG, using an open game license. The rights to a Thauminomics comic book were also sold to another mage, this time a comic book writer/artist. It lasted 12 issues, before being canceled by the publisher, its story left unfinished.

                                In all cases, these other projects made little money, and didn't aspire to being Grimoires themselves. Both of them covertly inserted real spells into their texts - aside from the dry and game-mechanics centered description of in-game spells for the RPG, obviously. The original duo largely felt dissatisfied with these works, since they ended up deviating from the Paradigm established in the Thauminomics card game. They felt the liberties taken by these other teams - not all of whom were even Hermetic - confused those who had invested themselves in the cards, and who Awakened to that game's world view. Little could be done, however.

                                    28) The Match Patch [1 or 2 pt. Artifact]:
                                    A black silk eyepatch, with a fine leather strap. The outward facing side of the patch is often adorned with a stylized eye or flame symbol stitched to its surface. Is usually built so that it can be worn over either eye.

                                    Pirates and other sailing men long knew the utility of the eyepatch when at sea, even when the wearer had two functioning eyes. When transitioning from the bright above to the darkness belowdeck - and vice versa - it can be disorienting and inconvenient to have to wait for the eyes to adjust between the differing light levels. A patch worn over one eye meant the person could adjust more quickly to the darkness, with one ocular organ kept in darkness. Some sea-faring Mages - including the Explorators of the Order of Reason - understood this principle, and crafted eyepatches that could readily maximize and exploit it. More mystical mages - and some of the more secretly superstitious Explorators - even found ways to make the patch give night vision with the patch even for sockets with poor, nonfunctioning, or missing eyeballs.

                                    System: When the user pulls the patch up from their eye after having worn it for at least half an hour, a Forces 1 Effect gives the user the ability to see in low light. So long as there is some light in the location, the user will see through the patched eye as if it were a bright sunny day. With the 2 pt. version, the Match Patch endows the user with true blind sight; even if there is no light - or the user is blind in that eye - they will see in night vision.

                                    Flaw: The color palette the user sees in will be monochromatic, either in black and while or in shades of one color (like red, green, or blue). Moreover, bright lights will blind the user if exposed to it while using the patch's Effect. Certain Match Patches have been known to cause the user's covered eye to glow orange-red in the dark when the Effect is active; a warm-colored flicker, like a match lit in the darkness. It's this Flaw that gave the Wonder its common name.

                                        29) Das Puppenspieler Handbuch [5 pt. Grimoire; Arete 2, Prime 2, Matter 2, Forces 3]:
                                        Literally "The Puppeteer's Handbook", a text preserving the knowledge of an obscure European Craft called Der Puppenspieler (though this is only their most wildly known name, coined by a German follower of the primarily Italian tradition, who also happened to be the book's author). A loose group of puppeteers, puppet-crafters, dollmakers, and a few actors and set designers, united in their use of illusions and magical puppetry. The author of this tome penned it in the middle of the eighteenth century, during the waning years of the Craft, in an attempt to preserve a portion of the group's knowledge. Being craftsmen and lay performers (albeit Awakened ones), the Puppenspieler made no habit of writing down their methods, preferring instead the personal instruction of master and apprentice to pass their methods.

                                        Das Puppenspieler Handbuch serves partly as instruction book on crafting a life-size puppet, partly a philosophical treatise on the nature of the puppet, and partly a discussion of what the Craft called "Die Strings des Lebens" (literally: The Strings of Life). The latter is a spiritual exercise, wherein the user "weaves the strings that then command the body, rendering life upon the simulation of man". In layman's terms, a form of Thought Form, combined with finger movements or a puppeteer's controller, that connects the user to the object (living, dead, or unliving) they wish to control. Hermetic commentaries on the text are quick to draw connections to the Platonic Forms, and to the Sphere of Prime, in regards to Die Strings des Lebens.

                                        Ultimately, the full benefits of Das Puppenspieler Handbuch can only be obtained when the reader goes the full distance, and crafts their first puppet. They cannot be as Gheppeto and give life to an inanimate replica of a person, until they first create that object.

                                        The text passed quickly into the hands of collectors in the Order of Hermes, who have since put a Hermetic spin on Der Puppenspieler ways. Still, the Craft is not dead, merely reduced from the slow hardening of the Consensus (animating puppets and dolls as servants has become quite Vulgar indeed). Yet they adapt, seeking workarounds to Paradox in taxidermy (a puppet made from a living thing will more easily pass for alive) and animatronics (with technopuppeteers identifying Die Strings des Lebens in electric wires and strings of code). Others study puppetry from throughout the world, including shadow-puppets. Das Puppenspieler Handbuch, meanwhile, is an old text, and hews close to the Craft's roots.


                                        For those wondering "the hell is this guy on about?", just imagine the puppet-using ninjas from Naruto. As gauche as it is, I saw that early in my life, and it informed my vision of what a magical puppetmaster looks like for all time. Folks directing life-sized artificial minions with shining, probably invisible threads leading off from their fingers. I'm not ashamed of my influences, and this image is close to my heart (even if Naruto generally hasn't done it for me in years).

                                            30) Ashgrave's Journal [5 pt. Grimoire; Arete 2, Forces 1, Life 2, Mind 3]:
                                            A cursed English Grimoire from the Victorian era, originally penned by obscure poet Alfred Ashgrave. Written by Ashgrave during his ten year imprisonment (for a crime he did not commit), the text chronicles his confinement and the events leading to it. Interspersed throughout are heady poems, accounts of dreams, existential and metaphysical musings, and venom-filled diatribes against everyone who ever wronged the man.

                                            It is guessed that the text's evolution into a Grimoire was unintentional - there is no evidence to suggest Alfred Ashgrave was ever Awakened per se. He likely could have effected his escape from prison if he had been, rather than dying there. No, given the artistry with which the journal was written and the passion that it contains, it is likely Ashgrave's Avatar reacted to his great need, and weaved magic into the text while he poured all of his ego into the work. Or perhaps he was a Marauder, who's Quiet prevented him from properly effecting his escape (it's not as though he didn't want to; his writings make his desire for freedom plain).

                                            As mentioned previously, the Grimoire is cursed. This is because, following his death of wasting disease in his cell (him scratching dilirious words into the pages to his last breath), Alfred Ashgrave's body and body of work were given to his only living relative. Annabelle Ashgrave, long having gone mad from the tragedies surrounding her brother's imprisonment, became obsessed with preserving what remained of Alfred. So, in their decrepit family manor, Annabelle bound the loose pages of her brother's journal with leather made from his skin, the spine of the tome reinforced with his bones. It's also worth noting that, while he frequently had parchment brought to him in prison, he either lacked sufficient ink, or simply desired to properly convey his hate; many pages in the original copy were penned in Alfred's own blood.

                                            Between Alfred's unknowing investment of his Will, the binding of the book with his body, and his sister's insanity, the tome was irrevocably cursed. For several years following the book's creation, the Gothic realm of Victorian England (and a few other places as well) was haunted by a shade of Alfred Ashgrave. Not a true sapient intelligence, but a specter summoned from his rage, sorrow, and hate; a floating, ephemeral version of Alfred, skin covered in scrawls taken from his journal. The shade was tied to the book, which Annabelle guarded well, and used its unearthly might to prosecute the late Alfred's vendetta against his many enemies. These included a couple moneyed families, a vastly immoral Victorian slum lord, the English crown, and agents of governmental authority in general.

                                            The original journal is said to be long destroyed - good men and women desiring to stop the killing and put the poor soul to rest - yet the text survives. Annabelle Ashgrave - surviving more than a few attempts on her life and attempts to destroy the book - took to making meticulous copies of Ashgrave's Journal, under the belief that her brother would never die if his beautiful words survived. She even had passages of the text tattooed all over her body; when she finally perished, her body was animated by her brother's grudge, and needed to be put down and destroyed again.

                                            Ashgrave's Journal was the axis around which a long series of tragedies were spawned, a pattern of haunting and disconnected revenge that has lasted to the present day. It is an item of grave danger, but also of great insight. Despite the grudge it carries for its targets of hate, it nonetheless is a work of art, from which the ecstatic, existential, and Romantic mage can learn. Ecstasy Cultists and Hollow Ones collect copies - sequestered in hidden places, where they are unlikely to encounter agents of authority - while Nephandi deliberately drop copies in the middle of crowded cities, hoping to sow mischief and suffering.


                                            System: It is curious how a text written by a man with no visible Enlightenment could provide insight into the mysteries of Awakening. Perhaps he was a sleepwalker or Marauder, or he Awakened shortly before death. Whatever the case, his other insights are arguably more important. Living for years in a poorly insulated cell, Alfred suffered - and therefore greatly understood - the vicissitudes of balmy summer heat and bitter winter cold. This, combined with his blazing fury and icy hate, can be instructive about matters of Forces. And his chronic, wasting illness - and his commentaries thereof - can teach a great deal about Life. As can his long poems about the virtues of the natural world, and his painful desire to see living, growing things within his cramped urban cell. Of greatest value, however, may be his extensive records of his dreams, and his travels through the dreamlands.

                                            The curse, meanwhile, functions on proximity. If a person falling into one of the groups that Ashgrave despised remains within a few blocks of the book for a certain period, a shade will arise from the tome and slowly seek the person out. Given the weight of centuries, the vagaries of fate, and raw attrition, most of the specific family lines that the book would target have died off (in an official, easily traceable fashion, anyway). Save for the English royal family, most normal people are exempt from the book's wrath; that said, one never knows who one has as an ancestor, or what they did. The most likely to attract a shade's attention, then, are agents of governments or government-like groups (which, to their dismay, include the Technocratic Union). Someone as low as a police officer or municipal bureaucrat can count. For this reason, collectors tend to keep the books in libraries or Chantries in remote rural estates or properties, where cops or government workers are unlikely to tread. Some Chantries even keep the book around as a defense weapon against Technocratic incursions, though this is seen by some as foolhardy and extreme.

                                            The shade counts as a spiritual entity - an Umbrood - and should be run as such. It wields spirit Charms (of heat, cold, and cloying confinement mostly), and can be hedged out (or kept in, though the shade hates that) by Wards or Bans. The original shade looked like a pale, ghostly Alfred Ashgrave, with skin covered in hand-written passages from his journal. If other (complete and accurate) copies of the text are bound in normal materials, the shade is generally weaker, and appears only as a figure of indistinct features with writing on the skin, or even just the writing that adheres to an outline of an otherwise invisible figure. If the copy has been bound with leather made from human skin - as many Nephandi-made copies are - the figure covered in text looks like the person or persons from which the book was bound, and is as powerful as the original copy's shade. At no point should the shade be considered a Wraith, though the appearance of such a spirit can still be disturbing.

                                            If the shade itself is destroyed in spiritual combat, it will remain so until sufficient passes for it to replenish its spiritual essence (like a regular Umbrood). Afterwards, it will reappear from the book, if an acceptable target is around to attract its attention (which may include the ones who destroyed its previous form, even if they didn't previously qualify; Ashgrave's shade is a being of hate and vengeance after all). Burning or shredding the book will instantly destroy the shade, as its existence is totally tied to the book.

                                                31) Bacchus's Own(TM) Red Wine [3 pt. Charm]:
                                                Nestled somewhere amid America's midwest wine country, a vineyard controlled by the Cult of Ecstasy stands. The compound itself stands as a Chantry controlled by the Tradition, and is a common stop on the routes of many Tradition mages. Sleepers tend to rank the vineyard's product as above-average, too, though their visits rarely involve going too far into the restricted areas. One of these off-limits spots is a special section of grapevines, set aside and well hidden. Growing atop a well-tended Node, the owners cultivate a special strain of Tass grapes, which are funneled into the production of the vineyard's most exclusive product.

                                                Called "Bacchus's Own(TM)", only a limited number of bottles are produced at any given time. The Tass and labor needed for its production, as well as its rarity, make the wine the most expensive item on their list (not that the wine is listed; one needs to be "in the know" to even request it).

                                                But for those with the proper means, it can be worth the expense. Because Bacchus's Own(TM) Red Wine can serve as a powerful catalyst for romance (or lust, if one prefers). If the user serves the drink to another, it promotes amorous feelings. It doesn't force a person into love or overwhelm them with desire, but a sufficiently smooth buyer can use the wine as a powerful social lubricant, making the "wooing" easier.

                                                Bacchus's Own(TM) is so popular among the Awakened, even members of the Syndicate have been known to buy it. Since the vintners behind Bacchus's Own(TM) know that Syndicate reps can just purchase through intermediaries, the Ecstatics have given up trying to stop them and just sell their product normally. Granted, it is done quietly, and Syndicate customers are subject to a 250% markup, but the sales happen. While many within the Syndicate commission Union resources to replicating the wine scientifically, the Technocracy tends to look done on money spent just to get its agents laid. Moreover, while a simple compound added to a drink can suffice, the taste of Bacchus's Own(TM) cannot be so easily replicated. So, more often than not, members of the Convention find it's easier just to cut a sneaky deal with the superstitionists.


                                                System: Life 3 alters the target's body chemistry, inducing a heady buzz and strong arousal. Mind 2, meanwhile, fosters feelings of attraction, making the target more amenable to the idea of a hook-up. It's a vague enough impression that it can be easily skewed towards either romance (if the user wants to foster a relationship with the target) or lust (if sex is the first and/or only goal), depending on how the user frames the encounter.

                                                The Charm is used over the course of an hour or two of drinking and conversation. Many users pour out the draught over dinner or discussion, so they can better capitalize on the physical and emotional manipulation of the drink, and guide their target towards a desired outcome. The target doesn't need to drink the entire bottle, but they must finish at least one glass (though light-weights may not even require that much). A user sharing the bottle with their target is usually safe, and their awareness of the wine's effects is more than sufficient to keep them in control of their faculties (though they will still be aroused, and may well desire this).


                                                Flaw: True to the mythical allusions in the product's title, Bacchus's Own(TM) will sometimes inspire ecstatic frenzy in those who imbibe. More than a few users have transitioned their quiet dinners into wild drunken revelries, tearing off clothes and running laughing around. The creators of the wine have, at least, reassured their customers that there is little chance of a batch causing the imbiber to murder people and consume their flesh. Honest.

Of course this implement can only be used by a mystic who accepts visual designs into the paradigm. The design is basically a simple charm activated by an arête roll. After use the ink crumbles away.
                                                            This would probably fall under the category of Charm, since it's a temporary enchantment. Also, I'd like descriptions in this thread to talk about specific Effects, not necessarily broad ways of employing them. Still, very creative.

                                                            I've been thinking about Charms as of late, and wondered why it was necessary that the physical form of the object (or organism) be destroyed in order to make the magic work. Sure, ephemeral or expended objects work very well for Charms, but that doesn't mean the vessel should NEED to be destroyed to make it work. Having the magic be used up but the vessel remain lends itself to more stories, and a greater flexibility in a character's Focus. Of course a wizard who wants to make an Artifact wand would practice it by making lesser wands with a limited number of charges. And there's always the chance that the wizard's spell scroll could fall into a rival's hands, even if most of the magic is used up; a rival could still study the text of the scroll and the loose Prime threads. (If the wizard really wanted their scrolls to not fall into the wrong hands, they either need to keep track of them, destroy them manually, or weave an additional enchantment into the Charm that destroys its vessel when the Effect goes off, probably using Matter 2/3, Entropy 3, Forces 3 + Prime 2, etc).

                                                            Getting back to your point, I would argue that if a mage weaved a Charm into a living Pattern (using one level greater Prime than with a physical vessel), it should allow that organism to use the magic themselves, without necessarily killing them. It doesn't break game balance (since a mage could, according to RAW, give another person an equivalent Charm as a physical object, and do so more easily), and is very flavorful from a mystic (or technomantic) perspective. In your example, the mage "writes" spells into a person's flesh using paint, ink, etc. Then they use it, and the enchantment vanishes (with perhaps some additional Spheres to undo the writing, like Matter 2 to change the ink into some other invisible substance).
