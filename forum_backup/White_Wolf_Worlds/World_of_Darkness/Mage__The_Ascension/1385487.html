    Confession time - I just can't get into the Traditions

    05-03-2020, 08:44 PM
    I can't get into the Traditions. I'm sorry, I just can't. I can play Technocracy games [and think they're magnificently evil] but the Traditions just don't make sense to me.

    The Technocracy are my favorite types of villains. I described Bester from Babylon Five as the best example when writing a villain. "Bester [as played by Walter Koenig] is a brutal, ruthless, sadistic, and evil bastard. However, Bester can explain to you in three sentences or less what his motivation is in a way that is consistent and understandable even if it's not necessarily sympathetic." The Technocracy is a totalitarian conspiracy of magical fasists and authoritarians who suppress the Truth of the world and carry out daily atrocities. However, they are people who are justified by their belief society will fall apart if people knew and are nominally against equally bad factions. It's not so much that there's a bunch of rotten eggs and good people, it's that most of the members are combinations of both. Even the bad ones genuinely believe they're leading into a Utopia--it's just they don't care what means are needed to do it. Oh and why not wet your beak while doing it. They are 100% confidant that they are succeeding too--even when they're not.

    The problem with the Traditions is the fact that they are based on Grant Morrison's Invisibles to an extent and are meant to be the plucky resistance to the Technocracy's Illuminati. They're Neo and his ragtag band of Virtual Adepts freeing humanity from its mind-prison. The problem is that's not really a very good thing for the World of Darkness as, unlike the Anarchs, this doesn't really have much of a shades of gray type feel.Some fans overcompensate by saying, "The Traditions are actually a bunch of people fighting against toilets, lights, and vaccines! They're a bunch of Luddite terrorists!"

    This is completely irreconcilable with the way they're presented. They are a collection of Buddhists, Mad Scientists, Christians, Thuggee, and Witches who are all united by their desire to help mankind. If they can overcome that, they're not going to sweat indoor plumbing. You could play up the tensions between the Traditions but it can't reach the level of being actual enemies or even "enemies after we beat the Union" because that's not their thing. They're not nine different organizations allied against the Union, they're one organization with a broad tent.

    As such, they feel too optimistic for the setting but if you change them to a bunch of reactionary bigots, the organization no longer makes sense. Oddly, I think the game woulds make more sense if you just ditched the Traditions entirely and instead just had the players be Orphans and Hollow Ones on the run from the Union's pogrom. They've won and now they're exterminating anyone who won't toe the line.

    Author of Cthulhu Armageddon, I was a Teenage Weredeer, Straight Outta Fangton, Lucifer's Star, and the Supervillainy Saga.

                      I think you've hit on the crux of the issue with your comparison. Mage isn't Vampire, it's not built on a degenerative framework. Mage: the Ascension is about striving for improvement, surpassing your limits and overcoming your own weaknesses. Outside of Golconda stories this is generally not how Vampire functions, occasionally you'll see a point or two when things start sliding far. Mage on the other had starts low and works upward. So yes if the Traditions succeed the world will be better, that's the point of the game. It's not about fighting a battle with your own moral degeneration but about pushing past your preconceptions to envision a brighter world.

                      As for the Traditions describing their motivations, they're all looking to bring magic and wonder back into the world. It's why they can have two fundamentally technological organizations under the same umbrella. The Technocracy is an organization that removes the spontaneity from existence. The idea of a single firebrand radically changing the landscape with an idea is fundamentally anathema to their goals. They want everything to be slow, controlled, boring, and eventually static. A world of grey dull things where independent thought is crushed under the weight of social conditioning. Meanwhile the Etherites and Adepts believe that individuality is key to innovation and things can, and should, advance in leaps and bounds.

                      Overall the view you posit on the World of Darkness is one that's too dark. Instead of a world worse off then our own you want one that's unremittingly dark where all hope is crushed and the characters are practically insignificant points against a victorious monster. Mage is about that one person being able to awaken themselves to a potential to fix that.

                                  Alrighty so!

                                  I definitely agree that the Technocratic Union and the spectrum it's been able to achieve over its growth these editions is really cool and makes them fantastic both as hard core villains, sympathetic villains, anti-heroes, and even the occasional genuine hero in the mix.

                                  I think that when looking at the Council of Traditions, it's useful to look at them as being surprisingly similar to the Union but of a radically different paradigm. This is also how I can go about differentiating them from the Disparates and Orphans.

                                  Let me explain. The Council of Traditions, if this were a millennium ago, could not exist because of the radical differences between its members. Their are deep divisive lines between and within every tradition. At many times the entire construct of 'tradition' fails to truly accommodate the Willworkers within. Yet, there is a reason why the Traditions are distinct from the Disparate Council despite their similarities. The recent creation of the latter aside, the Disparate Council is ultimately defined as something of a loose confederation of crafts trying to survive and being willing to help each other to do so. This is definitely similar to the Council, but the main difference is scope and scale. The Council of Traditions hasn't just banded together for mutual survival: they want to win.

                                  The Council of Traditions is made up of nine traditions, most of whom are ancient and powerful in their own rights. Their radical departure from the Union is their refusal to limit the scope of reality. Each one has a distinct paradigm and distinct goals but they are united by their desire to undermine and replace the Technocratic paradigm that currently dominates much of the world.

                                  The Traditions are the scions of the pre-Union world. A world that saw reality Balkanized between Willworkers and vicious battles for dominance between them and the self-proclaimed rite and privelage to steward humanity and its development. Many will-workers see guiding humanity as a duty and obligation and the Technocratic Union and Council of Traditions are identical in their zeal to uphold this duty. Few on either side stopped to ask whether humanity would actually want either side's help.

                                  If humanity actually wanted a world defined by concrete and immovable laws, where society was led by an enlightened few and the ultimate goal was a utopia of progression, safety, and security. If humanity wanted a world defined by magic and mysticism where anyone had the potential to wield the cosmic forces of the universe and bend them to their will, leading nations like-unto god-kings. Perhaps there are many who would support either vision, yet there would also be many horrified by either vision.

                                  The Council of Traditions and Technocratic Union both have many sympathetic ideals and pursuits and traits, but they are also both defined by an unwavering sense of Hubris. They both feel it is their right and duty to lead humanity to the One True Utopia they invision.

                                  Prior to the rise of the Order of Reason, most traditions (as they were then) couldn't stand each other and many will-workers actively battled over reality. What has bound them tougher is little more then a vague ideal as a common purpose and the understood common threat that the Technocracy represents. They need each other if they want to win the Ascension War. Once the Union's threat is dissolved, its back to each other's throats in the battle to ensure that reality takes The Right Path.

                                  Yet, this broad similarly isn't alone what interests me about the Council, or its relatively more accessible paradigm. It's the sheer amount of tensions between and within the traditions.

                                  Each tradition has a piece of beautiful truth and revelatory secrets. They also each are marred by terrible flaws. The Hermetics defined reality but are the heights of Hubris and domineering elitism. The Choristers are exemplary at creating social cohesion while simultaneously being buffeted by schism brought about by faith near-constantly. The Society of Ether offers a miraculous world of science while also having incredibly bizarre limits placed by their own zealously defended prejudices.

                                  The traditions each have long histories yet aside from perhaps the Order of Hermes, only this era could make them. Many traditions are loose structures connecting a vast spectrum of ideals and paradigms that loosely interweave. This balkanizes them all from within while they simultaneously bristle from proximity to the other traditions. Bloodshed, prejudice, atrocity, disdain, and loathing are liberally scattered across their histories and anything could set these proud will-workers against each other.

                                  Yet... they are still bound by a common virtue. The idea that humanity should have the freedom to embrace visions of reality beyond the static ideal that the Technocratic Union provides. Yes that reality may be dangerous and it may be unpredictable... but it was within the reach of anyone with the capacity to recognize it was there. To reach out and make the world their own. To defy the definitions of what could and couldn't be. To do utterly and freely as they Will.

                                      Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post
                                      The problem with the Traditions is the fact that they are based on Grant Morrison's Invisibles to an extent and are meant to be the plucky resistance to the Technocracy's Illuminati.
                                      I mean, the idea that the Traditions are based on a comic that came out after Mage was first published would be a very impressively Mage thing to pull off...

                                      Though to add to Cynic01's point about being overly biased by framing things via VtM, trying too hard to frame the Traditions in other pop culture is also going to lead to unsatisfactory results. Mage is by no means an attempt to keep itself separate from cultural influences from sources like The Invisibles or the Matrix (both have been cited by the books over the years as good inspirational material), but that doesn't make the Traditions whatever other cultural expression they resemble.

                                      The problem is that's not really a very good thing for the World of Darkness as, unlike the Anarchs, this doesn't really have much of a shades of gray type feel.
                                      I think the problem (discounting the people that are very wrong about the Traditions and technology; as I love to point out the Chakravanti created vaccines long before modern medicine figured it out) is that the shades of grey aren't really in the Council of Nine Traditions as an organization.

                                      This is one of the ways the VtM vs. MtA comparison is very flawed. The Camarilla vs. the Sabbat vs. the Anarchs in whatever combination doesn't really need to get into the nitty-gritty of each internal faction to understand the shades of grey of the game.

                                      The Technocracy, as befits it's origin as "The Monolith" of a single organizational enemy, is easy to view as a whole no matter how detailed you can get about it's internal structure and factions.

                                      The Traditions started as an alliance of convenience to fight the Order of Reason that's lasted long enough that most Tradition members have only known that alliance and manufactured new reasons to perpetuate its existence. But you can't look at the shades of grey by looking at the whole all that well. To get into my own comparison, trying to get into the issues of the NATO alliance continuing past the Cold War is fairly hard without actually examining at least some of the individual member nations; and how that reflects on the whole when you realize what that means the organization will tolerate from its members.

                                      If you don't get into the shades of grey of each Tradition, which are present in spades when you look into them, missing the shades of grey in the Traditions as a whole is inevitable.

                                      Without getting into a breakdown of each Tradition, a simple summary is fairly direct: the Traditions are fighting for a meritocratic society where they have inherently more merit by valuing magical power as the ultimate merit to society. If the Code of Ananda following Cultists won the right to define that society, things would be pretty good for humanity really. If the Order of Hermes dictated things, quintessence would be the basis of all economic systems and those that can control/manipulate it become the new billionaire class.. but they can fireball anyone that complains about it. If the Golden Chalice had it's way... well the setting would flip to the Technocracy being the good guys no matter how fascistic.

                                      And when you consider that the people that want to create a utopia where magic users ensure a post-scarcity society and humanity can reach it's ultimately potential are working with people that believe in extrajudicial murder as a tenet of faith to enact on the whims of fate... well it's not really a situation lacking in the moral gray of compromise.

                                      You could play up the tensions between the Traditions but it can't reach the level of being actual enemies or even "enemies after we beat the Union" because that's not their thing. They're not nine different organizations allied against the Union, they're one organization with a broad tent.
                                      This is exceedingly at odds with how the Traditions have always been written.

                                      The Traditions have been having internal violent conflicts for as long as they've existed, and even after centuries of alliance, they remain distinct organizations (and who the nine are has changed a bunch of times) that would struggle to remain unified if the Union, and Nephandi, and the Marauders, were ever defeated in full.

                                      "We're all unified beyond the Ascension War!" isn't the truth, it's Tradition spin on a hope that's never been tested, and with lots of reasons to doubt.

                                      As such, they feel too optimistic for the setting but if you change them to a bunch of reactionary bigots, the organization no longer makes sense.
                                      For the talk of "gray" in the setting... this is one hell of an excluded middle.

                                          Or to put it a different way...Mage's brand of personal horror is VERY different from Vampire's.

                                          Vampire it's a lot more passive and inherent: it's about the Curse of Caine that resides in all of the Kindred, and how their imposed condition fundamentally changes the way they live their life, with all the factions surrounding it informing how each vampire chooses to deal with the Curse. Conflict between Kindred comes from how they should all deal with it, as well as who deserves the right to blood the most.

                                          Mage it involves active engagement: hubris. Mages, due to being Awakened/Enlightened to the nature of reality and how to manipulate it, believe they have a duty shape the world into a better place in their eyes. Hubris comes from them not considering what others want reality to be, or thinking they can dismiss any arguments to the contrary because they believe they know better. The whole crux of the Ascension War is because all of the factions have different ideas of how reality should be shaped, and can't agree. All while be willfully ignorant of the ordinary people who would be affected the most as collateral.

                                          While the method of execution on expressing the personal horror is similar, the actual core of the horror conflict is not.

                                                          I would point out that Mage: The Ascension’s Traditions were, in fact, written before Grant Morrisons’ The Invisibles - and the similarities between the two are merely coincidental. Over the years, I felt that the Traditions could have been done better if the were more a category of the general paradigm of players, rather than group affiliations.
