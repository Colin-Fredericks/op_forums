                              My (admittedly limited) understanding of Japanese culture is that a lot of people who are religious have no real conflict with being Buddhist and still following Shinto rituals and traditions. Shinbutsu-shugo is the syncretist fusion of the two as an actual organized religion, if I understand it correctly.

                              Ryukyuan is roughly the Okinawa equivalent to Shinto. The Ainu people of Hokkaido have their own separate animist beliefs.

                              Most of the cultures in East Asia have their own "folk religions involving ancestors and other spirits, gods and shaman. Shenism (China), Muism (Korea), Thanism (Vietnam), Sasna Phi (Thai and Laotian), Natism (Burma) and Jhakri (Nepal and northern India) are the ones I'm aware of.

                              Tengerism is the term often used to refer to the Shamanistic beliefs of Mongolia and other parts of Central Asia and Siberia.

                              Bonpo is Tibet's pre-Buddhist belief system which involves certain shamanic practices, but I know almost nothing about specifics of the beliefs.

                              Most of the "hill tribes" in Southeast Asia (such as the Hmong) still practice animist religions.

                              Indonesia has a couple of different traditions, many of the practitioners apparently "officially" identifying themselves as Hindus so as to avoid persecution by the Muslim dominated government. Java also has Kebatinan, which is apparently an amalgam of animist, Hindu, Buddhist and Sufi beliefs.

                              Outside of East Asia and the classic "big three" of the Dreamspeakers (the Americas, Africa and Australia), there's a number of shamanic traditions in Europe (many of them discussed in Dead Magic 2), including the Sami of Finland and it's neighbors, and certain Celtic and Hungarian traditions. Polynesian traditional practices are also part of the Dreamspeakers, as are Bedouin fugara (desert shaman-mystics), and some strains of Pentecostal Christianity and New Age beliefs.

                              As far as Japanese Shinto Dreamspeaker characters, probably a lot of Miko (shrine maiden) characters you've ever seen in anime or manga would work as such. (I now have this weird image in my head of an all Miko cabal made up of a Dreamspeaker, Akashic, and Choruser.)

                                  There are other issues with the conceptualization of "Dreamspeakers" from the get go. It's not necessarily or only a description of the mystical practices of the members of the group. To a great degree, a group with a mystic praxis ending up being classified in the Dreamspeakers had a lot to with politics and perception. Namely, a whole shit ton of othering by the Big Three (Valoran/Chorus,La Salle/Hermetics, and Nightshade/Verbena) back at the beginning of the Traditions. Sure, there's a lot of animism (and some animatism) that crosses a lot of lines, but, seriously, some groups that had more in common with the fundamentals of Verbena belief than with anything animistic (like... say, pretty much anything we'd associate with folks like the Aztecs and their incredibly intricate and conceptualized blood magic) were forced to go sit with the "weirdos" over there, instead of at the "cool kids" table because they came from outside the Eurocentric mind set already in place. Several groups are noted as having just walked from the original meet and greets because of this kind of treatment (The Ngoma, etc.).

                                  Take it the other way and there are several VERY animistic groups that are NOT in the Dreamspeakers due to a coherent identity that allows them to organize politically as a group of mages (Kopa Loei, Bata'a).

                                  But, really, why would the Dreamspeakers get perceived as Native American/First Peoples/Indian in nature? From the get go, one of the two founders was an African shaman, Naioba. (Who, incidentally, was in cahoots with both the Ngoma and the Madzimbabwe who later joined the Euthanatos, that it throws into a talespin the whole idea of where to put her in terms of modern Mage groups... She's a Dreamspeaker mostly because she pretty much half made them up.... And, in its essence, this is the situation described above for the fundamental fractured, jury-rigged indentity of the Dreamspeakers from the outset. When the thing your group most has in common is "We're REALLY different from each other but those guys over there group us together", it's going to be an uphill battle...)

                                      Originally posted by Ajax View Post
                                      But, really, why would the Dreamspeakers get perceived as Native American/First Peoples/Indian in nature?
                                      The real reason? Because back in 1993, a number of people working at White Wolf (developers, freelancers, etc.) were really into the whole "Native American as Noble Savage" thing that was big at the time, especially in the wake of Dances With Wolves (as well as drawing on stuff from the 60s and 70s that were retro-cool for Gen-X-ers), and some bought into Marshall Sahlin's "original affluent society" theories about primitive hunter gatherers. This was really heavily apparent in 1st ed Werewolf stuff, and carried over into 1st and 2nd ed Mage, with the Dreamspeakers being often presented as "like the Garou, but mages instead of werewolves". But the single biggest part was one of the game's first sourcebooks, The Book of Chantries, which for one of it's four Tradition chantries had the Dreamspeaker's Lodge of the Grey Squirrel, made up almost entirely of Native Americans/First Nations characters. Even with the frequent mentions of African and Australian Dreamspeakers, and the discussions of other cultures in the original Tradition book, the North Americans face of the Tradition was usually the default stereotype back then.

                                      As for why the Tradition exists in-game, Naioba and Star of Eagles already had the idea of a grand fraternity/sorority of people bound together by their calling as shaman. But some other people (I'm looking at you, Order of Hermes and Solificati) decided it would be a great place to dump any group that wasn't Eurasian. Some, like the Ngoma and the Kopa Loei, politely told them to pound sand and left. Others, such as the various Mayan factions who joined the Ecstatics, Thanatoics or Verbena, just ignored them and joined who they felt suited them best. But, sadly, the "dumping ground" stigma stuck.

And people also play what they (think they) know as well. Since most of the people (developers included) weren't anthropologists, it's not an entirely unforgivable sin. Particularly one that a good cultural anthropologist wouldn't forgive them for. Unfortunately, when they tried to correct, like those Mayan groups joining with other Trads in "defiance" of the Eurocentrism of the Big Three, the writers pretty much made a hash of what those groups were all about as well, particularly in the sense that they were integrated parts of an overall whole. (On the flip side of the Eurocentrism keeping groups together BECAUSE they are European, when it seems like they should have broken them up, you don't see Nightshade going "Well, sure, the <insert word for European priestly group of "sacrificers" pertinent to the cultural group involved> are all about death, so let's take out of my club and put them over there with all the other death-obsessed dudes..." Since Death is part of the Cycle when it makes sense to my faux-druid sensibilties, but the concept of dharma is just too weird...)
