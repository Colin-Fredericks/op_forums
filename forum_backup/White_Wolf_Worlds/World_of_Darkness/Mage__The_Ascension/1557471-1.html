    As Dataweaver said, it's not that you can't play a Nephandus, it's that playing has direct consequences in the table that you are playing. Nephandi, beyond the many justifications for their existence in-universe, are abusers and corrupters per excellence, even the aforementioned Book of The Fallen has an entire section explaining and contextualizing in the Mage setting different kinds of abuse, be it emotional, religious, sexual, etc. Playing one in your table is bound to make someone uncomfortable and break boundaries, even in the case that all of you are playing Nephandi, since the "society" of the Fallen is predicated on the notion of "eat or be eaten".

    I agree that not every Nephandi is a omnicidal maniac. Just like any Mage when discussing the topic of Ascension, Ascension (or in this case, Descension), can be personal or global. But even in your examples, for the perspective of maintaining cohesion in a party of player characters, it's gonna get ugly really quick: for the Mammonites, since it's profit beyond any morals, you can sell out your party enough times that the only way to deal with that character is open PVP; for the Goat Kids, indulgence with no limits can get really, really dark (I don't think I have to spell it out here); for the Heralds, you are describing a cult-like logic that, sure if you aid us in our dark god's ascension we will help you, but a shred of doubt about the coming of the Basilisk warrants severe punishment, I cannot see a party of characters of diverse motivations working without eveyrone converting to the Heralds. And besides, although you describe these examples as ones that do not search for a destruction of reality itself, I think that's a superficial read, since profit, indulgence and a cult-like mentality for technology have pervasive consequences in society at large that a Mage game table surely would explore.

    But beyond all that, I agree with other posts here: all these concepts can work without the Nephandi and in much better ways. A Mage which values profit above all else, but don't want to see the world end, is not a Mammonite, but a Syndicate agent that, while ruthless in driving profits with hypereconomics, still recognizes the primacy of the Technocracy's mission and will finance ventures from other conventions (an ideia presented in Technocracy Reloaded); a Mage which values indulgence, even to extreme extents, but doesn't want to corrupt other people to the point of abuse is a concept appropriate for an Ecstatic with a Left-Hand way of Magick; a Mage that searches for the construction of an AI god could be an Iterator close to being a Marauder but that still holds the ideia that this will bring a kind of Utopia. All of these concepts have a shade of evil, but none of them comes close to how the Nephandi are portrayed. Heavy Arms is right that to view the Nephandi as just evil, like any other typical villain group of the WoD, is to dillute their importance in the setting, to the point that it's better to scrap them entirely from your chronicle.

    Another aspect I wanted to add to Dataweaver's argument is that beyond the consequences to other players, I believe that playing a Nephandus is not a good thing for the player playing it, an opinion that if I'm not mistaken is shared by Brucato. To play any character is to immerse yourself in that character's motivations and worldview, and sure, some tabletop players don't hold the acting part of roleplay to such a high bar, but even then we try to at least think from our characters perspectives. In order to play a Nephandus, you - the player, need to hold a collection of motivations while roleplaying that is gonna affect you in some way, shape or form. I'm not saying that this will make anyone playing a Nephandus an automatic Evil Wizard IRL, it's just a game afterall, but I think that like any other piece of fiction, it can emotionally affect you in subtle ways.

                            My two cents: when a Nephandi's psychological drive, enforced by their mangled Avatar, is to pursue Ontological Evil, purely for its own sake, a Nephandus PC will always be less interesting than a PC who is merely a bad person.

                            Regular humans do evil for all manner of reasons. Practical, emotional, ideological, or personal. All can be fascinating to deconstruct and examine. A witch who steals the youth from children? *Chef's kiss*. Classic! They could do it for vanity, or out of fear of death, or as a desperate attempt to buy time to accomplish some goal. Do they feel guilt over it? Or do they not care? Feel entitlement? Is it a revenge or jealousy thing?

                            A Nephandus who steals the youth of children does it for precisely two reasons: 1) for the lulz, and 2) to prolong their life so they can do more evil for the lulz.

                            Having complex motivations or approaches is nice to have for a Nephandi, but hardly required. Any reasons they give are just after-the-fact rationalizations for what the Nephandus wanted to do anyway: sow pain, madness, ruin, death, and destruction. Descent is not a rational motivation, and goes against everything humans stand for.

                            Which brings me to another point: you shouldn't play a Nephandus because it's bad for you.

                            It's very unhealthy to inhabit the persona of a being opposed to every aspect of human decency, even within the context of a roleplaying game. To run them as a character, and at every juncture ask the question "what's the absolute worst thing I could do in this situation?". And where the only reason your character restrains their impulses is because it would be unwise or risky. A fact the character resents for thwarting their "Dark Kantian" categorical imperative to do Evil, regardless of utility.

                            To constantly and willingly plot against even your fellow PCs (including Nephandi ones). To deceive, manipulate, abuse, and betray them, in the immediate term and over the long term. Not because your PC pursues an understandable goal that necessitates the deception, but because your PC seeks to maximize harm and cruelty. To be the ultimate asshole player, the ultimate "That Guy" at the table. Disrupting everyone else's fun because "it's what my character would do".

                            That's not fun. Not for the rest of the group, and probably not much for you either. It's exhausting, and probably not a good headspace to occupy for long campaigns.

                            At least when the Storyteller roleplays the Nephandi, it's largely in the abstract until the PCs finally meet them face to face. At sporadic intervals, and with the express intent that the PCs will ideally kill this mother-fucker and end their evil. With a Nephandi PC, you have to effectively BE that mother-fucker, and try to make them work.

                            Is it any wonder, then, that White Wolf's creatives (especially Brucato) didn't want to provide the tools to do that? They didn't want to give players a rope to hang themselves with.

                                                            OK, rather than doing my own big response to all that... I gotta ask... where do you think you're going with all that? If you're trying to prove that the Nephandi have a point, it feels a lot more like you're missing what the books are saying about them than communicating that conclusion. The idea that you're conflating a potential cyclical nature to the WoD (something hinted at by the number of factions that believe in a wheel of ages cosmology, but not something ever demonstrated conclusively) with the idea that the Nephandi just want to end this instantiation of the universe doesn't really hold. The Nephandi aren't trying to reset the world and replace it with something better. They're trying to end it - including an potential new versions of it - and will settle for making it as worse as possible in the meantime; including making worse new instantiations if necessary.

                                                            It also feels like you're grasping a lot. Not just from Mage, but from other game lines. You're pulling in things that seem far outside of their context or relevance to this discussion. For example while the WtA cosmology clearly puts the Triat in a cyclical nature, it never posits that it exists as part of a cosmic cycle of universal instantiations. They operate on a much more direct cyclical nature of creation, existence, and destruction that befits the WtA animist assumptions. The Wyld, Weaver, and Wyrm operate solely on the universe they currently exist in with no known care for preexisting or potential future ones. That's no what they "do" as much as one can discuss the motives of such entities.

"The end" can be used in an imperfect sense where it signifies a transition rather than cessation ("the end of an era" does not mean that time itself has ended), or a perfect sense where in means the total lack of what previously was ("death is the end of life" means at least for an individual that there was a living organism and now there is dead matter as what made it alive is now ended).
