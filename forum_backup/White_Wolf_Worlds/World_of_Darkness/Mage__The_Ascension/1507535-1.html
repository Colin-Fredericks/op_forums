    Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
    This doesn't actually follow based on your phrasing of the options. If the effect is coincidental within the Reality Zone, the presence of someone that would count as a witness in other Reality Zones doesn't make it Vulgar, so their witness status is irrelevant.
    The closest that the game comes to addressing the issue of witnesses to Coincidental effects is the optional Domino Effect rule, which could kind of be interpreted as a sort of “Coincidental with Witnesses” situation; though I personally view it more as a borderline-Vulgar effect: it's still technically Coincidental, but “affect[s] the scene in noticable ways: exploding gas mains, unlikely ricochets, unexpected appearances of the police… that sort of thing.” Which sounds about awful lot like “Vulgar” to me; so much so that my own house rule replacement for Domino Effect is to say that starting with the third time that a “wild coincidence” takes place in a scene, it becomes Vulgar.
    ​​​​​
    It's not quite “Coincidental with Witnesses”, because it doesn't actually require there to be any Witnesses; only that the Coincidence be unlikely enough that a Hypothetical Observer would balk at it happening repeatedly. But with the Domino Effect in play, there is a distinction made between two types of Coincidental Effects: those that are subject to the Domino Effect, and those that aren't.

    …which is a bit of a tangent, because it doesn't actually contradict your point that the presence of absence of Witnesses doesn't have an effect on Coincidental Effects.

A more specific term would be better. That or just saying "A witness is a sleeper whose beliefs conform with the local consensus of what is and isn't possible" which is something Mage already gives us.

                                        "Sleepwalker" isn't really caring that much weight when you look at what it does rather than try to mix in and out of game thoughts on it. The whole point of all the terms we're discussing is "who counts as a witness, and when of that's in question," so that's not really a functional word to use as it would just create a circular loop.

                                        But really this isn't complicated. There are three groups divided by two binaries. Are they Awakened, and is their default status as a witness to vulgar magic.

                                        Sleepers (N, Y).
                                        Nightfolk (N, N)
                                        Mages (Y, N)

                                        Then we have two modifier groups that both result in the same key point: they are exceptions to the default statuses according to their base grouping. The big difference is that Assistants are actively aware of, and involved in Awakened society on some level, letting them aid magic despite not being Awakened, while Sleepwalkers are generally unaware of their situational status as it comes from deeply internalized beliefs that can easily go unexamined.

                                        You keep saying that we're arguing for the same thing, but it feels like we're not because of things like "Sleeperwalker carries too much weight," even though your example is the exact sort of thing it was thought up to encompass. You keep trying to add more groups (even if they're just in-character names for the same thing), and it feels like you're just trying to muddy the waters around things that just aren't that hard. Hedge wizards, for example, are Nightfolk (unawakened, not witnesses), but have a much higher rate of being Assistants than other Nightfolk, and much higher rates of being Sleepwalkers if trained exclusively by Mages and other hedge wizards associated with those same mages (esp. the Technocracy).

                                        So we might be advocating for similar things, but I strongly feel like I'm arguing that this is way simpler and easier to grok that it can seem once you take the in-character stuff out of the equation, and you seem to be advocating that we have to account for all the in-character labels that are acknowledged by the books as not being 100% accurate to how the world of Mage actually is.

                                            I'm used to “Night Folk” being a term that means “the other playable supernatural types”: vampires, werewolves, wraiths, and changelings; and if using the End Nights plots, Imbued, mummies, demons, and projectors. While I personally would put sorcerers under that label as well, that's just me; mortals who have access to supernatural powers typically aren't classed as Night Folk, even if the powers they access are the ones that are otherwise exclusive to Night Folk (e.g., ghouls, kinfolk, mediums, and kinain).

                                            The technical definition of Sleepwalker in M20, which I quoted earlier in this thread, essentially covers the (N, N) category you mentioned at the start of your last post: they're people who aren't Awakened, but who don't count as Witnesses. The definition even strongly implies that they probably have access to supernatural powers of their own (specifically, anything that isn't Sphere Magick); but that's technically phrased as something that's optional.

                                            So you have Sleepers (N/Y), Sleepwalkers (N/N), and Awakened (Y/N).

                                            To the extent that there's mud in the waters, it's that there are Sleepers who don't always count as Witnesses, such as members of a Cult. These Sleepers are the ones you're calling Assistants; and they're basically individuals who are straddling the line between Sleeper and Sleepwalker. I tend to think of them as “Sleepers who have developed a Focus”: like the Awakened, they have a solid philosophy about how the world actually works, which manifests as a Paradigm, Practice, and Instruments — though in there case, the latter two only matter in terms of what approaches of Magick they're suited to support: they can't use Magick themselves. If they observe Magick that's compatible with their Focus, they don't count as Witnesses; if they encounter Vulgar Magick that isn't compatible with their Focus, they do. ​​​​​​​​​

                                                    Fair enough; and in particular, it uses it as an umbrella term for Extraordinary Citizens and Acolytes: Extraordinary Citizens are Technocratic Assistants, while Acolytes are the Traditions' Assistants. They, and Cultists (who are described as being like assistants, but with even more dedication), are the ones I was talking about in the last paragraph of my previous post: the Sleepers who nonetheless don't count as Witnesses to your Magick, even if they count as Witnesses to others' Magick.

                                                    And for what it's worth: where I disagree with M20 is that it portrays the matter of Assistants in terms of science vs. magic: Extraordinary Citizens don't count as Witnesses for Hypertech, but do count as Witnesses for all kinds of non-hypertech (so far, so good); but Acolytes don't count as Witnesses for any Magick: the book emphasizes that you'd need to come from a remote and isolated corner of the globe not to buy into hypertech, and implicitly assumed that Assistants either accept or reject every kind of mysticism the same way. The implication is that there's no such thing as an acolyte that buys into one style of mysticism but rejects others.

                                                    On the other hand, that may be a Traditions-specific thing, going along with that group's focus on ecumenical mysticism. Disparate Assistants (whatever they're called) might be a bit more like Extraordinary Citizens in that they don't count as Witnesses to their own Craft's Magick, but do count as Witnesses to Magick that clashes with that.
