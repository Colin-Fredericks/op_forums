      Short answer: Yes.
      Long answer: The Umbra is the 'spiritual' side to the WOD. There are three main 'layers,' with each one divided into sublayers composed of various locations that use different titles depending upon the level. There are multiple other portions of the Umbra that may or may not be connected these main layers, and their exact nature is hard to connect. This is because that the various Otherworlds are supposed to be at least slightly contradictory. When the idea first popped up in Werewolf, the rules were still being thought out for the future game lines. As those newer game lines came out, some of the older details got overruled. Some things got left behind, while others didn't get get joined together. I have been trying to come up with a system that contains all of the rules so that everything works together with both history and metaplot. It is taking awhile, but it is making sense. If you want to keep things simple, pick a description from one game line and stick to it for all layers of the Umbra. Change things when necessary. Since you're posting in Mage, I suggest Beyond the Barriers: the Book of Worlds. It was my first foray into the Umbra, and I still feel it is the best book in all the game lines for exploring the Otherworlds. One of its main suggestions is to just make some things up as you go along. For a more detailed approach, I would need examples of what you are planning to do.

      THWarted

              "...everything I've read up on is extremely contradictory."

              No, you got it perfectly. The contradictions are a feature, not a bug. It's the vast expanse of possibility positioned outside material reality. It is, in a sense, everything that isn't "real" in the sense of being part of humanity's normal lived experiences. Including mutually exclusive visions of the world, the afterlife, heavens and hells, and everything in between.

              It's also the case that when Mages travel there, what they experience is inevitably colored by their expectations and beliefs. When a Verbana witch Steps Sideways, she climbs the twisting branches of Yggdrasil into the realms of elves, giants, and gods. When a Void Engineer Steps Sideways, she drifts through empty space in a ship and fights aliens. No one is totally on the same page, because they see what they want to see.

                  The best way I have to describe the umbra is this: it's a dimension composed of a substance that, in its natural state, is pure possibility, undefined and uncertain but full of potential. It only becomes something specific when a conscious mind, or several, interacts with it. Since this dimension is not bound by our laws of physics, space or causality, many different "realities" or "visions" can coexist at the same time. So yes, it's a contradictory and chaotic place, just like our minds, which is exactly what it reflects. The umbra can be whatever you want it to be, but always influenced by the expectations and beliefs of the mages who travel in it.

                      Im trying to create my own houseruled "Umbra", based on the human mind. So it would basically have 3 layers: the Dark Umbra, which is where the Wraiths reside, is the Memory. Everything that once was material but is no more finds its way there; however, like our own minds, memories of the past can be distorted, which is why this place is not just a static representation of the past. And Oblivion is the unavoidable ultimate destination - time erodes all memories, after all. The second layer is the "Oneiric lands", the place of the Fae. This represents creativity, imagination. It's basically a "make-believe" realm. And then there's what would be called more commonly as "Umbra", which is the realm of cognition, of logic and abstraction, which would be the "Umbras" depicted for Werebeasts and Mages. I also try to introduce several other concepts of psychology - like Jungian "archetypes". For example, the "Abyss" inside the "Cognition Umbra" is a representation of the Jungian archetype of "The Shadow" (that inner voice of us that keeps us in line by constantly criticizing ourselves). Im now trying to think what would be good "realms" inside those layers to represent ideas like the Id, Ego and Superego, and other archetypes like Anima and Animus, but this is the basic structure of what it will be in my games.

                      And the "Deep Umbra" will simply be madness, it's something that goes beyond the rational mind, and thus is filled with illogical paradoxes and square circles. That's why in my Deep Umbra is where the Paradox Realms exist, and why the Marauders are ejected to it. The Deep Umbra is the representation of a broken mind that is not function, thus it is "dimension bizarro" where every impossibility occurs. And Im still thinking if I make it completely off limits (unless you wanna go utterly and irrevocably mad) or not (I mean, even trying to define it would be impossible - but since the Deep Umbra will be the realm of the illogical, maybe it can be done).

                          Im specially thinking on Anima/Animus archetypes to represent in the Umbra, and the Good Mother/Father and Bad Mother/Father archetypes; the most know example archetype of Good Mother to us being the Virgin Mary. It represents fertility, femininity, the "Garden of Eden", maternal confort. The Bad Mother is the Witch, the Maiden, the dragon or serpent. Perhaps the best mythological representation of the Bad Mother is the hindu Goddess Kali, the goddess of death that destroys everything in its path, and has blood dripping from her mouth.

                          The idea of the good father is the idea of the "wise king", the one that guides us, the teacher, the tutor. In the arts, it is illustrated by figures like Gandalf.

                          The Bad Father however is perhaps, the most famous of all. It is Chronos, the father of Zeus, famously depicted devouring his own kids (Hades and Poseidon in this case). It is the twisted figure of the tyrant. Captain Hook, the tyrant pirate against which Peter Pan decides to rebel by simply refusing to "grow up" - yeah, and you though that "Peter Pan" was just a silly children's play... No no, Peter Pan is a deep psychological allegory of the human condition. Captain Hook is the Tyrant Father, who is afraid of DEATH - represented by the Dragon of Chaos, another infamous and super interesting archetype. In the case of Captain Hook, the Dragon of Chaos is represented by the Crocodile - the terrifying beast that ate Hook's clock, and thus always have that terrible tic-tac of the merciless time, counting the seconds for Hook's inevitable end, and it has already claimed a piece of him, in the form of his missing hand. The crocodile is the allegory of Death itself, the personified archetype of the Dragon of Chaos, which has the clock in his stomach, which personifies Time. And because Peter Pan rebels against the tyrant father, he refuses to grow up, remaining under the archetype of the Child - which is bad for him, since for him to experience love (with Wendy), he has to grow and leave "neverland". That's also the motif on Lion King; it's a dispute between the Good and Bad Father (represented by Simba's father and uncle), and how you cant just spend your entire life as a child singing "hakuna matata".

                          So, I want to incorporate all those archetypes in my Umbra. "Gaia" of the werewolves is roughly good for both the Good Mother and the Bad Mother. Also the Virgin Mary, Afrodite, Athena, or Hera and Kali for the bad mother. But I need to devise something for the "Wise King" and "The Tyrant" archetypes too. It can be a powerful spirit or realm in the Umbra - for example, the "Garden of Eden" or the greek "Elyzium Fields" are good examples of "realms" representing the Good Mother, while the Underworld or Hell are associated with the Bad Mother, so that one is easy. But Im still thinking on ideas for the Good/Bad Father, best viewed in Gandalf vs Chronos, and how to represent that in Umbral reality.

                          And of course, the "Dragon of Chaos", Death, represented by Apophis, Jormungandir and Fenrir, or Hook's crocodile.

                          There's also the Trickster, another great archetype, which we know the most famous ones: Odin, Loki and Hermes.

                          And the Child archetype, the archetype of innocence and fragility.
