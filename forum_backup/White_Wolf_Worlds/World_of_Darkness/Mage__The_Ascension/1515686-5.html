    Let me point out that Techies have to acknowledge that Magick is real (unless they're in Denial). They can say that it's something distinct from Science, and that its intent chaotic nature makes it dangerous and something to be contained or eliminated. But they can't deny that it exists.

    And that has consequences. Ghosts are real, which means that a worldview that says that intelligence is “merely” an emergent property of sufficiently complex and structured matter is wrong. Reincarnation may be part of that Magick that Enlightened Scientists detest; but it's still definitely a thing, unless you're going to retreat into Denial. You cannot, therefore, seriously espouse a purely materialistic view of reality, message reality is demonstrably not materialistic. The closest you can come to that is to separate reality into Science and Magick and say that Science is materialistic but Magick isn't. And that has its own problems.

                    Originally posted by Magus Maximus View Post
                    Granted, I'll give you that. "Reincarnated" confirmed cases could indeed be people that weird cases of "memory transfer" naturally occurring through the Ether, or other such.

                    That however wouldnt be the way a scientist in the real world would think; applying Occam's Razor to eliminate the unnecessary complexities... Or at least, the scientist would consider both hypotheses to be equally unlikely, thus neither could be discarded.
                    Occam's razor doesn't discard hypotheses anyways. It often gets quoted as meaning that the simplest explanation is usually correct, but what it actually means is that the simplest explanation to disprove is the most efficient starting point. It's not a tool for finding truth, it is a tool for prioritizing effort. The conflation comes because when disproving a theory you generally start by assuming it is true and looking for contradictions. So in one sense the razor does say consider the simplest explanation is true, but it doesn't stop there.

                    For instance, in the game "Looney tunes", damage dont really hurts. If you KO an enemy, they spend an amount of rounds seeing starts around their heads, until they recover. That's the game mechanics about setting that is about doing funny stunts and nobody ever gets hurt, even when they are run out by a train.

                    Imagine applying those same game mechanics to WoD and saying "it's just game mechanics". The world wouldnt make sense.
                    Game mechanics are abstractions meant to let us at the gaming table quickly adjudicate results. They are not the physical or metaphysical rules of the world, they are quick hacks to get a very rough approximation of something much more messy and complicated. Because they are meant to approach specific elements they sometimes look a bit like the rules of the world (that's what's happening in your Looney Tunes example), but that's happenstance not physical or metaphysical truth.

                    You bring up gravity and that's a perfect example because falling damage rules in games are usually quite rough. Most games either implicitly or explicitly have the same gravity as our world and yet falling damage rules almost never match. You can't extrapolate the force of gravity from them and you'd get quite entertaining results if you tried. The WoD has lethal damage kick in at 100 feet, which Mage directly calls out as not matching our reality.

                    In your Looney Tunes example, the rules say the characters see stars for a certain amount of time, but in universe there isn't a rule that specifies how long. That length of time is an abstraction meant to translate the world we are trying to depict into game terms. The rules approach the world, not the other way around. Dr. Sylvester can't study head stars to put together a grand unified theory of concussions unless it is funny. A grand unified theory of concussions doesn't even exist in universe unless it is funny. Funny is the metaphysics of the world, not dice.

                    There was an old D&D third edition idea for continental travel by lining up a thousand miles of kobolds and using greater cleave on them. As long as you didn't miss you could travel a thousand miles in a single turn. It matched the rules of the game but it didn't match the rules of the world.

                    It's the same with Spheres. They are, first and foremost, a way of translating every type of magic into a single system that can be used at the gaming table. That's going to have bugs and unintended consequences. Sometimes that will result in capabilities that don't make sense in world, like the necromythrilmancer. That doesn't constrain us to change how we think of the in game world because the system is not the world.

                                Originally posted by Magus Maximus View Post
                                That would be a bit weird for some levels thou, upon which there would be no increment, but you'd still have to "learn".
                                The “necromancy only” mage is a fairly extreme example; but the idea that Lord Revan is describing is actually how the developers prefer to handle the issue: if the Sphere system says that you have enough dots to say that you can do something, but it's not something that your Focus permits you to do, then you can't do it.

                                Mostly. There's always the fallback to “Working Without Focus” which adds a Willpower expenditure and greatly increases the difficulty of the Working (+3); and in this way, a non-technomancer can brute-force an Effect that he doesn't believe in through sheer force of will. (Non-technomancers can also discard Instruments ahead of schedule, which isn't nearly as rough as Working Without Focus — +1 difficulty and no Willpower cost — but the principle is the same; so I tend to lump these together.)

                                But aside from Working Without Focus, if your Focus can't justify you doing something, you can't do it. Even if you have the Spheres you need to do it.

                                Originally posted by Magus Maximus View Post
                                I do work with an alternate system of "Realms" from Gurps, so my "Spheres" are custom tailored for each group. That was my work around, total headcanon
                                If you're not already familiar with the Book of Fragments, I would strongly recommend picking it up. It's a STV product, so it lacks official backing; but if you're willing to accept head-canon workarounds, it's a pretty good one.

                                Another one I like is the idea to partially decouple Rotes from Spheres: that is, make it so that someone who doesn't have the Spheres necessary to perform an Effect can still do so if they have access to an appropriate Rote. Spheres then become a measure of your ability to improvise Magick, representing an understanding of its principles to a level beyond mere book learning to the point where you just intuitively get it and making you a virtuoso with it.

                                Independently learnable Rotes, meanwhile, would let you pick up one or two (or three or four, etc) neat tricks, rounding out your options and letting a mage do things that his Focus says he ought to be able to do, but which his Sphere dots don't permit. The downside being that with things the mage knows only by Rote, he lacks the ability to improvise. He cannot Work Without Focus or grow beyond Instruments when performing by Rote; and his ability to vary the formula to do something similar but not the same is limited.

                                And, of course, once you have the Spheres needed to perform the Effect, the Rote ceases to be all that important.

                                        M20 pp.566–567:
                                        Working Without Focus

                                        Because mages are vessels of Will, they can sometimes will things to happen without using tools or a practice. Working through determination alone, a mage Cancer pass or focus and conjure effects without channeling belief through practice or tools.

                                        Working this way doesn't happen without cost. Game wise, the player spends one willpower point and suffers a plus three difficulty to his roll. Essentially, that Mage throws every ounce of self-conviction he has into the spell, using will alone to focus his intents.

                                        As well, the Magical Modifiers lists “working without instruments” as a +3 difficulty, but make no mention of requiring a Willpower expenditure.
