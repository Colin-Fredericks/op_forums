    I think there is a strong argument to say that Ars Magica was the game that first coined the notion of being a “storytelling” game, along with the whole indie/narrative game schtick back in the day. It was written by Mark Rein-Hagen and Jonathon Tweet and was independently published under the ‘Lion Rampart’ moniker. It also innovated things like troupe style play and had a magic system with a structured set of rules, but with freeform applications. It had a different core system to the WoD games, using singular D10s rather than dice pools, but some of the terminology and modes of play filtered through.

    The Houses of Hermes was a splat-book that established 12 Houses that your wizards could join up with, in a manner similar to the way WoD games structure themselves with Clans/Tribes/Traditions, etc. The game centred around a fictional wizardly order - The Order of Hermes - which was then used in Mage: The Ascension some time later and included House Tremere which later became Clan Tremere in Vampire: The Masquerade. The 1st edition (c. 1987) was actually setting less, albeit with an implied medieval set up, but the ‘Mythic Europe’ setting became developed in the 2nd edition (1989) onwards, as was the Order of Hermes and the various Houses and lore. Mark Rein-Hagen’s intent was to further develop the setting by producing multiple games in it - as was done later in his WoD games - while Jonathon Tweet had also mooted designing a modern magic type of game in the near future. Neither quite came to fruition as Rein-Hagen became involved with White Wolf games, and started to work on Vampire: The Masquerade as his next project, while Jonathon Tweet took a brief hiatus from game design, although he eventually went on to design Over The Edge for Atlas Games, which could be seen as his own ‘modern magic’ game.

    When Rein-Hagen joined White Wolf, alongside the Weick brothers and others who were big fans, he essentially took Ars Magica with him and a third edition was released about 1992, just after Vampire: The Masquerade was proving a big hit and in its 2nd edition release. One of the notable shifts in the AM 3rd edition, apart from the appreciable darkening of tone, was the introduction of the 'Realm of Reason’ and the fledgling 'Order of Reason’. Some fans of Ars Magica hated that inclusion as they felt it undermined the point of the game being set in a medieval paradigm, but it was being set up as a sort of historical setting for the WoD as a whole, including the advertised upcoming Mage game which had yet to be released. It is worth noting that Mage: The Ascension, written by the late Stewart Weick, was the only game in the original WoD series that was not directly created and developed by Mark Rein-Hagen.

    There were a couple of regional splat books and some other supplements released by White Wolf for Ars Magica, but they had dropped it as a title by the mid-1990s and this was then picked up briefly by Wizards of the Coast (with Jonathon Tweet employed as their lead RPG designer) who also decided to drop it not long after. Eventually, Atlas Games who had already published Jonathon Tweet’s Over The Edge, and would go on to pick up and develop other similar games to Mage like Feng Shui and Unknown Armies, picked up and released a 4th edition of Ars Magica with certain connections to the WoD, like the Order of Reason, removed from it. A 5th edition was published about 2003 and between both these editions they chugged out a load of finely researched and detailed supplements that make up the bulk of their line today.

    Mage: The Ascension also ended up with a load of supplements, that developed their own setting in its own direction, and by the time of the Revised edition’s final run, it had two historical settings of its own: Mage: The Sorcerer’s Crusade and latterly the Dark Ages: Mage supplement. These largely completed the severance of the connection between the Ars Magica and Mage lines.

            I never played Ars Magica, but I've read some of it for learning purposes and I though there were always some elements* that were nifty. I like the magic system for example, just as I like the Arcana of Mage the Awakening far more than Spheres (or DA:M's foundations and pillars lol.) My view of mage has always seen there being 'multiple potential paths to the same endpoint' with sphere magic perhaps being the most 'pure' or closest to original intent (and thus being more universal and easy to adapt to other factions unlike MtA's Arcana or Foundation/Pillars.)

            *I've sometimes toyed with the idea of the Ars Magica magic system being a good way to introduce some style of 'name'-based magic, sort of like the MtR 'Nomenclature' - or as a companion to DtF's lores and evocations (Lores were there 'built in' mechanisms for manipulating reality - to borrow a Matrix-esque analogy they were purpose built programs anyone could use to manipulate the system. The Ars Magica system would be more akin to directly hacking or programming... something that is more flexible and allows for more stuff to be done compared to the more limited Lores.. but also taking longer to do and with greater risks (more changes for errors to occur in the program, which have the potential to be catastrophic.)

                I have been playing Ars Magica for decades and I have even written for the game. I was excited to say a whole bunch of stuff, but others beat me too it, and they are mostly accurate.

                Ars Magica was created by Jonathan Tweet, the same guy who engineered D&D 3rd edition, and it is indeed the very first of its kind as far as a troupe style story focused game with flexible improvised magic along with magic formula. Mark Rein*Hagen joined in with the second edition of that game, published by Lion Rampart, and it begat a strong cult following. Jon Nephew was a major player at Lion Rampart, and wrote some for that edition (as well as a few becmi D&D books). Tweet and Rein*Hagen started sowing the seed for the 0WoD. References to Tremere magi (and some Boniagus) experimenting with the undead can be found in 2nd ed books such as Order of Hermes, Covenants, and others.

                White Wolf Magazine merges with Lion Rampart, and proceeds to cannibalize Ars Magica to create Vampire. I mean, I do admire aspects of 3rd edition Ars, but WW did a lot of mucking up with secret infernal plots everywhere. But a lot of 3rd edition produced the core of that games future, as well as the core for Mage. We have the introduction of Mistridge and Grimgroth.

                Ars Magica already had a cult of fans. They *hated* third edition. There were massive flame wars on the primitive internet of the day. I was there. Second became known as the One True Edition, and to this day, the Ars Magica community on the Atlas Forums and Facebook hate 3rd edition and Tremere vampires with a passion. They hate the entire world of darkness, and they are split on Mage. Most hate it. And most of them do not know White Wolf is dead.

                Jon Nephew, who used to write for the game, formed Atlas Games and bought the game from White Wolf and ran hard with it. The fans saw fourth edition as reclaiming thier game. The Tremere were expunged, the infernal references toned down, and a lot of work went into making Ars and Mythic Europe its own special thing. That version retained some terminology fron 2nd and 3rd that is found in oWoD games, such as the Talent/Skill/Knowledge categories and the names of Virtues and Flaws. It is not a dice pool game. Straight d10 for everything. The core mechanics of Ars Magica formed the basis of the re engineering of d&d3.

                Then we get to fifth edition Ars Magica, under David Chart as line editor. The edition I wrote for, the version that sold twice as many books as all versions before it and lasted longer than all of them together. They ended production a few years ago and the fan base is still powerful.In 5th edition, there never were any Tremere Vampires. Flambeau got retconned (one of the reasons I got into writing for the game is because I liked Flambeau the way it was and wanted to guide it back). The T/S/K division was taken out, and Virtues/Flaws became a matter of Major/Minor instead of a direct point value. Here, Ars Magica truly grew into its own. The setting, the mechanics, the philosophy, by far the best version yet.

                It is a very, very, very crunchy game. It makes you feel like a wizard with the formulas. And it is rewarding. It is a game for people who feel they did not get enough homework in school. It is much more troupe focused and story driven than any other game, yet it is crunchier and more precise than Gygax D&D. It is not anything like mage. It does not feel the same, play the same, or have the same tone. If you try to adapt pieces of it to mage, you will wind up giving up mage and playing Ars Magica instead.It does everything you wish mage would do.

                Obviously I have a bias

                Ars Magica 5th Edition: Light of Andorra
                https://forum.atlas-games.com/c/play-by-post-campaigns/ars-magica-5th-edition-light-of-andorra/32
                Adventure! Glory! Legend and Mystery! This is the bread and butter of the magi of Andorra. But power and prosperity are but mere distractions, and the covenant has some difficult choices and challenges ahead.

                                What happened with House Flambeau?
                                Ars Magica can kinda be seen as the missing link between AD&D and the oWoD. The original Ars Magica had not much of a setting. It was a home brew magic system ment to be tacked on to AD&D. This was back in 1988 or 89. I think. Second, the OTE, is where Mythic Europe and the Order of Hermes are developed. Third created this huge political landscape, because that is what WW games were all about back then. 4th was the retcon and refined the crunchy gurps like quality the game used to have. Fift refined everything and is not gurps like at all any more.

                                If you want to hunt around on e-bay, you can find lots of third edition material at a reasonable price. I *love* the art that WW used to put out back then! That is the stuff that is the most related to Mage and the WoD. The mechanics do not translate, but some concepts may. Third edition introduced Twilight, somewhat similar to Paradox in mage. That stuck around in 4th, and in 5th it was revised into a mechanic called Warping. Third is the one with all the story seed connections to Mage also. That is where Mistridge comes from. I have an extra if you want it
                                Davud Chart, the helmsman for 5th edition, he never wrote for Mage but he is a fan. He was promoting the Tecnocracy kickstarter and mentioned that, though he never wrote for mage, he was proud that his story about the White Tower (in 3rd) still persists as the backstory of that game.

                                As for House Flambeau it is a long bitter story.
                                See, though I started with that game in 4th edition, I also started with a bunch of 3rd ed sourcebooks from the WW era. My favorite is still Tribunals of Hermes-Iberia, by Peter Hentges. That is where the original story of Flambeau comes from. It sparked within me a love of Spanish history and language. 4th ed didn't touch anything there. The main retcon focus there was to reform and reinstate House Tremere and sweep the whole vampire thing under the rug. But they were still fascists. 5th edition did a deep retcon, and House Tremere is actually very interesting and fun to play. And history was respected. Changes were made, but it was recognizable.

                                House Flambeau was not treated as gently.
                                I fought with the guy about it. I blew up the Berklist with flame wars. But I made peace with the guy and wrote a fanzine re-retcon with his help. So, I have no animosity remaining. I am an active member of the fan community, and I have worked hard to insure the original is not forgotten. I demonstrated that ideas can merge. It is okay to retcon, but you should at least honor the original somehow in the new work.

                                3rd edition Flambeau (the founder) was Danny Trejo if he played a part in Magnificent Seven. 5th edition Flambeau (the founder) is Kevin Costner in any bad Kostner movie. His Hispanic identity was removed, and he was white washed into a Frankish dandy..

                                Old edition books are PDF only and can be found on Drive through RPG. Hardcopy can be found as resale used books. The current 5th edition is still in print and available from the Atlas Gamessite, as pdf or hardcopy.The three 5th ed books I worked on are Grogs, Tales of Power, and Faith & Flame.
