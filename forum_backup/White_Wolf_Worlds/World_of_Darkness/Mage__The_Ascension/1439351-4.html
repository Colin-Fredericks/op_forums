    It depends, there are ways to avoid that.

    *Arguably batteries of Quint containing Earthly Ressonance could be used for a time.


    We don't know what's "beyond the Deep Umbra", but the idea was to find another material world/s - no reason to think they will become Ephemera, if they can resist 'til they get there. Then again, they could end becoming very alien even w/o becoming spirits. They went very much outside any rulebook, at that point

    *High levels of DimSci (5?) allows one to protect against Void Adaptation.

    One of the theories about the fate of the VE it's that they never suffered from Void Adaptation OR that they actually Void Adapted on their own terms. The book says something across the lines off: "If someone knew about Void Adaptation, it would be the VE Masters"...

    Also, weren't there a whole heap of Tradition Mages and Archmagi who became spirits, too? Wouldn't there be a lot of Etherites and Dreamspeakers in particular?

    Just because they become spirits, why would that mean they lose interest in the Ascension War, or lose interest in protecting Earth from Nephandi or whatever? I get the idea of ex-Void Engineers who just wander off and forget the Solar System, but the rest would stick around.
    Yeah, there are. Altrough many also just died on the Storm (and the Horizon War)

    There has been speculation on the fandom about a Traditional version of Threat Null

    However, The Word of God it's that the spirits left by Taditional mages are more like individuals trapped on their own projects and rutine. They're like Wraith Drones, they have no agency, instead just going trough the motions.

    They're not seen as a power that could help, as much as a sad fate to be avoided.

    But, let's keep this in mind:

    The book that talks about Traditional mages turning to spirit, Infinite Tappestry, it's arround 20+ years older than the book that talks about Threath Null. I think that, more than likely, the goal-posts changed a LOT between these books.
    See:

    Infinite Tappestry was the book about the Umbra in an edition that had been very much against going to the Umbra since it started.

    Before that book, the big no-no about playing in the Umbra was the Avatar Storm inflicting damage and potentially Gilguling your character (that caused, perhaps, the biggest war in Mage fandom ever. The backlash was so huge it get's mentioned in some sourcebooks - from the pov of the authors, of course)

    Now, in the midst of this conflict - with ppl saying entering the Umbra was too punishing - comes Infinite Tapestry: A book about getting into the Umbra.

    This book offers THE largest compendium of ways to avoid the Storm. And not only trough rare Merits or rare locations: From mid to hard difficulty Rotes that avoid it entirely, to one sensory effect (I think it was Spirit 1/Entropy 1 - but I don't remember) that allows to find weak spots in the Storm in order to take less damage, you have many ways to protect against it. Hence the Storm it's no longer such a big problem, you don't need to be "super special" anymore to get trough more or less safely

    But that book it's still very much a product of Revised Philosophy: It just changes the goalposts - it gives a lot of options to bypass the Storm, but it focuses instead on something that was already in Book of Worlds but never was a big deal before: Disembodiment - what VE call Void Adaptation

    That wasn't such a big deal back then because being in a Realm avoided the effect. I'm not 100% sure, but perhaps Infinite Tappestry it's the book where Earthly Ressonance get's introduced as the reason why. In any case, it's stated that the Storm cut the threads that made Realms "connect" with Earth, thus erasing their protective effect on those who inhabited them.

    Clearly the intention was that becoming a spirit isn't cool, it's a pathetic fate you want to avoid at all costs. It's the Wassail from Vampire...it's a way to have adventures in the Umbra while at the same time making sure these take less than three months - thus Mages can't just go away and live there.

    Threath Null, on the other hand, was introduced as antagonists. So they had to be more active...

    Now, it's said that Treath Null it's so cohesive because it has Conditioning as part of their making. But that by itself doesn't explain why they aren't mostly harmless drones doing whatever they were doing before converting, like Traditionalists seem to be...being "on the same page" doesn't mean the page it's going to be particularly interesting. One needs agency.

    A simple explanation could be that powers with more agency (VE that were able to make themselves inmune to the effect, the Computer, etc. Who knows?) are directing them.

    Frankly, while I can see Traditional spirits being every bit as alien, and nowhere near AS cohesive as Treath Null...

    I also think that them having no agency, and pululating the same dusty Realms repeating tasks may be dramatic, but it's also kinda boring. The product of an Era that wanted Mages out of the Umbra, out of the War, doing "street level" jobs and stuff...I would rather embrace the ideas for a Traditional Threat Null than say Traditional spirits are incapable of doing anything

                                        I don't recognize or understand those sci-fi references, except the Borg. I do not agree that Mages who become spirits should all be like the Borg or like the drones in Wraith. When I played Orpheus, the drone we had to deal with was mindless. He pantomimed the same motions over and over again, unaware of his surroundings. He typed on a computer that was off. He couldn't see or hear us when were were right there next to him. He was utterly mindless in a way that spirits simply aren't.

                                        Spirits don't think like humans but they are people, not punch-card computers or undead drones. They have sentient, sapient minds and free will and agency. For gafflings, their goals and interests are exceedingly narrow, but they still have emotions and sensation of their surroundings, and are capable of conversation. Jagglings have full individuality and personality and broader interests, and are a much better analogy for what happens when a sapient individual human (or shapeshifter) becomes a spirit. Certainly when a shapeshifter becomes an ancestor spirit or a Greater Uktena, there's no indication that this is a terrible awful thing that strips away their mind, personality, individuality, sapience, and/or sentience. Even when humans become Wratihs, which are clearly messed up beings, they don't all become drones. Bargaining with a spirit is not like programming a computer, precisely because spirits are not mindless machines.

                                        Incarnae are very complicated individuals, but I don't think a Mage should have a chance to become an Incarna unless they were already a high-Arete Archmaster or an Exemplar.

                                        The thing is that becoming a spirit is like becoming an Exemplar, but more so: you lose all Dynamic Magick and the ability to gain more enlightenment, let alone Ascend. You can't even reincarnate to try again. Whatever you are is all you'll ever be, because you don't even really embody a concept that exists in the material world so there's nothing driving you to grow and change in an animist way.

                                        I don't think that every Mage who becomes a spirit should become as messed up as the Threat Null or the Borg. They are, like you said, the result of something unusually deranged and malevolent.
