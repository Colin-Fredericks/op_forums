    Originally posted by Lord Revan View Post

    I guys do realize that consequences impose limitations right?
    Not in the sense of the question proposed.

    Mana Points and Vancian tables are tools that recognize magic as a powerful option, and make it into a limited resource to compensate, so you can’t solve all your problems with magic (and has to let your non-magical colleagues do their shit).

    Paradox is not a power limitator. It is a STYLE limitator. Since Coincidental magic isn’t less powerful or significant than vulgar magic (just harder to think about), Paradox doesn’t keep you from abusing magic, weak or strong, doesn’t keep you from solving every single problem with magic if you want, and doesn’t balance the Mage against any ‘non-spellcaster’.

    What it does is to justify the setting. It justifies characters full of Permadox being secluded. It justifies (in a tangential way) fantastic things in general being locked in secluded spaces or in other worlds. And it justifies and reinforces Mages taking preference for being subtle with their Magic.

    That’s a very distinct form of limitation than what is tackled by points or slots of magic. It simply doesn’t serve the same purpose, in any way whatsoever, and that was your original proposition.

            Originally posted by Lord Revan View Post

            Ramnesis , I share some of your opinons. I'm not sure magick should be unpredictable or risky, just hard and maybe exhausting. And one thing I don't like at all is the unbelief rule, especially because there isnt one and it's just too vague...
            As an element of a fantasy world, I don't think magic has to be unpredictable, risky, hard, or have any other limitation. As a dramatic element, and especially as a dramatic element for a game, it probably should have some kind of challenge or barrier to overcome. And if there has to be a challenge, it is best if it fits the nature of the world or the themes of the story. For Mage, I would choose unpredictable and hard over exhausting. The former two speak to the nature of Mage's metaphysics, while the latter one doesn't.

            As for unbelief, I agree. I'm surprised there isn't at least some kind of structure. In another game I'd suggest that there was no need to define something that could be left up to the story, but Mage defines things unnecessarily all the time. Unbelief is something I actually prefer to handle narratively, though, so I'm not complaining.

            I suspect that the issue is that rules would cause some consequences the authors don't want to deal with. After all, Unbelief is not limited to awakened magic, it hits other things too (Bygones are a prominent example). If anything that sleepers disbelieved strongly enough would end in X scenes, there would be a few changes to the world.

                                    Originally posted by Dataweaver View Post
                                    A bit like C20's Unleashings. That said, Mage has Wild Magick, which is completely and totally under the Storyteller's control; even in terms of when it happens. As described, it only seems to happen in two cases: when you first Awaken, or when you're a Marauder.

                                    Deliberate Wild Magick strikes me as something that could be suitable to a number of different Practices: one might unleash a burst of Wild Magick through Faith, for example, by saying a prayer and asking the higher power that you serve to intervene however it deems best; or a practitioner of Chaos Magick could borrow a line from The Last Unicorn: "Magick, do as you will".
                                    Okay, now I kind of want to include a Houserule for Mage called "Magick, Do As You Will". Wherein at any time, or perhaps just in dramatically appropriate moments, the PC can trigger Wild Magick. But importantly, it's "Magick, Do As You Will". The Mage herself has no control over the Effect, its potency, how it behaves, or who gets targeted. The PC is effectively using herself as a conduit for the primal forces of Magick to act, in whatever way these mysterious forces want.

                                    Ex: The Cabal are in a tight spot in an art gallery, and either can't or won't use their own Spheres to solve the problem. So one PC says "fuck it. Magick, Do As You Will". Suddenly, all the paintings and statues come to life. Has it made the situation better or worse? The PC certainly had no input in it, but they can still respond to the change in whatever way they see fit.

                                    It's a sort of risky ploy, channeling raw Magick into an environment normally bound by a rigid Consensus. Like they've opened a crack in the shell of calcified Reality, and let the power of primordial creation and dreams leak in.

                                    (Naturally, Paradox does NOT like Mages doing this.)

                                    I'd consider tying "Wild" Magick to the Avatar: say that what it actually is is your Avatar working Magick on its own. So the higher your Avatar Background, the more potent and/or frequent Wild Magick becomes. I'm not sure exactly what mechanics I'd use; but one possibility would be to substitute your Avatar rating for Sphere requirements of an Wild Effect.
                                    This can act as yet another dramatic release valve. Avatars, by their nature, want their Mage proteges to advance. Not necessarily LIVE, mind you, because they can always reincarnate. But the Avatar is, at least nominally, concerned with the Mage. So if the Storyteller decides a situation is getting dire, they can have the player roll their Avatar rating instead of Arete.

                                    This may involve the Avatar appearing, to everyone or just the Mage, to render assistance. Or simply to goad the PC into continuing to struggle, or do whatever else the Avatar wants. Maybe the Avatar gives advice, or offers to guide the Mage's hand (possibly letting them use a Sphere or Sphere level they don't have, as a kind of taste of what power they COULD wield). Or the Avatar may act on their own, manifesting some Effect as appropriate to their apparent nature or interests.

                                    Ex: A PC's Avatar normally appears to them as a shadowy Roc that blots out the sun. In her time of need, the Mage's Avatar seemingly appears before everyone present. The ceiling, if any, falls away, revealing a sky that's then immediately blotted out by a terrible bird with titanic wings. It's an illusion, yes, but it strikes fear into the heart of every character present, as a being of a scale beyond mortal comprehension makes itself known.

                                        Originally posted by monteparnas View Post


                                        It could be that they were originally created by Magic or a somewhat similar power. In that scenario, what defines a bygone is its true history, even if this history isn’t known to the Mage. The creature having preternatural powers is then irrelevant to their status as a bygone.
                                        That leads to three possibilities that I can think of:

                                        1) Bygones, regardless of their lore, are the creations of mages. This works well on a case by case basis but it doesn't work as a broad explanation for bygones. That is, it reduces all the myths and legends to misunderstandings of the acts of mages.

                                        2) There is an arbitrary set of things that use awakened magic rather than anything else, and every bygone is one of these or the creation of one of these. This is workable, but it doesn't really tell us anything about the world. If the goal is to say that unbelief only works on awakened magic, this accomplishes that, but circularly.

                                        3) Bygones have their own origins in culture or lore or history, but this still counts as awakened magic. This is the other end of the spectrum, where unbelief can strike almost anything. It is functionally the same thing as saying unbelief can affect things other than awakened magic, just accomplished by opening up awakened magic to mean almost anything.

                                        The third option suits me just fine as I don't tend to draw much distinction between the effects of the Consensus and the effects of intentional magic, but I think it is simpler to just say that Unbelief has the same vast powers as belief, just applied negatively.

                                        Because unbelief is so open ended it is hard to pin down its limits, so attempts to define it purely in terms of awakened magic will always struggle to avoid the circular reasoning trap: What does unbelief work on? Awakened magic. What is awakened magic? Whatever unbelief works on.

                                                            I have become less convinced of the value of Paradox as an object in the setting over the years. I think that Paradox is best for emulating the trope of magicians tampering with great powers, or with struggling to perform great works of magic without something going awry. Of course, it is also quite obvious that Paradox was intended to be the masquerade-enforcer of the setting, and that is something that I do not really think is needed. The transformation of the Scourge to Paradox renders it quite clear that the focus of the latter is to force mages to use coincidental magic for the most part, and that is honestly something that seems more up the alley of sorcerers than mages.

                                                            More broadly, I just have a problem with with the thought processes behind employing masquerade-enforcers in one's setting in general. I think that people seem to assume that a few careless mages firing off a few vulgar spells is enough to clue in the entire world to the presence of Awakened magic, when such is not the case. First off, all but the most far-reaching vulgar spells would probably only be witnessed by a few Sleepers, and the testimony of a few witnesses is generally not enough to convince the average person that a supernatural event has occurred, let alone the populace. Second, this situation is already present in the base setting, as once a mage is able to actually perform a vulgar spell, the Sleeper just knows about it, the consequences of Paradox be damned. Paradox just discourages mages from casting vulgar spells; it does not prevent the consequences of vulgar spells to continue affecting reality. In general, supernatural phenomena need to have three main elements in order to be accepted by a society. They need to be strong, they need to be relatively frequent, and they need to be relatively accessible. Awakened magic fails in regard to the latter two points, as mages are not obligated to cast vulgar spells incessantly and not everyone has unfettered access to Awakened magic. You do not need to have a mechanical system limiting magic to justify why the supernatural is not generally accepted by society. We already live in a world where universities publish the results of experiments with psychic phenomena, people can see strange objects in the sky, and people talk with spirits. I think that people often think that a limiting mechanism is required in the cosmology of the setting for a masquerade to be understandable, but really, there are a lot of ways for mundane social factors to just prevent the acceptance of the supernatural by the public.
