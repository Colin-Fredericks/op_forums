    Should the Technocracy win?

    09-11-2023, 09:41 AM
    The title I chose is admittedly click-bait, so don't take it too seriously. But I do want to entertain the possibility that an eventual Technocratic victory in the next decade or so may not only be possible, but maybe even desirable. Bear with me…

    I have been listening to the Audiobook version of The Fourth Turning is Here, which argues that there's a generational cycle of history, with each generation lasting roughly 20 to 25 years or so and having a distinct character that is a reaction to their parents' generation and to which their children will react. This cycle consists of four generations — currently a Prophet generation (the Boomers), a Nomad generation (Generation X), a Hero generation (the Millennials), and an Artist generation (tentatively called the Homelanders). This cycle drives and is driven by a two-stroke cycle of cycle of spiritual awakenings and civic crises which in their turns remake and reinvigorate the culture (the Awakenings) and the government (the Crises). It's a fascinating read.

    Mage: the Ascension, first published in the 90s, is a product of Generation X, and bears our distinctive characteristics: a mixture of pessimism and advocacy for individuality. But according to the theory, we are now firmly in the middle of a Crisis, which shows every indication of being as consequential as the last three Crises: the Great Depression and World War II, the Civil War, and the Revolutionary War. Assuming that this cycle resolved successfully, we'll be living in a distinctly different world in ten years, as different from today as the post-WWII world was from the pre-WWII world, or post-Civil War America was from pre-Civil War America, or the newborn American Republic was from the British colonies that birthed it. And if the past cycles are any indication, the post-Crisis world ten years from now will completely invert the characteristics I described above: expect a more optimistic tone and a tendency to extoll the virtues of community, service, and cooperation over individuality.

    Which brings me to my click-baity title: if Mage is a game about collectivism vs. individuality, with the Technocracy representing the former and the Traditions representing the latter, then chances are that the post-Crisis audience for the game will be firmly on the side of the Technocracy; and if the game doesn't adapt, it may lose whatever relevance it has. So: should Mage (eventually) be remade to that a reformed Technocracy is ascendant, with the Traditions coming to terms with it and possibly even joining forces with it? Or should the game stick to its guns and take the (by then) unpopular position of declaring that the post-Crisis world's likely emphasis on civic-mindedness is fatally flawed, taking MRev's “The Ascension War is over, and we lost” attitude up a notch?

    My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

                  While my issues with the generational cycle models are many, I'll avoid getting into those because I'm not sure they really matter to what's going to happen to the future of Mage.

                  Honestly, however mixed the reception, I think the Disparate Alliance is actually the faction that comes out the best of the current state of affairs because they're the most flexible. If we'll pardon my foray into US politics, the Technocracy and the Traditions are a lot like the current US political parties that count. They've been competing with each other for ages, and in reality make the same decisions on many topics while in power. The small percentages of issues that really disagree with are big issues, but big issues attached to a lot of institutional baggage. Most voters are motivated negatively: they're voting for the least bad choice, not the best of two good choices, or an obvious good choice vs. an obvious bad choice. Even if the US system is in no place for an ascendant third party, the US is also in a place where historically speaking, a new party can displace one of the current top two, or we could see a massive realignment of political alliances that make up each party. The desire for something different, or at least that feels different, is very powerful, and flipping back and forth between two choice stops feeling like "different" pretty quickly.

                  The Disparate Alliance is different. It's not some sort of guarantee that the DA wouldn't just become the Traditions take 2, or even fall into the power-chasing rabbit hole of the Technocracy. But they speak far more to a generation that's tired of both major factions being entrenched in fighting each other, that all the bad actors they could be dealing with are growing in power in the shadow of petty philosophic fights. They can make the case that, "what we have now isn't working, and we have a functional system to try to fix things in a new way,"

                                                  So... one of my big problems with this model of generational cycles, is that it is very focused on Western industrial societies. It really breaks down if you go earlier than the Industrial revolution in Europe, and doesn't apply to all sorts of cases where the "crisis" was European colonialism and imperialism that didn't get "solved" in a generational time-frame. It also ignores how subcultures (esp. minorities) do not experience these cycles in the same way.

                                                  As an example of all of that: Haiti and the Indemnity issue. When Haiti revolted against France, and won the ground war for independence, the French demanded payments (indemnity) before they would recognize the Haitian government, for their loses during the revolt... including the value of the slaves that had been emancipated. While France eventually sold the debt, it never forgave the debt, and to this day refuses any claim that they hold responsibility for the impact of that debt and should have to give that money back. It took 122 years for Haiti to finally pay off that debt, and that crushing financial burden improvised the nation far more than other countries that won independence in the same period without that hanging over their heads. Haiti is still struggling to overcome the setbacks of over a century of inability to grow economically. That "crisis" lasting 122 years, and impacting well beyond that, means that industrial generational cycle never really set in. It has just been ongoing crisis.

                                                  The problem with how to keep Mage relevant and using this particular model, is that an Industrial era model is unlikely to be relevant if we actually manage to solve many of our current macro-social problems. Trying to predict based on a model that most likely isn't going to survive future changes isn't really functional.
