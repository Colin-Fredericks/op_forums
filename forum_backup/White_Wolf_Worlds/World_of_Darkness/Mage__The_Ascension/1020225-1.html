    Yeah, none of this gun stuff jibes with real history, where firearms shaped the evolution of the professional army. Prior to that military service was the prerogative of the landholding military, supported by levies and mercenaries. What it did change was the aristocratic fortress household, which was the focus of medieval military action. Even the American Revolution was a product of a nucleus of professional soldiers assisted by the logistical nightmare of moving British forces around.

    Anyway, for much of the Middle Ages:
    * You are never a face in the crowd. Few communities are large enough to provide that anonymity.
    * You require permission to travel and enter communities, though there is such a thing as religious tourism. The fantasy inn doesn't really exist.
    * You are probably physically stronger and have better teeth than your modern counterpart. You are not that much shorter -- that happens later -- and you live a good while provided you survive childhood.
    * You do not have a private life. Being alone is seen as a hardship, practiced by monks. Even when you're wealthy you sleep in the same room as your staff.
    * You reach puberty in your mid-teens, not earlier as we do now.
    * In many places, if you're a man and possess a certain amount of money you are required to own basic arms and armour in case you're called up. You practice military skills regularly, such as after church.
    * Church is the centre of your social life and where important community decisions get made. You might even write on a church wall to leave a message.
    * You probably can't read or write unless you're clergy.
    * You might go hungry in a bad year, though people help each other out. Hunting without permission is poaching, and illegal. Meat is uncommon.
    * You get married young if you're rich, but don't consummate for a while. You marry in your 20s if you're a typical sort of person.
    * In battle, your goal is to take or hold a castle. If you're a knight or noble, you prefer to take other knights hostage. If you're not, winning your side of the siege is more important than killing anyone, but of anyone is visibly sadistic or breaks their word, all bets are off.
    * If you're Jewish, you're subject to murderous persecution at times and may belong to a special set of professions the Church discourages Christians from participating in, or which are simply convenient for non-Christians to engage in, such as medicine, certain types of trade and moneylending.
    * Instead of fancy jousting tournaments, knights (mostly hereditary martial arts experts trained from an early age) practice the bohurt/behourd, where they fight in a constrained area (like "between two towns") and try not to kill each other. Winners get the losers' arms and armour. The Church disapproves of these.
    * If you wear any armour, it will be a gambeson, a padded jacket. You're wear a helmet too. If you're wealthy, your armour will be mail, supported by an increasing number of plates until the 14th century, when steel plating starts to become the primary form of protection -- still supported by mail. All "mail" is chainmail -- nothing else has that name, and ringmail doesn't exist.
    * People are not *that* superstitious. They believe in the evidence of their senses. The Church may ascribe symbolism to natural phenomena. You may believe in animals such as the Pard, which don't exist. You fear wolves, which will kill you if they're very hungry and you look like easy prey. People skilled in folk medicine combine ritual with pragmatic things, and don't always know where one ends and the other begins. People don't believe these folks have anything to do with the Devil yet. If Christian, you probably believe saints and Christian miracles play an active part in the world. You might believe in hidden spirits. You believe there was a literal Simon Magus so in that case, you believe in "magic." If you're educated you might believe that certain rituals with Christian and Platonic elements influence the world, but if you're not educated you don't know anything about that.

    In the World of Darkness, you might believe other things. You may know that the Order of Hermes has autonomous fortresses like monasteries, but you're supposed to stay away from them. You now that supernatural forces exist in the woods, and that some of the wise people you know might really be pagans of some kind.

        In addition to the above:

        *You receive nearly six months off from work during the year because of Sundays, Saint's Day, Market Days, etc.

        *A work day is considered to be six hours because people are expected to participate in family life. If you work twelve hours straight, you are considered to have worked two days straight, and the local clergy will likely castigate any boss who regularly works their people that hard outside of planting and harvest. When combined with the previous point, the average adult person in the High Middle Ages worked around 1200 hours a year, when the extended days for planting and harvest are included.

        *Slaves are rare outside of Eastern Europe and slave ownership of Christians is forbidden by the Church.

        *Serfs were paid in beer and meat during planting and harvest by their lord and the Church. Serfs also had right to land and, as long as they participated in planting and harvest, would not lose their land. Outside of famine, they rarely went hungry, as hungry serfs were unproductive serfs.

                Yeah, much of the sense of privacy and family life we have now were products of the little ice age. Heating small rooms with multiple fireplaces off the same chimney is considerably easier than heating a great hall off of a central fire. Later on there was a tradition of having witnesses to the consumations of marriages, but that tradition wasn't necessary when everyone slept in the same room.

                I'll add:

                *You didn't have good maps. Seafarers might have had accurate maps of port locations, but they didn't show much inland. Accurate mapping requires surveying and we didn't have many people good at that until cannons saw widespread use (surveying and aiming are the same skillset, basically). Maps were crude affairs that showed a few out of scale landmarks.

                *Quality of life for the poor actually went up in the last days of the (western) Roman Empire and the early Medieval period. The spread of Christianity pushed charity to new heights.

                *Memory was fantastic. Most people didn't know how to write things down and compensated by perfecting their memory instead. Chances are language was quite a bit more poetic too, since that serves as a useful mnemonic.

                *People were cleaner than we imagine. A lot of historians attribute Europe's aversion to cleanliness to diseases that spread as the Roman bathhouses broke down, or to the Black Plague (a layer of dirt does make it harder for the fleas to bite).

                *The Romans actually had large watermills, but like aqueducts they were a large scale endeavor. The technology wasn't lost, per se, just beyond the logistical capabilities of most communities. In that sense it was lost to those who didn't live near a surviving mill, but it was never lost completely. Concrete is the only thing I've heard of that has an actual claim on being 'forgotten,' and even that is a questionable claim.

                *This is the fun bit for Mage. While mystical traditions, mystery cults, astrologers, and a whole host of other magical traditions existed for millennia before this point, the Medieval Period was when our popular conception of the wizard was born. For almost a thousand years after Rome fell, if you wanted knowledge you looked to the past. Progress and thought never fully stopped, but chances were most of what the people of the day came up was already written down long ago. The image of the frail old man pulling arcane secrets out of squiggles in dusty old tomes started here.
