    The Dream Factory: Los Angeles And The Entertainment Industry In Mage

    08-07-2021, 04:04 PM
    Over at the wiki, it stated that the Technocratic Union didn't pay significant attention to Los Angeles until the 1990s, which doesn't make much sense to me. I'll explain.

    Feedback, thoughts, questions, and corrections are not just welcome, but invited:

    The entertainment industry is of enormous importance to the Union (especially The Syndicate and NWO), and the lion's share of mass produced entertainment in the world is either filmed in Los Angeles, produced by a Los Angeles company, acted in by people who live in the city and are represented by agencies and agents based there, or is somehow dependent upon Los Angeles money, talent, skill, or representation. That's influence over the eyes, ears, and minds of billions of people who consume that media. Moreover, there are few if any industries in which personal connections and being physically present at the right locations (awards ceremonies, charity events, club openings, and so on) is so pivotal to gaining influence and power, meaning that this operation can't be run from a distant boardroom. Worse, a certain amount of actual fame and publicity is key to success even for non-actors. This would make it necessary for Exceptional and even Enlightened agents to be thick on the ground in order to operate effectively within the milieu. The Union would have to pay attention to the city simply because what takes place there affects so much of the rest of the world.

    Los Angeles is briefly referenced in official material as being a Syndicate city. This strikes me as enormously likely. It's not likely that this is the whole story, though. The NWO would insist on having a say in the media output. Problem: Unlike Syndicate agents, who tend to be able adapt to a flashy, more public lifestyle, the Black Suits tend to prefer working from the shadows. This makes it difficult for them to engage in the high-profile whirlwind of the production side of the business. Solution: Take control of the end product before it hits the market. The Hays Code (and later the MPAA) were concessions forced upon the Syndicate by the NWO. They have agents in the Standards and Practices department of every network to determine what can and cannot go out on the air. They also use entertainment media and demographics-related Procedures to kill interest and buzz in media that may weaken the Consensus, and to push the same for media that enforces it.

    See? It really was a conspiracy that caused your favorite show to be cancelled!

    The Syndicate essentially runs the town. That much is true. They chart the courses of entire careers, raising up entertainers to stratospheric heights often only to send them crashing back down into obscurity and poverty, and do so with what must seem like gleeful abandon to an outside observer. There isn't a single significant studio, talent agency, publicity firm, or management corporation that isn't under their thumb, or at least their enormous influence. Their people are on every board, and in every executive suite, usually at or near the head of the table. Not a single major motion picture is released without their say, and not a trophy is given during awards season until they approve the winner (with the occasional surprise due to Statistical Inevitability). A common complaint among other Conventions in the area regards how many Syndicate agents have "gone native," living the high life and raking in the profits while ignoring their greater responsibilities to humanity.

    I don't see the Progenitors or
    iteration X as taking an enormous interest in the area, but the former could be involved with Cedars-Sinai or Children's Hospital LA. The Void Engineers likely have a presence at JPL and Edwards Air Force Base, but not necessarily a significant one.

    The Traditions are a bit more complicated, as one can rest assured that if there's an occult belief, mystery school, pseudoscientific perspective, fringe philosophy, yogic practice, or meditative path to enlightenment out there, then someone in Los Angeles believes in it. This has the downside of a lack of coherence among the various Council-aligned magi, but the upside that if you don't draw too much attention, you can get away with much more than you could in a city with a less diverse and eccentric population. The official White Wolf supplements say that the Hollow Ones pretty much run the nightlife along the Sunset Strip, which makes sense. CoE enthusiasts have had a huge presence since the founding of the studios, though, and maintain it to this day as a never-ending source of illicit substances for those in the right circles, and invites to the most decadent underground parties.

    The official material puts a combined Dreamspeaker/Akashic chantry somewhere in Santa Monica, but as it doesn't relate to the entertainment industry, it's not relevant to this post.

    A few random thoughts and observations:
    ​
    • The NWO and Syndicate would probably find themselves working at cross-purposes quite often, with the Syndicate tolerating the eccentric religious and spiritual beliefs of the major stars in order to keep them happy and productive, but the NWO trying to keep said beliefs from becoming overly popular or more mainstream.
    • Another potential source of conflict would be the imposition of the Hays Code, which was a response not just to films with content that didn't pass NWO muster, but also to what the Ivory Tower saw as dangerous examples being set by the stars of the day. The Syndicate would have probably been fine with this until they realized the impact on the bottom line.
    • The Syndicate controls the trade papers and glossy entertainment websites and magazines, but the NWO does a brisk trade in TMZ-style gossip and scandal, and uses it as a weapon against stars who have a reputation for promoting extreme lifestyles or alternative belief systems if they seem to pose a threat.
    • It's interesting that the split of the Etherites from the Technocracy happened at about the same time that movie production started to relocate to Hollywood from New York. It's especially worthy of note that one of the reasons for this move was that Thomas Edison's litigious company had a monopoly on the mechanics of film production, much as the Etherites first grew disenchanted with the Union when they sided with Edison over Tesla.
    • Famous (or infamous, depending) LAPD Chief William H. Parker was definitely at least an Exceptional citizen, and possibly an Enlightened one. Either way, he almost singlehandedly brought the department under the firm control of the NWO in the 1950s, and used his officers to brutally enforce any threat to the Consensus. There's probably at least one special unit patterned after Rathbone's Skeleton Keys, and tasked with hunting down Reality Deviants.
    • There are a number of places in the city where the Consensus may be weaker. Venice Beach is one, but also the Angeles Forest, Skid Row, and at least one club on the Sunset Strip.
    • Supposedly JPL founder Alan Parsons was a committed occultist, and attempted a major ritual to bring the Antichrist into the world with future Dianetics author and Scientology founder L. Ron Hubbard. Someone with better legal representation and no fear of litigation could probably do something interesting with that.
    I hope that this hasn't been too much of a wall of text. I'd love to know the thoughts of others when it comes to the entertainment industry as it relates to the Traditions and Conventions, and vice versa. I'm especially curious about how certain aspects could be expressed through game mechanics, or rotes and procedures could be used to navigate the industry. I haven't even gotten into the celebrity aspect, which would require a re-examination of Fame and Influence backgrounds from the ground up, or at least an expansion along those lines.

                          Okay, I'm getting a rough idea of a history together. I'm focusing on the Technocracy mostly right now due to the relationship between the Syndicate and the NWO defining so much of how I see things developing. We can circle back to the history of the Traditions later, and hopefully I'll find a way to combine it all into a coherent and reasonably succinct whole)

                          Going far back, the place that would become Los Angeles was likely Dreamspeaker and Verbena territory, at least for those mages who allied themselves with the Council at all. The takeover by the Spanish obviously started with the Explorators, who eventually ceded control over the immediate territory to the Cabal of Pure Thought as part of the mission system. The area probably didn't attract significant notice after that other than as a port of trade. The switch from the Spanish to the U.S. government didn't lead to significant change in this regard, as the discovery of major nodes in San Francisco had led to the Gold Rush of '49, and the Union's attention was focused on that region. The dawn of the 20th century changes all of that.

                          1900s: One result of the feelers put out to the Traditions by the Electrodyne Engineers is a friendship and eventual romantic attachment between the artistically inclined Electrodyne Engineer Sophie Auclair and the Orphan and future Hollow One Daphne Miller (these are placeholder names). Chafing at the restrictions placed upon her by the Technocracy's strict control over motion capture technology, Auclair uses her Convention's split from the rest of the Union to provide cover for her flight to Los Angeles, where she can experiment with new techniques away from Edison's overly zealous patent lawyers. Their initial pictures prove enormously popular with the public, though no prints are known to have survived to the present day: By the time the Union realizes that the films being produced in Hollywood use technologies that don't fit with the timetable, they do their best to confiscate every existing reel, but the damage has been done. The Syndicate moves in to reassert control over the technology.

                          1910s: Other filmmakers take advantage of the mild weather and distance from New York to set up their own operations, with the first official Los Angeles studio being founded by the Syndicate in 1915.

                          Auclair and Miller eventually find themselves shut out from the industry created around them by the Syndicate, but remain enormously influential within the Los Angeles social scene, and mentor many up-and-coming actors, directors, and screenwriters. By 1917, the NWO has a presence, but the Ivory Tower hasn't fully caught on to the impact of this technology on the masses. The following decade changes all of this. The Cult of Ecstasy also establishes a strong presence at this time, helping to ensure a steady supply of untainted alcohol to the film community of Hollywood during the dark days of Prohibition.

                          The first Nephandic caul known in this area is established at around this time.

                          Late in the decade, Aimee Semple McPherson of the Celestial Chorus brings her ministry to the city, and gains immediate and immense support among the masses.

                          1920s: Hollywood begins to gain a reputation for decadence. The NWO tries to bring them into line through public shaming in the national newspapers and film magazines, but this only adds to the mystique. The highly publicized trial of Fatty Arbuckle causes enormous public outcry, but leads to little change in the culture.

                          To make matters worse for the Union, the Syndicate seems to not be trying to exert any actual creative control over the film output of the industry, meaning that creative types sympathetic to the traditions have been able to push their propaganda across the United States, and even overseas. Things come to a head in 1925, when the Auclair-influenced film The Lost World is released, and the astounding visual effects for the time cause a slight ripple in the Consensus when many viewers believe that they're watching actual dinosaurs filmed for the movie. This is quickly debunked, but the Ivory Tower grows concerned enough to demand action.

                          In response to what the NWO sees as the Syndicate brushing off their concerns, they use their agents in pulpits, in radio and print media, and in government offices to denounce the content of the more provocative motion pictures, especially those with a focus on sex, drug use, violence, the occult, and non-Western religious and spiritual beliefs. This meets with growing success among the masses. Writers, directors, and actors influenced by the Traditions begin to find themselves shut out of work.

                          The introduction of talking pictures serves as an excuse for the NWO to further their influence over the Industry, causing enormous resentment among Syndicate members who see this as an unnecessary overreach by their colleagues, one that could affect the bottom line.

                          In 1926, The Syndicate and the NWO put aside their differences for long enough to destroy the public reputation of Chorister Aimee Semple McPherson, leading to massive disillusionment for her followers, and growing skepticism towards religion in general. The Celestial Chorus never fully recovers its influence in the region. As an unintentional (?) consequence, however, reports of Nephandic activity in the area increase over the upcoming years and decades.

                          1930s: The NWO takes advantage of the Syndicate's weakened influence following the market crash to exert further control over the film industry. This results in the creation of the Motion Picture Production Code in 1930, and its strict imposition in 1934. As a consequence, the mold is set for what is considered "appropriate" entertainment for the masses for decades to come. This is an enormous victory for the Ivory Tower. A Syndicate faction in favor of rapprochement with their NWO colleagues takes over, their disgraced predecessors reassigned to oblivion.

                          The renewed congenial relationship between the Conventions means a renewed focus on discrediting the beliefs of the various Traditions in film: Films such as Frankenstein discourage budding scientists from pushing too far beyond the realm of what is thought possible. Substances and practices sacred to Ecstatics and Dreamspeakers are now fodder for lurid exploitation flicks showing innocent young white American boys and girls seduced into addiction and disease. Ritual magicians are portrayed as evil sorcerers, and worst of all is the blatant racial stereotyping that comes into play when Akashic beliefs are spouted by "Oriental" masterminds, all tribal shamans are shown onscreen as violent savages, and the Euthanatoi are reduced to mindless "Thuggee' cultists by Hollywood. Even the Verbena are mocked by the portrayal of the Wicked Witch of the West in The Wizard Of Oz.

                          Working together, the NWO and Syndicate create a studio system that controls the lives of their stars to such a degree that even a hint of scandal or impropriety means the end of a career, preferably before it hits the papers. For studio favorites and stars too popular at the box office to simply fire, black-suited "fixers" go out with a mixture of money, implied threats, and limited but effective violence in order to cover for their indiscretions before they're known. In order to satisfy the insatiable appetite of the public for new content, the Pharmacopoeists are brought in as studio doctors to dole out highly experimental stimulants to actors and writers, which in turn leads to more scandals requiring intervention by studio fixers when the this leads to a crisis in addiction, several mental breakdowns, and more than one death from accidental overdose.

                          The masses are fed an endless diet of lavish musicals, screwball comedies, mawkish melodrama, and gangster films where the protagonist meets a grisly end, all of which reinforces desired behavior and none of which contradicts the Consensus. Truly, it is the Technocracy's Golden Age as much as it is that of Hollywood. There's some concern over a handful of film buffs becoming a bit too enamored of the bad guys, but by and large, things are exactly where the Union wants them to be.

                          1940s: With the dawn of World War II, of course, the focus changes almost entirely to the defeat of the Nazis. The temporary truce between the Traditions and the Conventions allows a few experimental and even socially subversive anti-fascist works through the cracks, but by and large, the focus is on more of the same stuff that was being done in the 30s, only now with more people in uniform.

                          While the end of the war means the end of the truce between the Traditions and the Conventions, it doesn't mean that every personal relationship developed during that time ends as well. In business meetings and at Hollywood parties, a specific way of doing business has developed, and it requires an ability to tolerate the eccentric beliefs of others to a certain degree. Some lines of communication remain open, and when the Virtual Adepts later bolt the Technocracy, a number somehow still manage to find work in the Industry in fields requiring technical expertise and creativity. This doesn't escape the notice of the Ivory Tower, and their reaction is severe: Dozens of the Syndicate's most effective operatives and creative talents in the Industry are targeted by the House Un-American Activities Committee for suspected Communist activities, with their Syndicate handlers subjected to extensive interrogation and occasional reprogramming. Almost simultaneously, the NWO strips control of the LAPD from the Syndicate, placing the Exceptional (possibly Enlightened) NWO uber-loyalist William H. Parker in a place of influence, and making him Chief of Police in 1950.

                          1950s: In a hugely public "anti-corruption" sweep, scores of the Syndicate's agents in the LAPD are reassigned or dismissed from duty. While a level of corruption still exists, Parker's men (and yes, they are all men) occupy all of the most important positions. They proceed to inflict a reign of terror on the local Reality Deviant population and their allies. Minority and immigrant communities find themselves the target of special attention. Local papers blare lurid headlines of drug rings rousted, juvenile delinquents arrested, voodoo cults smashed, and sex orgies busted.

                          Worse for the Syndicate, the NWO has established authority over the television airwaves since TVs became available to the general public, meaning that the people ostensibly in charge of creating the programming have no actual say in the content, and are entirely reliant on the dictates of another Convention regardless of what it means for profitability or prosperity.

                          [I'm getting a bit drained, so this seems like a good place for me to take a break. I'll write more at a later time and/or date, but invite comments, questions, and so on in the meantime]

                                      I ran a Mage chronicle set in LA, starting prior to M20's release but incorporating the idea that the Traditions were heavily on the rebound, with momentum gained by the internet's leveling of the information disseminating play field and the Syndicate's failure to "win" in 2007, and the blowback it created within the Technocracy.

                                      I agree that the Technos didn't leave LA alone. Rather, there has been a long tug of war between the Technocracy and Traditions from the silent era onward. I think the OP's point on the timing of the Etherites' departure from the Technocracy coinciding with this era is wise, as "mad genius" served as the basis for many silent films (Cabinet of Dr Caligari, A Trip to the Moon, etc), and the Hays Code was certainly an attempt by the Technos to roll back excesses. The NWO practically wrote the script on WW2 era news reels, which continued into the 50s with the Korean War. The Syndicate and NWO collaborated on a lot of feel good films, rolling in the money and putting out conformist messages. One producer of the era, whose name I forget, said "I want width, not depth," referring to the big screen wide view pictures of the era; I think that a very NWO motto. The advent of TV brought the conformity message to every home that could afford one, and radio was still a thing for those that couldn't.

                                      But the Technocracy's greatest adversary isn't the Traditions; it's plain old humanity, with all its inventive genius. People want to tell stories, and not just happy, mundane Doris Day/Rock Hudson types of stories. The NWO let a lot of sci-fi ride in the 50s: both to introduce terrifying creatures -- so that the masses would grow to fear the strange and unnatural -- but also to placate the Void Engineers, who wanted to rush space travel into the Consensus. This all worked, but also served to fortify public desire for strange tales, especially among the youth. The Hollywood machine put out movies more efficiently than ever, but as the studio system broke down it became less tractable.

                                      Then we have the 60s, where the Cultists made increasing social headway, followed by the 70s, with the Virtual Adepts joining the Traditions, disrupting the Technocracy's clamping down on 60s era social mores. The VA's made moves to put movie making into the hands of as many dreamers as possible. You didn't need a studio any more. Home video and pirating tapes became a thing in the 80s, with a lot of weird stuff showing up in the shadowy corners of Reagan conservatism. In the modern day, anyone can make a movie, and while Hollywood is still the home of the international blockbuster, human ingenuity is as much a driving force as Tradition myth introduction and Technocratic restraint.

                                      I also had a group of old vampires, collectively called the Producers. Being good vampires, they were always well in the background. I don't think a PC even saw one until nearly the end of the chronicle. But they were always there pursuing their abstruse goals. LA is a terrific setting.

I think the OP's point on the timing of the Etherites' departure from the Technocracy coinciding with this era is wise, as "mad genius" served as the basis for many silent films (Cabinet of Dr Caligari, A Trip to the Moon, etc), and the Hays Code was certainly an attempt by the Technos to roll back excesses. The NWO practically wrote the script on WW2 era news reels, which continued into the 50s with the Korean War. The Syndicate and NWO collaborated on a lot of feel good films, rolling in the money and putting out conformist messages. One producer of the era, whose name I forget, said "I want width, not depth," referring to the big screen wide view pictures of the era; I think that a very NWO motto. The advent of TV brought the conformity message to every home that could afford one, and radio was still a thing for those that couldn't.
                                          The advent and popularity of television is going to play a huge role in the Syndicate/NWO rivalry as I see it, and the Syndicate's next moves in Hollywood will be partially in response to the NWO's stranglehold over television in the U.S.

                                          But the Technocracy's greatest adversary isn't the Traditions; it's plain old humanity, with all its inventive genius. People want to tell stories, and not just happy, mundane Doris Day/Rock Hudson types of stories. The NWO let a lot of sci-fi ride in the 50s: both to introduce terrifying creatures -- so that the masses would grow to fear the strange and unnatural -- but also to placate the Void Engineers, who wanted to rush space travel into the Consensus. This all worked, but also served to fortify public desire for strange tales, especially among the youth. The Hollywood machine put out movies more efficiently than ever, but as the studio system broke down it became less tractable.
                                          This is one of the aspects of later Mage editions that I really like: The Technocratic Union is enormously influential, but there's a certain limit to their abilities. I was going to get into the unintended consequences aspect in the 1970s, when the rise of films like The Exorcist, The Omen, and the original The Wicker Man gave rise to a new wave of interest in occult matters despite none of them being particularly pro-magick, but I think that your idea is a better time to introduce the concept, especially as regards what happens later on with the Void Engineers.

                                          Then we have the 60s, where the Cultists made increasing social headway, followed by the 70s, with the Virtual Adepts joining the Traditions, disrupting the Technocracy's clamping down on 60s era social mores. The VA's made moves to put movie making into the hands of as many dreamers as possible. You didn't need a studio any more. Home video and pirating tapes became a thing in the 80s, with a lot of weird stuff showing up in the shadowy corners of Reagan conservatism. In the modern day, anyone can make a movie, and while Hollywood is still the home of the international blockbuster, human ingenuity is as much a driving force as Tradition myth introduction and Technocratic restraint.
                                          The Cultists are going to nearly define L.A. life in the 60s and 70s, and their influence will reach into the 80s before the Just Say No campaign starts (which will itself lead to unintended consequences).

                                          I also had a group of old vampires, collectively called the Producers. Being good vampires, they were always well in the background. I don't think a PC even saw one until nearly the end of the chronicle. But they were always there pursuing their abstruse goals. LA is a terrific setting.
                                          I always like introducing other aspects of the WoD for a moment, if only to remind the players that they're not the only ones working behind the curtain.

                                                      If you've never read it, I recommend Gary Krist's Mirage Factory: Illusion, Imagination, and the Invention of Los Angeles.
