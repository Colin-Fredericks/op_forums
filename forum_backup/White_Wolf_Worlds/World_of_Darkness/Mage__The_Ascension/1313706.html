    1980s Mage

    05-22-2019, 02:06 PM
    I've been playing with the idea of a Mage chronicle set in the 80s. But I'm still looking for ideas and in-put. What cool stuff do you think this decade has to offer in the context of M:tAs? I think I would like to draw heavily on the horror and science fiction of this era. And what was the canon situation of the setting?

      The 1980s is a fun decade for Mage in a number of areas. I would personally suggest using the year 1984, not just for the George Orwell reference (and the film version which was released that year), but also because it was the year of a number of cultural/fictional touchstones. A sizable number of fantasy/science fiction films which came out that year, many of which became classics for that generation, in addition to various books and other media. One of these was William Gibson's Neuromancer, one of the earliest cyberpunk novels. Also, in a number of ways, this was sort of the last year of the "dark 80s", as compared to the much brighter "Back to the Future"/Glasnost/Hair Metal & HipHop late 80s.

      I don't think there's a lot of canon stuff happening in the 80s. I think a few of the main things are that Nightshade, the last surviving founding member of the Grand Council, dies in 1988. And House Shaea becomes a full Hermetic House in 1982. The Grand Council has their scheduled 58th meeting in 1988, with the 57th being in 1979. Only three people (the Verbena, Etherite and Hermetic members) show up.

      As for period influences, The Terminator is a big one. It's the inspiration behind the HITMarks, and is, IMO, a good example of how someone might find themselves recruited into the Traditions at the time (being marked for death for some possible future event predicted by Iteration X's Statisticians, only to be rescued by someone totally off the grid and spouting a bunch of stuff that sounds completely crazy). And, quite honestly, Southern California is probably the most iconic place to set a game in that era (unless you're doing the Cocaine Cowboys thing in Miami, the Cold War in Europe or New Wave Anime in Japan). TRON is another big thing, as it gives a good idea of what the Digital Web was like around that period. I also highly recommend WarGames as a good example of the Virtual Adepts of that era (at least the ones who aren't totally into the whole cyberpunk look). Dreamscape, involving the combination of psychics and technology to enter peoples' dreams, is a somewhat forgotten gem of the period, and is good inspiration for experiments by Virtual Adepts, technomancers and the NWO. It also has some fun conventional FX tricks that make for interesting Umbral imagery. Films like Firestarter, Ghostbusters, Brainstorm, Innerspace, The Philadelphia Experiment, and The Thing can offer ideas about other Technocracy activities. And, obviously, the textbook icon for the Sons of Ether in this era is The Adventures of Buckaroo Banzai Across the 8th Dimension. Visually, Blade Runner and Liquid Sky are also a lot of fun for 80s influence, as is Streets of Fire.

      I'll try to have more later, as real life is demanding my attention.

      Update: A few odds and ends:

      As far as the more fantasy oriented elements of the Umbra, you can get a lot of mileage and inspiration from films like Legend, The Dark Crystal, Labyrinth, The Never-ending Story, Excalibur, The Secret of NIHM, Time Bandits, The Last Unicorn, Flash Gordon, The Black Cauldron, and Return to Oz. Also perhaps Hellraiser II.

      In terms of gateway arts for Do and the Akashic Brotherhood in the 80s, the big one in ninjas. They were all over the place, in film, TV, comics and and elsewhere. However, 70s style kung fu still has a place, especially in areas of the urban black community. (The Last Dragon is fairly cheesy, but still fun, and would honestly be the biggest fusion of kung fu and black urban culture until the coming of Wu Tang Clan.) The Karate Kid would also help continue to popularize martial arts in the US.

      Regarding the Dreamspeakers, at least in North America, Native American Chic is at kind of a low point during the 80s. It's popularity in the 70s had wained a bit, and wouldn't make a come back until Dances With Wolves came out in 1990. A kind of weird exception is the second Poltergeist film. And to a much lesser extent, the offbeat John Cryer punk western Dudes.

      Incidentally, that film was directed by Penelope Spheeris, who is most famous for her excellent 1981 documentary The Decline of Western Civilization, about punk rock music and culture, and it's 1988 follow up The Decline of Western Civilization II: The Metal Years, about LA's heavy metal scene. (There's also a part 3, from 1998, about the homeless youth "gutter punk" culture; I recommend all three.)

      While the slasher movie genre was a big thing in the 80s, most of the decade's earlier entries all involved completely mortal killers who just happened to be deranged, very talented at murder, and fairly lucky. Besides the first five Friday the 13th films, and the first two entries in Halloween, there's My Bloody Valentine, Prom Night, Hell Night, and too many others to name. (The 1980 film Fade to Black is, IMO, a sadly forgotten work, whose main character feels like a Hollow One gone wrong.) The big game changer was A Nightmare on Elm Street, which led to the later half of the decade's proliferation of nigh-unkillable slashers, and eventually degenerating into farce by the early 90s.

      The early 80s is what took Stephen King from being a top writer to being a cultural phenomenon. Between 1980 and 1985, there were nine films made from his works: The Shining, The Dead Zone, Christine, Cujo, Children of the Corn, Firestarter, and Silver Bullet, and the anthologies Creepshow and Cat's Eye.

      The Dead Zone was directed by David Cronenberg, whose work was also well known to horror fans in the 80s. Videodrome and The Fly are must sees, and Scanners is also a good inspiration for 80s psychics and Progenitor experiments involving them. (Videodrome, especially, is excellent inspiration for seeing reality start to break down, and possible Marauder awakenings.)

      If you want to incorporate other supernatural types, most of them have a few 80s touchstones and influences.
      For vampires, the biggest ones are Near Dark and The Lost Boys, but earlier in the decade, there are a lot of good ones like The Hunger, Vamp, and Fright Night.
      For werewolves, An American Werewolf in London and The Howling are the two big ones. Wolfen and The Company of Wolves are also worth watching.

      (Fun fact: The Hunger and Wolfen are both based on novels by Whitley Strieber, who claimed that in 1985 he was abducted by aliens, and wrote a best selling book about it called Communion. Aliens have been the focus of the majority of his writing output since then. )

      For ghosts, Poltergeist and A Nightmare on Elm Street are the two biggest. Also worth seeing is The Changeling, The Fog, Trick or Treat (another film that has an interesting idea for period Hollowers), and The Wraith.

      For changelings and the fey, there isn't all that much. Troll isn't a bad film, but not exactly like the fey of the WoD. You can mine Something Wicked This Way Comes for ideas, thou it's not set in the 80s.

          In England the eighties were the height of the Thatcher years where the Liberal policies started to bite. There was massive unemployment and a lot of serious drugs and crime going on with the police still having a terrible reputation for corruption. Watch Trainspotting or read Irvine Welsh's books for an idea of what life really was like in those times - it might come across as fantastical but I can assure you that it really was like that at the time.

          Poverty and ridiculous inequality are great breeding grounds for stories and adventure. I ran a small Changeling campaign set in 1984 Edinburgh slums based on Shopping and it really worked well.

          Lastly, don't forget that there's often a very long time between when the events that inspire a writer happen and the book or movie comes out. Many books or movies of the nineties were based in events of the eighties: World of Darkness being a perfect example.
