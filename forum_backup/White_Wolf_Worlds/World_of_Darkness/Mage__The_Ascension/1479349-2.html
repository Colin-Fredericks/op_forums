    Having gotten my Masters in History for examining the link between Calvinism and the Enlightenment choosing the Republic as the preferred governmental form (preferred not required), I found out that most pre-Enlightenment Western societies believed the Earth was decaying. The also believed that each generation of human beings was less in all ways than the preceding generations. The Calvinists rejected this belief as rejecting the goodness of God. The Enlightenment secularized this belief (as they did with many aspects of Calvin's thought).

    The Romantics, in rejecting the Enlightenment (mainly because of the horrors of the French Revolution and Napoleon's wars) also embraced the view of the world as decaying. The fading of magic and wonder became a standard trope among the Romantics. From there it became a trope so standard in fantasy fiction that it was nearly unconscious. Just think how rare before the turn of the century Magic in the distant future was. Other than Ashton Smith and the animated film "Wizards" examples are thin on the ground.

    White Wolf simply followed an ancient trope.

                    Originally posted by Ravnos View Post
                    While the fact that 41% of americans believe in psychic powers (if that's true) is why magick still exists in modern day world...
                    On this point, I would argue the continuing existence has as much, if not more, to do with humans just having a seemingly inborn tendency towards Magical Thinking.

                    It's why perfectly secular and scientifically rational people will still smack a malfunctioning machine and tell it to work properly. As if this object of metal, plastic, and wires was a stubborn beast of burden, incompetent servant, or misbehaving child. Humans just project agency on things. Anthropomorphizing the inanimate and insensate, even if, by all rights, they should "know better".

                    Even in these days of scientific reason, people will still default to a kind of Animism.

                    People continue believing in jinxes - that speaking of something will cause it to happen. They believe in strings of bad luck, which in truth are little more than confirmation bias. They repeat mantras like "Make America Great Again" or "Vote Blue No Matter Who", as if doing so enough times will manifest that into reality (and that if it didn't, it's because they, or more likely their designated scapegoats, failed to follow the mantra hard enough). People don't like to live in murder houses, even when they don't believe in ghosts or know the murderer is safely behind bars, if for no other reason than unspoken "bad vibes". And every adult disbelieves in monsters hiding in the dark, up until the lights go out.

                    No matter how well informed or how much they internalize science, quirks of human psychology come to the fore that are of a more magical nature. It's not merely enough to "know better" about the facts concerning the world. For there is always more ignorance, more of something we don't, as individuals, understand. We must come to understand how our minds work - the cognitive pitfalls in our psychology - before we can actually catch ourselves succumbing to magical thinking. Even then, it still happens.

                    This, I think, is where the lingering effects of Magic are in the Mage setting. Call these psychological forces Earthly Foundations local to the human species. It means humans, even at our most "rational", will continue grandfathering in mysticism.

                                            That... isn't really what magical thinking means as an academic concept. Magical thinking is, most broadly, assigning a casual results to non-correlated actions. Or to put it another way, believing that one thing you did caused something else to happen, despite no (internal) logical connection between them. The way many pop-culture uses of "karma" are magical thinking: that something good/bad happened to someone because they did something previously that was good/bad (the actual religious concept of karma is a lot more complicated). Thinking that your boss bumped into a coworker and got hot coffee all over themselves happened because they just assigned you mandatory overtime despite knowing you had important personal business to see to is magical thinking (unless you have good reason to believe said coworker did that on purpose). Your 4th paragraph in post #35 had better examples of magical thinking (strings of bad luck are a good one),

                                            What you're talking about is anthropomorphism: where humans assign human features to non-human objects. Such as treating our tools as if they understand us, and can follow our directions/demands/pleas/threats/etc. even though we intellectually know they can't.

                                            Both mental activities probably have similar neurological roots (our brains are not built for rational processing, and most irrational-but-common behaviors can be linked to so form of our brains trying to develop an energy efficient way to apply what our brains are good at doing to approximate rational behavior, or to de-stress after too much energy spent on actual rational behavior. The idea that we can actually become Vulcans and stop doing these things is nonsense. All we can do is budget our emotional energy so we can recognize and avoid them when it is important to do so.

                                            You're also equivocating a lot between the concept of magical thinking using the word magic, and mysticism.

                                                    Paradox it's described on the books as, rougly, the reaction that it's caused when the Mage's Will clashes against Reality(tm). Just like the mage changes Reality, Reality changes the mage, when Reality *breaks*, it hurts the mage - That is Paradox

                                                    The Consensus enters in the equation as a way to shape the rules of Reality (on Earth) in ways that are beneficial to some mages and perjudicial to others. *How much* the Consensus, or belief in general, shapes Reality it's ST discretion.

                                                    Alternate things that could change Reality - at least on a limited fashion - are, of course, more than just possible, but a fact.

                                                    We know high level Spirits do this because they can define the rules of their Private Realms. And so can Mages when they set or alter the rules of their own Realms or Formatted Sectors of the digi-web. In the Horizon Realms, in particular, the creator's domain it's so strong that some rules suggested magick may not generate Paradox even on a Botch.

                                                    Note that you can suffer Paradox - and even more severe forms of it - on Umbral Realms even if there's no other human being in there, but the wizard.

                                                    So: Who sets the rules for Earth?, Who can change them?. It's ST discretion

                                                    If we want the forces behind the Consensus to be what caused Immortality and other spells to become harder, I don't think that *has* to be related to an increase in "rationality" at all. If humanity's collective power to, so to speak, "punish mages" increased overtime - as it did - it would make every bit of sense that the cappability of mages to act "against the norm" would diminish, precisely because humanity was always thinking like that, in every era and every place. And that way of thinking - magical or otherwise - isn't conducent to *every* effect being equally possible

                                                    If we don't want to assume the power belief has over relity in general and magick in particular. Just pick your favorite "A Wizard Did It" Heres a couple:

                                                    *It was the Weaver making reality more Static.
                                                    *It was Banality encroaching over the world.
                                                    *It was God, tired of mage shenanigans.
                                                    *It was a mage that Ascended and did that to Reality as per that old suggestion that ascended mages can affect all Reality at once (that was latter rescued for Awakening)
                                                    *It was a Mummy. While higly unfeasible, they do have a Hekau that can do that, if a mummy had a way to beat the Willpower of every magick practicioner in the world.
                                                    *It was a Prime 10 Effect.
                                                    *It was the increasing in human population, and human Avatar activity, demanding more Quintessence than what the world can give, causing Reality to become more frail (so, the Consensus but more malthusian. Euthanatos kinda believed this once, I think)
                                                    Originally posted by Dataweaver
                                                    A mage cannot cast a spell that alters what kind of Feat a given working counts as; nor can she cast a spell that causes a ritual to be performed as a Rite when it would normally bea Great Work (M20 pp.541–542). You cannot alter the way Acting in Concert works (M20 pp.542–543).

                                                    In short, the rules of magic are not something that can be altered by magic. I mention this because the original question was about just this sort of change: alterations to the way Paradox works. It doesn't mean that the rules of magic can't change; they obviously can, because they have. But that kind of change isn't even something that the Awakened are capable of (except maybe in the aggregate, and over a long period of time, much like Sleepers are only capable of some of the things that the Awakened can do in the aggregate), let alone the Consensus                                                    .

                                                    Let's not conflate RAW rules with the Lore about "the rules". In Mage these get mixed a lot, but there's still a Doylist and a Watsonian perspective that is very important to avoid confusion.

                                                    The Lore says rules changed, but the rules that the books use have a lot more to do with Doylist convenience than with the Lore. That's why you can find ancient mages with Spheres. M20 has no Pillars, and Phill stated very clearly that "Mage it's Spheres" - so you could very well say that Pillars were never a thing, and would still be working within M20 RAW. Or with 1e RAW, or with a lot of 2e RAW.

                                                    And thank God - otherwise one would have to invent a new RPG for every major change mage society had in the Lore, when playing in old eras - and perhaps even a new system for "special characters" that have different beliefs (like how the new Black Hand book declares the Pillar system can still be used with some changes, if you didn't "got the memo" that magick has changed)

                                                    That the rules say magick can't change the rules, doesn't mean so much that magick can't change the rules et all as that the player characters cannot use magick to that end. Because otherwise the game would be unplayable. If magick can do that, it has to be on a storytelling level (like, say, winning the Acencion War).

                                                    The book doesn't say that the difference between a short ritual and a Great Work are anything like Earthly Foundations - in fact they cannot be because otherwise going against them on a limited fashion should just cause Paradox rather than being impossible (like how you *can* float against gravity). The book doesn't equate those rules to *any* sort of in-game construct that was made to interact with the players IOC, wheter solid or soft.

                                                    In general that's the case. The big exception it's the Spheres, and the book recommends players to never talk about them IOC. And I think it was and is a mistake, that only served to cause a lot of confusion, to conflate these two levels.
