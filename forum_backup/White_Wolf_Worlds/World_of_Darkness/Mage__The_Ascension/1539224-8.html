                    Originally posted by Gryffon15 View Post
                    So, anyway…

                    I am by no means as well read on South Asian history, theology, or mysticism as I’d like to be so this will probably just be surface level brushes, but here are some thoughts that come to me when thinking about how irl interactions between European polities and the polities of South Asia might effect the development of the traditions.

                    European, particularly British, imperial penetration of India was a major factor in development of 19th century occult philosophy in the European world (here taken to include euro-settler colonies outside of Europe itself). Perhaps most famous is the case of Madame Helena Blavatsky who is considered to be one of the earliest and most popular occultists to introduce concepts from “Further Asia” into the milieu of Western Esotericism (prior to her, Occult Orientalism was more likely to focus on West Asia/North Africa: Persia, Turkey, Egypt, etc.). As one occult historian I follow put it in an interesting fashion, she was one of the first who could claim some firsthand experience with Indian mysticism and Buddhism, in an era where one couldn’t necessarily double-check her claims via a copy of the Vedas from a local library. Therefore her peculiar idiosyncratic (and dubious understood) interpretations of Buddhism and Indian spirituality are brought into Europe and, with the phenomenal success of Theosophy, become nigh-inseparable from all later western occultism. She’s kinda like Crowley in that, even if you aren’t a Crowleyan, if you’re in western esotericism after Crowley you’re still influenced by his work; same with Blavatsky.

                    Mage canon actually has some good examples of this: for one, the Akashic Brotherhood are a direct reference to the theosophical concept of the Akashic record. Blavatsky’s fascination with Tibet is also clearly reflected in the White Wolf’s writing (it’s not just because of how huge Tibet was in the 90s). Plus with Crowley downstream from Blavatsky you have his massive influence over the Order of Hermes depiction and Mage in general (and downstream of Crowley you have Robert Graves and his influence over the conception of the Verbena), the list could go on.

                    A project such as this definitely deserves a serious consideration of the myriad pre-colonization Awakened orders of South Asia, which is something I’m not sufficiently familiar with to touch on too much, but there’s also definitely the proliferation of adopted and appropriated materials to consider for the development of western schools of Awakened.
                    It is also important to remember that the Theosophical ideas of Madame Blavatsky would also be merged in the weirdest and most lunatic ways with Nietzschean ideas and Social Darwinism to create the awful ideas of Nazism. That was actually the reasoning behind my idea of having WW2 being an "unholy alliance", where mystics and technocrats united under (secret) Nefandic influence to form Nazi Germany and control Imperial Japan.

                                Trying to map Traditions to spellcasters from D&D tends to fall apart rapidly upon any analysis beyond the surface. Not really the most important tangent, I admit, but it's there.

                                Originally posted by Magus Maximus View Post
                                The Order of Hermes are the "classical Wizards" of D&D
                                That mostly tracks, but still not entirely, since the adoption of Enochian gives them a very heavy religious angle that Wizards only have if the campaign world requires wizards and specialists to worship a god of magic.

                                Celestial Chorus are the "Clerics"
                                They would fit into 4e's Divine power source somewhat snugly, but in other editions, you'd have to go far afield to detail the class or combination of classes that they might be, including Avengers of various sorts, Paladins, Crusaders, and custom priests of which there are about a hundred (no exaggeration, the books even admit this in later 2e). They wouldn't just be Bishop Turpin clones crossed with Van Helsing (the original cleric was custom-made to whoop up on the vampire PC Sir Fang in a fit of adolescent passive-aggressiveness regrettably exhibited by adults).

                                Eutanathoi are "Necromancers"
                                Not even close. Euthanatoi don't typically go around raising skeletons and trying to, say, cheat mortality or kill death (Voormas is the exception that proves the rule). They're more like priests of a TN death god, which in later editions shakes out to being priests of the Raven Queen, which are a rather far cry from any type of Necromancer.

                                Dreamspeakers are "Shamans"
                                There are probably a lot of even less savory, even more stereotypical kinds of D&D spellcasters that Dreamspeakers would be stuck with that don't usually get PC time, mostly in older editions. Alternately, they could be preservers from Athas, or take the next spot from...

                                Verbena are "Druids"
                                Verbena fall out as more of an odd kind of wizard in D&D terms (from 2e on, the Transmuter specialist stands out prominently). In 4e, they even drop the last fig leaf and just have a witch sub-class of the wizard. They're kind of like another side of the coin of the Hermetics.

                                The Cult of Ecstasy are "Divinators"
                                The CoE aren't allowed anywhere near the sanitized, family-friendly brand of D&D, but some of them might have something in common with Illusionists, given their approaches (when Sahajiya aren't messing with Time they're messing with Mind, and Illusionists in 1e had a lot of other specialist spells too).

                                The Akashics are the "Monks"
                                Monks can't really be reasonably described as classic wizards of D&D, as they were only spellcasters for part of one editions (2e), which folded them into the priest class for two or three supplements; they were then separated out in the Scarlet Brotherhood book in '99 to more closely resemble their 1e non-caster version.
