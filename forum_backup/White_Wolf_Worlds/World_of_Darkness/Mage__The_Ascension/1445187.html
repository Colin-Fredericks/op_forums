    MtA Campaign Essentials

    04-21-2021, 10:40 PM
    What would you say are the essential elements, or features, to a MtA Campaign? I am in the early stages of planning a MtA campaign, and wanted to ask the hive-mind here its thoughts.

      A Mystery, Mission, or Quest. I'm really a fan for an open world, but I'm starting to think that most people really want a story to pull them in and along.

      Some kind of jaunt into the Otherworlds. Exposure to a wider, and wilder, cosmos, danger and opportunity.

      A few surprises, weird stuff that isn't tied to the bigger Quest but still needs to be "figured out."

      Opportunities for Hubris. If you want to play to that theme, probably want to set up the temptation, since I think a lot people tend to play "safe" and need to be baited at least a little bit if you want them to consider genuine Hubris.

          The very old players guide to Mage, the Book of Shadows has many interesting ideas, but one particular insight may be of interest here:

          Nevertheless, the system is still incredibly open, diverse, and, like the metaphysics it is based upon, subjective. It is open to interpretation by each person playing the game. For some storytelling groups, this will be a curse that fulfills a power-mad player's fondest dream; for others, it can open up what I see as an evolutionary step the storytelling experience, because in Mage, the players inevitably become assistant Storytellers.
          I like this idea of players being assistant Storytellers.

          Because of how fluid it's the system of Mage, your players are capable - and should be encouraged to - be very creative with the rules, thus hard to predict. While "no plot survives contact with the players", that's doubly so when you don't even know what your PCs are or aren't capable off.

          That's not to say you shouldn't make a plot. I think players totally need a plot and a setting, all the more because Mage it's so free that w/o a solid foundation of what's going on, it can be hard to even think of a character, much less what to do.
          Because you can use Mage to tell almost any kind of story (from street level paranoia to gonzo spacebattles in space, and that's being conservative about it) I think players can use some help rigth from session 0 to overcome the "anxiety of freedom", so to speak.

          However, the plot should be "open world" enough than it can survive when it get's "edited" by your "assistants". Give your mages something to do, and then be amazed at how they go about doing it, and then some. This I find true for all RPG, really, but more so for a game like Mage.

          Just try to imagine keeping Mage players onto a linear plotline where the Storyteller wants events X, Y and Z to happen in order right after one another. The players will inevitably get to scene X and then perform some magickal feat that make scenes Y and Z meaningless and catapults the storyline in an entirely new direction
          ....
          Some argue that Mage's magick system is inherently flawed by being so open to interpretation that Mage game sessions will inevitably break clown into arguments between players and Storytellers over what it says on page 123 versus what it implies on page 243, etc., etc., etc. Who's having fun in that situation?
          Ultimately, the folks holding this book, the players,determine the quality of any Mage story. I argue that the magick system's openness is not a flaw, but rather its primary strength. It allows the players to participate in telling a good story. I cannot think of any other storytelling game that requires as much creativity, self-discipline and storytelling ability from its players as Mage does.
          ...
          More important than a creative player, however, is player self-discipline. Does turning a vampire into a patio set contribute to the mood or theme of the story? Does the story become more meaningful or entertaining by your character's action?
          ....
          In Mage, you are not so much a player as an assistant Storyteller. Most of a Mage story's excitement must come from the players helping to create the saga.
          This is a counsel for players, not STs. But I think taken with the above, it hints towards an ambitious plan.

          In a sense, I see it similar as how more modern narrative games - like Urban Shadows - are intended to be played. Where the rules themselves force the players to enhance (and sometimes derail and twist) the story and setting in ways that are impossible to predict, but that still allow (and require) the ST to be the final arbiter. This often risks to introduce a lot of chaos into the plot and the setting. But this is seen as a feature, not a flaw, and I had a lot of fun playing such chaoitc campaigns

          Trust in the ST it's required because as the final arbiter of a game where the rules are intended to be "open to interpretation by each person playing the game" one it's heavily encouraged to bend and re-interpret the rules in ways that beneffit the plot ... but also I think the ST shouldn't be too hard on the rules when the players interpret them, bending the rules to intent when possible and reasonable (when not too hard). The ST has to trust the players, too.

              Things I'd want to work out ahead of time: What is the status of the world, in particular the relative positions of Traditions and Technocracy? Maybe it won't be relevant for your game, in which case that part is easy. How about the Avatar Storm? This is more important, as it will dictate the interaction between players and the spirit world. What kind of myths do I want to interact with? Local tales, or has globalization carried over into the local space? Is there a common thread I can weave between the PC's without knowing anything ahead of time? Perhaps an NPC they all know.

              My current game started this way, with the NPC being murdered in the first session. This stunned them, especially the player who had her as a Mentor (the NPC's closest friend took over that role), but it led straight into the first story.

              I'd also learn what you can about your setting. My current game is set in New York City, and local lore and history has played a significant role in story crafting over the years. Lastly, keep in mind the prior advice about using PC backgrounds and goals as story fodder. Good characters drive stories forward and decrease your workload. Good luck~!

                  To add to Otto D'Fey's point, when crafting NPCs - especially villains - it helps to know a lot about what motivates them, and how they tend to react to outside pressures. This means not only knowing what their Plans are, but what their Goals are. It's a distinction that matters. The former will give you an indication of what NPCs will do on their own, given no input from the PCs. The latter informs what they want, and thus what they'll course correct to when the PCs inevitably derail their plans. Since the players will inevitably derail your own plans, knowing how NPCs react in general will be helpful in spinning new plot threads on the fly.

                  Knowing their quirks, character flaws, and beliefs will further make it easier to improvise. Over in D&D 5e, characters have not only the classic Alignments, but also Personality Traits, Ideals, Bonds, and Flaws. It's not a bad idea to crib these details from D&D for use in Mage. It tells you what an NPC cares about, what will motivate them to action, and how they falter.

                  Similarly, knowing how NPCs interact with each other can be helpful. Vampire: The Masquerade made heavy use of linked character webs, charts for relationships between NPCs. Creating one of these can help visualize the tapestry of your local setting. This chart can be further organized by allegiance (including things like sects, or the various kinds of spirits in the area) and thematics (ex: grouping together idealists, characters from the same older generation, members of a now defunct alliance/conspiracy, or anyone with a Primordial Avatar; this regardless of what "side" they're on). These provide additional dimensions to proceedings, and keeps the themes of the campaign in mind by seating them in the personages themselves.
