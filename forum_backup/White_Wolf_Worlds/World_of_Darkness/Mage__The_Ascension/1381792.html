          My various not-so-warm feelings about V5 aside, a lighter combat rules system was never going to appeal to me and my players, who have over the course of 15 years house ruled into the sytem more nuance and crunch. It's my view that the thing about combat systems is that speed of execution and smoothness are directly proportional to the lowest common degree of familiarity with the system present at the table--i.e., even if it's a chunky system, if everyone is familiar with it, you can just glide through it fairly quickly. It's like when I go to a con and watch the 40k wonks wage their multiple-thousand-point battles against one another... it's a lot of moving parts, but those people can do it in their sleep!

                      04-19-2020, 02:17 AM
                      Originally posted by Argonot View Post
                      We have been playing WOD for around 15 years too, but we never house-ruled combat. When we implemented the V5 one we were like "WTF? Really? Is this over? Last year we wouldve spent the whole session on this particular fight!".
                      Undoubtedly paring down a combat system is going to make it faster -- if you had a system where everyone simply had a single stat called "combat" that represented a dice pool people just rolled against one another until one person got more successes than the other, and there were no modifiers (personal, circumstance, or otherwise), you would fly through combat. I'm sure there actually are systems like that, just like there are systems like Phoenix Command where you take into account the powder loads on your gun's ammo, and have to check a table depending on your range to determine how a number of stats affect the accuracy of your shot (including but not limited to barometric pressure, wind speed, and bullet drop). Everyone finds their own mix of crunch to speed that they find acceptable. As I said above, my group likes crunch and nuance--if two characters have Dex 4 and Melee 4, and one uses a rapier, but the other uses a scimitar (not actually an example that's come up, but theoretically), we wouldn't be happy with this being a numerically and systematically identical match up. Having said that, every player (and I as the ST) have put in the work to streamline the process--we use a sort of combat stat block for characters with all the math already figured that distills the attack (whatever it may be) down to number of dice, which potential modifiers listed near that number for ease of reference; everyone has a PDF with copy/pastes of their powers (from the actual books those powers comes from) for ease of reference so we're never hunting things down. When combat begins, players pick out the the dice they need for attack, a separate pile for their dodge or block, a separate stack for damage, and a separate stack for soak (assuming we're not just using an electronic dice roller that has still programmed in). There still some futzing with adding or subtracting dice here and there based on wound penalties, ramping attack successes into soak, etc., but it's fairly minimal--no one is using the same dice for all the things and therefore having to reconstitute 4 different dice pools over and over; no one is stopping for even five minutes to look up simple rules, or even how their various powers work.

                      Was this a lot of prep--yes. At one point this was a lot of prep, and I have plenty to do as the ST when I have to build enemies for encounters, but the players are more or less good to go now; and it's generally business as usual for me (and it certainly helps me to plan an appropriate challenge because how we're forcing ourselves to annotate combat as raw dice pools). Encounters between 3 fairly well developed player characters (i.e., mages with lots of bells and whistles), and an equal number of tricked out lieutenant-level bad guys (so no slouch, but not meant to be the equal of one of the PC's), and twice as many "mooks"/extras, takes like 20-30 minutes; tack on another 40% or 50% to the time if it's an encounter where the environment or circumstances makes positional play important and so we're on a grid.

                      Having said all that, in case anyone is wondering, I think the single greatest time saving things we did were the separate piles of dice I mentioned above (everyone needs a shit ton of dice, but if you've been a WOD junkie for 15 years, I doubt this will be a problem lol); and the implementation of the minimum damage house rule--i.e., the least amount of damage an attack can do is the number of dice in the damage dice pool divided by 3 (rounded down). Depending on the sort of attack it is, sometimes the target can't soak it, and the minimum damage is enough to just put them over into a death, so we don't bother rolling; sometimes the minimum damage would be enough to kill, so we roll the defenders' soak first to see if their roll would even save them from the minimum. These may sound like small things, but they're the changes that will give you the biggest ROI re- effort vs. time saved.
