    Dropping Instruments-wise, what constitutes a Technomancer?

    06-05-2017, 04:01 PM
    First and foremost, we already know that some people on this forum disagree with how M20 does the process of overcoming Instruments. There's an entire other thread discussing it. For that very reason, let's avoid tracking that subject in here, and assume M20 RAW. That Mystics start overcoming Instruments at Arete 3, Technomancers start overcoming at Arete 6, and Technocrats keep their Instruments until Arete 10. With me so far?

    Okay, except I've been thinking about the division between Mystic and Technomancer. And, more specifically, about which groups belong to which category. Where the dividing line is.

    Obviously, Traditions like the Virtual Adepts and Society of Ether undoubtedly fall into the Technomancer camp, even when some of their number dabble in occult science (for, indeed, sufficiently analyzed magic is indistinguishable from Science). These are uncontroversial placements.

    But what about factions within otherwise Mystic Traditions and Crafts, who don't just dabble in science and tech, but use them heavily? At what point does a mage cease to be a Mystic whose tools include tech, and become a Technomancer with a Mystic's worldview? I suppose a better question would be at what point a mage becomes so dependent on their tools, they cease being able to readily see their magick as separate from them?

    If we assume the latter is more important, then certain groups that are nominally Mystic might be better run as Technomancers. The Children of Knowledge are a big example, as their Alchemy is just as much Science as it is Art. As far back as the Book of Crafts, they were stated to have many of the advantages of a Technomancer - tools that blend into the Consensus more readily - and were not allowed to overcome their Foci. Obviously, M20 is more charitable in this regard, but the logic is still sound. So it's fully reasonable to expect Solificati to not be able to overcome Instruments until Arete 6+.

    A slightly different example that raises questions is Hermetic House Verditus. The parts of that House that were holdovers from House Thig are much more obviously technomancer. But it's the "Classic" Verditus magi that interest me here. Their tools are more ancient and their worldview very Hermetic, but they still use those tools extensively. Going back to MtAs's spiritual predecessor, Ars Magica, House Verditus were Gifted individuals who had a House-wide Flaw: a dependency on ritual tools, when their fellow Hermetic magi could get away with just verbal and somatic components. While the Order of Hermes in Ars Magica is not the same Order of Hermes in Mage: The Ascension, they are parallel Orders with parallel ethos. It stands to reason, then, that a craft wizard might be so dependent on their creations, they would count as Technomancers for the purposes of overcoming Instruments.

    As with all things in the World of Darkness, how this whole matter works out is dependent on the Storyteller.

          I think it depends on what the Storyteller believes is the source of the mage's increased dependence on the tool.

          I tend to see the tool dependence as less dependent on whether or not something is Science vs. Art (I think most of the Hermetics would arguably fall on the former, by the same logic that the Alchemists do. Even the most Mystickal Hermetic uses geometry, formulas, and tried and true, for them, methodology) and more as a reflection of what society believes is possible right now.

          Basically, the average person might look at most of the bog standard Etherite or Virtual Adept tricks and think "oh yeah, that's possible", and not think too much about it. It gives them an advantage when it comes to vulgar vs. coincidental, but a disadvantage, I think, because the Mage arguably is still relying on that faint glimmer of possibility like a crutch. Somewhere in the back of their mind, a low arete Scientist may still be thinking "yeah, but this is ACTUALLY possible. Not like those wand wavers."

          In contrast, the Solificati might be using a tried and true method to turn lead into gold, but almost no one in modern society actually believes that's possible. So assuming the Solificati has a modern American upbringing, she's got a head start in realizing "Oh, crap, I am really doing the impossible." And I think that once you make that mental leap, it becomes easier to realize that a lot of your limitations are self-imposed. And therefore, you can shed them.

          The Etherite WILL come to that conclusion eventually too, but in a society that grew up on sci-fi movies, it's going to take a little longer for him to realize exactly how impossible his work is. And until he does, he will reinforce his mistaken belief that his tools are necessary.

          I think, for Thig/Vertidus technomancers, I wouldn't use the technomancer limitation. Because even granted that they are technomancers, they're still being trained and educated in the overall Order of Hermes. They're still going to have the foundation in the more impossible arts, even if their own methods are a bit closer to home.

              The reasoning stated in Book of Crafts for why Solificati can't overcome Instruments is that, regardless of the deeply symbolic and mythical underpinnings of the art, Alchemy LOOKS a whole lot like Chemistry. If someone takes a vial from a pocket, pours its contents on a concrete wall, and a section of the wall dissolves, most people would assume that it's some kind of strong acid. It doesn't matter that the character is using a fluid derived from crocodile bladders and Nile river sand, and used it to perform a Matter 3 Effect. If a Hermetic, meanwhile, pointed a wand at the wall and uttered a phrase in Latin, and obtained the same effect, that would be obvious mysticism.

              Same thing if the Crowned One caused someone to hallucinate by spiking their drink, or made their shoes quieter by applying some weird powder, or caused someone to become ill by hitting them with a smoke grenade. Those Instruments were all created using Alchemical principles, yet they conform to Consensus expectations more readily than the Hermetic's showy wizardry. It's the adage "Better Living Through Chemistry" put to practice, and modern people trust Chemistry as a Science.

              That's the reasoning from the book, anyway. The Solificato believes they are doing Science (or, rather, Natural Philosophy), and that it's their use of the right materials in the right proportions that makes the magick happen. Their tools serve them well in today's ideological climate...but that also makes them more likely to lean on them as a CRUTCH (not crux). They are dependent on outside resources for their magick, making it all the harder to let them go.

                          My go to distinction for the difference between Mystics and Technomancers is whether they internalize or externalize their magic. Or to put it another way, do they separate themselves from their magic or not?

                          Those who consider the Mage to be an intrinsic part of the magic fall on the Mystic side. This includes the Akashics who consider their forms and meditations to be a guide to real enlightenment, the Choristers who consider themselves to be one part of the conversation with the divine, and the Chakravanti who consider their proper place within the wheel to be what guides them. Even most Hermetics fall on this side because they consider an Enlightened Will to be a vital component of their otherwise highly formalized magic.

                          Those who think magic is a mostly independent matter of cause and effect are Technomancers. The mage may be special for being able to see and understand how and why these things work, but she doesn't make them work. By happenstance most of the mages who fall into this category are Etherites and VAs, since technology tends to be external by default. Some few Hermetics might as well, as would many members of Sorcerous groups who awakened to True Magic.

                          Separate a Technomancer from his tools and you deprive the mage of his magic. Separate a Mystic from his tools and you deprive his tools of theirs.

                                                          This, exactly.

                                                          It's why I like how M20 explained Focus better than how previous editions explained Paradigm and Foci. With Focus, the three elements - Paradigm, Practice, and Instruments - are given equal importance to how a mage does magic, and how they believe they do magic. It creates a, for lack of better word, Paradigm Shift in how players think about the subject. In this case, a mage's magic isn't just the tools they use, but the methodology behind those tools, and ultimately the worldview that makes that methodology possible in the first place. Even when a mage gets Enlightened enough to surpass their Instruments, that doesn't necessarily mean they've abandoned their Paradigm and Practice.

                                                          I've maintained before that characters with Arete 6+ are not required to reject their Paradigms to explain why they don't need the Instruments of their Practice. They come up with reasonable explanations for their increased mastery, based entirely within those Paradigms. A Hermetic master believes he's honed his True Will to an edge sharp enough to cut Reality into any shape he desires. A Chorister sees their mastery as a relationship with the One that is so solid, they need not make open and involved requests, because their very life is an active prayer. Even technomancers can do this with the right rationalization. The Virtual Adept understands the hacking process well enough that their minds do the work their computer systems once did. A Solificati arch master has truly become a Crowned One, her spirit having been transmuted to True Gold, and can alone act as a catalyst for changing the world around them.
