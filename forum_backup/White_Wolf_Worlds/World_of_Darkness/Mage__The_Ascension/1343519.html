    Social rolls against PCs

    10-24-2019, 08:24 PM
    Hi. I'd like to know how you guys deal with social skills against PCs. Of course someone (PC or NPC) that uses Mind 4 to possess a PC or a vampire that uses Dominate, is going to take control of said PC victim actions. But what if instead of directly taking control of their actions, a more subtle approach is taken. Do you allow social skill rolls like manipulation+subterfuge or any other roll (I think intimidation is allowed in almost all tables) that change the behavior of the PC and how you do it?

    I've seen people say that we should never mess with a PC "free will", but as I see, just like a Dominated PC, a persuaded one should follow the character and let himself be persuaded in the case of a successful rol against him.

    For the ones that answers negatively (as in, doesn't allow persuasion rolls against PC), what if a char uses magick to influence the outcome of the persuasion? For example a Mind mage that alters the PC's mood so he is more prone to accept suggestions. Or a Life mage the mess with hormones or, even better, simulates drugs that takes part of the free will of a person (scopolamine or some kind of anesthesia). Normally that would affect the rolls of the skill modifying the diff. But if you don't allow rolls against PC, how do you rule, for example, a PC using Life to drug another PC to try and manipulate him??

      I allow NPCs abd PCs to make social rolls against PCs, though the WoD mechanics are lacking a bit in some of the more recent design approaches to help make this go over more smoothly.

      If I'm playing with people a bit resistant to this, I use the following:

      If you go along with a social roll following the letter and spirit of the statement, regain a WP point.
      Following the spirit of the social roll to the best of your character's ability, including circumstances changing to make it reasonable to not follow ("hey can you sneak this into the club for me" when there's no obvious security, but then three guards step out to watch the door), has no benefit or cost.
      Following the letter of the roll but knowingly going against the spirit of it, or trying to subvert it in a significant fashion costs a WP point.

      It helps ensure mundane social rolls aren't viewed as mind-control, but there's incentive to go with the flow.

              This is something we ought to break down into several different categories, in order to keep wires from getting crossed and statements that apply to one thing not be applied to others. The way I see it, there are three distinct "levels" of manipulation that may be applied by NPC's to PC's.

              The first being the simplest, straight up controlling effects -- Mind, certain applications of Life, extending into other game systems Dominate, etc. These represent direct loss of character control, and thus player agency, and are almost always going to stem from supernatural abilities. Honestly, these are going to be the easiest to adjudicate; due to the loss of control over their character however temporarily, and generally handled in the most straightforward manner. No player likes losing that degree of agency over their character and won't be happy about it, so it's my personal policy to not piss on the player's head and tell them it's raining, but on the other hand this is the most sparingly-used layer.

              The second being more aggressive -- threats of violence or other consequences, browbeating or bullying, peer pressure, and the like. This would be typically mundane, but supernatural abilities that place characters in a certain state of mind would count as well. These actions compel characters or players to act in a certain way, but not necessarily forced in the sense the player loses complete agency. Here, it's best to use the old carrot and stick so the player complies, but of their own volition -- experience points for roleplaying manipulation well, mandatory Willpower expenditure to resist, or the looming threat of whatever consequences for resistance -- and certainly free to choose how they may comply.

              The third being almost-always mundane, but may be aided by supernatural abilities (such as using Mind to reduce the difficulty of a roll), efforts where the player retains full agency. This is where bargaining, appeals to mutual or self interest, and most deception attempts lie. Here, it's a "take it or leave it" scenario where the player decides for themselves how to react, and in these situations it's best to only incentivize a given course of action.

              Obviously, there's a spectrum here between supernatural and mundane actions, with less agency to the player the "more" supernatural the attempt to manipulate them. Some mundane things, like the influence of psychoactive drugs, being the exception, but realistically they follow the same scheme and spectrum and really don't need much discussion to their own. And of course, degrees of success and external considerations matter as well -- basic success on a supernatural controlling effect may give the player a bit of leeway, but dramatic success on a totally mundane persuasion attempt may push matters into the second, rather than third, category.

              The only major odd man out are attempts to deceive PC's, which can get really dirty, really fast because there's always that X-factor that, on no matter how good a roll the ST makes for the NPC, there'll always be some inconsistency, factoid, tell, or leap of logic players can draw upon to say "well it sounds good, but my character doesn't completely buy it". To really get to the heart of that statement, let's do a quick character study: Walter White from Breaking Bad.

              Walt's hands-down the shittiest liar on the show, but he's the best manipulator. I don't get how or why the show's viewers and fans have it in their head he's this amazing, world-class liar; he oversells and draws attention to his lies, and the best he's capable of is providing those who want to believe him an excuse to not think too hard about it. Which is the key point, whether targets are predisposed to believing the liar, and in terms of game mechanics that's not something directly controllable by a storyteller. Where Walt excels is weaponizing truth and the flaws of those around him.

                  Originally posted by Lord Revan View Post
                  That is a good rule, but I think the player will always try to find an excuse to try to fit into the no benefit, no cost situation.
                  That's not been my experience, but it's not really that bad of a result, since it means they're honoring the successful social roll against them, and in a way that affirms player agency instead of dismissing it (even if funneling them into a forced choice, but all player agency is constrained by forced choices on some level).

                  The WoD mundane social rules (as inconsistent as they are over multiple editions and multiple game lines) have a general commonality of "resolve the roll(s), and spend WP to ignore the results if you don't like being the loser because it's not mind-control." As such, players tend to feel like ignoring having their characters being good at resisting the initial roll is less important than having that WP, since that WP is the only way to exert agency despite it just being mundane social resolution.

                  The three tiered approach gives players more options. Given how valuable WP can be for mages in high stress situations, taking a free WP is usually good incentive to entice some level of accepting successful social rolls against them. As well, the "subvert" rather than, "ignore" language is important. Subverting the social roll made against you means you're still engaging with it, rather than the RAW encouraging you to just shut social rolls down completely.

                  And what if the PC was magickally druged to obey (Life 3 or Mind 2)?
                  As neither of these are direct mind control, I would simply apply the Magic Enhancing Abilities rules, and reduce the difficulty of mundane social rolls used after the PC is the target of such influence.

                  Also, what if the request is something that won't put the PC in risk, but it is something he would never do, sober at least, like imitating a chicken?
                  House rule 2: Have a standard table of difficulty modifiers to mundane social rolls, layered on top of the default difficulties (also consider modifying the default difficulties based on what the roll is actually about). Include standard traits (Nature/Demeanor, Essence, Resonance if using the Revised/M20 optional rules for that), faction philosophy, familiarity, and social preconceptions as all things that can be a positive or negative modifier based on situation and approach. A Technocrat is going to have an easier time bluffing about threatening a Tradition mage with scrutiny or worse, than a Technocrat is going to have convincing a Tradition mage that they mean no harm and have something of mutual benefit to offer.

                  This easily adjudicates in a more predictable fashion - and thus lets players build characters appropriately towards their intent - but never really makes you a social wall people can't get to; they just need to approach the right way (or use supernatural powers to smooth over the difference). If someone comes at your PC with a social attempt that ends up a diff 13 or 14, either it's going auto-fail or get bumped to the threshold rules... and good luck pulling that off. Even with magic helping (opposed to overt mind control) it's still something that requires some finesse instead of powering through.

                                      Ways you can use the social stats and rolls of NPCs, on PCs:

                                      - make it more about playing to the crowd; if the NPC gets 5 successes on an Expression roll to convince the PC to follow their plan, and the PC says no and demands they use their plan, then bystander NPCs (and perhaps PCs who feel 100% happy playing along) will call out the PC for rejecting such a brilliant plan. This applies to lots of situations - you're ALLOWED to storm out when your senior, a very commanding mage, tells you that you have to stay. His 5 successes don't force you to stay. But if you leave, those who observe this will think much more poorly of you.

                                      - place emphasis on the PCs keeping autonomy, but losing some of their effectiveness. Intimidation doesn't force you to flee, but it might give you a -2 dice pool modifier.

                                      - place emphasis on what the PC observes, rather than how they feel. Lying is a social roll. The player absolutely knows this NPC is lying, but you have to frame it as "they seem to be telling the truth, very convincingly" in an in-character sense. Because otherwise there's no point to the NPC rolling Manipulation + Subterfuge and the PC rolling Perception + Empathy. And then you can expect the PC to play it out as if what they were told was true, OR as if the NPC who lied to them believed what they were saying was true.

                                      - Same with pity, or flirtation, or being charmed... don't tell the PC that they feel pity, or that they're turned on, or that they feel charmed. Tell them what they observe, and perhaps highlight how an "average" person might respond. Lots of my players will respond with stuff like "oh yeah my gal is an idiot, she'd fall for that" or "well my guy doesn't have much experience with beautiful women and he'd probably be taken in by this, at least for now". On the other hand they might say "my character was raised by narcissists and wouldn't buy into a pity parade - she's seen this guy being a piece of shit, and she isn't buying what he's selling" or "the flirting is impressive, but they're REALLY barking up the wrong tree since my PC is utterly oblivious".

                                      So I think the bottom line is that it's easy to get this right with the right table dynamic, and a focus on how the character would respond. "How would you respond in character" breaks down ENTIRELY with bad players though, since their character always has to be right and always has to win. Thankfully I've only experienced that once and I have nothing like that at my current table.

                                                  I do pretty much the same thing as Heavy Arms in my games. NPCs can make Social rolls against PCs, and if the NPCs win, I give the players a stick-and-carrot incentive to abide by the roll results: if they go along with whatever the NPC is trying to persuade them into, they get a small amount of XP, or they can spend Willpower to not go along with the NPCs. If the NPC's roll beats theirs by enough successes, not going along may cost extra Willpower.

                                                  I also use these mechanics when two PCs are making Social rolls against one another.

                                                  My players have been pretty happy with it. It's not mind control, so they're still free to do what they want, but there is incentive to do what the NPCs want. I've seen some players take the XP and I've seen others spend Willpower to stick by their guns. It's really depended on what the context is and what's at stake.

                                                          This statement makes it significantly feel like you're not really reading the totality of my posts and just cherry picking stuff to disagree with.
                                                          That's not exactly the case. We've discussed crunch versus fluff enough for me to realize you're a crunch-heavy player and I'm not, and there are certain areas with which we simply will never see eye to eye. Ergo, I'm presenting an argument for managing social conflict through narrative as opposed to mechanics as a counter-position to your own. As I see it, either are valid depending on players' and ST's priorities and interests, and what is important is both positions are represented.

                                                          The inequality of weight here is in consequences and stakes...The WP costs might be the same, but there's a lot of other stuff going on too.
                                                          This is part of the issue -- which rolls, and the immediate impact thereof, are "worth" requiring players to spend Willpower to resist? The implication of this line of thought, is that player agency is suspended unless the player chooses to preserve it by Willpower expenditure, and that in itself requires a deeper examination of which social actions are capable of exerting that level of influence, and at the end of the day that's going to be a judgment call by the storyteller unless you do indeed treat all social actions as equal.

                                                          When we're talking about social actions, that's a huge net to throw, one large enough large enough blanket rules cannot and should not apply. The OP's implication is clear -- actions of sufficient assertiveness and consequence they may lead to degradation or outright loss of player agency. The reality is that's a fairly narrow sliver of potential actions that may fall under the "social" category, and we need to be careful about how and why we discuss this lest invoking the spectre of unintended consequences or disproportionate costs and benefits.

                                                          Having better social mechanics is one way to ensure this isn't the case, and one that the game can add to the table rather than everything being put on the GM. There being mechanics to follow helps assure players that social situations + dice = fair outcomes, not getting screwed over, because they know there's a process that the GM isn't deciding ad hoc and by fiat.
                                                          We're talking past each other on this point. You're discussing what is to happen once dice are in hand; I'm calling for a deeper examination into the thought processes that lead players or storytellers to reach for the dice in the first place. More to the point, if the storyteller is only reaching for the dice in circumstances that lead to loss of player agency, players are going to figure that out and react accordingly. The fix is to attenuate circumstances such the loss of agency is not the only possible outcome of a dice roll in the ST's favor, so to speak; the impact of that roll should be in proportion to the action that called for it.

                                                          In other words, if I call for a roll-off in the course of haggling between a PC and NPC, the result of the dice roll should determine that NPC's final offer. Final say as to whether that offer is accepted or refused should fall to the player, without consequence or incentive. That roll shouldn't have the same determinant payoff matrix as, say, an order to do something backed by a threat of physical violence.

                                                          My ideas are not meant to override the, "only roll when it it matters," general mentality of the game; nor have I asserted they should....this isn't a case where a roll would be called for in the first place...
                                                          This is at loggerheads with this statement,

                                                          ...because it's against the characters interest and thus worth rolling in the first place...
                                                          and this strikes to the heart of the matter. Opposed rolls in this situation arise when character (player or non-player) interests are in conflict, and the intent of the roll is to determine whose interest prevails. You previously argued yourself this is exactly why a roll should be called for, but amended your statement when I pointed out this really wouldn't be a situation in which dice needed to get involved by default. The reality is neither one of us are necessarily right or wrong -- the circumstances behind that action are what determine whether a roll by any involved party would be necessary, namely the NPC's intent in this example. Something I suspect you clued into, by arguing if the orders in my example were so detrimental to the PC they may be interpreted as invalid.

                                                          This is why "one size fits all" rules shouldn't apply. The storyteller needs to determine what degree of player agency is at stake based upon the totality of the circumstances behind any given roll, and adjudicate costs and benefits proportionate to that degree of lost agency. Because, as has just been demonstrated, even the act of reaching for the dice is already a judgment call.
