    The issue with the Technocracy vs. the Traditions is that the early Technocracy wants to control all of human advancement and shove it into a narrow box. Maybe even remove anyone Awakening but the way they want. But the latter Technocracy does want to do the Mass Ascension the same way as the Traditions. So it becomes a question of, "why are they fighting at all?"
    That's not actually true, though. Even in MRev, "the Trap" I mentioned before, the one that tends to capture intellectuals and that the Technocracy is emblematic of? It involves being so in love with the elegance of a model of what Reality is supposed to be like that when the model and Reality clash, the intellectual favors the model.

    In particular, when humans refuse to behave the way the Technocrat's models predict, the Techie then sets about trying to alter humanity to fit the model, "shov[ing] it in a narrow box." M20 Technocrats don't have anything that contradicts this, and a big part of what Technocracy Reloaded presents is an attitude of "the Masses have made a royal mess of everything; we need to save them from themselves!"

    As well, while most of the Conventions have a utopian ideal that they're striving for ("Mass Ascension", if you will), the various visions they have tend to be collective in nature, with humanity being reshaped to facilitate the vision. And "Mass Ascension" is a bit of a misnomer, because the visions involved tend not to include the Masses Ascending, or even Awakening: the Awakened are, by definition, "random elements" that tend to disrupt the Technocracy's models even when the Masses aren't. And everyone who Awakens is a potential Marauder or Nephandus. So pushing for anyone and everyone to Ascend is seen as the height of irresponsibility.

    So all the way through from 1e to M20, the Technocracy does Bad Things; the difference between them and Awakening's Seers of the Throne is that the Technocracy is able to couch its Bad Things in terms of doing it for the Greater Good.

    And, as I pointed out in an early thread that you started here, the Traditions also do Bad Things in service to a Greater Good; just not the same Bad Things that the Technocracy does. Even the Hollow Ones can get caught in a trap where they potentially end up pushing for anyone and everyone to Ascend, regardless of the consequences. (Yes, the Technocracy has a point that indiscriminate Awakenings will lead to more Marauders and Nephandi; and mages who don't take that into consideration are making the opposite error from the one being made by the Technocracy.

    Mage doesn't have any Good Guys, except perhaps the player characters.

                    Old mage made a point that the protagonists are young mages, and old mages are insane and lost - that's the aesop of Porthos: "power it's polish on blunt blades". Voormas was a chief example in that he doesn't want to die, mere agelessness ain't enough for him because he could still be killed (as an Euthanatoi, he probably killed many "immortals" himself) ... and he's so elite he can actually enforce this vision at a great cost to everyone.

                    It's part of the "punk" narrative ... "Technocracy exists because of people like you" sort of deal.

                    Ultimately, weter they're right or not, the Technocracy doesn't exist because of some nipicks of how magick "really works", and if what the Traditions do it's "insufficiently explained science", or they are different branches of a power to alter reality, but from the idea that magick - all of it - needs to be controlled.

                    Their point would be that the Consensus curbs the worst excesses of mad wizards at normal people, and forces monsters that can't "lay low" out of the planet, while their own safer style of magick remains unaffected, and can be shared more freely with sleepers. And the world it's a better place.

                    While at some level this it's undeniably true, the Traditions aren't entirely wrong. Not just because they could be able to produce alternative utopies, maybe?, but because this world has it's own unique horrors: Who needs fighting Dragons when you can fight swarms of killer Drones instead? - hence why Tech-guys are so angsty about people being people, and want to "well-intendedly" control everyone. Not that that makes them look any better.

                    In my opinion.

                    While the old way of seeing this as hippies vs. yuppies it's philosophically poor, our real world has made this whole message about shifting world views, thinking if "our way it's the only way", and "power" being wasted on evil or blind people, perhaps more important and surely not less.

                    *We accept now levels of surveillance and control that were distopic not many years ago - so much so that even "The Man" finds it oddly inconvinient (see how USA "chinese scare" about tiktok "spies" was completely uneffectual and laughed to - maybe if we weren't giving all our data to closer-to-home boogiemans all the time, would've more scared by it?). Yet the world doesn't feel that much safer.

                    *Man made weapons of different kinds can destroy humanity more readily than any natural plague or predator ever could (at least since we spread trough the world), but even if we agree that to use them in that capacity it's stupid, and nobody was threatening nobody with them, climate change appears to be in it's way to reset society anyway - we're already past many "points of no return" w/o so much as even globaly agreeing on trying to slow it. "The end of the world it's easier to imagine than the end of Capitalism", not in a small capacity because the "end of the world" has become rather easy to imagine - I think this *is* the intended use of the phrase, the point it's to imagine a better world not a worse one.

                    *Science has provided lives that are both longer and better, but there's a huge problem with *whom* can access these benefits. While plenty of people fight to make these more accessible, there's plenty of people wanting to terminate any sort of political intervention to make healt and stuff more accessible with all sort of elitist arguments about scarcity that ultimately boil down to that if you don't have a lot of money, you probably don't deserve to live that well in the first place.

                    All of this with the very enthusiast intervention of Tech-Bros fighting to have even more power than they already have, and spending in think-tanks whose only purpose it's to deny there could be a problem with any of this: Real life ideological warfare to ensure the goals and ideologies of those who pay can't be resisted.

                    If the idea that "tech-holds-all-the-answers" was ever up to any sort of debate...

                    And I think modern Mage can, or could, serve as a theater for this debate, questioning what's perceived as normal, and what that means to people with power, under the safer guise of punks fighting for unicorns and cyborgs.

                            Originally posted by DarkKing View Post
                            "Magic is Magic, just as Water is Water. It differs only in how it is used." - Solas, Dragon Age Inquisition.
                            Yet this is not Dragon Age and Magic definitely isn't that clear cut in Mage. Or WoD.

                            A few things have to be clear here:

                            1. Each Paradigm has one (or more) theories on why what they do work. Each also has one (or more) theories on why what the others do work. Full stop. Those theories don't have to agree that they're doing the same thing or that it comes from the same phenomena. It only explains what they see.

                            Corolary: their collective history made many of their thesis circulate among the main groups, so there's convergence and cross pollination, but not an actual common ground.

                            2. A lot of the concepts seen in the game are not canonical facts despite narrator presenting as such. Paradox, Paradigm, Consensus, and the very notion of Awakened Mages are actual Hermetic theory, sanitized for neutrality and sponsored as the basic framework of the Traditions.

                            Corollary: most Mages actually disagree with something in those theories, some disagree with a lot. Only the Order of Hermes really has a significant belief that they all do the same thing, because this is precisely their Paradigm.

                            3. Mages cannot follow skepticism or scientific method. This would require falsifiability, and by their own nature Mages can't be falsified. Every Mage is capable of producing evidence of their belief, not really proving it, but rendering the question itself moot.

                            Corollary: there's no possible distinction between what a Mage is and what they believe they are. If a Mage believes to be a shaman, no test will ever prove they're actually a Mage. The Traditions are united by historical recognition and a common cause, not by the belief they are the same thing, even if mist agree this belief is plausible.

                            4. Similarity is layered. Believing that they all change reality, or they all channel the One, is not believing their distinctions are just window dressing. They may believe in the methods and that different methods require different kinds of people. What it takes to do Magic as one Tradition isn't the same to do it as another.

                            Corollary: the distinction, not the similarity, is objectively correct. If each faction is actually a completely independent phenomena, they will still work exactly as they do, so the difference is real. And if they're indeed Willworkers, their reality-shaping beliefs cause the distinctions to exist, so the difference is real.

                            5. High Caps Magic is but one type of magic on the setting. Sorcerers are real, kinfolk ritualists are real, Gifts are real, Thaumaturgy is real, Hekau is real. The difference has observable precedents. Any Mage is indistinguishable from an extremely competent practitioner of such "lesser magics".

                            Corollary: Magick is one theory in a world with demonstrable alternative explanations. The rules may, indeed, be lying to you without breaking canon.

                            6. Paradox changes manifestation to match a Paradigm. Avatars do the same. And Seekings. Those actually contradict standard theories exactly in the few evidences of commonality.

                            Corollary: the very notion of Magick can only exist while making itself impossible to confirm, as it has to accommodate contradictions to itself at every turn. The one thing that unifies all Mages as a splat is unknowable if correct.

                                                        I would say that if you like the idea that all sorcery is downstream from mages, you can easily implement that in your games by focusing on mages as individuals rather than mages as represented by groups. Perhaps the single most genius move that M20 made was to separate Focus from allegiance: you no longer have to play an Etherite to play a mad scientist, or a Verbena to play a witch; you need only select Weird Science or Witchcraft as your Practice. And since, as if M20 Sorcerer, sorcery also uses those same Paradigms, Practices, and Instruments, sorcery is effectively “downstream” from Awakened Magick.

                                                        As for the thaumaturges featured in Hunters Hunted 2, the animists featured in Kinfolk: Unsung Heroes, and mediums featured in Ghost Hunters: while they are based in the metaphysics of Vampire, Werewolf, and Wraith, respectively, and they don't import the Focus system from Mage, that Focus system still allows you to make mages who are compatible with those other gamelines' metaphysics. And those thaumaturges, animists, and mediums can be considered to be “downstream” from those crossover-friendly mages, if you want to go there.

                                                        But to reinforce Heavy Arms' point, it's at least as valid to say that HH2 thaumaturges derive their power from Vampire metaphysics, that KUH animists derive their power from Werewolf metaphysics, that M20 Sorcerers derive their power from Mage metaphysics, and that GH mediums derive their power from Wraith metaphysics. And since Vampire, Werewolf, and Wraith metaphysics don't involve Mythic Threads, the associated thaumaturges, animists, and mediums wouldn't be the result of tapping into Mythic Threads; they'd be ordinary mortals who tap into the metaphysics of the other gamelines.



                                                        Personally, I'd like to see more diversity in the gameline-adjacent “mortals with supernatural power” idea. Changeling handles it through a system whereby some mortals have an ability to tap into the gameline's Arts and Realms; the “Year of the Ally” supplement for Wraith (The Quick and the Dead) had a Merits-and-Flaws based system for defining mediums; and the “Year of the Ally” supplement for Vampire (Ghouls) had a system built on ghouls being able to learn and use a limited selection of Disciplines. I wouldn't mind expanding on one or both of those concepts instead of the Hedge Paths system: allow mortals with the right supernatural Merits to learn and use a subset of Thaumaturgy or one of the other “Blood Sorceries” that vampires can access; allow appropriate mortals to learn and perform some Werewolf Rites; allow something downstream from Mage by giving some mortals the ability to learn and perform Focus-appropriate Rotes; and allow some appropriate mortals to learn and use an appropriate subset of Wraith's Arcanoi. Likewise with Mummy's Hekau and Demon's Lores. (Hunter has Bystanders; but almost by definition, they don't and can't have the Edges that the Imbued have. Just Supernatural Merits that give lesser versions of the innate powers that every Imbued gets.)

                                                        Supplement this by using Supernatural Merits and Flaws to represent mortal-specific powers and conditions related to that gameline's metaphysics: one or two “gateways” that define what it takes for mortals to access the gameline's usual powers, and a few other traits that define other things that the mortal can do or has to cope with that the gameline's usual protagonists don't. (See Changeling's system, particularly the Bestowments that the Enchanted can acquire, for an example that could be treated as gameline-specific mortal-only Supernatural Merits; and see the aforementioned The Quick and the Dead for a similar system that actually manifests as Merits and Flaws.)

                                                        If this concept were to get implemented, Numina would either go away, or they would stick around as something that isn't downstream from any of the gamelines. Path Magic would be completely disconnected from every gameline, including Mage; Psychic Phenomena would be their own thing, as would True Faith. (Though in the case of True Faith, I can see ways either to emulate it or to your it to a specific gameline: Mage could do something like True Faith through a mage who Practices Faith and has an Avatar that's willing and able to perform Magick on the mage's behalf, along the lines of Wild Talent; Hunter has a Dark Ages: Inquisition counterpart that explicitly uses Hunter-like systems to build a substitute for True Faith; Mummy: the Resurrection's Balance is stated to be of the same broad nature as True Faith, which might allow a mortal who has both True Faith and a Merit granting access to Hekau to use their True Faith as of it was Balance any time Balance figures into how Hekau work. And finally, Demon assigns a Faith rating to mortals, though it operates in a completely different way than True Faith does.)
