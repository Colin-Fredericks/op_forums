    To preface, I've not read M20, so I'm not all all familiar with it's mechanical changes. And I've been using house-rule tweaks for so long (like at least a decade) that I'd have to go back and reread a lot of 2nd and Rev edition stuff to even remember it's specifics.

    That said, I was never a huge fan of DA:M for a number of reasons. For one, I found it sort of odd that a time period in which magic was supposed to be easier seemed to have a more limited magic system (at least that was how the Pillars appeared to me on reading, and I never got around to using them in order to see if this impression was objectively true or not, so I may be completely wrong on that one). And, since for the sake of my own sanity (one can only hear "so why don't elder vampires remember the world being flat?" or something similar before wishing to fling angry ferrets at people), had decided that the Spheres are an objective metaphysical system for how reality warping via changing the Tapestry that underpins everything works and that the people's beliefs impact what they will accept seeing without summoning some sort of mystical backlash. And the Awakened have known this since the days of Atlantis/Meru/Before the Flood/Coming up from the 4th World/whatever. So, when I would run a game in the medieval period/Dark Ages, I'd still use the Spheres, though with some name changes to reflect period concepts (Mind being Soul, for example).

    The other was that the Pillar system made it annoyingly difficult to have Dark Ages Mage characters go anywhere outside Europe or the Mediterranean without having to come up with entire new systems, or to allow someone to play a character from India, Africa, China or whatever. Especially frustrating when one of your campaign ideas is to have a group of proto-Euthanics traveling together as a lead up to the first big gathering of the Tradition.

    I also admit to never being a big fan of most of the Fellowships. My preferred set up is to have a number of groups who are either stands ins for or prototypes of the modern Traditions - the Subtle Ones (whom no one really associates with the Muslims because they're just that subtle), the Messianic Voices of Mercy, the Gardeners of the Tree of Life, the Memento Mori, the Craft Masons, the Keepers of the Sacred Flame, and the Sacred Passion - with the Order of Hermes being a stand alone thing (bordering on being the stand in for the Technocracy of the period as they carry out their crusade against the Tremere and vampires in general) and the Sacred Order of Pure Thought, with their Illuminated Inquisition, being an outright enemy of everyone.

    I would say more, but real life is intruding, so hopefully I can pick this up later.

            There's this concept in gaming called a "Build". In RPGs - video game or tabletop - where the player runs a character that grows over time, it's expected that you can't do all the things you might want to do from the word go. Not unless you're starting from a higher level or more experience. If you don't do that - the default assumption in most RPGs, because starting weak and growing strong is half the point - you have to build up to the full concept you want. And that is not a failing of the Sphere or Pillar systems, but a feature of Role-Playing Games as a whole. If I'm playing D&D, I have to wait until my Wizard can get the Polymorph spell, before he can start turning people into toads. If I'm playing Dark Souls, I have to run through all the areas, grinding soul levels and finding all the pieces of equipment I need to realize the concept I'm going for.

            And if I'm playing Mage: The Ascension as a necromancer, I need to reach a certain threshold in Arete and Spheres before I can start diving into the Shadowlands. You know, unless I spend Background dots and Freebie points on a Wonder that does it for me. Or take the Deathwalker Merit from Euthanatos Revised, and cross over with just Spirit 3.                                         (Just jumping in because this paragraph, in particular, struck me as missing a fundamental perspective that Aleph -- and I, and others in threads -- having been making for a long time... well, long time for me, anyway -- lol -- I shouldn't speak for anyone else in that regard...)

                                        It isn't about whether the magic(k) in question is more "powerful" -- the mages in DA aren't, overall, more powerful than many mages in Ascension are, in several contexts -- but rather that the mechanics and metaphysics of *how* the Pillars (or Minor Spheres, or whatever term one wants to use, really) more adeptly reflect the (P)aradigm of a given mage more directly than the Spheres do.

                                        To continue with Aleph's much further up example, and cross-compare it via applied-paradigm: a given necromancer, under Ascension/The Sphere Paradigm(tm), would need Spirit:3, Life:2 and Entropy:4 to project into the Shadowlands/further into the Low Umbra. And as far as that goes, that's all fine and good, in-and-of-itself. The problem is, that metaphysical point is off-paradigm for the necromancer in question, and the requirements -- as delineated -- are a bias of applied metaphysical paradigm vis-a-vis The Spheres. In DA:M, The Messianic Voices and The Old Faith witches can "descend" into the underworld (physically or by dream-walking) more easily, metaphysically-speaking, than implied/indicated by The Spheres (and this is largely supported via Real Life(tm) metaphysical beliefs... regardless, of course, of whether said beliefs are "true" or not, which is a completely different discussion). The MVs "only" require Uri-El:3 to do so, and the OF witches "only" require Winter:3, as that particular metaphysical application is *simpler*/*easier* in by dint of their Paradigm(tm) than as-indicated in the Sphere System(tm). And the mechanics/metaphysics reflect that.

                                        To be fair and also present a counter-point on how Paradigm(tm) can work in the opposite way relative to the Spheres, let's take the OF and, say, teleportation (as understood within a modern context/comprehension). Alone, none of their Pillars (which many don't even define or perceive as such, but again that's another discussion) can simulate teleportation as "easily" as the Sphere System(tm) does -- and that's more-or-less as it should be, as many/most translocative legends that are umbrella'd under the OF are more often better simulated by "stepping sideways" (Spring:4, Autumn:3 or Winter:3 for the OF, depending on their need and/or methodology) and "reappearing" elsewhere some moments/time later. For them to effect teleportation as understood today, they would use Spring:4 (to wrench the barriers between worlds as well as create the "possibility" of "here" and "there" not being so far apart), Summer:2/3 (to create/propel "motion" between "here" and "there", with the level dependent on if they had company or not) and either Autumn:3 (to further transform, albeit only briefly, the essence of the witch into something more easily transported -- i.e. ephemerized -- as well as "grasp"/sustain their sense of self (Pattern, in the modern lingo) ) or Winter:3 (which uses the Low Penumbra/Shadowlands vs. the Penumbra of the Middle Umbra in Autumn). The flexibleness of the OF is sustained in that regard (having a tail-end option and different -- if potentially dangerous -- variation of how to do it), but it also shows how, for them, the concept in question *isn't* as easy as it is for, say, a given Batini (Al-Hajj:3/4, which is almost line-for-line Correspondence:3/4) or a Valdaermen (Fara:4), for both of whom such magic(k) is very "in-house", paradigm-wise. (It also matches, again cross-comparatively, with how the Heremetics would effect proper teleportation, but for them it would be Primus:4, Vires:3/4 and Corona:1-4, the later part dependent on salient factors and explained in another thread.)

                                        The above counter-example shows better the idea, properly, of how a given Sphere/set of Spheres (as indicated since almost Day One in Mage) can accomplish "x" via different means. ("The Lady Into A Tiger" example in 2nd edition is misunderstood by many as an example: it's not simply showing how different dots can accomplish a variation on "x" theme, but rather even more so showing how to think about "x" -- and why you're even *thinking* about "x" -- in the first place. I'll elaborate on that if/when a rebuttal arises, but my posts get notoriously long as-is, so I'll let this float for the time being....) It's the same concept being presented in/by the Minor Spheres and/or Pillars (or any other name one would want to give them). Yes: the Sphere System(tm) is good at delineating out most (not all, but most) metaphysical concepts in a relatively neat, orderly manner as well as included some concepts (and applied-concepts) that some people literally may never have even thought of (on a player level). However, the flip/other side of that is that the concepts (and "sub-concepts"/extra-concepts that *aren't* delineated in the same manner as the things in the Spheres are... such as Aleph's example of needing Spirit:3/Life:2/Entropy:4 to accomplish something that is more directly mirrored by an 'x" level:3-4 in a different Paradigm(tm) configuration) *are* delineated "x, y, z, a,b,c..." in a way that *doesn't* always reflect the (P)aradigm(s) in question. Some things, for some (P)aradigms, *won't* be as easy as laid out in The Sphere Paradigm(tm) -- teleportation, for example, which in Star Trek(tm) is simulated via Forces:3/5, Matter:2/4, Life:2/4, the barest breath of Correspondence:2 (and sometimes that's not required with pre-set coordinates), and Entropy:4 (see the variance again in the "Lady...Tiger" section). They most specifically and emphatically do *not* use Correspondence:4 to effect teleportation (as laid out, by comparative example, in TNG, when/where the distinction between spatial-warp translocation and how the transporters worked were specifically two different things). Downside: their teleportation required longer time to achieve/cement and has more involved aspects than for most other "mages". Upside: their methodology/applied-paradigm let's them do things via "teleportation" that Correspondence, alone, can't do (filter diseases via the transporter, deactivate mechanistic weapons, hold someone in suspended animation for protracted periods of time, etc and et al). Again, though, on the flip, some things are more readily/easily simulated via a given (P)aradigm than the Spheres show -- the further up travel to the Low Umbra, for example, among a great many examples.

                                        *****

                                        None of this is to say that the Spheres, as-presented, are "bad" or "wrong" -- I still run for plenty of people who use them for ease of convenience. It's more about how -- much like with the ongoing Frankenstein-system that is the various incarnations of DnD, wherein certain concepts are preserved "jus' 'cus" that's how they've always done it -- it's easy to only see one side of the coin when it comes to applying the concepts indicated by the Spheres. Yes -- you can simply use them as-is, and there's nothing wrong with that. However, to more properly "square the circle", the *other side* of the coin should also be considered: just as different Spheres can be combined to create "x", so too can the concepts laid out in the Spheres be themselves re-combined/perceived-and-interacted-with differently than what a given POV -- The Sphere Paradigm(tm), in this case -- demonstrates and shows. Minor Spheres and Pillars are much more accurate representations of how magic(k) in a truly belief-driven system would be more accurately reflected. Aleph's necromancer wouldn't -- and, more importantly, *shouldn't* -- be required to have access to abilities he otherwise *wouldn't* have (such as, for example, the various aspects of Entropy that his Paradigm *doesn't* include and/or account for), which both in-narratively and meta-physically doesn't make sense for him to have (again, relative to his essence and world-view) as well as is a grumpy-point relative to having to "buy points" in a game that you will literally never use. Minor Spheres and Pillars resolve that by having the aspects -- for both better and just as often for worse -- in question be worked out relatively closely to how magic(k) works for the mage vs. how it might work for someone else. You can easily have both systems running concurrently for different characters -- I have for many years now, both as an ST and as a player in others' games -- as each has it's own advantages and disadvantages: the Sphere System has the broadest metaphysical reach and potential (with the drawback of taking a great deal of time -- often multiple incarnations -- to attempt to master) while the Foundation/Pillar/Minor Sphere variants trade strong in-paradigm effects for greater difficulty simulating out-of-(P)aradigm effects (and some being flat-out impossible... though that, too, exists in Ascension's rules, so that's not as big a drawback/hurdle as it might appear at first).

According to the rote in the book, I need both Autumn and Spring for that. But why should I have to buy both Pillars just to explore my desire to build my own man-made forest?

                                                It's the same thing really. Aleph's necromancer doesn't have a Paradigm as expressed to us. He has a very weirdly applied aversion to one aspect of a relevant Sphere. It isn't actually a necessary sphere to the concept.                                                 Aleph has never shared his necromancer's paradigm, so I have no idea why his necromancer doesn't seem to incorporate anything involving fate or probability into his world view.                                                 And that's the thing, if you are creating a character using a particular system, with the deliberate intention of limiting that character from full use of that system, that's a problem with the player, not the system.                                                 The thing about the sphere system is not whether or not it's "Paradigm-Neutral", because that's honestly a ridiculous, meaningless label whether you're arguing for or against. So ultimately, Mage gives us a system that's designed to achieve the titular goal. I admire you for making a fun game experience by mixing systems, and that you've presumably been able to balance them very well, but it's not something that appeals to me. I'm honestly not even very interested in the Minor Spheres. I appreciate other folks' love for them, but they've never felt particularly necessary in my games.
