            I think some of it is a time and place thing. In the 90s in the US, there was a distinct subculture of "pop" paganism and witchcraft. There was just enough books, magazines, and so on out there to cobble together an idea of a synthesis of Western occult traditions that was cohesive across large parts of the US such that you could have shared conversations about it with people from wildly different areas and backgrounds. The film The Craft came out at about the same time as Outcasts did, and it's about four girls that pick up witchcraft from some library books and a visit to a new age shop (run by basically a Verbena who fails in her initial attempt to recruit them to teach them a more measured approach). While ultimately you could argue the film feels more like "Hollowers bad, join a real Tradition," the main characters are very Hollower-ish for most of the film. And as someone growing up in the US adjacent to plenty of people in on the neo-pagan subculture of the time... it felt like that could happen in Mage's setting in the 90s. The idea of stumbling your way into being a Hollower wasn't so far fetched.

            In 2025? Not really. Those in it for the rebel-fad have moved on, and those serious about it have developed far more detailed spiritual systems that has led to much more fragmentation as people have sought out to connect to less artificial occult practices (most of those people would be much more likely to resonate with the Verbena than the Hollowers... kinda like the movie predicted) than a bunch of teenagers finding the same small sets of books and writings sitting around in small bookstores (also something that's not really a thing in the US much either).

                While I can't say I know much of the phenomena, I'm generally aware of that.

                But for the purpose of how Mage works, and taking this movie as an example, it looks to me more like a group of Orphans that resonates with the Hollow Ones, and would be prime material for their "recruitment" (AKA hang around until they're recognized as members as far as it goes), than actual Hollow Ones.

                After all, a similar situation could happen with any other group. All Traditions, Crafts and Conventions are heavily based on ideas and cultural trappings that exist outside the groups. Someone could start out resonating a lot with the Hermetics, the Choristers, or the Dream Speakers, nearing their ideas and Paradigms, without having any real connection with standing members of those Traditions. A member of an ethnic minority could feel the pull to the traditions and magic of their people outside the direct influence of the respective Craft, and so on.

                I wouldn't personally call those people members of the respective group, however similar they already are, because at the end of the day this is about affiliation, not belief (despite how important belief is in the matter, especially back then).

                Outcasts adds the problem that it is, in theory, about characters that have no access to the main social support networks of their splats. They're cut off from any established group that can offer them the benefits a typical member can take for granted, they have trouble even with basic interactions (give or take local nuance and how worse Ronins have it), and even with basic information, being the more likely to be ignorant about basic aspects of their condition and/or what's going on.

                Hollow Ones may be underdogs in Mage society, but they're not that screwed up. They're in the know and they have the same access to basic instruction as anyone else. They know the jargon, the people, what's at stake. And it isn't as if Mage didn't have a better example of an Outcast. It has, actual Orphans.

                        In 1e, "Orphan" and "Hollow One" were synonymous. Outcasts was in the 1e era. 2e made the two distinct, though it still presented Hollowers as something that could easily be mistaken for Orphans if you weren't careful. MRev really leaned into defining what the Hollow Ones are, to the point that they got their own Tradition Book.

                        And then, as I've mentioned before, M20 redefined the Orphans so that the old stereotype of Orphans as makeshift mages was no longer true. In M20, an Orphan is merely a mage who hasn't aligned with any faction; she can have a Focus every bit as detailed as the most OCD Hermetic would have. And the key phrase that I'd use for the M20 Orphans would be "diversity of views." This is very distinct from the M20 Hollow Ones, who deliberately embrace a very specific approach to Magick: a blend of Chaos Magick and Gutter Magick.

                        I could see M5 defaulting to M20's Orphans; because again, their defining trait is a lack of membership in a group. A setting where the Council of Nine is gone and the Traditions have fallen apart and everyone identifies with approaches to Magick such as "devotee" or "magus" or "witch" instead of institutions still strikes me as viable. I can't see M5 defaulting to the Hollow Ones.
