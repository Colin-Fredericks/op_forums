            Right; but the devil is in the details. What does it mean for magic to be faith-based?

            As I mentioned in my earlier posts, I think the defining quality is that faith-based magic is performed in service to a higher power that is in some way personified and makes demands of the magic user's conduct. That's why you can't have, say, True Faith in "the Scientific Method" or anything else that's impersonal; and "True Faith" in an entity that simply doesn't care what the "Faithful" does undermines the single biggest downside of True Faith: that you can lose everything if you disappoint said entity. The nature of that entity isn't as important; as Astromancer points out, it doesn't even heaved to be monotheistic — though I suspect that someone with True Faith in "the gods of Olympus" would either not work (if the character is free to decide at any moment exactly which god they're serving, effectively treating the set as something that you can't really disappoint) or would be incredibly punishing (if the character doesn't get to choose who is going to be making demands). This, as opposed to having True Faith in one particular Olympian such as Zeus or Ares, where there will be demands on you but at least some consistency in terms of what those demands will be.

                                Ares and Mars (who yes, is also an agricultural deity and was generally far better liked by the Romans than Ares was by the Greeks) are two of the more disparate deities between the Greek and Roman pantheons. While there's enough overlap between the beliefs that they're frequently conflated, there are also a number of fairly significant differences (including fairly major entities that exist in one and not the other). Though it's hard to say how much those differences are truly representative across time and history since most of our most comprehensive sources tend to be from later periods so we can't entirely reproduce variations over the entire breadth of Greek religious history.

                                One of the reasons Athena tended to be more popular over Ares is that she could be said to be a goddess of warfare, while Ares could be said to be the god of the battlefield. Ares being depicted as being accompanied in battle by Eris, the goddess of chaos, and his sons Phobos and Deimos only heightens the perception of Ares as personifying the horrific sides of combat. The brutal reality of fighting to Athena's higher order strategies.

                                Mars was generally treated far more neutrally (and rarely with such terror inspiring company), and very much reflected the Roman dedication to victory via logistics and infrastructure. Roman soldiers were trained to be combat engineers, road builders, and were even expected to start farms to support distant fort-towns. Mars having a stronger duality of both the violence of war and the need to make sure soldiers are fed let him be far more popular. This is also reflected in Minerva, Athena's counterpart, having a different role as well. While still a war goddess in her own right, she blessed the rulers (Senate or Empire) who directed the war from her temple in Rome (privileged to sit on the Capitoline Hill with only Jupiter and Juno's temples) rather than get involved with more hands on affairs.

                                ---------------

                                Though the way the Greco-Roman deities reflected the different values of their peoples creates an interesting interaction with the concept of True Faith and some of the ancient Greek philosophies. The nature of their gods was intensely debated among the Greeks, and a prominent formulation was that each of them (or each of the original six Olympians, or the later twelve...) represented a virtue to live up to. Sort of like the Ten Commandments or the Noble Eightfold Path... but with personalities. From that theological perspective True Faith in the pantheon as a whole is a more coherent structure as such an adherent would be less concerned with the idea of interacting with the gods on a person-to-person level, and more concerned with working to "be in the favor" of each of them by living in accordance with the virtues in question. Show the bravery of Ares amidst stark conflict would be more important than trying to juggle cultic rituals between so many deities as one person.

                                                            Originally posted by Heavy Arms

                                                            Okay; but what does that mean? Hedge Paths are a Numina, too; and so are Psychic Phenomena. Saying "it's a Numina" only says that it's supernatural and that mortals can access it. It doesn't say anything about its nature.

                                                            Originally posted by Heavy Arms
                                                            Religions (good luck defining what that even means)
                                                            I wasn't meaning religion in a formal sense; but rather in the sense of the following contrast:
                                                            The main difference between magic and religion lies in their approach to the supernatural: religion involves faith, worship, and seeking a relationship with divine powers (supplication), while magic attempts to control or manipulate those powers for specific, often personal, outcomes (coercion). Religion emphasizes obedience, community, and moral codes, focusing on a broader worldview, whereas magic is more instrumental, technical, and focused on immediate, tangible results for the practitioner, often through secret or private rituals.

                                                            Religion
                                                            • Goal: To understand the universe, find purpose, and foster a connection with the divine.
                                                            • Method: Worship, prayer, devotion, faith, and adherence to moral codes.
                                                            • Relationship: Seeks to please or propitiate higher powers (submission).
                                                            • Community: Communal, building shared beliefs and institutions (churches).
                                                            • Scope: Often provides a complete worldview and long-term spiritual goals (afterlife).
                                                            Magic
                                                            • Goal: To achieve specific, immediate, and practical results (e.g., wealth, love, healing).
                                                            • Method: Technical acts, incantations, charms, and manipulating natural/supernatural forces.
                                                            • Relationship: Seeks to control or compel supernatural forces (domination).
                                                            • Community: Often private, individualistic, or with temporary ties to practitioners.
                                                            • Scope: Focuses on instrumental action and immediate, measurable effects.
                                                            Key Distinction: Control vs. Faith
                                                            • Magic: "I know the laws, so I can make the power do my bidding".
                                                            • Religion: "I have faith in the power, so I ask it to help me".
                                                            While distinct in theory, these practices often overlap, with some viewing magic as an archaic form of religion or a subset of it, and different cultures blurring these lines significantly.

                                                            Originally posted by Heavy Arms
                                                            Trying to have True Faith in being a Jedi, or The Power of Friendship, isn't impossible. It's just a lot harder because it's a more brittle belief structure without generations of adaptations and apologetics to help you get past all the surface level problems of putting so much personal stakes on such matters.
                                                            I am not saying that True Faith needs to conform to the views of an established, real-world religion. But at the same time, I don't see how one can reasonably argue that True Faith isn't religious. Different Faithful may disagree on what "the divine" is that they're trying to foster a connection with; but every last one has a notion of some higher authority that is to be respected and obeyed.

                                                            In particular, every version of True Faith that I've found in a World of Darkness book includes an element in it to the effect of there being some sort of moral code that the Faithful must adhere to or face consequences; usually, the consequences are phrased as "you lose your True Faith". The reason why True Faith is considered to be rare in the World of Darkness is because it's so easy to lose it.

                                                            That's why I distinguished "belief in the magic" vs. "belief in the morals": there's often a tendency when talking about True Faith to focus on what it does for you while neglecting the part about what it demands from you. While "belief in the morals" isn't precisely correct, it's closer to the truth than "belief in the magic" is, in that it acknowledges that True Faith involves some sort of worship and obedience of something greater than oneself, whereas "belief in magic" comes across as an acknowledgement of magic's existence.

                                                            With that said, I will agree that the line between magic and religion is fuzzy. I've said as much from my very first post in this topic, which is why I pointed out that it's entirely reasonable for the Storyteller to handle True Faith as Awakened Magick Focused through the Practice of Faith. This shouldn't be controversial; the revised Celestial Chorus Tradition Book says as much. But then there's the Faithless Flaw mentioned in Book of Secrets that Practitioners of Faith are vulnerable to but other mages aren't. Which brings me back to "religion demands worship and obedience; magic doesn't."

                                                            And this goes for the True Faith Merit, too: if your belief system doesn't include some sort of ethical framework that constrains your behavior, it isn't True Faith. ​
