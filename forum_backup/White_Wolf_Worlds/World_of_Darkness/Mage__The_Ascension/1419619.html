    Overwhelmed by Magic System

    10-22-2020, 02:59 PM
    I'm trying to get into Mage (from a Storyteller perspective) and just cannot make sense of the spellcasting system and how it works. I have an idea for a challenge that requires a magickal solution, but I cannot for the life of me make enough sense of the system to figure out how big the challenge is.

    The Set-Up: There is a library full of books. The library is underground. It's roughly 50' by 35', with about 15,000 books. A pipe burst above the library and damaged the ceiling, damaging all of the books. They've been sitting there for 53 years, rotting away.

    The Challenge: Restore the library to useable condition.

    After hours of pouring over the books and consulting the internet, I think this is a Matter ●●●, Time ●●● effect. Beyond that, I have no idea how to figure out how to cast the spell. Because it also seems that its     a Matter●●●, Time ●●● effect to repair one book, and the spell would have to be cast 15,000 times. How do you make it an area of effect spell? How do you determine the difficulty of the spell?

    This seems like it should be a fairly simple effect, and its giving me such a headache I'm wondering if I can actually run this system. It makes Vampire and Wraith look positively transparent.

      OK, this is why M20 specifies to use Process Based Determinism (PBD), where you seem to be coming at this from a Results Based Determinism (RBD) angle.

      PBD means you have to walk through what the magic is doing; aka the process. What is the magically actually doing to resolve the challenge? Are you rewinding time back 53 years for that room alone to back before the pipe burst? Are you looking back in time too what things used to look like, and then restoring things now? Etc. It doesn't have to be in super detail, but think about what the magic is doing not just what the final product is going to be. This makes Sphere assignments easier, and sometimes lets you break up a task into multiple smaller effects that will be easier to pull off instead of one big thing.

      RBD tries to assign Spheres based on what the impact of the effect is going to be, which as you're noticing, is much harder to reliably adjudicate.

      Generally speaking, from the way the game is designed, you're probably best off using two effects here rather than one: Looking into the past to see what things used to be like (Time 2 minimum, 3 if you want to go even faster, Mind 1 to create a picture perfect mental image of that much stuff and also process faster, and Correspondence 2 so you can see the room as a whole instead of treating each item in it separately, Matter 1 wouldn't hurt but unless there's a specific need to get the materials exactly correct in the modern time it's not going to make a difference), and then fixing it (which at that point really just needs Matter 2 and a lot of successes).

      You could also go the Entropy route, which would cut down on the number of different Spheres needed, but generally take higher levels.

      ----------

      Beyond that, in a practical sense:

      First, you need to set up a base difficulty for a magical effect in the form of a number of successes. Successes beyond that judge how big you can go. If you say that fixing a book is a relatively standard magical feat (it's more than a nifty little effect, but not anything spectacular) the base is 2 successes. Every success beyond the first two then goes into things like scale/area, number of targets, how well you restore the book, and so on.

                  Base difficulty is based on whether the effect is coincidental, vulgar without witnesses or vulgar with witnesses and based on the highest sphere used.

                  If it's Matter 3 and Time 3, the base difficulty is 6 for coincidental, 7 for vulgar without witnesses or 8 for vulgar with witnesses. Since you're talking a room which is 1750 square feet, or about the size of some good-sized homes, that's your area for the number of successes required.That's either a Mighty Feat or Outlandish feat, since it's less than blowing up a building or running a whole mob and fixing a whole room is probably more difficult than blowing up a few walls. Somewhere between 5 and 20 successes, most likely on the lower end of the scale, would be my call.

                  If you used Time 3 to reverse the non-accounting depreciation (in accounting it's a math thing) of the books, you're turning back time. I would raise the difficulty by 3 for that, making the difficulties 9, 10 and 11. The only way I see turning back time as coincidental would be in a Reality Zone where time works. Since those are uncommon and difficulty 10+ uses Threshold, I'd add in Threshold dice of (difficulty-9) for difficulties above 9.

                  For anything other than a short-lived sensory effect, you need 2 successes. As Heavy Arms has said, additional successes beyond the base go to area or to duration.

                  If you look at page 505, the Time magic table has a good answer for you: A 25'x25' area taken out of time or age to decrepitude is a 8-success feat. The next level up is 10-20 success feat. Time feats that don't affect personal perception are almost always vulgar.

                  25x25 area is about half of what you're fixing, so in one shot, that's about double the successes, or 16 successes. I'd probably make it 20 success+threshold successes to use Matter and Time to reverse the degradation and rebuild the library.

                      As for coincidental, vulgar without witnesses and vulgar with witnesses, you might find the subtle/uncanny/extreme ideas from Mage Made Easy to be helpful.

                      Think of how reality works in the area. If it's modern-day U.S., think of an action movie and what would make the audience go 'What?' If it's something that happens pretty commonly in genre and is set up and not overused (dodging one explosion is plausible but dodging 5 in a row gets believable), then it's subtle. Same difficulty as coincidental. Something which could happen in that movie but which is a stretch is uncanny; same difficulty as vulgar with witnesses. You know the car shouldn't make that jump, but it's a car chase movie and it's not completely ludicrous. If the car is doing things like safely dropping from orbit, then go with uncanny, which has the same math as vulgar with witnesses.

                      Things are less clear in Mage than other games because it's realm magic instead of linear magic with fixed spells. That's why I tend to go with 'how does your character want this to work?' It does mean it's hard to say for sure what each effect requires because there are many ways to do it. However, if you have Enlightened Grimoire or the older works, you get a good idea of the spheres required.

                          To some extent, YES. Difficulty it's set by Vulgarity and witnesses, but number of successes it's something that depends on many factors, including your perception of how migthy it's a given feat. Although, as has been stated by many, there are many good guidelines (and also some hard rules) about this in the books, so it's not entirely subjective.

                          Still, you will have to be prepared to make up stuff here sometimes. You may find it a blessing in disguise. The ruleset doesn't (arguably can't) cover everything a mage can imagine within the rather ample scope of the Spheres - and mage shenanigans can get pretty wild, especially if your players are very imaginative and/or knowledgeable. So this is the tool you as ST can use to make shenanigans easier or harder acording to your judgement.

                              The task may be made easier if we go with the option Heavy Arms suggested about using Time to look backwards to when the books were undamaged, and then Matter to repair them. I don't think you'd need as many Spheres as he suggested, though, just Time 2 + Matter 3 should be fine. I also don't think you really need to break the two functions into separate Effects, if you don't want to. One Effect can just look backwards in time and then force the books to conform to the states they were in before.

                              This method also sidesteps the need to reverse the flow of time, which as baakyocalder stated adds difficulty due to how extra unhappy Reality is about that kind of Effect.


                              If we're being generous, area of effect per success could increase exponentially, the ones on the upper range counting for "more" than the earlier ones. Your mileage may vary.


                              On the other hand, there's an argument to be made that restoring a damaged library of books is the kind of thing that would qualify for a long-term project. The kind of thing one or more Mages would be grinding away at over the course of months. In which case yes, you'd probably need to cast the restoration Effect on each and every book individually. It's like regular restoration work, except with magic.

                              Only do this if you don't mind it being a long term project, though.

                                      One way I'd consider running it is to decide how many dots of the library background it's worth in pristine condition, decide how many successes it would take to restore one dot of those, and then using the dividing successes rule add an extra "target" above the base amount of successes for each dot. So, if your library was a 10 dot library, They would need 10 successes above whatever is decided to fix 1 dot worth of it.

                                                  One last thing to consider in all this: Mage does not presume permanence. This is especially true of any sort of time rewind or summoning things from the past sort of spell. The game really doesn't explain this that well in where to draw the line, but rewinding something's time back to how it used to be runs a serious risk of the spell ending and everything reverting to how it was before you tried to fix things up.

                                                  One of the things that probably biased a few answers are people that are more used to Mage's somewhat wishy-washy guidelines for what sort of effects are considered done and you don't need to worry about duration, vs. ones that tend to cut off eventually.
