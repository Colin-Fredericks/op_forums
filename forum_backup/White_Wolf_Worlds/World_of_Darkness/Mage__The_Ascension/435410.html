      Pillars in the modern era are an interesting idea, but they run up against issues to do with modern paradigms being very loose. The Dark Ages mechanics have very strict paradigm limitations, because the setting has very strict paradigm limitations. That doesn't mean you can't do it, just that I find the Traditions as presented don't have a great deal of unity in their paradigms beyond the broadest outlines.

      Or perhaps a simpler matter. Keep the Sphere system but help me impose limitations on the Spheres for the various Traditions, help bring out what the various traditions are good at/bad at.
      I think you might be overcomplicating it. If you don't want a character to be good at something, don't give them the sphere levels. The Order of Hermes has a handful of master Forces users, and they're the Forces tradition.

      Also, remember that most Traditionalists are going to be played by the ST, so if you want the Order of Hermes to be unable to... I dunno... slow down time in combat. Then make it clear that though the players might find a way around that limitation, the NPCs can't.

                  Michael has a point in that one of the themes of the modern Ascension setting is that the Traditions are (supposedly) striving for a degree of unity. Porthos' Ambassador program, the cross-Tradition cabals and factions in Guide to the Traditions etc. are examples of this in action.                       There was a thread a bit ago about redesigning the Spheres. My idea was to basically add in 3 sub-Sphere level traits per Sphere (Ok it was bigger than that, but it's the part that matters to this) that allowed a bit more flavor/customization. The point of this was to keep the general Sphere structure and the cross-compatibility stuff, but have more Tradition/Splat flavor around. So a Verbena and an Etherite might have the same dots in Life, but the Verbena might have "Healing, Shapeshifting, and Plant Mastery," to reflect how they relate to Life and what kind of spells they're good at, while the Etherite might have, "Body Modification, Genetics, and Homeostatic Control."


                      I guess some of this is rather than looking at "limiting" mages into their Tradition/Splat paradigms, doing more of a carrot to encourage them to play to the strengths of their paradigm.

                                          Sphere system is excellent because it's one clear system that can be used in the background so to speak to represent all magic that is it gives us common mechanical ground to build upon while it is the individual Paradigms that limit and guide how the Spheres can be used. That is to say you should look at Spheres as framework of your magical might and the theoretical limits of what you would be able to do had you ascended Paradigms but always look at them trough the lens of your Paradigm you can only do things you can fit inside and explain through your Paradigm.

                                          Pillars are a fine method of enforcing Paradigmal thinking if your having trouble with it but essentially it is a crutch and you need one for each Paradigm out there, Orphans have their own unique and personal Pillars while each Craft, Tradition and even many intra-tradition factions have each their own though they may share aspects. This is the basic reason why the Sphere system is so brilliant it allows for all this with less work as you do not need to create a dozen or so unique Foundations and 30 or more individual Pillars to get an idea of general limits to the Character imposed by the system. It allows one system to represent all of the above with each player filling in the details on top of that common frame of reference.

                                              Possessed: I think that you aren't taking the next step in the process. While the Sphere system is, of course, very good at creating a list of metaphysical concepts that can give various players/STs ideas that they may never have thought of, the system is still, itself, just another paradigm. This wouldn't be an issue if, say, the general setting was one of the numerous, default DnD settings, where few characters (and even fewer players) actually go that deep into questioning why/how magic works for them/their world -- they just accept it, by-and-large, and get about their business. That's almost antithetical in terms of Mage, where paradigm is stressed left, right and center, and much of the fluff of the narratives involved is about the characters coming to question not only Reality(tm), their own beliefs, and the beliefs of others, but also coming to understand how said beliefs have a direct influence on how Reality(tm) works (and, in an equal fashion, vice-versa). On that level, even the most simple of meta-game discussions reveal the fundamental truths (and flaws) of the Sphere system: *plenty* of players back in the 90's/00's (and today, still) ask "why do I have to spend "x" points to get "y" up to "z" level, when in my character's paradigm, he only needs -- and, more to the point, only *does* -- "y" facets of a given Sphere, as described...?". This is exampled in the oft-brought up SoE mage who wants his teleportation platform (Correspondence:4 in Ascension), but doesn't use any of the other facets, at all, of Correspondence:1-3, and can cite in innumerable fictions how the facets described therein -- while metaphysically sound -- aren't employed in said cited fictions, yet "Correspondence:4" still occurs. And that, of course, is just the most *simple* of examples, as there are many, many more, but it drives home the point that while the Spheres give the ideas, the Pillars (or Lesser/Minor Spheres, per STG, or any of a number of terms one might use) actually do a much better job of expressing those ideas within the context of Paradigm, Affinity and applied belief.

                                              That is, a great many expressions of magical thought/praxis for a number of characters *don't* match up with the scaling/division of the Spheres, at all. At all. Some of the mystical spells/advanced scientific processes/mutant powers/psychic abilities/what-have-you for some characters *aren't* performed at the comparative level indicated by the Spheres: some are more difficult in a given context (requiring a higher level than listed), while others are comparatively simpler (working for the character as a level lower than the one cited, in some cases); some workings are not as complex for some (they don't require having a dozen dots spread among 4-6 Spheres to simulate an given effect, but only a few spread among one or two Pillars, or even just within a single Pillar, if the aspect is appropriately focused within the character's paradigm), while some workings are actually *more* complex (wherein a given effect might be worked per a single Sphere, as presented, but for the mage it requires 3-4 Pillars to simulate). *THAT* is why the Foundation/Pillar system is superior in that regard and actually does a better job of expressing the narratives of Mage than the Sphere system does, as presented: it leaves open a true expression of paradigm for any player who wants to make his mage's magic truly reflective of what he -- player or character -- thinks it should be. The scaling system is still balanced, as the ST would of course still be the final arbiter of what is or isn't allowable, and while the mage may be quite good at things that are strongly in-paradigm for him (aspects that would require an Ascension mage a number of dots in several Spheres to simulate only require the DA:M character a single Pillar), the balance of it all even on a rules level will come to the front as things that are more remote for him (not generally well expressed by his Pillars) either require more dots among Pillars than he may have (just as with Ascension vis-a-vis Spheres) and/or will have a Dif# into the teens, requiring him to use foci, sanctums, conjunctions, Quintessence, Willpower, et al, to a degree that truly makes the effort quite daunting, both by "fluff" and by "numerics" -- more so, readily, than again reflected by Ascension.

                                              Does it involve a little more work on everybody's parts? Sure. Of course it does. But it also leaves the door much more open for broad creativity and a more truly unique sense of style/flavor for the mages/characters in question. For cross-comparison purposes, any ST worth his salt should be able to recognize the Spheres/Arcana used to create "x" in a given Pillar, either overall or at a specific level, and said ST would also recognize when/where to balance out things if something seems inappropriately "heavy" or "light", with little more than a few moments glance over the Pillar work-ups. (Sidenote: same thing with the Foundations. If they're treated, say, as "Ranks" are for Garou -- in the sense of "Fostern", "Adren", etc., for getting the "gist" of it over for Werewolf players, for example -- then the scaling for that, as well, becomes quite clear, and the *fluff* involved to getting a rating of 4-5 will balance out relative to any XP spent to get there; much like with Humanity/Paths in the 8-10 range, or a Rank of Elder in WW, et al., it isn't something that is achieved, alone, by XP, but a considerable amount of role-playing, as well. That aspect, much like with getting an Arete rating of 6+ -- let alone 9-10, is something that is oft overlooked by many players and a perhaps disturbing number of STs, too....).

                                              >shrugs< But to each their own. For me and my friends, the Foundation/Pillar system is what Mage should have been about from the start. That is, the descriptors of the Spheres, as presented, would -- as they do -- make a fine example of gauging the relative "weight" of many metaphysical values, but in the end should be used as tools for making/expressing the mage's/character's magic, not the metaphysical paradigm that they are, as written. As stated further up, this does of course make things a little more complicated, but as expressed in Ascension concerning the Spheres -- "...once you get it, it's simpler than it sounds..." the seeming complexity of the Pillars are, in fact, much simpler than they appear, in terms of building/expressing.

                                              (DISCLAIMER: and, as always, when I use the terms "simple", and similar, it's always relative to playing a Mage game, regardless of the specific system being used: the game/setting has never, ever been for the "faint of heart", as it were, in terms of comprehending metaphysical concepts and "thinking outside the box". All of this is appropriately "geek-level" in terms of discourse, and has been since Day One. But even in that context, a little mental exercise goes a long way to having it all work out... )
