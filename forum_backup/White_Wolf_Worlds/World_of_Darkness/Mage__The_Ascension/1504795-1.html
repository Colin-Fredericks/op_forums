        Space 1889, especially Conklin's Atlas of the Worlds (the only sourcebook from that game I still have, strangely), would be an influence. I'd have to post more later, but part of my headcanon for Mage in general is that each of the planets has multiple umbral reflections and attached pocket realms based around things like Mage metaphysics and mundane mythology/beliefs. So Mars, for example, has the real physical Mars, the Forces realm(s), the Court of the War Gods, and - importantly for this - "Forgotten Mars" that looks a lot like preWW2 fiction (including War of the Worlds and John Carter; the first chapter of volume 2 of Alan Moore's League of Extraordinary Gentlemen is a potential example). Modern day would probably include a second such realm based on post War sci fi (Martian Chronicles, Stranger in a Strange Land, Total Recall, etc.), with the barrier between the two getting thinner. Similar realms would exist for the Moon and Venus, and these probably all tie to the Dreaming if you want to work in some kind of Changeling cross-over, especially with Knocker æthernauts.

        As for the Void Engineers, I have to find my notes, but my general take is that the modern iteration are less explorers than they are paranoid obsessives convinced that the Earth and humanity are under constant threat from alien invasion (Wells's Martians, Lovecraft's Mi-go, Heinlein's puppet masters, and things like Village of the Damned, Alien, Invasion of the Body Snatchers, The Thing, The Astronaut's Wife, Virus, Event Horizon, The Mist, and Annihilation, among others). That is just the 20th century version though (and owes a lot to 90s cultural zeitgeist). While there is a thread of that paranoia in the Victorian era, it is much lesser. I think there probably was (or should have been) a War of the Worlds type incident at the turn of the century that precipitated this shift. Something they may have even kept secret from the other Conventions for fear they've been compromised. But the Victorians would still have a more colonial imperialism feel to it, with this grand mission of going out and civilizing Mars and Venus the same way the various empires think they are with Africa.

                    As an aside, I have the Sphere Realms slightly altered as far as which planet they are connected to. This is mainly because Uranus, Neptune, and Pluto are all relatively recent discoveries.

                    Sol/The Sun - Prime
                    Mercury - Connection
                    Venus - Life
                    Earth - Spirit
                    Luna/The Moon - Mind
                    Mars - Forces
                    The destroyed Phaeton/Astroid Belt - Fate
                    Jupiter - Matter
                    Saturn - Time

                    Uranus and Neptune are often viewed by the Awakened as two halves of an Infinity Loop, possibly tied to either a theoretical tenth Sphere or Ascension itself.

                    Pluto is, among other things, a gateway to places beyond Creation and the Tapestry, where the beings the Nephandi serve appear to originate.

                    There was (and perhaps still is) a "Lost Vulcan" in the Umbra, but many believe it has become Iteration X's Autochthonia.

                    Several of the solar system's moons also have their own unique spirit-scapes, such as Mars's moons being a gateway to something similar to the Supernal Realm of Pandemonium from Mage the Awakening, a place where visitors are forced to confront their own fears. The spirit-scapes of the moons of Jupiter and Saturn are weird and diverse, which is probably why Ethernauts spend so much time exploring them in something akin to pulp science fiction and space operas.

                        So, a theoretical alternate Mage would have initially introduced the concept of The Invisible World, aka the Umbra or Spirit World, and the Spirits which lurk throughout the World of Darkness, usually just on the other side of the Gauntlet but sometimes working their way across into the Material World. These would include Elementals, Ghosts, Demons, Conceptuals, Paradox Spirits, and other types. That would all be in the core book.
                        The book that comes with the Storytellers Screen (Secrets of Magic) would have started introducing the wider world of the Umbra/Otherworlds and what was out there for Mages to do. (Also the Digital Web, which in 1993 would've been all the New Hotness.)

                        The Skyvaults (aka as The Aetherial Realms) - The gateway to the Planetary Realms, as well as various astrological related places (realms reflecting the various Zodiac signs and other constellations, for example), ancient prophetic wisdom and other secrets.

                        The Halls of the Elemental Courts - The four courts of the great Elemental Lords (Air, Fire, Earth, and Water), each with its own unique environments, customs, etiquette, and mysteries. There is an apocryphal fifth court of Aether (or perhaps the Void).

                        The Babylon Metropolis - The spiritual ur-city, a reflection of every human city past, present, and future, jumbled together into a single chaotic maze of streets, buildings, and denizens. The cyberpunk streets of Blade Runner butt up against the fog enshrouded Victorian London, Cleopatra's Alexandria, Medieval Edo, and modern Mumbai.

                        The Hollow Earth - A seaming pocket dimension within the Earth itself. Effectively an organic Dyson sphere surrounding an internal sun, populated by lost civilizations, strange megafauna, and unexplainable ruins.

                        The Thousand Hells - Seemingly countless realms of doom and despair, reflecting every culture on earth, even those apparently long forgotten. Home to various devils and demons, these realms range from classic infernos and dark voids to Baroque torture chambers and post modern prisons.

                        The Seasonal Realms - A quartet of archetypical and idealized reflections of Spring, Summer, Autumn, and Winter. Each has its own denizens (including what seem to be faerie courts), places of power, and hidden secrets. The realms sometimes shift to reflect the cultural expectations of the visitors, be it European, Native American or Asian. There are also believed to be a similar set of Realms reflecting the Wet and Dry seasons of the tropics.

                        The Dreamlands - A vast collection of astral realms reflecting the sleeping minds of individual humanity. Some of these dreams are strong enough to become permanent realms, or even merge into larger reflections of cultural zeitgeists such as Ye Olde England or the Gernsback Continuum.

                        The Mythworlds - A land of adventures and miracles born out of all human stories, reflecting humanity's collective hopes and fears. Many visitors report never finding it the same as when they last left it, but this may be as much because of its vast size as it is its mutability. Various gods, angels, and other beings can be found here.

                        And all of this would've been vastly expanded upon in a book about the Otherworlds, with entire chapters on the Planetary Realms, the Astral Planes, the Heavens & Hells, and the Furthest Depths.

                            Cultural Zeitgeists within the Dreamlands

                            The Dreamlands, where essentially Mage's Astral Realms meets Changeling's Dreaming. Born from the sleeping (and sometimes daydreaming) minds of humanity, most of these last for a few seconds to a few hours before fading like mist in the morning sun. But some have staying power, being revisited so often that they gain a sort of permanence. And some are so similar that they might merge into a larger and more stable realm. The largest and most consistent of the Dreamlands are the Zeitgeists, realms that reflect widespread cultural aspirations, historical nostalgia, and popular fictions.

                            Zeitgeists by their very nature paint with incredibly broad strokes. They reflect a wide range of imagination concerning their topic. While some of these might include historical accuracy or other forms of realism, escapism and wish fulfillment are as much - if not more - common. When a Mage (or Changeling, astral projector, or other traveler) first enters a Zeitgeist, it can be comparable to entering a theme park attraction or film. Everything tends to be idealized and enjoyable. Possibly even a bit sanitized and safe. But the longer one spends in a Zeitgeist, the more the shine tends to wear away. Less pleasant aspects begin to crop up, eventually with many of the subject period's neuroses coming into full display. Sometimes a Zeitgeist will devolve into a total nightmare. Those who repeatedly visit certain realms report that they tend to reset themselves, reverting to their idealized state upon reentry.

                            [If you want a really good example of what I'm getting at, see the 1998 film Pleasentville. Also the 1974 film Westworld. ]

                            The Gernsback Continuum
                            A world of the Pulp and Golden Age science fiction. Flying cars and zeppelins fill the skies alongside Art Deco skyscrapers. A "ray-gun gothic" mash up of original Buck Rogers and Flash Gordon comics, Fritz Lang's Metropolis, and Doc Savage stories. The realm is frequently visited by Etherites. As one spends more time in the Continuum, they come across some of the less pleasant aspects of period politics (especially various strains of socialism, fascism, and communism), racist or sexist individuals, and corrupt business practices. Eventually, one may have to deal with inhuman Aryans or Things From Beyond the Stars.
                            (While period sci-fi provides good inspiration, I'd also recommend things like the Gernsback alternate world from GURPS Alternate Earths 1, William Gibson's "Gernsback Continuum" short story which was apparently Bill Bridges's original inspiration for the realm, and perhaps the 2004 film Sky Captain and the World of Tomorrow.)

                            Hollywoodland
                            The golden age of Hollywood under the old studio system. Perfect Southern California weather. Glitz. Glamour. Fame. Fortune. At least one the surface. Time seems fluid, with the late 20s and 30s flowing into the 50s and early 60s without anyone really noticing. Classic stars litter the place. Studio back lots are constantly bustling and the Brown Derby is still there. But when you dig behind the scenes, you start to find the less pleasant aspects of the business. Back room deals, abusive contracts, vice, degeneracy. Sometimes even murder, Satan worship, and worse.
                            (See 1952's Singin' in the Rain, 2011's The Artist, 2016's Hail Caesar!, 2006's Hollywoodland, and 1999's RKO 281, as well as things like LA Confidential and Mulholland Falls.)

                                A few more.

                                Bakuhan - The Tokugawa Shogunate of Japan's Edo Period. A feudal land of samurai, geisha, monks, and merchants, full of castles, shrines, and mysterious forests. Modern visitors often compare it to being in a historical drama. While generally peaceful and prosperous, with a great deal of classic art and culture, it eventually becomes apparent that the society is built upon a rigid class system, strict legal codes, and a samurai class constantly in debt. After a time, visitors seem unable to escape encounters with political backstabbing, brutal executions, and peasant revolts.
                                See various Japanese media such as 1974's The Last Samurai (not the Tom Cruise film), 1978's The Fall of Ako Castle, Lone Wolf & Cub, The Kouga Ninja Scrolls, among others, as well as Laura Joh Rowland's Sano Ichirō series of historical murder mysteries.

                                Ye Olde England - Medieval England, in a form likely to make medievalists cry. Visitors find themselves in something that feels akin to a Renaissance fair or a Medieval Times excursion. Knights, damsels, troubadours and the like abound. The King seems to vary, often with John Lackland or Richard the Lionhearted being common. Encounters with Robin Hood are also frequent occurrences. As time passes, some of the sheen tends to wear off, becoming a bit more realistic. Brutish knights and rebellious peasants pop up. Curiously, those who have spent the longest amount of time in the realm report encounters with vampires.
                                See the various adaptions of Ivanhoe (especially the 1952 version) and Robin Hood. Also the original Westworld film, the 1964 film Becket, 1968's The Lion in Winter, and Ken Follett's The Pillars of the Earth.
                                (Obviously, similar zeitgeists exist reflecting the Middle Ages of parts of France, Germany, Spain, and other parts of Europe. The one for Poland seems to have grown in size and stability lately.)

                                Libertatia - The mythical pirate paradise and the Golden Age of Piracy. Multiethnic crews sail the Spanish Main in search of treasure and adventure while living under the Pirate Code. The main port of call appears to be Nassau, but others seems to exist. Many describe it as a mix of Robert Lewis Stevenson's Treasure Island and a popular theme park ride. Famous pirates from different eras appear with little rhyme or reason. The longer one stays, however, the less pleasant the surroundings become. Sea sickness and disease start to crop up. The locals become more violent. Anti pirate fleets become more common. The existence of the slave trade becomes unignorable.
                                Obviously, the Pirates of the Caribbean films are a big influence here, as well as the various versions of Treasure Island (including its prequel series Black Sails). Also a lot of the classic Hollywood films like Captain Blood, Against All Flags, The Golden Hawk, and The Sea Hawk. Also 1995's Cutthroat Island, which is not terrible.

                                The Wild West - America's frontier. Or at least a pop culture version of it. One typically finds a small but quickly growing town surrounded by small farms or ranches, perhaps a silver or gold mine, maybe a railroad camp or an Army fort. The stage coaches run regularly. Saloons seem to compete with churches for attendance. A new school has usually opened. Gunfights happen on occasion. If one travels far enough from town, you'll likely find another similar town. Or perhaps one smaller. As per usually, the less savory aspects begin to appear. Casual racism (especially toward blacks or Chinese), prostitution, snake oil salesmen, violent temperance movement activists, bandits, and conflicts with native inhabitants.
                                The list of influences here are almost too numerous to mention. When one first arrives, one may be reminded of the films of Roy Rogers or John Wayne, among others. (Back to the Future III is also pretty good.) Later, one may find Clint Eastwood films (Pale Rider and The Outlaw Josie Wales, as well as Unforgiven) more suitable. And eventually HBO's Deadwood. (I'll also through in Blazing Saddles, just because it has always been one of my favorite films.) And 1993's Posse.

                                Countless others exist, including Madinat Alsalam (Baghdad of the Golden Age of Islam flavored by the folk tails of 1001 Arabian Nights), Beaverton (the small town suburban world of black and white era American television), Caesarea (Ancient Rome as it transitions from Republic to Empire), Dystopia (a mashup of dystopian sci fi futures, mostly from the 1970s, but with a few more modern influences as well), Mannheim (Viking age Scandinavia tinged with Nordic mythology), Magnolia (the Antebellum South), Nevermore (a Gothic mid-19th century Eastern US coast), Cyber City (urban cyberpunk), and L'Ancient Régime (pre-Revolutionary France).

                                (If anyone, especially anyone from other countries, wants to suggest more, I eagerly welcome them.)

                                    (Fleshed out the above Zeitgeists if anyone is interested.)

                                    A book that never was, Witchhunters: A sourcebook on the enemies of the Awakened.

                                    [This would've been part of a theme for all the WoD lines, with Nightstalkers (Vampire), Wolfsbane (Werewolf), The Quick & The Dead (Wraith), Grim Fears (Changeling), Wolves at the Door (Dark Ages), Gods & Monsters (Savage Rome), and Shadows in the Fog (Victorian Twilight).]

                                    Chapter One: The Natural World
                                    Rules for weather, including damage from tornadoes, lightening strikes, hail, high winds, tidal waves, earthquakes, avalanches, mud slides, and forest fires.
                                    Rules for exposure, including dehydration and starvation, drowning and suffocation, heatstroke, hypothermia, and frostbite, and natural poisons.
                                    Rules for sickness and disease.
                                    Animal life, including Insect/Vermin Swarms, Poisonous animals, Apex Predators (wolves and canines, big cats, crocodilians, sharks), Trained Animals (attack dogs and hunting falcons).

                                    Human Threats
                                    The Nosey and The Clueless, including bothersome neighbors (meddling kids, neighborhood gossips and snoops), the gullible (true believers, cult dupes, amateur occultists), know it alls (doctors, bureaucrats, debunkers), investigators (reporters, hackers, parapsychologists), and the Arcanum.
                                    The Mad, including insane asylums and the danger of being committed, and dangerous individuals (stalkers, violent schizophrenics, and serial killers).
                                    The Criminal Underworld, including street crime (dealers, gang bangers), specialists (forgers, smugglers), organized crime (the Mafia and other organizations, and their foot soldiers), and international terrorism.
                                    The Law, including the local police (beat cops, detectives, narcs, SWAT teams) and federal agencies (including intelligence agencies, the military, and various Twilight Agencies).
                                    The Church, including various Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Buddhist, and Hindu denominations, their priests, exorcists, and possible fanatics, as well as the Society of Leopold and other faith based hunter groups.

                                    Chapter Two: The Invisible World
                                    The hierarchy of spirits, from the weakest fragments and minions to powerful Incarnae and Celestines, as well as powers and weaknesses.
                                    Nature Spirits, including Elementals and their courts, the Courts of Beast Kings and animal spirits, and the Aetherial Courts of the heavens.
                                    Conceptual Spirits, including those associated with Dynamism, Order, and Entropy, and various Epiphanies and Abstracts.
                                    Mythical Spirits including gods, angels, demons, ghosts and faeries.
                                    Paradox Spirits
                                    Things from Beyond Reality that the Nephandi associate with and serve.
                                    The Possessed, both the spirit-ridden and the spirit-claimed, and the powers they develop.
                                    Chapter Three: Awakened Foes
                                    Rival and Rogue Mages, including false friends, out of control Orphans, loose canons of the Ascension War, ruthless ladder climbers, Rogue mercenaries, and mentally unstable Masters.
                                    The Marauders and their uniquely broken avatars and chaotic magic.
                                    The Forces of the Technocracy, including recruiters, assassins, robots, clones, super-soldiers, spies, and modified animals.
                                    The Nephandi and their dark magics, including corrupted Tradition members, traitors, destroyers, and even worse things.
                                    Chapter Four: Supernatural Menaces
                                    Vampires and the secret society of the night, from lowly street punks and sirens to elder manipulators and ancient blood gods, including blood wizards.
                                    The savage and xenophobic world of werebeasts, including werewolves, fox women, jaguar men, and more, both as urban predators and rural monsters.
                                    Bygones, including ancient beings such as fey cats and giants, to modern legends like the Moth Man and UFOs, as well as dinosaurs and other time lost creatures.
                                    The Unhuman, including zombies, homunculi, robots, and Prometheans.
                                    The Unknown, urban legends among the Awakened including the Dark Carnival, the Midnight Express, and other oddities.
                                    [This book would've predated the release of Wraith and Changeling, hence them not being detailed as much.]

                                            (I'm experimenting with a new keyboard, so any typos are probably because of that.)

                                            Orphan Factions. Too small to be considered Crafts but too large or wide-spread to be mere Cabals. Most of these never achieve more than a dozen members if that. But they still have their place, often among the lowest and most personal levels of the Ascension War.

                                            Devotion House
                                            A shelter and halfway house for runaway teens in Boston, Massachusetts. Founded in 1983 by Edmond Devot, an Awakened Orphan who worked for the city's department of youth services, it was created as a safe place for young runaways and abuse victims. Over the years, Devot has found that several of his wards were also Awakened, often spontaneously on the streets or prior to running away. He has always provided mentoring and counselling, both the mundane and the mystical type, to any who seek it. Many "graduates" have remained devoted to the House long after adulthood, and continue to volunteer their services or offer other support. A few have even tried to establish satellite houses in neighboring cities and states with mixed success.

                                            Division 10
                                            A small and scattered group of low level cube farmers and office drones who have all been guided to Awakening by a mysterious Mister Smith. It is unclear just how old the group is, only first coming to attention in the late 90s, but some evidence points to their existence as far back as the early 70s. Members communicate - and receive instructions - through untraceable and encrypted emails, texts, and short phone calls. "Mister Smith" deliberately keeps his agents at a low level of Enlightenment, the better to keep them unnoticed by and unaware of the wider Awakened world. Members believe that they have been recruited into some sort of secret heroic conspiracy, one tailored to appeal to the agent's personal prejudices and vanities. Many of their covert missions seem to involve apparent nonsense errands and petty criminal acts, but their random actions sometimes result in impacts far greater than one would expect.

                                            Mason Libon
                                            "Live. Leave. Survive." That is their creed. A small and extremely secretive tradition of ritual magicians scattered throughout the Western world, they keep their mystical talents a closely guarded secret. Most only know their own mentor and the rare apprentice, but sometimes two or more members who share the same mentor (or more rarely geographic location) may work together. They try to go about their mundane lives with friends and family none the wiser. If outed, they will almost certainly uproot, disappear and relocate, sometimes even abandoning their families. Those few Tradition mages who have observed one in action claim that their workings bear several striking similarities to the Arts of the Order of Hermes.

                                            The Star Council of Thal'hun
                                            Perhaps one of the largest and most organized collections of Orphans known, the Council consists of at least a dozen Awakened members and their various Acolytes and followers along the west coast of the United States. Founded in 1971, the group began as a small cabal (cult) devoted to an "immortal energy being" known as Khuvon. Near the end of the decade, they were joined by a group of highly devoted (fanatical) UFO investigators, developing into the current form known today. Their mystical theories are based on a mixture of "primal vibrational sounds of the universe", the "alien" language of Luz'at that was taught to the founders by Khuvon itself, and visualization of the abstract mental concepts which lie behind the sounds and language. This is augmented by a number of technological devices and designs that the UFO researchers brought with them and continue to develop, many of which appear to be non-human or non-terrestrial in origin. The truth behind the group, including that of the technology or of Khuvon, continues to elude those who've investigated them. While San Francisco continues to be the center of the Star Council, smaller branches exist in Las Angeles, Los Vegas, and Portland, Oregon.

                                            (All of these were preexisting groups, either from various Mage books, or in the case of Division 10, from Hunter: The Vigil, just tweeked and modified to fit the sort of WoD I was aiming for, and to offer a bit of potential variety and comradeship for Orphan characters.)

                                                        Sorcerer Societies.

                                                        Obviously, the dividing line between a Sorcerer group and a Craft is going to be a bit vague. (For my purpose, it sort of ended up being that I had these nine groups (10 if you include the Hollow Ones) who not only generally paired up fairly well in different parts of the globe, but also ended up having each of the nine spheres as their specialty. And then a bunch of interesting other factions that seemed to work really well as different variations of path wizards.) But these are groups we generally had some fun or interesting use out of a few years ago. Most of our games tended to be set in the US, so tended to involve groups you'd be more likely to find lurking around the cities and mystic undergrounds of that part of the world.

                                                        Again, several of these are groups that've appeared in Mage/WoD books over the years, and a few others are from Hunter the Vigil.

                                                        Ashwood Abbey
                                                        Originating among the 18th century Hellfire Clubs of Britain, the members of Ashwood Abbey pursue mystical knowledge primarily for thrills and excitement. Drawing from the ranks of the wealthy and the famous, they include the jaded and the deviant as well as thrill seekers and daredevils. Their workings frequently involve demonology, sex magic, and drugs, as well as necromancy and blood magic. Summoning and warding magic is their primary stock in trade, but curses, illusions, and even shapeshifting also fade in and out of popularity.

                                                        Children of Osiris
                                                        A secretive mystery cult and occult order, they date back to at least the resurgent interest in Egyptology following the Napoleonic Wars. (Their leadership claims the group has existed for much longer, but such claims are common among Victorian era occult orders.) Their ritual magic draws heavily upon ancient Egyptian forms, including true names, astrology, and alchemy, as well as necromancy. Many of them claim to have led past lives among ancient Egypt. While the Victorian sect was very strongly of the upper classes, since WW2, they've become more open, especially to those trying to heal damaged aspects of their souls. Strangely, many of them are also skilled vampire hunters.

                                                        The Ascending Suns
                                                        Another sect claiming decent from ancient Egypt by way of Islamic Sufism. The Suns first came to notice in the United States in between the two World Wars, being heavily involved in both African American Masonic groups and early black nationalism versions of Islam. Today, they are heavily involved in inner city urban culture, including entertainment, civil rights groups, Afrocentrism, and organized crime. Members seek spiritual purification and consciousness expansion as well as personal power through alchemy, vision quests, and holy war.

                                                        Keepers of the Source
                                                        A collective of New Age mystics going back at least to 1960s hippy culture, 1950s Beatniks, and earlier Bohemianism. Their primary focus is on spiritual harmony with the Earth and in the protection of sacred mystical sites, especially from exploitation by other supernatural forces. Their magic frequently blends New Age mysticism, parapsychology, and Neo-shamanism, as well as a grab bag of influences from around the world. While it is easy to dismiss them as harmless, many of them can become quite fanatical when it comes to protecting their various causes.

                                                        Maidens Blood Sisterhood
                                                        Hermetic feminist Wiccans organized among the Ivy League sororities during the 70s and 80s, now spread to colleges and alumni around North America. Members practice magic in the pursuit of self empowerment, self discovery, and revenge (allegedly against the patriarchy, but frequently in games of mean girl one-upmanship). Protection and cursing magic are popular pursuits, as divination and summoning. Because of their upper class backgrounds and college connections, many also hold a strong decree of social and political power within the mundane world.

                                                        Brotherhood of Prometheus
                                                        Those who pursue magic purely for the sake of personal power and evolution. Working together to seek out and acquire the power of the gods, often by any means necessary. Using ideas of transhumanism, psychic awakening, and spiritual fusion, they will often summon supernatural beings in order to imprison and experiment upon them. The mental stability of some members is questionable at best.

                                                        The Pinball Wizards
                                                        A quasi-anarchist movement of technomancer troublemakers originating in the late 1960s and exploding in size with the invention of the internet. Parapsychology, thermodynamics, chaos theory, UFO studies, and computer hackings end up as gateways into stranger realms of discovery. The political views of the group sometimes lead them into political activism and even terrorism.
