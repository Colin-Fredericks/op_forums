    Achieving the Impossible?

    05-03-2017, 03:02 AM
    I've often wondered, as a Storyteller, what I'd do if my players wanted to do something that, per the source material anyway, is completely impossible. Like, for example, redeeming a Nephandi or curing a Marauder.

    On one hand, of course, the books make it clear that these things simply cannot be done. At the same time though, I also tend to look at it as a "a great story comes first" and assuming that my players actually put the time, creativity and emotion into it, I think that "redeeming a Nephandus" or "curing a Marauder" would make for a truly epic story. It doesn't feel right to just slap down players, when they're being truly ambitious and enthusiastic about an idea. And while failing at this kind of quest could make for an epic story in its own right, it doesn't feel fair to allow the players to start on this path if I'm not willing to at least entertain the tiniest possibility that they could succeed. So I'm very conflicted.

    (This is of course operating under the assumption that the players are actually legitimately invested in the outcome and willing to put the massive amount of work in to succeed.)

    What would you do? As a Storyteller, would you go for it? And more importantly, would you actually give the characters even a glimmer of a chance to succeed?

    And if so, what would you require? I'm not really talking sphere levels here (though I certainly would be interested in those too), but what would need to happen on a narrative level for you, as a Storyteller, to allow even the smallest possibility of success? What would be needed to make the impossible feel deserved? How long should it take?

    I know one obstacle would be that the Nephandus would need to want to be redeemed. Is that something that could be accomplished, do you think? Obviously, as Storyteller, I'd be handling the Nephandus. And certainly, I'd never start with the idea that the character was remotely interested in redemption. But are there any circumstances under which you would find it believable that the player characters could convince the Nephandi to change his mind?

          Every player thinks they can cure a marauder or redeem a Nephandus. It's human nature to want to try and fix things. Part of the tragedy of the World of Darkness is that good intentions like this are exactly what cause the worst tragedies.

          A nephandi would use the mages naievety to their own ends, makes sure they believe they are helping the nephandi on the road to redemption until it becomes too late to undo their plot. Double points if they can break the Mages spirits so thoroughly that they become ripe for the cauls.

          Healing a marauder is much more challenging. After all, if it were easy then we wouldn't have marauders. Just being around a marauder for too long can cause a Mage to get lost in Quiet. More often than not the well intentioned Mage simply goes marauder themselves, perhaps even falling to the same delusions as the one they wish to heal.

          But the players do not have to know it is impossible, they can have rumours or perhaps hear of the success stories which give them hope to pursue this task. But perhaps it turns out the success story was much less clean cut than described, perhaps it was even an outright fabrication on the part of the Mage involved to save face.

          This would be a story of overcoming hubris. The players are allowed to think and encouraged to try, but each attempt should be filled with so much risk and produce so great a consequence that the Mage is left wondering if it is truly worth the struggle, or if other plans are to be considered.

          They risk their sanity, their integrity, and their reputation simply by conversing with them. The nephandi may use this to isolate the mage, while the Marauder may simply help he mage realise what the others cannot see.

          It is only the Mage's own arrogance which tells them they must be able to heal the world in such a way. The story therefore would not be about their attempts to find a way out of an impossible situation, but on how, when faced with the truly impossible, they come to realise their own limitations.

          Sometimes recognising the impossible is the most difficult feat a Mage can perform.


          In summary, make it an epic quest of failure and of overcoming hubris. Avoid easy solutions and punish them dearly for every well intended step.

                      Rather than explain what is and isn't possible from the start, my approach is typically to say that everything is a grey area, and all mages have their own opinions. I throw in NPCs with conflicting views about the subjects, and allow the players to come to their own assumptions.

                      I think that's the key thing here, make sure the players are making their own mistakes. Perhaps they put too much value into the words of an old Hermetic, perhaps they are hanging too much hope on a mistranslation of an ancient document. Maybe they have heard of a technique that failed, but they think they could "do it better", forcing them to see how it failed first hand. But also learning why it failed.

                      The current game I'm running is based quite heavily around a Marauder causing trouble in London simply by being there. My players have found him, and until recently have been looking for a way to "cure" him.
                      They asked the local wise woman for advice and all they could tell them was that each marauder is as unique as the visions they have. Even if it was possible, the same solution may produce vastly different results. She then mentioned that it is difficult to know how a marauder sees the world unless you go into their mind and take a look.

                      My players thought then that the next thing to do was to Mind 3 mental link themselves directly into the Marauder's mind. They mentioned this plan to a few other NPCs, and every NPC they spoke to thought it would be far too dangerous, and not worth the peril, but they ignored the warnings and tried it anyway.

                      It did not turn out well.

                      They have since started to change their mind on how possible it is to cure him, and instead are trying to figure out how to best deal with him.

                          4) How do you protect yourself during the whole process?

This kind of thing is the stuff of legends and it demands an epic story One full of pitfalls and setbacks, of triumphs and tragedy, of personal growth, and of at least a few monkey's paws.                                           Originally posted by kalinara View Post
                                          I think perhaps my problem is, I'm not sure entirely what I would require for the characters to earn it. I'm fairly certain that I would recognize efforts that were NOT enough.

                                          What kind of things would you expect to see from your players if they were going to have any hope at all in achieving this miracle?
                                          I think the last two questions I listed have the most to do with this. How do the characters protect themselves during the whole process? Why has no one even come close to accomplishing this before? Answering these two questions in a believable and interesting way is the key to a good story.

                                          To use Healing a Marauder as an example: no one knows the actual process of becoming a Marauder unless they are on the verge of becoming one themselves. If they refuse the change and step back from the edge, they forget the secret. That's a hell of a barrier to overcome. Either they have to find a way of staying on the edge (where I'll constantly push them to take the final step and interpret resistance as stepping back) or they have to find a way of dealing with forgetfulness (which has to be real since knowing things puts them back on the edge). And that's just the first step.

                                          There's all sorts of other questions they'll need to tackle. How do you manipulate Paradox? How do you handle another Mage's Avatar? How do you handle this Mage's Avatar? What does your own Avatar think of this whole process? If you do succeed, how do you prevent a relapse? Are you suffering from Quiet? How about now? How about now? Surely by now.

                                          And at every turn, I ask the most important question: Could anyone have done this before? And as long as the answer is yes, there's more to do.

                                          Then there are the narrative beats. At least once I will try to put them in a position where they have to become Marauders or die. At least once I will make them choose between killing their target and protecting what they love. At least once I will throw a barrier at them they cannot actually pass and they will have to completely rethink their plan. There will be enemies who want to stop them from changing the world. There will be allies who want them to succeed for all the wrong reasons. There will be hints that trying and failing would be disastrous, and hints that trying and succeeding will be more so.

                                          Basically, you need to get to the point where accomplishing the goal makes sense and the characters have faced the emotional and narrative fallout of their actions. If it doesn't feel like they've changed the world and themselves they probably haven't earned it.

                                              A few players wanted to cure a nephandi in a game I ran. This was after the death of many masters and that whole mess that was the avatar storm, so there was little in the way of central Tradition government. Each cabal was free to do as it pleased but had limited support and contact with other cabals. I decided I'd allow them to if they could give me a proper rationale using the Spheres on how they were to accomplish this and somehow follow through with it or (since one of them got the True Faith Merit) if one of them achieved True Faith 4 and fixed the Nephandi Avatar as a miracle. Note that I expected a character with true faith 3 to be a living saint and true faith 4 as something akin to a prophet of the One and wrote for myself actual conditions to watch out for in granting such levels of faith. I never told them what was required though, only that they could if they tried hard enough. To give them hope and show them I was allowing this in setting without actually saying so, I created a former nephandi character who was converted by an exceedingly persistent member of the Celestial Chorus. Of course, no one; not even the nephandi in question; knows how this was done and the Chorister who performed the deed vanished shortly thereafter in a blazing inferno that destroyed the nephandi group that came to "rescue" the converted nephandi. I tried to make sure that any outsider they'd meet would find their having a nephandi around as thoroughly scandalous. They kept it secret for the most part.

                                              The game ended after numerous adventures involving fighting drug lords, establishing a moon base, slaying demons, negotiating something of a ceasefire with certain branches of the technocracy, and facing a Taftani cabal that had raided the treasure trove of King Solomon and none of the players had managed cured the nephandi. Heck, the nephandi became something like a bizarre dangerous mascot prisoner that was cooperative but also trying to tempt them to the dark side. I wasn't surprised thought. it's supposed to be hard. Surprisingly, the character with True faith managed to achieve True Faith 3... mostly by hijacking the crew on all manner of holy quests that did actually improve the lives of many people... and was working on True Faith 4.
