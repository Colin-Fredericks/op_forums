    Understanding Sorcerers/Hedge-Mages vs "True" Mages

    11-23-2022, 11:03 AM
    There seems to be some confusion and misunderstanding concerning what makes a sorcerer or linear mage different from a so-called "true" mage. With the recent, release of M20: Sorcerer, I think it is good to have another discussion on this once more.

    What are Sorcerers?
    I have seen comments indicate that hedge-mages (I hate this term as it is meant to be offensive.) could be best explained as being inferior and weaker compared to true mages. I even saw one comment saying hedge-magic was equivalent to D&D/Pathfinder 0th level Cantrip/Orison spells. This is a terrible comparison as it is not true! A more apt comparison would be that WOD sorcerers are the equivalents of the adepts of D&D/Pathfinder. Adepts, also often called hedge-mages/witches in most descriptions, created to originally to represent the rural or rustic magic user of the village or town. They were designed to be used by the DM/GM in place of an actual wizard or cleric to represent a low-level wise man, witch, or priest who knew some spells and had some knowledge. Adepts are limited to only having access of up to 5th levels spells compared to full-fledge spell casters like the wizard, cleric, and sorcerer who could learn up to 9th level spells. The idea here being that adepts learned their magic through alternate means outside of normal study or natural talent compared to the others and thus diminished in their full potential.
    But they are by no means push overs. They still have access to some potent and nasty spells that can cause unprepared players a headache. They are still spell casters after all and can cast up to 5th level spells so they cannot be underestimated that easily. This is also how sorcerers are in WOD. They may have learned a form of magic that is limited in scope, but they can still be deadly adversaries or valuable allies.

    Difference between Hedge/Path Magic and Sphere Magic
    So, what exactly is the difference between their magic? This is a complex question as the official answer has changed from edition to edition. First and early second edition sorcery or hedge magic was defined as not “real” magic but tapping into a sort of arcane realm that allowed its users to perform some supernatural effects on the world itself. They could do some cursing, some fortune telling, and even make alchemical potions, but what they were doing was not “real” magic like what mages did. Once revised edition hit, the line between the two became a little blurred. Hedge magic or linear magic was viewed as legitimate as sphere magic. The only difference being that linear magic was within the consensus, thus limited to the laws of reality, and did not produce paradox compared to sphere magic that could do anything because it broke the laws of reality via the users will. Sorcery on the other hand was bending the laws of reality using mythical trappings built into the power of rituals, prayers, objects, etc. that are infused with supernatural energy and part of the consensus itself. Outside of this, both were still considered magic.

    Now with the 20th anniversary edition, it kind of a mix of these two views with the prevailing belief that, while sorcery is still magic, sorcerers are inferior to mages and will not be able to stand up to them. This is true to a point as again sorcery is static/linear and there will always be things a mage can do that a sorcerer could only dream of doing via magic and sphere magic is very flexible and can be done on the fly compared to the more narrow, deliberate, and ritualistic way of sorcery. But they still should not be seen as pathetic low levels that can never go against a mage. The books have shown that sorcerers can be very powerful in their own right and master and legendary sorcerers can work miracles and destroy cities. I think I heard that one of the conspirators against Horizon was a hedge-mage, and they were able keep the archmages on their toes. There also was/is Nicodemus Mulhouse of the Order of Hermes. The oldest living member of the order and the head of order’s largest library on Horizon. He also was a master sorcerer, and it was well known that he just could never achieve awakening like his peers, but despite the order’s…discrimination against sorcerers that do not awaken, he was highly respected and held an important position.

    Conclusion
    Magic is magic and the main difference between the linear/sorcerer/path/hedge-mage and the dynamic/sphere/”true” mage is maximum scope of their magic. Path magic is narrow and limited and cannot break the laws of reality or fundamentally alter it permanently. Sphere magic is flexible and in theory could do anything with enough time, energy, and luck. The sorcerer relies more on rituals and deliberate spells that can become very powerful and deadly as they become more learned. Mages become in many ways, demi-gods whereas the sorcerers stay as the traditional occult wizard or scholar relying on incantations, gestures, etc. even at the peak of sorcery…unless they awaken.

                  In my take on the World of Darkness, there are actually two different types of “sorcerers”: there are the hedge magicians, who conform to the first edition's explanation of what hedge magic is; and there are acolytes, who are more like the SRev concept.

                  For the hedge magician, there's magic in the world; and the hedge magician has become attuned to it, granting him or her some facility in manipulating it. This magic is no more related to Awakened Magick than vampiric Thaumaturgy is, or Garou rites, or any other Night Folk powers for that matter. It is not in any way related to concepts or game systems found in Mage. The hedge magician uses the Hedge Paths system.

                  The acolyte, by contrast, wields the same powers that the Awakened wield. It's just that the acolyte's Avatar hasn't (yet?) Awakened; he hasn't had that epiphany where he realizes, deep down, that he has some sort of innate quality that empowers him to change the world. But he has a Focus, just like the Awakened; and that Focus lets him use Wonders as if he were Awakened, and allows him to study and learn Rotes. In terms of crunch, the Acolyte uses the exact same game systems that Awakened Mages use; but he learns it by Rote instead of by Arete or Sphere.

                  My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

                      Yes, this second type of your (Acolytes) is exactly what I use in my games. And I charge 1 xp per Sphere rank of the Rote - for example, learning "Control Mind" at lvl 1 (Mind 4) costs 4 XP for the "first dot"; a theoretical effect of Mind 4, Life 3 Prime 2 for instance (whatever this effect might be) would cost 4+3+2= 9 XP for the "first dot".

                      This way, my Acolytes truly are limited in what they do (just about a half dozen of "spells" that they learn), but they can actually become pretty skilled at their one tricky poney, since I let them buy Rotes up to lvl 10.

                      Thus, while not capable of holding a massive bag of tricks, they can be pretty good at their one narrow specialized trick - an Acolyte specialized in the "Terror Spell" can get amazing results by having a massive 6-8 dice pool to the single purpose of causing panic on enemies (Mind 2 Rote). Despite not being able to make Astral Travels, or controlling minds, or reading minds, or creating minds, or walking over dreamings, impriving minds or any of the other countless uses of the Mind Sphere, this Acolyte is still a precious resource to have, when a gang of enemy invaders are left in a shaking state, cowering in a corner. Likewise, a "master healer" which cant shapeshift into a wolf nor can turn princesses into frogs, is still a valuable asset for a Chantry when they need their members patched up properly. And an "Expert Indoctrinator" exceptional citizen, someone who has become the master of Conditioning Procedures, is an absolutely necessity for everywhere that has a room 101, and the Union have classes for it to graduate students in those constantly.

                      In fact, with this view, I can actually envision Acolytes/Exceptional Citizens having a far prominent role for the setting, where they actually contribute A LOT for the Ascension War. Just imagine that, for each Progenitor doing cloning researches, there would be hundreds of "Junior Assistants", each one a "Molecular Biologist" PhD working in their specific field of specialization.

                      Meanwhile, the Shaman is the general diplomat with the "Shadow World", but each one of his tribesmen have a role to play - one learns how to talk with the Fire, the other is the one that talks with the Water, another Wind and Earth, a woman of the tribe helps the Shaman to talk with the Plants, and each one of the others in the Tribe has learned to "speak" with one animal. Thus, when the Shaman needs to deal with the "Great Serpent", he asks for "the Serpent's Daughter" to aid him in his rituals - aka, the "Serpent Daughter" has learned the Rote to Summon the Serpent spirits (Spirit 2), which can go up to lvl 10 in that single Rote, thus far surpassing a "common" Mage in that single aspect of Magick use (which usually will only go as far as Arete 5), and therefore, when the Shaman needs to talk with the "Great Serpent", no one is better to help him than the "Serpent's Daughter". But in the tribe, only the Shaman can speak with ALL spirits.

                          I use the Rote rules found in the STV product Enlightened Grimoire, with one addition: a mage or acolyte/extraordinary citizen can, at increased cost, learn a rote that she doesn't have the appropriate Spheres for, as if she did. The rote rules in that STV supplement let you buy Rotes with “rote points”, with the primary benefit being that you get to roll Arete+Sphere with the Rote instead of just Arete. Acolytes, not having Arete, would spend experience points instead of rote points, and would just roll the Sphere rating for any rote that they've learned.
