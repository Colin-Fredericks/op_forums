    Originally posted by Aleph View Post

    I think that's a fitting comparison. After all, in Mage, research IT'S a way to magick. And a mage's life it's more or less dedicated to magick. However, I think that to say that "magick it's the new normal", "I don't get a kick using magick anymore" it's undestating the value and depth of this activity - even when it IS the new normal, in a sense.

    Now, Magick it's hard work, to be sure, I agree on that, but it's very rewarding - it has to be because otherwise you won't get to the point of bending reality with your will. Imagine not your average PhD researcher, but dat guy that's obsessively researching all day and night, and in vacation, and in the weekend, and that his family hate because he doesn't have time for them, he's researching.
    That's the life of many Etherites: "We're mostly uncles, we can't have a girlfriend because we're to deep in the lab and magestuff" .

    The Mage it's like the programmer that really, REALLY, likes to program - to the point of asking a computer while hospitalized with his bones broken because he wants to code (for fun, not to gain money). Sure the programmer it's going to be criticizing his own work, "this old code/rote of mine is shit", but that's because he's constantly thinking ways to improve it.
    This guy sees coding as the new normal? - In a sense it IS and he DOES, but don't dare to tell the guy that this code/rote he's working right now isn't the best thing that happened inside a processor ever because he will bite your head off: this code it's supreme beauty, it's going to be perfect.

    The mage it's the artist that dies for his Art. Would you dare to tell that artist that his current work (that's both her rotes AND her life) it's "the new normal"?. Of course it is, but it's a very rewarding, constantly growing, new normal that leaves her past life as meaningless boredom - Hence Gilgul it's bad. She may not regard ALL of her art with the same devotion - but it's not "work" it's "ART!".

    Or at least that's my opinion.

    You also have to factor that not all mages do magick with the same frequency (i.e: not all mages can afford flexing their Vulgar muscles very often, for fear of Paradox and the Technocracy). And not all Paradigms are equally comparable with research. Some are more lax or more violent/emotional, others are all about breaking habits and doing new stuff.

    Personally, I would think that only Masters in their umbral magic-realms would think that "magick it's the new normal - of course there's no stairs, just fly or take the flying carpet like normal people dummy!". There you can cast your stuff all day long without risking Paradox or enemies to catch upon you. Then again, that's why they often don't get to Ascend nor to reach Archmagedom - they loose that spark in their alienation. Those Masters that retain the spark of curiosity and see the glamour of what they're doing are in their way to Archmagedom (or Ascencion)

    That's why I tend to think there's a strong tendency for adepts playing the administrative rolls of the various mage factions. They get far enough a long on their path to attain a leadership positions, but get stuck transcending their paradigms, which leads to the ossification of mage society. Meanwhile the masters and archmasters (which there's not a lot of), tend to be off pushing the boundaries of paradigms. Not trying to imply they are above hubris, but their fixation on reaching the heights of magick leaves them unable to relate to their younger charges, let a lone the great mass of sleepers. Having this view is what annoyed me about the Horizon War, which had too much of a vampire-esque generational conflict.

        And what's baked into it is the notion that if you don't “transcend your paradigm”, you can't be a master. One thing about M20 is that it has abandoned that requirement.

        In fact, my reading of the book is that even once you've gotten to the point where you no longer need to use Instruments at all, your Focus is still needed to work magick; and coming to the point that you truly internalize the belief that it's all the same is something that only happens when your Paradigm (in the M20 sense; Philosophy in older editions) includes some version of “We are All God(s) in Disguise”.

        Which, to be fair, is implicitly true of all of the Traditions; it is, after all, the glue that holds the Traditions together. So from the Traditions' perspective, mastery of the Art involves “transcending paradigm”. And to the extent that the Hollow Ones have made that Paradigm a core feature of their Focus, they tend to see this the same way the Traditions do.

        But almost nobody else does. The Technocracy certainly doesn't; and I'd argue that most of the Disparate Crafts don't either.

                        So, how often do you go "Oh my god! Look how high I can kick!" Or have you moved on a bit since then? Do you still feel a thrill any time you stand in the correct pose, or is it what you do with your movement, and how you respond to what you're faced with that is of greater import to you now?
                        Similarly, how often do your musician friends revel in the glory of playing the C major scale, or even a song with two chords. How many of them are still super impressed with themselves for being able to draw a trebble clef, a time signature, and perhaps a few notes of melody, and how many of them see those things as mere tools for the real art that is composition?

                        When learning a language, you could find the syntax of the sentence a huge deal of fun, you could take pleasure in trying out the written words, drawing the glyphs, and you can have fun practising the way to move your tongue about to make the sounds, but none of that means you can actually speak that language.
                        Once you do understand that language, you'll probably be focusing on more complex aspects of grammar, how the ideas are packaged up and shipped, the way the written and spoken versions complement each other, and even learning how to understand and be understood. Heck, you might even get so thrilled that you understood a a few words spoken to you that you forget to listen to the rest of the sentence. Are you speaking language yet? More than before, certainly, but not really.
                        Work on it some more, now you can have conversations, you can follow a thread of ideas and express yourself clearly. You don't think about how to form these words with your mouth because it is now natural. You don't worry about which word belongs in what context because it's become obvious. The wonder of that period is gone, replaced with the new and of what can be attained.

                        Can you say you speak the language yet? To the outsiders, yes, but to the native speaker, only barely. There is still much to practise.

                        That which provides the mage with wonder to begin with becomes the tools for which to attain greater wonder. The mage that stays satisfied with the early tools are the ones who never evolve.

                        If you can't make the wondrous mundane, then how can you possibly have enough room in your mind for new and greater wonders?

                        How can you dance when you're still amazed by the first step?
