    Rituals and their usage

    10-04-2019, 08:19 AM
    Hi All,

    I've recently started leading a MtA game to a group of friends.
    However, since it's been a while since I've last played it, it seems hat I have either forgotten some things or I was a complete idiot when I was playing it.
    But, from what I can see and what my players (namely, one of them, the one that tends to break games) showed me, it looks like, if you have Life 3 and an odd other sphere, you can basically make yourself a beast.

    I mean, using rituals it seems they could (inside 10 days or so) basically make themselves have all attributes at 5, ability to soak lethal/agg damage, have a mind shield and several other things for the next 6 months. And since they cast the ritual in a sanctum and have a unique instrument, they basically have a difficulty of 3, which makes it trivial to achieve the successes needed.

    Am I really missing something or is it really possible to perform a multitude of rituals over 2 weeks and basically trample over stuff for the next 6 months? I mean, I could resort to moving the sanctum or the unique instrument out of the picture, but I would rather have other options.

    So, any input or help is appreciated

      With rituals/Extended rolls, it's indeed possible to get some very potent effects going. And all the things listed are pretty much a staple of basic Mage offense/defense, if they are indeed inclined to go into the thick of it; The mind shiuld though, that's pretty much a thing that most have going, if they know what's out there.

      Now, regarding the possibility of making it all last six months...yes, also doable. Given that they are all spells that the mage in question does not need to focus on constantly, they can pretty much all be in effect.

      Howeverrr....if we go into the topic of raised physical stats, there are some safeguards in place, so to speak; If the Mage pushes stats above their human limits, or otherwise changes form 'purely human', they introduce Pattern Bleeding (i.e. 1 unsoakable lethal damage per day, unless feeding on quintessence daily) and permanent paradox. The pattern bleeding only goes away if the changes are made *permanent*, and pushing outside of the human range can also introduce physical or genetic flaws as per Storyteller call.

      But, essentially, to answer your original question? Yes, yes that's all doable. But - and it's a big but - that Mage also radiates 'I AM ZE MAGIC' to everything and everyone that can detect such things. The more a Mage has running effects going, the more they become a beacon for anything that might be interested in things like that - good and bad - and last but not least, the Technocracy; Walking around with a bucket of effects running is like shouting to them "Hey, I'm a Tradition Mage!". And the moment the Technocracy finds that out, they are going to start to monitor, dig into the character's background, watch his financial transactions, check out the extended family, etc. etc.

      Of course this is just what might happen if the whole Ritual shebang actually succeeds. Remember that rituals, especially those with a lot of built up successes, also can end disastrously if Paradox (i.e. botches) strike. Sure, Willpower and Quintessence can stave things off, but it's still a risk. The best way, by the way, to limit such things is to keep the amount of Quintessence available to characters low - Quint is the easiest way to lower Arete difficulties, and handling anything aggravated.

      Last but not least, hubris is a big theme in mage - hubris and subtlety. If the character's hubris grows so much that he thinks he can walk around all pimped up and radiant, it's a simple thing to also suffer the consequences if they dare to piss off the wrong people. See the aformentioned Technocracy agents. Or heck, if the Mage getting into a pissing match with an (unknownst to them) Werewolf might make that character realize sooner than later, that there are things out there that have headbutting contests with agg-soaking, high-stat characters for fun and rank.

          Also, depending on whether you count an Effect having an extended duration as being an "active Effect", you start to run up against the Domino Effect. Every two concurrent Effects the mage has running increases the difficulty of subsequent Arete rolls by +1. Some like to say that it doesn't count unless the Effect requires active maintenance ("concentration"), but otherwise every plate the mage has spinning makes starting up additional plates harder. This is why mages tend to be selective about what Effects they have running, because it could complicate matters if they need to weave new Effects on the fly.


          Additionally, a mage's magick has a consequence above and beyond what Ambrosia and I mentioned. Namely Resonance (and Synergy). If you've got ANY Effect running continuously for six months, I as Storyteller would use that as license to start giving the character dots of Resonance/Synergy to match the "flavor" of the Effect. Making the mage even more likely to stand out, as even Sleepers begin to notice the uncanny air surrounding the mage.

                          Ah, the classic problem.

                          So, your player has discovered the sweet, sweet, nectar of "Ritual abuse". What to do?.

                          Well, there's several things that were already mentioned in the thread, I will add a few:
                          *A lot of magick makes a lot of noise that will be heard. This ain't always *bad*, but make it known that actions have consequences in the world. Even magical actions (especially magical actions). Not even Arcane 5 it's going to hide you forever.
                          *A lot of magick generates a lot of Resonance. Seek it up, it's a very important and complex rule, if very disregarded. This and Paradox are the representation of "if you change the world with magick, the world will change you back"
                          *Risk it's to be rewarded, rituals aren't free. The player it's taking a lot of (calculated) risk, it's ok if he reaps great power. That being said, he still needs to roll each 6 months. Dice are the friends of the Storyteller and the enemy of the players in all RPG: Given time he WILL botch, no matter the amount of Quint, WP and Sanctums used.
                          *Don't allow downtime w/o explanation. Characters are people that need to eat, crap, sleep, get money...and most likely can't be up all the time casting rituals
                          *No free Quint. If players wasted all their Node in the story, chances are that the downtime it's going to see them expending Quint too.
                          *Cults are people too, they aren't ritual bateries you can abuse. They expect stuff from the Mage and the rituals, if they don't get it they leave. They can also be targeted by enemies if it's evident they're empowering the mage

                          All that being said, Rituals are intended to be the most powerful tool of mages. And it shows, if you can capitalize them.

                          ...

                          But make no mistake, as poorly balanced as Mage undoubtedly is, what you're depicting isn't a rules problem but a social problem that happens when different players have different expectations about the game they're playing.

                          This it's not something that can be solved with any amount of ruling (nor RAW nor Homemade). Much more "balanced games" can have this problem (I know D&D players have this problem. And that's a game that actually cares about balancing stuff). It's ultimately a problem of playstiles. Of players (and ST) expecting different things of the game. It's a problem with the "social contract" of the game, the way people wants to play. That is, is a social problem.

                          It's solved by talking. There's no other real way. That's how you will know what people wants, and people will know what you want.

                          ...

                          Somethings I think you could try:

                          Rather than sending the Technocracy to crush everyone (knowing that the PG it's the most likely to live trough it), let it be known that he's "the best gunslinger in the west" . This is bound to happen either by feats of the player (that's, I imagine, not hidding his power) or by his massive magical aura. People will want to go after him, either to duel, manipulate or even to learn from him. That can be a lot of fun, aknowledges that he's very powerful and "on a different level" (which ain't always good, but can be flattering if done in good fun).

                          Have challenges of different natures (and levels) that the other players can enjoy. Think about what they're good at and engineer situations where those powers could come in handy. Maybe someone it's better at talking, maybe one it's better at getting intel about the enemies. Make situations that can't be solved by kicking the doors and being the strongest man arround. This works very well if the different players have different agendas and strengths.

                          Fights aren't always symmetrical. Make figths against big groups of enemies that demand to split the party, since the PG can't be in more than one place at the same time (until he reaches Correspondence 4) the rest it's going to have their fun. If they're cooperative, having more (if weaker) people tossing stuff at a stronger enemy may be what wins the fight in the end, even for the PG point of view (not everyone has to be the MVP, right?).

                          This doesn't work if all the players want to kick assess the same way, and will feel useless if one it's much more "valuable" in figths than the others. In that case, me thinks you will need to either tone him down (again) OR tone the others up giving them more "optimal strategies" (heck, your PG may even help you with that)

                                      Welllll.

                                      Cults in particular are actually a high-maintenance background. The Mage has to take *care* of their followers. They have to talk to them. Perform acts that fit into the cult's belief. Keep the social bonds to them up. Be the Cult leader they want them to be, while steering the Cult towards how they want the cult to be. It demands good social skills.
                                      Cults are also where the Technocracy can put in the prybar, especially the NWO. The NWO can make the lives of Cultists bad through subtle ways; Spreading the word to their employers that they are in some cult. Subtly push the rumor mill among their friends and families. Let society do the rest. It *can* backfire by drawing in more cultists, but the NWO has a lot of experience in making the stuff seem bad.

                                      Sanctums? Well, a Sanctum first needs to be found. Then it depends on the type of sanctum. A rented home? Oh dear, the renter somehow got convinced to toss the Mage out. An owned home? Let's have the city take a close look at the building regulations. That don't work? Too bad it wasn't protected well. Those burglars sure did a number on it.
                                      Depending on how much that Sanctum is seen as a threat, that gas line explosion was a shame. A real shame.
                                      Sanctums out in rural nature and surroundings have an easier time staying hidden. So do Sanctums that quite frankly are simply not the Mage's home in the first place.
                                      The best way to protect your Sanctum is not to make your Mage-ey affiliation known to big enemies in the first place. We come full circle to Mages with lots of running effects being a beacon

                                      Nodes on the other hand are always something that tends to lead to more drastic measures. There are times when rival Mages from *the same faction* try to get their hands on anothers. Nodes are Big Targets in the Ascension war. Most Mages make sure they are well hidden, protectied, and on a need-to-know basis unless they are actually fortified by a Chantry in the first place.

                                          I can understand the reluctance of taking away backgrounds, and it will always be a challenging point depending upon the nature of the players themselves. I don't think it's "deep in shit" question, so much as what investment is required to maintain them.

                                          I think that there are three important points to bear in mind:

                                          1. The possibility of change is known to everyone at the start, along with the kinds of in game actions that may lead to change
                                          2. The players' actions influence whether they go down or not as a natural consequence of their in game actions
                                          3. They can go up as well as go down as natural consequences of those actions

                                          Natural consequence may take some explicit explanation: "If you guys and gals use lots of vulgar magick you are going to attract Technocracy attention and they are known to do all of the things that Ambrosia mentions. This is targeting your characters through your background points rather than their health levels." (For instance)


                                          Part of establishing your answers to these three points is also asking yourself what kind of game you want to play. If you're after a game of Cultist Simulator, then Cults do need a lot of screen time to maintain. If you are more interested in Cults as being a mechanical way to get more dice for the Rituals, then it can be hand-waved away with no great loss or benefit to the character / player. The middle path, that the leadership of the Cult is important but not dominant, means that there are potentially story complications. For instance, perhaps they need to attend the cult centre every Sunday morning without fail, having spent time preparing a speech to deliver to their loyal people.

                                          Establishing up front that if they don't meet that commitment means the background would reduce with some fictional parallel to reflect that (e.g. the cult fractures and half of the cultists join up with a rival leader). But if they knock those speeches out of the park, funnel some incentives into a recruitment drive... Well then the Cult could expand as well.

                                          One missed meeting is neither here nor there - but that extended trip to find a mentor to learn the Spirit suddenly becomes a question of balancing that priority with the maintenance of the Cult.



                                          An alternative way to look at this, if you want the story but don't want to lose the mechanic is that the dots are temporarily lost for part of the story but the character has a really easy time getting them back. For instance, through attracting the wrong kinds of attention, the NWO raid the sanctum causing the character to flee. Fortunately, they already had a backup plan and over the course of the next session or two they quickly build the Sanctum back up without having to reinvest XP or other resources. This may only work once or possibly twice without stretching believability ("Hey its nine Sanctum Sammy moving in next door... again!" but provides a softer way to show backgrounds are fluid.
