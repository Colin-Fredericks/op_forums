                                    As well, I do think that in the Victorian Age, it's more a matter of being free to object then being free to hide. Between the destruction of the Cabal of Pure Thought and the induction of the Skeleton Keys, the Order didn't really have anything resembling a secret police to punish people who stepped out of line. That happened more on a convention by convention basis. And even when they did have “secret police”, it wasn't like the Ksirafai (who had disappeared shortly after the Craftmasons were purged) or the New World Order (which brought in the notion of “thought crime” — that you could and should be punished for what you think, not just what you do).

                                    And I believe that the standards of what constitutes a fitting punishment were different then than after the rise of the NWO. A Milo would face reprisals for interfering with the greater goals of the order; but those reprisals would not be Room 101, and likely wouldn't be anything comparably draconian, largely because his opposition wouldn't be as likely to be viewed as criminal or traitorous, but rather merely wrong headed.

                                    All that said, the Victorian age idealists are definitely losing. Not because of institutional punishment, but rather because their views are in the minority. Invoking a more recent concept, the Overton Window within the Order doesn't align with their views, so if they express them openly they tend to be mocked and shunned.

                                    As for the destruction of the Craftmasons: their destruction was 200 years earlier, and the Watsonian justification for it, as understood by the Order, is still unclear. Also unclear are the details as to how it happened: all we know is that it was carried out by an alliance between the Ksirafai, the Cabal of Pure Thought, and the High Guild; and by the start of the Victorian Age, two of those groups are gone — one has effectively been wiped from history; and the other, let's just say that many who think that the purge of the Craftmasons was wrong might take some small satisfaction in the Cabal of Pure Thought's demise. I don't see Victorian idealists hiding out because of the destruction of a convention that took place relative to them about as far back as the American Revolution is to us.
