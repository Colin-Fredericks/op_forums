    Originally posted by DarkKing View Post
    I mean, that's kind of the whole concept behind the Demiurge, is it not?
    The physical world is inherently evil, and so is its creator, while everything that is good exists in the spirit world?
    Kakost would enjoy this. Gnosticism was a favorite topic of theirs. It's not all that widespread, though, despite having a disproportionate presence in fiction and movies.

    I'm saying that if people find somebody who is just fundamentally, objectively, absolutely evil is an absurd notion, then the idea of somebody being pure, absolute, divine goodness should also be just as absurd.
    In an equal and opposite form of dualism, yes, in other frameworks no. For example, one common framework is that evil is the rejection of good, making evil lesser. There's also the idea that every action a person takes is for the purpose of bringing about or attaining some perceived good. In either of those (there's significant overlap between the two) evil for evil's sake make's considerably less sense than good for good's sake.

    And as Michael points out, it's also not a good fit for storytelling as it doesn't give clear or understandable character motivations.

                                                            So... She basically can operate like any normal Mage and not be affected by the whole "Descent" thing, despite having an "inverted Avatar" due to some occus poccus that Senex did.
                                                            Comparing those entities is complex because they're not quite the same concepts, even if a link does exist.

                                                            A Fomori is contaminated and then fused with a Bane, and that's definitely irreversible. But the "free will" of the resulting entity is up in the air. That's why it's a valid thesis to say that Kindred may be Fomori.

                                                            For them a bigger issue is what mutations the Bane causes, as they may cause mental illness or inhuman hungers. But the lucky ones can be independent and even fight the Wyrm.

                                                            But Nefandus are made into avatars of Oblivion through 4 distinct layers:

                                                            1. Mental illness, but extreme and with a deep starting point. A Widderslainte is mad since childhood and that won't stop, a Barabi started with the Caul and that's a whole pack to deal with. It can be said their state is more akin to a Quiet, but aligned with absolute nihilism.

                                                            2. Fate. It's subtle, but life conspires to keep a Nefandus in the path of Descension. It's more visible with a Widderslainte, and includes the eventual finding of other Nefandi.

                                                            3. The Inverted Avatar. It's not just another flavor of Avatar, the thing is far more intrusive, demanding and wiling to hurt and torture the Nefandus. Even if the person don't think about erasing the universe as a goal, the Avatar does.

                                                            4. Finally, their "Magic". Everything a Nefandus do with their spells is tainted with a Nefandic Resonance and starts to corrode reality in the area. The game doesn't goes in length about a mechanic for this, but the overall rules both for Resonance and Paradigm give you all the tools to build fitting answers.

                                                            But the gist of it in short:

                                                            - Nefandus don't follow the rules for Focus or whatever. Their personal belief does not affect their powers.
                                                            - Their Instrument is the intent of the Effect, not the execution. Every effect must directly or not contribute to further oblivion.
                                                            - Resonance is always intense and uniformly corrupted or destructive. Every single spell will leave this mark, no matter anything else. Some effects in some editions hide this, but nothing erases it.

                                                            And that's it. The individual doesn't matter to how their powers work. They can still believe in a Paradigm of sorts, but it's window dressing, no mechanical requirement or benefit. At most being destructive or perverse helps build the Resonance and set the consequences of the Effect, but the actual execution matters little.

                                                            For the last part, it's hard to tell because it was an early text never referenced again (I never read it, by the way), but you can take as an experiment and imagine what happened from there assuming Senex could only watch and hope.
