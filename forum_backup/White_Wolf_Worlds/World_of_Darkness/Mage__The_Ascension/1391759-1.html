                I would recommend for the structure that the group working on the 'Isle of the Mighty' update go by region.

                Isle of the Mighty had England, Scotland and Wales. Ireland would require some more work.

                Although there's a brief discussion of the Nephandi in Isle of the Mighty as being able to take advantage of the class-based nature of English society, there's not a lot on the Nephandi. I'd amplify the class tensions and ethnocentrism of the British Isles to figure out the Nephandi factions which would be present.

                Also, Britain isn't the United Kingdom until the Act of Union of 1707, which establishes William and Mary (also a significant early U.S. college located in Virginia) as the recognized monarchs and their line follows. So, definitely consider that although there is a United Kingdom, there are regional variations and fractures.

                        Yup, this is very important in any game set in the UK. Here's a broad-strokes overview...

                        UK, British, and English are all different things. You'll see them used interchangeably, but they're not at all the same. You'll hear people refer to "a British accent", for example. That term is so broad as to be meaningless. Someone from the East End of London, or from Llangollen in Wales, or from Inverness in Scotland, or from Durham in north-east England all have British accents. But apart from the fact that they all speak English, they don't really have much in common at all. When people say "he speaks with a British accent," they usually mean something from down south. The vast majority of the country sounds nothing like that . And there's a compelling argument that people from Northern Ireland don't have British accents at all...

                        England, Wales, and Scotland are three separate countries. Together, they make up Great Britain, which is the largest of about 6,000 islands that make up the British Isles (which is only a geographical term, not a political one, although it's controversial because it includes Ireland - see below). Together with Northern Ireland, they make up the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland (which is the proper name for the UK). So a "British accent" would be any one of dozens of accents in England, Scotland, and Wales. An "Irish accent" would be one from Ireland (either Northern Ireland or the Republic of Ireland).

                        "Britain" and "British" don't actually refer to anything meaningful at all, other than being shorthand for bits of the above, defined only by context (and some weird Roman stuff that hasn't been relevant for about 1,800 years).

                        Ireland is the second-largest of the isles, but it's divided into Northern Ireland (part of the UK) and the Republic of Ireland (not part of the UK). The latter doesn't recognise the term "British Isles" at all, really.

                        There's also the British Islands, which is a political term, and refers to the UK, the Isle of Man, Guernsey, and Jersey - but you won't hear it used generally.

                        As baakyocalder points out, there very much are regional fractures, many of which have become exacerbated in recent times (and have seen armed conflict as well, with the Troubles being an obvious example). For a Mage game, these divisions are excellent territory for developing in-game factions, adventure seeds, and plot and NPC motivations and personalities.

                                The WoD is awful. Any work on this dark mirror of our world needs to have a plausible reflection.

                                As for Scotland, I wrote a paper in a college course on nationalism about why the Scots were still part of the United Kingdom. This was in 1994, when there was talk of Scotland becoming its own country. My argument at the time was that the benefits of autonomy (the Kirk and much of the educational system not being the same as in England) and being part of a larger economic unit, the British Commonwealth, outweighed the costs to Scotland. In the World of Darkness, all the regional tensions and differences are worth examining as possible fractures to park supernatural factions in.

I'm not the writer for that project--to do it well would require a knowledge of the culture beyond what you can pick up in a few college courses in history.

                                    The divisions in the UK are fascinating. Even within England you have a north/ south divide which may as well create two different countries (ones with a bitter rivalry going), and neighbouring counties can have vastly different dialects and customs.
                                    I agree that to do such a supplement justice would require a huge depth of knowledge. Here I am willing to settle for something rough and ready, with a balance between providing a framework for a British campaign while remaining as true as possible to both the canon wod, and the British isles of the real world.


                                    Next we come on to demographics, and the numbers of awakened present.
                                    How often do mages awaken? This is the one I am least sure about. The default is 1 in 100k to 1 in a million.
                                    How safe is it for new mages? Given the slow burn of the ascension war here, and the gentlemanly respect between willworkers, it seems to me that it should be fairly safe for new mages in the UK so long as they tow the line. Mages who cross lines and act out before being taught the rules might fall afoul of regulations and be taken in by technocratic authorities before they know what's what however.
                                    How long do mages live? Again, with a cold ascension war it seems like mages should be fairly long lived as long as they avoid doing anything stupid, at least in England. Things can be a bit rougher over the border.

                                    That suggests that unless mages awaken with lower regularity than default then they should be relatively common. 1 mage per 100k is probably a lower bound, and the upper bound is around 1 mage per 10k people.

                                    this could be reduced by lowering the rate of awakening, but doing so should have implications for other countries.

                                    assuming these numbers, England would have between 560 and 5600 mages. (With Scotland having 55 to 545 mages and Ireland having 70 to 700 mages)

                                    I think I am trending towards the lower end of the scale, but many change my mind as I look deeper.

                                    Thoughts?

                                        As far as London goes, I do recommend Ben Judah's This is London. Judah is primarily a foreign correspondent, mainly in Russia, parts of Asia, and North Africa, but in this case he goes fairly deep into modern London and especially its immigrant cultures. It's a pretty interesting look at the subject, and probably fairly useful for thinking about some of the modern subcultures the Traditions and Crafts call home.

                                        Also, historian Peter Ackroyd has written a number of books about English history, including London: The Biography and London Under. The former is an overview of the city's 2000 years of history with a number of anecdotes and bits of folklore, while the latter is the sometimes bizarre world that exists beneath the city's streets, from ancient catacombs to modern infrastructure. The London book has a companion volume, Thames: A Biography, about the history of the river. Also of potential use is his Queer City, which looks at London's history, from Roman times to modern day, entirely from an LBGT+ perspective. And if you've seen the movie The Limehouse Gollum, it's actually based on one of his fiction novels.

                                        Martin Latham's Londonopolis is a fun overview of curious bits of historical oddities about the city. He's done a similar book about Kent, Kent's Strangest Tales.

                                        Finding decent books about London's occult history and legacies is kind of hard. Most tend to focus only on specific time periods in extreme detail, are super spotty and shallow, or are built around really weird conspiracy theories (usually involving the Queen of England being a reptoid alien or something). However, The Book of English Magic by Phillip Carr-Gomm and Richard Heygate isn't bad.

                                        Fictionwise, Ben Aaronovitch's Rivers of London books are a pretty good modern urban fantasy series. Also Benedict Jacka's Alex Verus series. Then in the realm of the very way out there crazy ideas is Simon Green's Nightside series. And the classic Neverwhere by Neil Gaiman, and China Mieville's Kraken.
