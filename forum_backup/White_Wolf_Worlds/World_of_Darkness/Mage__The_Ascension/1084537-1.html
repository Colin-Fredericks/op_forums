    Ultimately, I think my issue with the pillar/foundation system is two fold.

    The first is while I understand the argument that the pillars incorporate practice/paradigm more than the spheres do, I don't necessarily agree. I mean, I don't get the impression that we're supposed to believe that every magical group uses the terms "foundation" and "pillar", but it's still really bizarre that each group seems to have a remarkably similar structure as to how it works. And it encompasses such a narrow idea of methodology. Are we to believe that EVERY Chorister calls on the Archangels? Even the Mithraic Singers? Or folks who follow non Abrahamic monotheistic deities like Ahkenaten? Does EVERY Old Faith person call on the seasons? And while in a roleplaying sense, I appreciate that it makes it very different for mages to learn one another's practices because they're working from completely alien mindsets, I don't think the system really accommodates innovation or invention very easily. Hermetics spend years on end holed up in libraries and laboratories trying to extend and reinvent their craft, but well, say they invent a way to time travel? Or even just scry for visions into the past? You'd either have to stretch one of the existing pillars to accommodate it (...maybe Corona for the scrying thing anyway?), or create something completely new.

    Which ties into my second issue: while I can absolutely see why the system is a blast to play, and I like the pillars that we see in the Dark Ages Mage book, it is pretty near impossible to find a way to extend it to other groups without creating the pillars completely from scratch. Like, if I want to make a pre-Spheres Akashic Brother or Ngoma, I would need to design not just methodology/paradigm, which I would need to do with Spheres anyway, but I'd need to basically set up an entire system from scratch. I'd need to first look at the kind of magic I'd want the characters to do, find a way to divide it into four culturally appropriate themes (which definitely seems off for the Akashic Brotherhood considering that a few East Asian cultures have very negative connotations regarding the number four), and then find a way to scale it appropriately to balance what's already been created. That is a LOT of work.

    In a roleplaying sense, I like the way that a Hermetic using the Vires Pillar and a Chorister calling on Gavri-El might both use magic involving fire, but conceptually include so many other concepts. But I honestly, I think that idea works just as well as fluff, while still using spheres for the actual effect. (I'd probably use Arete + Highest Sphere Rank as a dice pool, to represent how much more oomph magic has in the mythic ages.)

    I won't pretend spheres aren't a mess, but I really appreciate that they're focused primarily on effect instead of methodology. Sure, some literal folks may refer to the spheres in character, but others don't think of their magic as using those spheres at all. Heck, if you want to, you can describe your characters talking about using the Vires Pillar or Gavri-El, or even Ars Magicka's system, while describing and creating the effects with the spheres. It might take a combination of spheres to do it, mind you, like how the second rank of the Corona Pillar involves things like forging mental bonds and sensing spiritual energy, so to properly do that effect, the Hermetic Mage would need to have access to both the mind and spirit sphere, but I don't really see much of an issue with that. It might be a little bit more expensive in character creation, but that can go both ways. The Old Faith rote "Verdant Slumber" requires four ranks of Autumn and two in Spring, and basically just involves turning a dude into a tree. In the sphere system, you'd only need life.

    The advantage to the sphere system is that it covers nine very distinct categories for magic effects. We might quibble about whether or not certain things SHOULD be grouped as they are (I know some folks much prefer Awakening's split of Entropy into Fate and Death), but it's fairly easy to look at any hypothetical magic effect and translate it to spheres. Medusa turns a man into stone. Life and Matter. Sailor Moon turns her tiara into a discus that harms monsters: Matter, Prime, maybe some Forces or Spirit depending on what you think is actually happening. Granny Weatherwax is using Mind when she mentally bonds with an animal. Chrestomanci uses Correspondence (maybe Correspondence + Life) when someone calls for him three times. You can translate pretty much anything.

    The scale/magnitude issue is always going to be a bit problematic. But at least with the sphere system, if you know what you define as being a 2-dot forces effect, then it's a 2 dot forces effect whether it's done by calling on Gabriel or using a ceremonial ritual. It makes it a lot easier to bring in a heretofore un-established faction. Just figure out the paradigm/practice, and use the spheres to translate what you want to happen.

    It's also much easier to apply to other time periods. We have no idea what Mages might have used prior to the pillar/foundation ideas, but with the sphere system, we don't necessarily have to worry about that. If we want to create a Neandertal who influences her tribe's mood, i.e. firing up their passion or calming their tempers, through cave paintings, we can. She doesn't have to have any idea what a "mind sphere" is for us to say that she's found a way to use it.

    Though I admit, it would be a very fun mental exercise to try to design a foundation-pillar system for Neanderthals.

For *them*, as-written, teleportation isn't as "simple"/"easy" as just using Correspondence:3/4 -- it takes a bit more work.

                        One of the ongoing conversation points about The Spheres -- and a point I've been voicing since Mage came out in '94 -- is that they game-mechanically effectively enforce a metaphysical paradigm onto a narrative wherein belief (and Belief(tm) ) actually have a direct influence on How Magic(k) Works(tm). That is, by delineating out the "x, y, z's" the way they do, they make the expression of Paradigm(tm) -- which is an important aspect of the game, if albeit a largely abstract one -- very difficult to "properly" work mechanically. Paradigm(tm), contextually, is more than just the trappings of how a given mage does what they do; it actually has a direct impact on *how* things work, and meta-mechanically that should be better represented. (M20 did do a better job of that that Mage 1st-Rev., but still has a little ways to...) One major fault/flaw of the SS is the game conceit that (para-phrase/quote) "every belief system has the ability to manifest each of the aspects of the Spheres in one form or another".

                        Bullshit(tm).

                        Complete and total Bullshit(tm).


                        While it's contextually true that *some* aspect(s) of each of the Spheres (as-written) can be found in most all mystical belief systems, it isn't true that the meta-mechanics (the specific aspects listed at each level of each Sphere) can be. Very little in the way of Entropy (as-written) can actually be found in, say, most of the metaphysical beliefs/"essence" of The Celestial Chorus/Messianic Voices. Some *applications* can found; some specific *qualities* that are linked/adjunct to a broader metaphysic can be found (that is, a conjunctional aspect wherein a quality ascribed to Entropy that supports another Sphere/"x" can be found, but in the same relative context of how, with the Hermetics, the Prime:2 aspect of being able to conjure "something from nothing" is built-in to their Vires pillar -- they can conjure (F)orces from "nowhere" without having to use a conjunctional Primus/Prime:2 spell because that applied-aspect is already deeply a part of that Pillar/manifestation of Paradigm(tm)... regardless of whether they do or don't have something akin to "Prime" -- which they do, but again that's not relevant -- in their "Pillars"...)

                        (...and speaking of " " around the term Pillars...)

                        Another concept-misperception when it comes to the Spheres/Pillars/what-have-you is the inter-meta-mechanic concerning how Game Speak(tm) and in-narrative terms are often blurred. While in the game setting it is true that most 21st century mages who are brought through the Traditions share/are taught the "basic language" of The Spheres (that is, a Verbena and a Hermetic and Chorister all can recognize a given mystical working as "Prime:2, Life:3", and in some cases literally call it that -- Ars Vis:2nd tier, Ars Anima:3 tier, for Heremetics), there are still a number of them -- particularly among Orphans, Hollow Ones, and Crafts, including others -- who don't use those concepts, as-presented, at all, save for when their magic(k) is "viewed and catalogued" by a given Traditionalist mage (particularly a Hermetic) who then described "x" as "y and z". Doing so game mechanically, however, removes a very important dimension of Belief(tm), Resonance(tm) and Paradigm(tm) from the game, or regulates to under-stated "fluff" that literally kills a veritable half of what Magic(k)(tm) is supposed to be.

                        (Sidenote: now, from a meta-analytical perspective, this is *exactly* what is/has happened, game-and-in-narrative-wise, over the centuries in Mage: as the world becomes more tightly bound and metaphysically structured, How Magic(k) Works(tm) *does* become more delineated and less flexible -- not just along one or two concept-dimensions, but along several. This, though, doesn't mean that Minor Spheres/Pillars are "flawed" compared to the Spheres; quite the opposite, in fact. They represent a dimension of magic(k) wherein flexibility and belief have a real-and-true bearing on magic(k) that the mage does, regardless of trappings and/or over-arcing metaphysics.)

                        It's similar to how, to cross-example, some people work on refining certain Alt-Spheres for Ascension ("Data","Dimensional Science", among others, which in some cases are essentially just a name-change but in others actually represent a tweaking/changing of what metaphysical aspects are in-play), so as to "properly" fit a given Paradigm(tm) (the Technocratic one, in this case) into the system. That is a small example/deviance from the Sphere System/Paradigm, so it's one that people can often handwave or simply overlook altogether... but it very much represents the idea of how belief *can* and *does* change even the metaphysics (and therefore meta-game-mechanics) of how a mage does what he does.

                        I, for one, hope that if DA:M is redone, they *do* put a little more work into so as to fix the clunks (and there were several, no question) in it so the cross-comparisons -- but also examples of unique, paradigm-specific applications/mechanics, such as the Dream Magic(k) of the Spirit Talkers, among other things -- can be better related and understood to both the mid-experienced readers and more so to the new-to-Mage readers.

                                    I have to admit, I don't really see the problem with your pop culture necromancer scenario. You're able to get him to the power level he needs to do what he needs to do. There's no requirement that a Master of a sphere utilize every possible application of it. If the fate stuff isn't in his paradigm then he doesn't use it. Similarly, a high ranking Flambeau Forces Master could, mechanically, do all sorts of interesting manipulation of gravitational energy or atomic forces, but if that's not the way he understands the world, then that's not what he can do.

                                    The interesting thing about Spheres as a mechanic vs. Spheres as an in game idea is that one really isn't reliant on the other. The Flambeau Mage might well have taken chemistry 101 and knows, intellectually, that atomic forces fall under the "Forces Sphere", but that doesn't mean he has any idea how to use that, or any desire to. He can leave that to those guys over in Thig. Your necromancer may not have any idea that the Entropy sphere exists at all, or that it might also include the idea of manipulating probability or decay.

                                    That's actually what I really enjoy about sphere mechanics. The spheres are such broad concepts that the characters are capable, mechanically, of far more than they are consciously able to understand or fit into their own paradigms. Basically the Mage is literally limited only by his own world view. Which seems appropriate given the nature of the game.

                                    It's a lot easier, I admit, to make a themed Mage with the Foundation/Pillar system. You don't have to create a paradigm to narrow down the sphere mechanics to an appropriate theme, because the Pillars are created around those themes already. Most things are a lot easier, though a few things require more investment here and there. It's really a lovely system. But I don't think it works very well outside of the Dark Ages setting concept.

                                    I suppose, in the end, I tend to look at it as: if I'm running a Dark Ages Mage game, specifically set in Europe, specifically set during that time period, I like the Pillar/Foundation system. But if I'm running a Dark Ages Mage game that's meant to go outside of Europe, or meant to have a lot of ties to Sorcerer's Crusade or Mage the Ascension. Or if I was using a different time period altogether, like Ancient Egypt or Greece or Rome, then I like the sphere system better instead. I'd probably still use the Pillars/Foundation material for paradigm inspiration and rote ideas though.
