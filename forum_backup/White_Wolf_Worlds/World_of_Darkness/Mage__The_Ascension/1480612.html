    Why were there only two books published for Dark Ages: Mage?

    05-09-2022, 09:51 PM
    Many of you are already familiar with Dark Ages: Mage, so I wont go over it. If you don't know what it is, a quick google search will suffice.

    The two books published for DA:M were its rulebook and DA:M grimoire, which was its source book. It makes no sense to me why there were so few books published for DA:M. Not only was it really simple to understand, it was also extremely fun to read. Foundations seemed like a great replacement for Paradigms, and had mechanical benefits. Pillar magick was a lot more interesting than sphere magick and seemed super fun to play. Fellowships were a lot more interesting to learn about than the traditions, technocracy, and crafts from M:TA. The setting had a high fantasy feel to it, with a dash of gritty realism that is inherent in all WOD game lines. This in itself would have made it perfect for a champaign about wizards and magic in the dark ages.

    So why weren't there more books published? From what I have seen on the forums, many of you seem to have enjoyed it. Ars Magica had like 10 books, and I consider it pretty boring compared to DA:M. Dark ages vampire was pretty popular, with people still talking about it to this day. So there was clearly a demographic which the company could have profited off of.

                                  There was never really supposed to be a Dark Ages: Mage. The original idea was that each splat would get an historical setting with which to play. Vampire got Dark Ages, Werewolf had the Wild West, Wraith received World War 1, and Mage initially had The Sorcerer's Crusade. Changeling had a proposed setting, but I cannot recall what time-frame was pitched. Vampire: the Dark Ages sold so well that White Wolf expanded the line, thus the title flip to Dark Ages: insert splat. Later, Victorian Age Vampire was published, as the Dark ages became its own line.

                                          The actual reason was complicated.

                                          First off, the games were showing their age. After over a decade, the systems and the settings had grown Byzantine to the point of turning off new players as the early 00s saw a movement away from big systems and big settings for anything that wasn't high fantasy. As well, the teams working on the WoD were growing to hate the games they loved because fixing the problems kept getting to the point of needing such a massive rebuild that you'd need to reboot things to do it. The WW bosses insisted each game line was it's own independent domain, so each head developer came up with their own solutions, while the fans were begging for more consistency and were instead getting the exact opposite. According to a lot of the WW people at the time, things had just gotten to that point: the current iteration of the WoD had to end for the line to progress. I think this is TyrannicalRabbit's bigger source of objection. The decision to end the original WoD came first. The idea to do a new WoD that went in a different but similar direction instead of a more straight up reboot came second.

                                          Second, the economy of RPGs in that time period was bleak. The OGL Boom was in full force, but the signs of the impending bust were obvious (WW has taking part in the boom, so they were in a great position to see how bad conditions were). The general economy was also killing the small brick and mortar stores that defined gaming in the 80s and 90s. Big stores carried some RPGs, but didn't have the atmosphere to encourage gaming groups. More stuff was going on-line. The conditions that fuels the supplement treadmill era were on the brink of collapse. So WW had to pick between ending the original WoD while there was time, or trying to end it a few years later after the whole RPG marketplace shat itself.

                                          Finally? The audience was aging. While in the early 00s WW was still a big enough brand to attract a significant number of new and young players (nothing like being the "mature" brand to get youth to buy your stuff), in general RPGs were facing an issue of a lot of people that were playing these games in highschool or college suddenly having jobs, and families, and all that annoying adult stuff eating up their time. This created a big division between the old schoolers that wanted their (doomed by circumstances) familiar way of doing things, and new schoolers that wanted to see things adapt in the hopes of keeping their fun but in a way that better suited where they were then instead of a decade previous.

                                          Basically, the WoD as it existed in 2003 was obviously at the end of it's practical lifespan as a product (not necessarily a brand, there's a reason the later revival worked and not trying during the second half of the 00s is a big part of that). WW chose to pick the ending they wanted and start something new rather than leave even more hanging than they did when the market would have killed the WoD anyway (keep in mind that despite their popularity Changeling and Wraith had already been cancelled previously for lack of sales).

                                                  I'm not a fan of V5 (or things I've heard about W5 and H5) but the WoD is not dying because I don't like an edition, and the RPG market conditions aren't what they were like during the Clinton economic boom (again, economics far beyond RPGs matter, the 90s were so big for niche luxury items aimed at relatively low price points because it was boom economy for the middle class that has never truly been recovered from after multiple globally impacting economic disasters over the last two decades).

                                                  V5 isn't going to reclaim VtM's position as the second biggest RPG. The WoD brand is, however, in general, the best it's been in a long time. The new reality of the RPG market is, once you stop trying to be a D&D killer or caring what D&D can do with Hasbro's monetary backing, currently pretty good and lots of older games are getting revisits by people that haven't though about them in ages, or never gave them a shot for being "traditional" during the post OGL-bust anti-traditional design mindset.

                                                  The whole reason why we call the OGL era a boom-bust one, is because things got bigger than they could ever really sustain. Things looked more popular, but aren't necessarily more enduring popular, because people were spending way more than was justified. Not reaching those heights again isn't dying, is living sustainably.
