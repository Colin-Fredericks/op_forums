    Do you like paradox?

    10-04-2024, 11:00 AM
    Just curious, do you guys like the concept of paradox and the implications in game?

    I'm not asking about its mechanics per se, but the fluff. The role of paradox is to limit magick somehow, otherwise mages would fly around rebuilding reality as they please. So, do you think it's a good way to do that?
    or would you prefer another option like the famous "mana" points? Or DnD spells per day?

    If you could (or did) change how magick is limited in your game, how it would be?

          Paradox isn't meant to limit magic in any way, shape or form. It not only doesn’t keep Mages from flying around rebuilding reality as they please, it in fact messes things even more when it comes down.

          I think it fulfills its true purpose of reinforcing the theme of responsibility vs hubris. When Paradox happens, the Mage can blame lady luck as much as they want, they’re actually the only one responsible for whatever consequence comes their way.

          Other than that, I think it must be toned to the kind of game you want. I repeat, it isn’t really meant to limit anything. Even Revised-era Paradox does a crappy job at stopping Mages. Instead, it is there to make Magic dangerous, be it Gritty Survival mode (everything causes loads of Paradox), or just Flavored Failure (only big backlashes cause Paradox).

          What limits Mages in WoD is other Mages. Unless they’re unnecessarily fearful without ever experiencing it, what players will really avoid is the response of something bigger and badder.

                                                      I do like Paradox conceptually a lot. If not always it's particular mechanics

                                                      Originally posted by Lord Revan
                                                      I guys do realize that consequences impose limitations right?

                                                      In your very example, you had to keep low profile for three weeks to bleed of the rest. If there wasnt paradox, your mage not only wouldn't get that (small) damage and flaw, but he could keep doing vulgar magick on a daily basis.

                                                      The very consequence of paradox made him avoid vulgar magick.
                                                      I can understand Paradox as a limitation of sorts. But it's not a limit in the sense of "you can do X magick and not more". It's not so much a hard limit as a psychological one. It's more akin to a guts game.

                                                      In D&D you know exactly how much resources you can expend. There's tension in knowing that, by making this figth easier, you may not be able to face dangers afterwards. Once you've reached the limit, your magick goes away

                                                      On Mage you can always push your luck harder. Because there's so much breadth in the RNG, the mage of the example could easily have chosen to keep firing Vulgar stuff, and not die. There's a level of "resource management" here, but to calculate the point where reciving hard consequences it's a given it's not just hard, but pointless, realisticly you're never going to survive until you reach the "hard limit of Paradox". Ultimately the sampled player chosed to stop early and avoid the risks

                                                      So, rather than "resource management", there's more of a "risk management" going on
                                                      I've been reading Dying Earth, and it's interesting how the author goes about his "Vancian magick". The characters know the adversary has a limit, but there's tension in that they don't know *when* they're going to reach it. An adversary may seem impossibly powerful. But ultimately they know that exhaustion it's a viable strategy.

                                                      On Mage that's not the same! , pushed against the ropes a mage will figth harder. Trying to "invoke" Paradox as a tactical limit it's not very feasible, and even if it does show up, you don't know if it's going to affect things to your advantage

                                                      Interestingly, however, there's quite an amount of fiction on Mage that *does* show the figth ending when the mage *explodes* on Paradox. Meaning that the authors maybe thought of Paradox as a real tactical limit...And maybe that's why it's so common to hear about Paradox as that wich limits the power of mages, rather than a risk of practicing magick.

                                                      Originally posted by Lord Revan
                                                      Of course, the Technocracy are a real menace too, but fighting them is inevitable and part of the game (for Trads).
                                                      On Mage you're always the Underdog.

                                                      If you're Tradition, you know that the Technocracy has you beaten, but also that it isn't taking it's full force against you rigth now. You can "push it", but the more Vulgar you get, the more the Technocracy will prioritize you as a target, and at some point you're going to lose. So Mages try to figth subtly, as to make the "risk/reward" calculus of the Technocracy check them as "not worth it"

                                                      If you're Technocracy, you have your superiors limiting what you can do in a similar fashion.

                                                      Ultimately, it's all about "consequences".

                                                      But in practice the Technocracy it's the more certain consequence of going Vulgar, compared to Paradox. They're the reason to keep a Masquerade more than anything else (if you think about it, the Masquerade itself it's also a reacion to "hunters", so it's not that different).

                                                      Narratively, Paradox does act as a sort of obstacle of the more "high end" powers, and protects reality to some extent. Like how super old and powerful mages are keept in realms because they can't sustain their immortality on Earth. Or how super powerful rituals can end in catastrophic reasults. Or how Unbelief kicked Bygones out of reality, and whole places (like Lemuria) even. From the PoV of the Lore of Mage, Paradox and similar forces have been a stone in the foot of the true Fantastical, one could say.

                                                      But players rarely experience any of this. For Players Paradox it's more of a reminder that magick it's inherently risky, a powerful but dangerous force that needs to be treated with caution. And that, perhaps more than the above, it's not something a standard "Mana pool" could easily replace

                                                          Paradox seems to have a few major applications and how much I approve of it depends on the application.

                                                          The first is keeping magic unpredictable and risky, and I think the idea of paradox works well in this sense. The rules

                                                          The second is highlighting the nature of magic and the consensus. This is most prominent in the role of witnesses and unbelief. Again, I think this functions reasonably well.

                                                          The third is keeping the feel of earthly reality reasonably grounded. In this sense it works well for explaining why the major factions aren't waging a hot ascension war, but it doesn't do much to keep individual events from spiraling well out of control.

                                                          The fourth is to punish specific things the authors or STs don't want to deal with. It's usually easy to tell because all the normal paradox rules go out the window and the points start flowing like someone cracked open a fire hydrant. Time travel is the most obvious version of this, but not the only one. I think this is the worst application of Paradox. It undercuts all the metaphysical and thematic elements and turns Paradox into a blunt punishing instrument.
