    Originally posted by Michael View Post
    Ramnesis


    Frankly, I don't think being a mage is really compatible with remaining an orthodox believer in basically any religion.
    That's somewhat true, but not because of any of those things.

    First, while Mages frequently encounter phenomena that goes against orthodox belief, they can explain those phenomena away however they want. What my fellow Mage says about their Magic has no real bearing on what I believe about their Magic. Just as IRL religious fundamentalists are perfectly able to keep their beliefs while inhabiting a world where so much information and technology is predicated on scientific truths they don't believe in, Mages can completely ignore the paradigms of other Mages and either explain their powers in some other way, or not really care enough, without having their faith blown up.

    Second, orthodoxy itself means something else for Mages. In fact, it already means something other than what you think for many theologians. It isn't necessarily about setting in stone specific or interpretations, literal or not, it is about holding to particular traditions and worldviews. Accepting any particular phenomena isn't necessarily hurting orthodoxy, because that's not the point (although it may hurt a given orthodox thinker's faith, but that's completely on the person, not on the orthodoxy). Orthodox theologians debating the meaning of a given information within their worldview isn't just something that happens, it is common in every religion.

    A Mage that adheres to an orthodox worldview and tradition is then perfectly normal to begin with. But Mages go a step further, because by the very nature of being a Mage their powers reinforce and prove their beliefs, and their belief in the "righteousness", so to speak, of their world view is more important than it being objectively correct. For a Mage it isn't so much about proving that the world works the way you think it works, it's about fighting to make it work that way, and to stop real threats on how the world works. At the end of the day, being a Mage is about moving past the stance of imposing your worldview upon others, into imposing your worldview upon reality itself. It's within this framework that an orthodox Mage is orthodox.

    What really makes it hard for a Mage to abide to orthodoxy completely isn't any external threat to this orthodoxy, but the fact that being a Mage also means being defiant. Any person that simply accept reality as it is (or as they think it is), simply isn't a Mage. They're Willworkers by definition, imposing their own personal views, not those of others. Even in the Technocracy it's a fact, even if an unpleasant one, that truly beating Agents into conformity doesn't work. Any Mage is, at the end of the day, someone who defies, explores and creates, and that can't really, fundamentally, accept anyone's else imposition on their beliefs. They do the imposing, with prejudice.

    But then, that only means we have to understand orthodoxy under a very different light when it comes to Mages, no matter which group they belong to or which belief system they ascribe to. But that doesn't means there's no merit on talking about orthodoxy, so it means we have to expand the concept for it to make sense in their context.

        There's also an additional aspect where "orthodox" has a very specific meaning in Jewish theology that is separate from larger discourse about orthodoxy (religious or otherwise).

        Judaism is actually a pretty easy religion for being a mage because Judaism doesn't claim exclusive access to things like mystical powers or communion with spiritual entities. You can sum up a huge amount of Jewish theological writings as basically, "here's why our way is better than other peoples' ways of doing things." Which is already a starting position that other people can do stuff without requiring it to be reinterpreted. There's a very famous part of the Biblical story where Moses has a miracle show-down with some Egyptian priests. The Egyptians perform a miracle, and Moses one-ups them. This isn't presented as because the Egyptians are charlatans or worshipers of false idols, but because the Lord is the real supreme deity and the Egyptian pantheon are something lesser; hence Moses gets superior miracle powers. There's no need for a rationalization of why Egyptian priests can do some miracles despite not worshiping the Lord. When it's a given in Jewish metaphysical thinking that Jews don't have a lock on metaphysical power by virtue of worshiping the correct deity... adapting to the realities of the Mage setting isn't such a hard leap.

                        Originally posted by Ramnesis View Post

                        There are several of the Traditions that are basically 19th and 20th century occult organizations with the serial numbers filed off and backdated several thousand years.
                        I think all of them are that to some degree, most to a very large one. The CC is definitely Theosophy, just as the OoH is Telema.

                        Originally posted by Ramnesis View Post

                        Same for Christianity (depending on denomination).
                        Not in the same sense Heavy Arms said, when speaking of the specifics of Judaism.

                        For most Christian denominations, the orthodoxy is that if demons and other spirits exist, they're almost powerless. They may be, sometimes, able to do possessions and cause symptoms through that, but that's about it and even that isn't common. Outside Charismatic Christianity the belief in witchcraft, profane (as in non-sacred) magic and regular possessions isn't really orthodox, although it has always been common among the common people. Within the Roman Church it is accepted, but's still fringe, and very few if any Exorcists are really deemed orthodox. Most other denominations follow something similar.

                        The thing with Judaism is that their texts don't ever claim YHWH (it's a matter of respect to read this as Adonai or some other name, not as written) to be the sole god in existence, they don't even claim other gods to be anything but true, actual, gods, if lesser ones. In fact, in many texts YHWH doesn't even works alone, although certainly works from a position of leadership. Now, it doesn't mean a "strictly monotheistic" belief isn't possible within Judaism, or even uncommon, nor that you can't interpret passages in a way to see demons in those gods. It's just that this is on you, not on the text, and it doesn't affect Orthodox views. YHWH is the God of the Hebrews/Jews, and the superior god, but other gods for other cultures exist and that's perfectly fine.

                        It doesn't just means Jewish Mages can have Orthodox beliefs while still accepting that other magics exist. More on point and distinct from Christianity, it means Orthodox Jewish Mages can accept others' Magic at face value, at least to a far greater extent than other religious Mages. They can accept the explanation other Mage gives, even if a religious one, at least as possible. Now, this is also common on "polytheistic" religions, but the details depend on each case and is beyond this discussion.​

                            Originally posted by Ramnesis View Post

                            The problem for both Christianity and Islam (which covers a lot of religious people on the planet), is that both embraced some level of evangelism/proselytizing as a matter of doctrine. While both religions accept the Exodus story and thus have to acknowledge that there isn't the demonization of some non-Abrahamic practices (but is of others), other narratives need to come in to justify the need to actively convert people. Both also came into being after Judaism had moved from a more henoistic/soft-monotheism to a hard monotheism, and thus started with more distance from the earlier less exclusive roots of the shared religious background.

                            While I'm painting with a very broad brush here, it's worth remembering that a lot of the more modern accepting attitudes in most Abrahamic faith practices is a product of a post-Enlightenment need to stay relevant to shifting broader social norms than a deep set religious push towards such a position.

                            The problem for a Christian mage, is that their belief system includes (whether they value it strongly or not) that spreading Christianity to non-Christians is a fundamental good. The Chorus's status as heretical is most strongly found in its inclusivity. "Your job is to support everyone on their journey to finding divine enlightenment through the One," is at odds with, "Jesus is the only path that gets you there." Judaism doesn't hold as a fundamental doctrine that you need to actively worship the Lord to be a righteous person that will find redemption and spiritual fulfillment, so there isn't the contradiction. Again, lots of Christians do not personally care about converting non-believers directly, but one has to wonder how many of them are going to actively combine Christianity and magic as a mage if presented with that decision vs. taking a more "heretical" stance on their relationship with divine duty.

                            Haredi (which is an endonym, even if a recent one) is probably the best term for both orthodox and orthoprax, where as Haskalah movements that don't reject orthoprax teachings (aka mostly the Modern Orthodox, and some very traditional Conservative practices that would find themselves pushed out of mainstream Conservative rabbinical teachings) would cover the two major groups of Orthodox Jews using Hebrew language and terms of choice. Though very few people would actively identify as Haskalah any more, since Haskalah as a movement basically succeeded and doesn't require active pursuit anymore; it's just the origin point for most non-Haredi dati traditions in modern times.

                            For anyone not familiar:

                            Haredi translates as "shaker" or "quaker" which is no coincidence because the Christian movements that use the translated names also derive the name from the book of Isiah where being in awe of the divine caused people to hared or "tremble." This is interpreted as an ecstatic state, not a negative one, and is seen as requiring strict religious life-styles to achieve.

                            Haskalah translates to "wisdom" but more in context, "enlightenment," and was a set of Jewish movements to go in the opposite direction and balance Judaism with a more modern assimilated life when post-Enlightenment social norms started taking root. With the Enlightenment leading to broad acceptance of religious tolerance as a social good within cultures that integrated the era's ideals, the idea of needing to live separately to maintain personal beliefs weakened (though ideals vs. reality did end up spiking things in Haredi favor for quite some time).

                            I'm not sure there's a single "correct" to recall here. Though I feel that the more intuitive interpretation of Pharaoh's disappointment with his court magicians/etc. stems more from them trying to one-up Moses and Aaron in causing destruction and still ultimately proving less powerful. Given how much of that part of the Exodus story revolves around "softening" Pharoh's heart until he relents and lets Moses lead the Hebrews out of Egypt, whether or not the Egyptians could have done powerful "healing" miracles to counter the destructive plagues isn't really addressed over their desire to try to prove more powerful... the Pharaoh's country getting wrecked in the process be damned. It's a bit easier to see why the Pharaoh has a change a mind when his side is both losing, and it's costing him dearly in practical matters of governing beyond losing face.

                            Trying not to get too tangential here, a lot of archeology and history shows that these sorts of "miracle showdowns," were at least culturally normal (even if they seem to be told in stories rather than witnessed events) for that part of the world. It wasn't really some unique event, or something that would have been considered explicitly "religious" at the time; but rather more of a political gesture between peoples that all assume their deities are real and part of their politics. "The gods don't have our backs on this one, so I'm not going to start a war with those people for that thing they did," is a potent tool for state-craft that doesn't force either party to reject their peoples' belief systems even if they didn't come out on top of this particular divine display of power.
