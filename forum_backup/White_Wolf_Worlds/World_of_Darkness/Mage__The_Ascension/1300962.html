    The downside of fiction inspiration.

    03-29-2019, 06:25 PM
    Media in the fictional inspiration thread, or found in the world by chance, can be good resources for Mage games. But it's a good idea for players and storytellers to think about how popular media can give the wrong expectations for a Mage chronicle. Spectacle-driven media that explicitly deal with magic are often the worst influences, because the way they show magic tends to conflict terribly with the style that emerges from Mage's magic rules. It took me a long time to get past the fact that the number of successes required for a lot of Mage effects is too high to reliably achieve for a starting character in a single turn. The fact that even starting Mage characters are very powerful can be lost when you're expecting on some level to be able to shout a word and have a thing always happen. For some reason players equate Instant with Powerful, and will give low power of starting D&D wizards a pass while complaining about their "weak" starting Mage character that can teleport to Antarctica (if you give him an hour or so). Part of it is that ritual magic is not a big part of many other tabletop RPG's and is pretty much never a thing in video games. I know I'm not the only one who's (wrongly) gotten hung up about this. I've seen multiple threads here, at least one quite long, about how Arete pools are unfairly small and that Mage should add the Sphere level like Dark Ages does. Dark Ages wizards are more powerful because it's set in the Mythic Age. Mage proper is the Storytelling Game of Modern Magic, and it has a unique style that rarely involves hucking fireballs at people. That style should be considered when deciding what popular influences to accept.

    Another issue is that protagonists in blockbuster genre fiction make bad Mage characters. Usually the protagonist is some unremarkable schlub the average audience member can relate to, who through the work of fate becomes exalted through magical gifts or lightning-fast skill development. Without their abilities these characters would still be unremarkable schlubs. This does not work thematically, or even metaphysically in my opinion, in Mage. Boring people do not Awaken. People who see the world the way everyone else does are by definition part of the Consensus. If fate is involved, it is in the matching of wakeable Avatars with humans who are worthy of them.

    I'm not trying to shame people for being influenced by incongruous media. It's really hard to resist this stuff, and we're all susceptible to some degree.

          Completely agreed. A starting Mage PC can make a Charm (Prime 2) that teleports them to a familiar place of their choosing (Correspondence 3). It's just that this is slow and deliberate, while in another game (V20, DnD) you could start with some sort of basic teleportation, like the Path of Mercury from Thaumaturgy.

          The problem is that this Mage PC can give these charms to their sleeper boyfriend, who believes strongly enough in their magick to make them work for him. This Mage PC can throw one to her mentor who is trapped in an alley with a gang of Vampires. This Mage PC can sell them for a favour at the local Chantry. She is so much more powerful than a random Tremere who can blink through a wall.

          Mages are:

          - by their very nature, people with something interesting to say about the world, someone who cares about something (even if it's something terrible). They're lovers and poets, terrorists and warriors, saviours and visionaries, tricksters and artisans.

          - FLEXIBLE. All this power can take whatever form they choose, as long as they can justify it to themselves and the world.

          So I'd recommend giving such players a really clear statement about the nature of Sphere Magick, perhaps with an example like our Teleportation Potion witch. And then you can swap it out and say "or the charms could be ones where you throw them on the ground and they summon an object from your Sanctum". "Or she has Forces 2 instead of Correspondence 3, and the explosive contents of the potion bottle are whipped into an inferno when you chuck them". Etc. etc.

                                  Originally posted by Faradn View Post

                                  I don't entirely disagree with the "boring people" bit (but I don't entirely agree either, but that can wait for another discussion). However, I am trying to figure out what kind of characters you are referring to who are the "unremarkable schlubs." It is entirely possible we're watching different shows, and I know that there have been discussions about the "everyman protagonist in unrealistic situations" trope that have come up (though more often with unrealistic expectations created by having the likes of, say, Jason Statham's chiseled abs somehow being representative of the average blue collar guy's physique), but a lot of the media I do see has characters that may SEEM boring but who have something weird happening in secret or under the surface. Take Neo - Thomas Anderson absolutely is not the kind of person who should Awaken, much less into that kind of destiny, but that is a facade. He does that to pay the bills because even the wads he gets from selling illegal programs isn't enough to actually live on (or perhaps he started sell those programs to supplement the income and have some fun). But he already was on the path to something out of the ordinary - given that computer hacking is effectively part of the paradigm, he was effectively starting to learn sorcery as he built up towards awakening (admittedly the real paradigm is a mix of VA and Akashic but it's still a step on the path). Dr. Strange could be seen as part of the system, but he also wasn't really average by any stretch of the imagination (I can say that I don't know any brain surgeons in real life that I know of).

                                  In fact, if you look at the MCU through a Mage lens (and it is on the list of inspirations for a reason - almost every superhero origin film can be reframed as an Awakening, albeit a technomagical if not Techocratic kind), I'd argue that of the actual heroes on the field in the Avengers film series thus far, only Steve Rogers and Scott Lang can be argued to have been "average people" in the origins we've seen (Peter Parker may qualify, but we have luckily not been subjected to yet another retelling of how Uncle Ben got killed and it was all Peter's fault in this iteration). Tony Stark was already a billionaire - part of the system but not ordinary and got into trouble because of that system and turned it against itself. Bruce Banner was exploring strange scientific theories and projects. Natasha Romanov was raised to be a super spy.

                                  Now, again, I may be just watching the superhero stuff and missing something else in the blockbuster field. But it really doesn't seem like those characters are truly ordinary, at least not in the modern genre. Maybe Sarah Conner and John McClaine COULD fit the bill to some extent, but it's been a while (and only the first time around for both of them).

                                  --

                                  That said, I do understand the issues of modern media featuring magic, especially films and TV shows of all sorts (animated and otherwise). I think the best way to handle that is to look for the movies on the list that capture the theme but aren't as explicit. The Matrix works really well - not only is it so classic an example of an awakening and Ascension's themes that the authors of the Revised VA TradBook credited it with increasing the number of Awakenings in-universe, but it also is a great example of how coincidental magic and magic that doesn't look like magic can work. Is it possible to punch through a wall in a cheap tenement apartment? Sure. But it's hard to do, and harder if you don't believe you can. Even vulgar changes don't have to be flashy - they boxed in Mouse with insta-brick walls and the only reason they realized was because Neo had deja vu (paradox, because the spell was still vulgar given the timeframe but went unobserved) and someone watching from without spotted something odd (which doesn't really have an analog readily in mind). Leaping across a street is impossible for just about anyone - but it isn't an obvious act of magic if the tools/foci are your will and the "knowledge" that the world isn't real. That movie is a great example of how battle magic can work without it looking magical (the mechanics do kind of need to catch up, I admit, but there are ways to make it work); it is all visually impressive, but many of the effects are potentially coincidental until you string them together, and even the impossible ones, the "vulgar effects," are impossible not because they are flashy and "magical" but because they do things (up to just stopping the bullets) that are extensions of theoretically possible things taken to impossible levels (dodging projectiles is possible at some speed, but dodging bullets is more a matter of luck, and that many in a row hits impossibility). Almost everything you see is either the result of ritual preparation (the gun room, setting up an "escape" point with the land lines) or an example of on the spot casting (hell, downloading skills can be seen as the Dream Background), all done because they believe they have awakened past the world around them and are fighting the system in a war for belief. My suggestion is to use that as your basis and build from there.

                                  -

                                  EDIT: I actually figured out what the Operator would correspond to, and when I realized that the entire idea of Matrix-as-Mage introduction clicked (and re-emphasized the VA elements along with the Akashic). The Operator is the voice of your magic when it gives you information or warns you of things. The role could be seen as an Embodied Avatar of a sort, but also could just be the result of a magic spell that tells you the dangers around you and guides you through trouble or uncertainty - a long-lasting Time spell warning of danger, or an Entropy spell whispering the luckiest routes out. But you can't ask that voice for advice or help without a proper tool to channel your will... like say, a cell phone, like the ones they use to reach out to their operator all the time.

                                          One other idea to consider for showing unobvious magic (not quite as useful for getting the themes of the world across unfortunately, but still relevant): there are a few trope pages that may be worth looking into for examples. A lot of magical visual media (movies, TV, animation) run off of the spectacle that would bring down paradox as you point out - anime, video games, and Doctor Strange both get a lot of mileage out of Instant Runes, for instance, and Power Glows are all over the place. But there are examples of invisible powers. A big one is the Coconut Superpowers trope. It's usually derided or at least politely ignored (the former when it comes across as a conscious choice to be cheap or lazy, the latter when it's admitted that the budget doesn't support the concept), but a lot of "psychic powers" and powers that "happen off-screen" can be great examples of how magic that isn't visually spectacular can work. Maybe Magic Maybe Mundane has some odder examples, but some of those may be useful as things that could be mundane enough to slide under the Consensus.
