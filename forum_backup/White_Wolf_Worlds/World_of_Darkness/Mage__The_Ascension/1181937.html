          For starters consider a lower cap for spheres (1 or 2). It helps quite a bit to cut down on the possibility of the players to turn your plot in a unrecognizable mess. You ought to be familiar with the Paradigm and the possible applications of the spheres (and the other stuff) of the PC's.

                  Come up with a loose plot outline for the game you want to run.
                  Develop some NPCs with a few footnotes.
                  Come up with a few important locations to what story your are telling.
                  Look at the characters people create, pay attention to their Paradigms and choices in spheres
                  Work out what is relevant from the characters they have created to the type of story you have crib notes for.
                  Look at the challenges, looks at the character notes, work out HOW the characters can overcome those challenges, add footnotes accordingly. Seriously DO THIS if nothing else.
                  Be ready to change the course of events to fit the employment of MAGICK against the antagonists or story challenges
                  Work from Session to Session with your Plot Notes as a guideline for what the Antagonists are up to, not as a script to be followed. Writing loads of material in advance to have it get changes massively in session one is a waste of time and effort.
                  Let the characters succeed but also let them fail.
                  If the players pull some Effect that's going to 'skip' them way ahead in terms of where they are in the story, let them, give it to them, revel in their ingenuity and then refer to your antagonist notes and plot notes and react to events accordingly.
                  Flexibility is your watchword, understanding the world that your running the game in is essential (this means you can improv on the fly because you already know the whys and withertoos of your chronicle). Essentially think of the Setting + Chronicle as Your Character. Also do not be afraid to say to the player group "Yes, that works, however guys I need a moment or two (or need to call the session early) because that caught me unprepared and after this scene I have nothing". You'll do this less and less as you get used to the characters and you settle into STing a given chronicle, but it's always a tool rather than trying to push on when you're on the back foot.

                  Hope this helps.

                      7 players is a bit much in my opinion. My most recent game had 6 and that was already above my limit to deal with.
                      You simply cannot give so many players equal weight in the story, someone is going to get the short end of the plot stick, feel left out, and disengage.

                      It might be worth dividing the group up or even turning people away, especially if this is your first game. 4 would be a better number for a first time STer, though if you’re confident, you may be able to go to 5.

                      Anyway. To start planing you only need two things. A rough idea of the story you want to tell, and the stories your player wants to experience.
                      Sit down with them one by one and go through character creation. Find out who they want to play, how they do magic, what their backstory is, and what sort of narrative they want, then fit that in around the story you want to tell.

                          Agreed with Saikou. Seven players would be a challenge for even an experienced Storyteller. Don't be afraid to break up the groups or turn people away. Otherwise, no one wins in this situation. Not you, and not the players.


                          As for game-running advice, make sure every player can answer a simple question: "What are your characters goals, both short-term and long-term?" Player characters should have reasons for why they aren't living normal lives (or as normal as Mages can get). If the players are to remain invested in the events going on - if they're to avoid being reluctant participants in this game, who need to be dragged everywhere - they need to have goals. Real goals, rather than common goals like "get money", which they could accomplish without getting involved in Ascension War bullshit.

                          It's like the advice I'd give to DnD players/DMs: If this is an adventure, give a reason why the PCs are Adventurers, rather than commoners.

                          Making the players invent compelling reasons to participate in the game has multiple benefits. As stated before, it keeps players invested. It also helps create stories, which is the whole reason we're engaged in this Storyteller System to begin with. Lastly, if the players tell you their characters's goals, you can then integrate them into the larger game. Use their long-term goals as plot hooks and incentives.


                          One more thing: Consider your antagonists and other major NPCs, not as foils in some scripted story, but as independent actors in the world. Just as the PCs have their goals, so too should the NPCs. Goals they seek to fulfill, and that they work towards. Consider, then, what an NPC or antagonist's goals are, then determine what they will do if allowed to pursue them.

                          The villains should pursue sordid aims, as befits them. Don't make these all-or-nothing goals, though, that the players MUST engage with. Like, if the villain's plans succeed, it shouldn't destroy the world or the city or topple a major sect or whatever. If the stakes are too high, the players have no choice but to intervene, which subtracts from their agency (in addition to being more than a little trite and melodramatic). Especially in the early parts of a game, it's best to keep the plots and plans down to earth, with personal or local stakes. It means the PCs aren't overly punished for failing to stop the plan, or even in failing to investigate it.

                          But there ought to be consequences, obviously, for such failure. It just tends to be ones that lead to small problems, that can then build into bigger problems. If the PCs fail to stop a cult from summoning a demon into the world, well now there's a demon running around, doing stuff. That can't be good. It's best to make villain plots play out in successive phases, so if a villain succeeds in their plan, they move on to the next phase in their larger strategy. This is why determining the long-term goals of NPCs is as important as that of the PCs. It helps, when that "front" advances, to answer the age old question: Now What?

                              First off, if you want a campaign, you have to have some campaign management plan. I suggest books such as Odyssey by Engine Publishing which help you think about how to manage a

                              campaign. Campaigns where you put thought into what the players want, how much time you have to prep, how you prep and where you get the prep done are going to last longer than those where you just 'wing it.'

                              I don't know if there's some formal term for it, but every campaign of mine now that I'm not a teenage munchkin running the game uses a few basic premises:

                              1. I figure out the general themes and tone of the campaign I want to run. Mage has a good set of questions to get you going.

                              For my Traditions (and one Disparate since I have two players right now) mage game set in Portland, Oregon, here are the concept, themes and mood options I am using:

                              Concept: The long winter is over and the flower of the Traditions can rise again with proper gardening.
                              Themes: hope amidst a world stuck between declension and ossification, the power of a small group of people to change the world, sense of wonder at the complexity of creation.
                              Mood Options: high righteous action, skullduggery, erudition, at a moral crossroads.

                              There are some big words in there, but since Portland is the Rose City (among other names), the idea is that the Traditions will rise and find a path between the descent of the Nephandi and trend towardless meaninglessness in our society and a rigid society of the Technocracy. Part of the game is an exploration of the crazy nature of reality, which is both erudite and wonder. Depending on how the players are acting, they could be engaged doing deeds of greatness against clearly bad people, engaged in more gray practices or struggling with a decision to make.

                              The high action righteous action was taking down some Nephandi terrorists, the skullduggery was a job finding a flower for the Technocracy (mage plot #89 from these forums), erudition happens when the PCs start learning about new reality and there have been a lot of moral crossroads with a Nephandi wilderslainte in bloom, a mother and child in need of help and a cabal member who's sleeping with the enemy and who some people want dead and others have not acted to eliminate as a security risk.

                              I make things work with 'villain-first' plotting. That is, I figure out some villains that fit the general themes I want to run with and which are challenges to the players. Some I define and others are 'need someone who will oppose the melee fighter PC.' Each NPC in the world who's significant has goals and a history and will work towards their goals and act based on that history, regardless of what the PCs do. Each NPC is not omniscient or omnipotent, so they will be surprised when the PCs do something unexpected and they will lose if challenged in the proper way.

                              There are always consequences for PC actions or for PC inaction, but unless the campaign is about an apocalypse, PCs should change the world but not have the world revolve around them.

                              Mages are special people, so consider what the players want to do. As for mechanics, at rank 3 in a sphere, a mage can do a lot. You don't necessarily have to limit spheres to 1 or 2, but be aware that creative players can do a lot with magick. Even just having rank 1 in a sphere, the perception level, lets a mage know a bunch of things ordinary people wouldn't know if the mage's player has any investigative acumen.

                              Mage is a game about belief and does not require a traditional adventuring party, so be sure to work through character creation with the players. You'll want to provide challenges and stories that the players want to engage.

                              As for the number of players, 7 is a lot for many games and especially a story-focused game. If you can make a trusted player an assistant GM handling parts of the world or helping with the mechanics, that might make the game a bit easier to run.

                              Finally, have a series of pitches on the game varieties you'd like to run and be willing to accept some changes to those ideas. For example, if everyone wants ass-kicking adventure with magick, and you had one city to use for the game that doesn't fit, consider another site. If your campaign idea was a rigid society and the players aren't into those themes, consider whether you want to let them be rebels or maybe consider softening some of those edges to make play better.

                              Good luck!

                                  Oh, and as for published resources, Mage 20 has a ton of suggestions and you have things such as Tales of Magick: Dark Adventure. Other RPGs may have scenarios you can use or maps you can use.

                                  You can also mine the various threads for how to do a particular fiction piece as a Mage game. Finally, do not be afraid to borrow bits of the real world and the fictional world that are interesting for your Mage game.

                                  For example, I lived in Portland at one point, so some of how the city works is based on my memories of it and there are events inspired by my time there. Other things are more drawn from fiction--so if the players ever run into a Void Engineer space marine pilot they might recognize Captain Vansen from Space Above and Beyond.
