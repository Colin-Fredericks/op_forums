    Spellcasting Revised

    02-08-2019, 10:12 AM
    Hello!

    So, I realize that some players still love the original 1st Edition Magick Rules. I, however, am not one of those. I believe that the new system is much closer to the metaphysics of the system, which is what I love about Mage. My one complaint about it, in fact, is that it did not go far enough in removing itself from First Edition.

    Therefore, I decided to make some changes to the Spellcasting System, incorporating some rules from Awakening, Ars Magica, FATE, and even something from the new Kult, but mainly by removing some inconsistencies from it - for example, including a table for success based on Spell Area, which surprisingly didn't exist - and trying to move it even closer to the way Magick works in the setting and established fiction.

    This is very much a work in progress; not only it is highly incomplete, being comprised of just what I wrote so far, it is completely subject to change. I hope you like it, and would LOVE to receive feedback on it, as well as constructive criticism that may improve it as it is written.

    Mage: The Ascension
    Spellcasting Revised



    Basic Premises: although this text mostly does not deal with the metaphysics of True Magick, but mainly with rules systems (the “crunch”), there are two premises of the game itself that define how the “crunch” interfaces with the “fluff” (the act of playing the game itself, the roleplaying and metaphysic of the setting), which are fundamental to understanding how the whole system works, and one that derives from the sum of the initial two.

    The first, The Fiction, defines how the rules interact with the game itself, and the second, The Axis of Coincidence, determines how spellcasting interacts with reality. Their result, PBD, or Process-Based Determinism, mixes both premises to show how Magick works in the system.

    The Fiction: The fiction consists of the established facts within the game world’s narrative. These constitute the laws regulating what makes sense in the setting. These are rules known only to and agreed upon by the game group.

    For example, the player’s character having the attribute Appearance 5 is not an aspect of the fiction. But, if the same player describes their character as a lithe build, long, lustrous hair and gleaming almond eyes, then this is a part of the fiction. Game rules affect the fiction and provide guidelines for what might happen in the story, but only the players know about these rules on the meta-level. The player characters’ reality is ‘the fiction.’ Part of the GM’s job is make sure the fiction is equally ‘real’ for the players.

    When the GM asks a player what their PC’s Deadly Stare merit looks like and the player describes her character’s piercing, ice-cold eyes make people feel uncomfortable, it becomes a fact established in the fiction. It affects what the PC is capable of and how other characters in the game world react to the PC.

    This is the basis upon which all rules of the games are built. In the example above, the character’s Deadly Stare does not represent a generic bonus to a skill roll. If an NPC is not in a situation where they may look at the PC’s eyes, their Deadly Stare is irrelevant.

    The actions in the game are always derived from the fiction. If a PC is shot in the leg, they will be in pain and any actions requiring two fully functioning legs are now an ordeal. When adjudicating die roles, the Storyteller should always consider the fiction and what’s already been established to determine if it makes sense. If the rules of the game contradict the fiction the group has established, precedence should always be given to the fiction.

    Another way to say this is: when fluff and crunch meet, fluff wins.

    To say that this affects spellcasting is an understatement; it is better to say that this defines spellcasting. When a Technocratic agent uses a Fo3/Pr2 Effect to fire bolts of plasma from his Surtr MkII Rifle, the rifle is not just a “prop”. It is an actual gun in his hand, firing plasma. If he forgets it in his car, he won’t be shooting plasma anytime soon. Same if some Supertitionist takes it from his hand. However, in the same vein that it is a real object in his hand, if push comes to shove, he might just bash the Supertitionist’s head with it…

    In the same vein, if an Euthanatos Killer Saint develops a Formulaic Spell called “Spitting Tongue of Shiva”, where he writes the name of the Goddes in his bullets, which burst into flame when shot from his consecrated handgun (also a Fo3/Pr2 effect), if he is out of marked bullets, he’s also out of fireballs, unless he freecasts some prayer on the spot.

    The Axis of Coincidence: Coincidental Magick is not called such because it can be disguised or explained away as “coincidence”. Magick is Coincidental when it coincides with the Paradigm of the Reality Zone where it takes place. By the same logic, Magick is Vulgar when it goes against said Paradigm. This conflict between Magick and Reality is what creates Paradox.

    What this means is that the system assumes the stance of Hypothetical Omniscient Observer, meaning that reality itself is the ultimate judge of coincidence. However, this does not mean that nothing the Mage does is Vulgar. What it means is that Coincidental magic is not just the act of “tricking” Sleepers. An Effect is not Coincidental if a Mage summons a gun inside his pocket just because no Sleeper witnessed the summoning happening. This Effect is Vulgar without Witnesses, seeing as it went against the local Paradigm (guns don’t just appear in people’s pockets), but no one saw it take place (people may see the gun itself, but no the act of summoning it).

    If an Akashic Master, fighting with a Technocratic Agent in a mall filled with Sleepers uses a Time Effect to foresee his punches a few seconds earlier, his Effect is Coincidental, as he is only affecting his inner reality; the Sleepers are only seeing the outer effects of a very good fighter. The same happens if the Agent uses Entropy and Forces, along with his superior training and VDAS, to assist his aim. Unbelief may still happen, and if the fight takes long, the Domino Effect (several Coincidental Effects stretching Reality to its breaking point) will eventually affect both of them.

    However, if both were fighting in an abandoned warehouse, and the Akashic Master decided to summon Wu Long himself to fight on his behalf, this Effect would be Vulgar without witnesses.
    Under different systems, the separation between Coincidental, Vulgar without witnesses and vulgar with witnesses breaks down, and at least one of the three stops making sense in most cases.

    PBD – Process Based Determinism: in a quick summary, Process Based Determinism means that the process you follow determines the results you get. To use a famous example from the Mage Core Book, to summon a taxi to get somewhere, a Mage might use three different processes:
    • Employ the Spheres necessary to grab an existing taxi (maybe Correspondence 3 to find a taxi, Mind 2 to nudge the driver in your direction, and Entropy 2 to tilt the odds of finding a taxi in your favor);
    • Employ the Spheres necessary to create a taxi and driver from thin air (Matter 4 to conjure the complicated mix of materials; Forces 3 to propel them using internal combustion and electrical currents and to conjure them from nothing; Life 5/ Mind 5 to create a capable driver; Prime 2 to fuel the entire mess; several dots in various Crafts and Science with specialties in Biology and Human Anatomy; and a metric shit-ton of successes scored on an incredibly vulgar Effect); or…
    • Employ Entropy 2 to tilt the odds in your favor; score a LOT of successes unless you’re in a busy urban area; and expect to be waiting a while before getting whatever happens to arrive.
    Merely tossing out a Correspondence 3 teleportation Effect and then hailing a taxi as the coincidence that happens to manifest in time to transport you to your destination will not do the job. Unless, of course, you want to play Mage: Toon Edition.

    To sum up, in order to create an Effect, a Mage must follow Process-Based Determinism to define the Spheres / Skills / Tools required to achieve it, fit it to the Fiction of the Game and determine, based on the local Paradigm, if it will be Coincidental or Vulgar.

    Spellcasting and Effects: Magick, essentially, is the application of Enlightened Will in order to affect reality. These alterations in Reality are called by many names, depending on the Mage’s background, culture and practices. They may be called spells, procedures, prayers, etc. In game terms, however, they are called Effects.

    To obtain an Effect, the Mage must first visualize it in his mind. This mental image is called and Imago, our thought form. Again, in game terms, the player decides all the aspects of the Effect before rolling the dice. These aspects are: Method, Action and Intent. After that, the player rolls, and the Effect’s Result and Consequences are determined.

    Methods of Magick: There are two methods of Spellcasting: Spontaneous (or “freecasting”) and Formulaic, or “rote”, Magick.
    • Spontaneous Magick: the creation of effects without known formulae, based only on the Mage’s Will and knowledge of the Spheres themselves. Any spell may be cast spontaneously, but the risks are higher, and the chance of success is lower. Spontaneous magic cannot be ritualized. A mage rolls his Arete to cast spontaneous spells.
      • Roll: Arete.
      • Extra Successes: 1 / 1
    Formulaic Magick: formulaic magic makes use of formulae (or “rotes”), either handed down from tutors and mentors, learned from books or created by the Mages themselves over the course of their lives. Formulaic Magic can be cast in an instant, channeled of ritual manner.To learn a formula, a mage must spend experience points equal to double the highest Sphere used, modified by any relevant variables in the spell, as seen on “Formulae Cost Modifiers”, below.
    • Roll: Arete + Formula’s designated ability.
    Casting Actions: instant (or fast) casting, extended casting (or channeling) and ritual magic.
    • Instant Casting: instant – or fast – casting takes only a moment, allowing the mage to cast a spell as part of another, usually combat, action. In many cases, a mage in the heat of combat is fast-casting – that is, making stuff up in the heat of that moment. A fast-casting character adds +1 to her usual difficulty. By its own nature, instant casting only allows one roll.
    Extended Casting: extended casting, also known as channeling, is the most frequent form of spellcasting; neither as complex as ritual magick, nor risky and sloppy as fastcasting, it involves focusing the mage’s Will in a deliberate manner over the course of seconds or minutes until the desired result is obtained. Every roll takes a full-turn action, and the spell is complete as soon as all the required successes are met. The maximum number of times a mage may channel safely is equal to their Arete. After that, it gets dangerous.A Mage that wants to keep channeling after that must roll they Willpower, with a difficulty equal to (Effect difficulty + times they rolled their Willpower), of the Spell will fizzle. If they botch, so too does their spell.
    • Ritual Casting: more complex Effects, which cause long-lasting results, or affect a wide range of targets or area, demand ritual casting. The rules for ritual casting are similar to the rules for extended casting, but they differ in that the caster may role a lot more times, increasing the chance of obtaining stronger effects. The price for this, however, comes in cost, Quintessence and time. To cast any ritual more complex than a brief rite, the mage must spend a point of Quintessence and, after determining their Intent, check how many successes are required. The number of necessary successes determine the length of each spellcasting rolls, as follows:
    Ritual Necessary Successes Roll Time
    Brief Rite Up to Caster's Arete X2 1 minute
    Ritual Up to Caster's Arete X3 10 minutes
    Ceremony Up to Caster Arete X5 1 hour
    Great Work Over Arete X5 5 hours






    Also, magickal rituals stir up a lot of reality. Every roll after the first one adds one point of Paradox to the caster’s total. If the ritual concludes successfully, then those extra points of Paradox go away. If the ritual is botched, however, then the Paradox backlash adds those additional points of Paradox onto the Paradox that the mage would suffer to begin with.

    The upside to this, however, is that for ritual spellcasting, the mage may roll up to (Arete + Willpower) times before starting to roll his Willpower and endangering the spell. That is, if their body can take the stress.

    Rituals and Stamina: extended rites are exhausting. Barred outside modifiers, a character may work for one hour without penalty for each dot in their Stamina Trait. After that, the player must make a Stamina roll for each subsequent hour, with an initial difficulty of 6. A successful roll allows the mage to go on for another hour; the second roll suffers a +1 penalty to its difficulty; the third suffers a +2 penalty, and so on.

    A failed roll means that exhaustion has set in. At that point, the mage may can either call off the ritual or spend a point of Willpower to keep going. If they keep going, the next Stamina roll suffers an additional +1 penalty as above… after all, the mage is seriously running out of steam!

    A botched Stamina roll counts as Interference, below. In story terms, the mage starts fumbling around in a daze and must struggle to keep the rite from ending in catastrophe.
    Interference: if an outside party disrupts a ritual – say, by attacking the rite or distracting the caster – then the mage in charge of that ritual must make a Willpower roll, difficulty 8, or else botch the entire deal. If they successfully keep things together, the ritual proceeds as if there had been a botch rolled; a second turn of interference brings the hammer down and the ritual to a crashing halt.

    Preparation: By their nature, rituals tend to employ mundane Abilities – Art, Computer, Esoterica, Technology, and so forth. In story terms, the activity provides the focus for that ritual; and in game terms, a previous Attribute + Ability roll not only is usually necessary for the ritual itself but may lower the difficulty of the rite.
    Each success with a mundane activity roll during the preparation of the ritual reduces the Effect roll difficulty by -1, to a maximum reduction of -3. The difficulty for that roll is the base difficulty for the ritual itself, and may take as much time as the ritual itself.

    Taking Breaks During a Great Work: Great Works aren’t usually one-sitting projects. A mage could spend days, weeks, or longer dedicating themselves to a Great Work. In game terms, a mage may stop for break during a Great Work, hanging the process by spending a Willpower point and then resuming the effort after a short interval.

    To pick up where the ritual stopped, the mage must make a Wits + Esoterica roll (or Wits + Technology for acts of enlightened hypertech). That roll’s difficulty begins at 7, and then goes up by +1 for each break taken after the first one. So long as the work area remains undisturbed, and no more than 24 hours pass between sessions, the mage may continue to extend the process. One failed roll, however, ruins the process and forces the mage to begin again… which explains why so many Great Works take a long time to perform.

    Acting in Concert: a sympathetic crowd can make a ritual feel coincidental, and assistants can lower the difficulty for a magickal Effect. Allied mages can also help out by acting in concert with the caster, and a Cult (as in the Background Trait of that name) adds to the caster’s dice pool. In short, then, a group ritual can be incredibly beneficial, provided that the mage is willing to deal with the increased risks and potential complications of getting a bunch of people to cooperate on an important project.

    Common Ground: when several characters collaborate within a single ritual, they must all share a certain amount of common ground:
    • Each mage involved must have at least one dot in each of the Spheres involved in the ritual’s Effect.
    • All participants must remain in contact with one another for the duration of the ritual, able to communicate freely through whatever means work best for them: telepathy, speech, Instant Messaging, texting, etc.
    • Those participants need to work out a shared approach to the ritual; after all, an Etherite in a shamanic rite will probably do more harm than good. That said, mages from distinct yet allied groups can collaborate if they approach one another’s practices with respect and attention. Story-wise, those collaborators need to hash out some common cues and protocols… which, in game terms, will probably involve a few social feat rolls before all is said and done. Once they’ve established a common base to work from, the allies can focus their intentions through a shared ritual.
    The Collaborators: the casting group can work together in one of two ways:
    • Equal Collaborators – that is, mages who have memorized the ritual – can roll their Arete and then add their successes toward the success of the ritual as a whole. The Effect’s normal difficulty and modifiers apply equally to all such collaborators.
    • Enlightened assistants: other mages add one automatic success to the Effect, up to a maximum of five successes, total. These successes, however, DO NOT APPLY to cancel out a botch. If the caster botches the roll, then those assistants’ successes disappear. The additional successes count only to enhance a successful Effect. Only one option may be used at a time – not both.
    • Sleeper Assistants: acolytes, cultists, citizens, and so forth can add certain benefits to the ritual as well:
      • The Cult Background adds one die to the caster’s dice pool, up to a maximum of five dice. And yes, those extra dice may also help to botch a casting roll – cults can screw things up as well as help things along.
      • If a group manages to include over 100 people, certain vulgar Effects (summonings, gateways, raisings of the dead, and so forth) may be considered coincidental if there are no other witnesses around to contradict that impression. A solitary rite on the Moors is far more effective in that regard than a concert in a nightclub downtown.
    Downsides and Risks: the downsides of collaboration include the difficulty of wrangling several people toward a common goal (especially if they don’t share a common belief system); coordinating logistics for large gatherings (which usually requires time, work, space, and often money); and the potential to screw up on a grand scale. Grand, as in if one caster botches a casting roll (see below), every participant suffers the Paradox, either by adding the Paradox points to their individual pools or by enduring a large-scale blast that affects everyone – Awakened and otherwise – who happens to be near Ground Zero at the time.

    Despite the risks and costs, collaboration provides a sense of security and fellowship. Solitary rites do have their own kind of significance, but we humans tend to be sociable by nature, and our group rituals – magickal and otherwise
    .
    Intent: The Intent of the Effect determines what changes the caster wishes to perform on Reality. This is divided in the following aspects: Potency (or Damage), Target, Duration and Range. Usually, at least one success must be spent for each aspect. The first success is called the basic success.
    • Potency: Brief, simple spells are easy to cast; larger, more complicated effects take time and effort.
      • Personal Effects that make minor changes in the mage’s own reality require only one success. These include sensory-perception enhancements, divinations, self-healing,
      • temporary Trait boosts, minor cosmetic changes (hair color, skin color, height, etc.) and the like. Extreme or long-lasting alterations – radical shape changes, multiple selves, transformations into elemental forces, and so forth – require three successes or more.
      • Effects that change reality for another person or object require at least two successes. For this reason, a single success inflicts no damage upon an opponent – damage begins at two successes. An unwilling target can also try to resist or dodge an attack. In order to get around that resistance, a mage might need to roll additional successes; the more successes, the more damage you inflict.
      • Effects that significantly change the mage’s surroundings, even if that’s only on a local level, require at least five successes. Large-scale alterations can demand 10, 20, even 30 successes, whereas small and subtle ones are much easier to achieve.
    This aspect of an Effect is both the most variable and subjective, and demand both the GM’s judgement and approval.
    A mage may also add successes to Potency in order to make the spell harder to counter or dispel, at a rating of one additional Potency per extra success.
    • Range: The reach of a mage’s powers is limited to the extent of their awareness. Of course, with magic, a mage’s awareness can extend very far indeed. Effects may have four different Ranges: Personal, Sensorial or Sympathetic.
    • a personal effect affects only the mage themselves. Personal effects do not require successes for range.
    Sensorial: Most spells rely on a mage’s mundane senses. They can cast a spell on any target distinguishable by sight, hearing or touch and who’s in the mage’s presence. If there’s some question as to whether or not the mage can distinguish the target, a penalty may be applied the mage’s spellcasting pool. Usually, methods other than sight are not ideal, since even the best attempt to pinpoint a target gives them partial concealment. Targets that are still within sight but that are extremely far away, such as a sniper on a distant building, are not considered to be within the mage’s presence. Generally, mages cannot affect targets more than 100 meters away without the use of the Correspondence Sphere.

    Targets that cannot be sensed, or that can be sensed but are farther than 100 meters away, or that are visible or audible to the mage but not in their presence — seen on live television or heard over the telephone, for example — require sympathetic spellcasting. In the case of someone seen or heard live over the television or telephone, the sensory impression of the target provides a strong connection.

    Sensory range requires one success.

    Sympathetic Effects: Students of the Correspondence Sphere learn that distance is an illusion, a limit magic can overcome. By using Correspondence, a mage can extend the reach of their magic virtually anywhere in the Tellurian.

    Effects including a Correspondence element to increase range are called sympathetic Effects, since they take advantage of what some mages call the Law of Sympathy: “Like produces like.” Sprinkling rain onto a map, scrying into a bowl of ink mixed with a drop of the subject’s blood, sticking pins into a puppet containing a lock of the subject’s hair — all of these are examples of sympathetic spells. Despite the name, members of the Union use the same rule for sympathetic spells; DNA tests, satellite images and cellphone tracking are as effective as the more arcane methods of Council mages.

    To cast a sympathetic spell, the mage must have at least (highest Sphere – 1) in Correspondence to cast the Effect. The stronger the connection between the mage and the target, the easier it is to include the target in the effect.

    Instead of demanding additional successes based on the distance involved (distance is an illusion), these are added based on the vagueness of the connection to the target. The less a mage knows about the subject, the higher the penalty — the greater his sympathetic “distance” from the target.
    The difficulty for casting a spell without knowing the name of the target increase by two. However, the difficulty of casting sympathetic spells on a target whose True Name is known by the caster is reduced by one.

    Sympathetic casting can be very powerful, but it is not without risks. According to the law of sympathy, a mage who casts a sympathetic spell is forging a link to the target. Because of this, the target may affect the caster as though they are at sensorial range, and may cast spells at them without the knowledge of Correspondence, for as long as the spell lasts, and for additional rounds equal to the highest Sphere rank used in the initial casting.

    Very familiar Close possession or companion 2
    Familiar Possession or casual friend 3
    Visited Once Casual acquaintance, or object used once 4
    Described location Briefly touched or met object or person 5
    Anywhere on Earth No connection 6+

    • Duration: There are four types of spell Duration: instant (or lasting), concentration, transitory and prolonged.
    • a lasting Effect is a natural part of reality; it has no defined Duration, since it persists as long as it is natural for it to. For example, an Effect that sets a target on fire is lasting. The fire is real and continues to burn normally until it is put out or there’s nothing left to burn. The target’s injuries are also real and must heal normally. Likewise, a spell that heals injuries is lasting. The healing restores the target to its natural state, so wounds do not re-appear when the spell ends. Lasting effects do not require Successes for duration.
    Concentration-based, transitory and prolonged spells all create an unnatural effect, such as transforming someone’s shape. Their effects end once the spell’s Duration expires. That is, reality restores things to normal. Spells with a lasting Duration do not count against the maximum number of spells a mage can maintain. Concentration Duration Effects do not require successes for duration.

    Concentration: A concentration-based Effect lasts as long as the mage applies mental effort to keep it going. The mage may take no other action, except moving, talking (if the act of concentrating doesn’t demand somatic components) and defend themselves. If the mage is attacked, they must make a Willpower roll (difficulty 7) to maintain concentration.
    If the mage stops concentrating for any reason, voluntarily or not, the spell ends. The exception is if they decide to add Duration factors as if it were a transitory spell before the spell is cast, in which case the spell lasts for an additional time equal to its assigned Duration, and then lapses. The mage cannot start concentrating on a spell again once they have stopped, even if the spell hasn’t lapsed yet.

    Transitory: transitory Effects are those cast by either fast casting or channeling, which do not have instant and lasting effects. Their default duration is one turn.

However, seeing as their Patterns evolve and change, permanents spells can’t usually be applied to living beings. They may, however, be renewed.

              I think that to have a consistent system one should allow people to be surprised like that w/o changing the Axis. One can't change Coincidence post-hoc . However an aditional rule could be used to fix this hole, like to reward the mage with a dot of dox for his effort, or something.

              Another important thing: Some would argue that sligthly changing the path of a bullet mid air with Forces should be Coincident under OO because bullets do change path mid air due air friction. However one could say that an Omniscient observer would sense the air, presure, or energy output of the bullet shifting suddenly, under world physics and meteorological conditions that wouldn't normaly produce such change. Unlike a sleeper the Observer knows the state of the universe with more pressisiont than with any scientific instrument and should be able to predict the natural outcome of any action. Under such a strict omnisciense to change the path of a bullet it's always Vulgar, and that the change in direction is small only means that it would be w/o wittnesses, as nobody would be able to demonstrate that it wasn't a natural change.
              Since this is an effect that affects outside reality I think you should inform if your Observer it's that strict or if it can't know stuff that a human would need to employ advanced instruments in a controled enviroment to know
              So, first I'd like to thank you for the well-thought and considered response. It is a rare thing to see on the internet. and the exact reason why I posted my ideas on this forum.

              Now, to answer your question, and Muad'Dib's as well, to start with the broader response, I believe that the main point to understand is that this Observer is a metaphor, not a literal entity watching over every Effect performed by the mage. It's utility is to point out that Vulgarity and Coincidence are not defined in terms of Sleeper Witnesses, but by the contrast between local reality and the alterations performed by the Mage.

              Regarding the examples, I'll start by the ones I believe are easier to answer. I don't believe that slight alterations to a bullet's trajectory made by Forces would be considered Vulgar, as they don't create create waves in the Paradigm, and winds altering the course of a bullet are, up to certain degree, possible.

              By the same token, seeing as each individual has his own reality, changing yourself in discreet manners is not Vulgar. Internal impossibilities, are, therefore, "kosher".

              I like your telepathy example very much, as I believe it reaches the new grey area in the system, where Coincidence and Vulgarity meet. If you consider that each person has their own inner reality, it might depend on how you perform your Effect. If you read micro-reactions on a person's face, as well as their minuscule reactions and queues, it would be Coincident. Invading someone's mind, however, might be Vulgar.

              I entirely agree that Coincidence shouldn't be changed post-hoc. An Effect doesn't become Vulgar after the fact, unless it is a long duration ritual, for example, a Ward, that exists in a place which looses its local reality, which then would erode the Effect with Unbelief.

              To go fully to the rules side of the issue, I would argue that most cases in which Magick assists Abilities (Page 533 of the Core Book) are Coincidental, as well as most subtle Effects. The main change in this system is that acts of prestidigitation do not turn an Effect Coincidental anymore by just hiding the Effect, like the example of summoning a gun inside your pocket, above - unless the prestidigitation is, itself, an Effect!

                          Originally posted by Sardonis
                          • Effects that change reality for another person or object require at least two successes. For this reason, a single success inflicts no damage upon an opponent – damage begins at two successes. An unwilling target can also try to resist or dodge an attack. In order to get around that resistance, a mage might need to roll additional successes; the more successes, the more damage you inflict.
                          I've never liked the official mechanic to do this, that treats Dodge as Countermagick.

                          It's counter intuitive because, beyond the simple logic of things that can't be dodged and humans shouldn't be able to resist (like most curses) there's the fact that there are attacks that should be harder or impossible to dodge in it's entirely and things that, if dodged, should nulify the damage rather than merely lower it (a boulder materialized atop your head, usually dodging these it's an all or nothing affair)

                          It's also an odd combat mechanic because, normaly, when you fail to dodge you still have to eat the base damage of the weapon plus the extra successes . It's never useless to dodge because extra successes go to damage, but Dodge never deducts directly from the damage - and this allows to distinguish between weapons that normaly cause great damage, weapons that are harder to dodge for some reason (like machineguns), weapons that require extra skill...

                          To that I propose to include among the subsystems of magick a "targeting system". This may be as simple as the Revised option of: Stuff that need to be targeted (i.e: Fireball) you roll Atribute + Skill vs Dodge - using magick like any weapon. Or a more nuanced system where each different sort of targeting (can't be evaded, can be partially evaded, "all or nothing" effects) has different rules and costs (complete with "minimum damages", like the fireball causing, at least, half damage w/o special conditions like cover)

                          Formulae Cost Modifiers
                          Since you're using the "divide successes" rule, that was criticized as not allowing mages cast anything meaningfull on the spot, I propose to craft modifiers that allow to ameliorate the number of successes needed. For instance: A wizard likes D&D and want's to cast a "chain lightning" effect, this normally would require great luck or several turns - or, alternatively, to create a specialized Rote costing extra xp per aditional target
