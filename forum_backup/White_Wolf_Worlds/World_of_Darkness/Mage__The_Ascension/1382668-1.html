    The balancing factor to using cult/unawakened acolytes from 2e onwards is that the rolls take a lot more time to do and that obviously you can't just...do that in the middle of a fight without some very creative sphere usage. And hey, if you've managed to get all those sleepers (hoping there's not a leaker/spy among them) and find a space to do an arduous long ritual for a buff, talisman or something of the sort...why shouldn't you, exactly?

    I think a lot of these discussions about how overpowered things are or can be in Mage are almost universally specific to white room situations. I'm not really convinced that Cult 'needed' a nerf, it comes across as sort of poorly thought out and a kneejerk balance meant to quell white room conversations on message boards than actual gameplay. You can't brush aside the narrative / logistical issues that you have to solve and optimise for as being irrelevant to the mechanical balance in a set of games where mechanics and narrative are encouraged to be interwoven.

    Also Cult as a Background didn't exist in 2e, you'd have to have used Guide to the Technocracy's allies above five (and be very specific) or used the Backup Background with 10 freebies to get 20 people and 4 automatic successes to the ritual.

                                    I think we've covered the original question well. The original question was, 'why was the acolyte contribution to rituals nerfed?' While we don't have a definitive answer from the designers of Mage about the changes to the use of acolytes for magic and any changes to the Cult background, having helped on the new edition of HackMaster and serving on the HackMaster War College for the AD&D 2e licensed version, I have often had to deal with the issue of the Rules As Written and what the intent was.

                                    Specifically, when a rule or statement in the game material didn't make sense to someone and they wanted to have an errata correct it, we had to recommend to the game's creators one of three options:
                                    1. The person wanting the correction didn't understand the rule or the intent. The recommendation was the rule should stand as is.
                                    2. The writing as it stands is unclear and needs a correction because it impacts play. We asked for and suggested a correction.
                                    3. There's a typographical error here or the rule is more clearly explained later with slightly different terminology. In this case, we recommended that the errata reflect the clearer interpretation.

                                    After this experience for a game that generated a 20-page errata sheet for official play, we got Rule 1 in the current edition of HackMaster.

                                    'In HackMaster, any rule ambiguity related to character creation and PC powers is construed against the player character. If you, as a player, find yourself arguing that a rule is

                                        I'm also an attorney--not surprised at all that that rule was written by a lawyer. The common law rule with regard to ambiguities in contracts is that when a provision is subject to two or more reasonable interpretations, the provision will be construed strictly against the drafter. The idea is that the drafter is in a superior position to choose wording by dint of being the party who initially formulated whatever language. Ambiguous provisions of insurance policies (which are contracts after all) are also construed strictly in favor of coverage--the insurance carrier being in a superior position to the insured, inasmuch as they drafted the policy, deliberated upon the language, and ostensibly knew what they were doing when they put pen to paper. The legal rule is obviously concerned with traditional notions of fairness: if you get the draft the language, it's only fair that you should be bound by it, otherwise you could continually change the terms on the other party by saying any given benefit for themselves they find is the result of a misinterpretation, because your intentions were actually otherwise.                                         Granted, but it's still just "The Golden Rule" with more steps. No matter how much procedure you hang around it, the rule still requires that someone exercise a Judge's fiat to determine first, whether a given rule is or isn't ambiguous (ambiguity being defined as being subject to two or more reasonable, conflicting interpretations); and then to choose which is most logical. The issue here is that whether an interpretation is "reasonable" (and therefore triggers the ambiguity rule) is not technically a matter that is objectively determinable. What is reasonable and logical is ultimately an 'eye of the beholder' problem. So even if during organized play, the GM wants to fall back on this neat little procedural trick, the issue that arises is that the player may say "well, this isn't ambiguous, because this rule we're talking about is only subject to one reasonable interpretation." At that point, the GM can only say "that's not for you to determine" and exercise their plenary authority at the table to do whatever it is they're going to do. Same for the later part of the analysis re- "logical"; a player could say "well that's not the most logical choice at all," to which the GM would say, "in my opinion, it is--moving on..."

                                        My point is that you can dress it up however you want--the solution to incessant rules lawyering that slows down play is always going to be to exercise the golden rule, and (or) simply not play with that person at all. Because you're not going to find some bit of wording that eliminates completely the need for the GM to say (and remind everyone) that they have the discretion to do literally whatever they want to in the game, and that theirs is the only discretion that matters.
