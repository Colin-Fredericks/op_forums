    Actual Science implications with the Spheres

    08-03-2017, 06:24 AM
    Hi, as a preamble, please accept my apologies if this post substantially duplicates anything that has been previously asked on this forum.

    I have two remarks about Science as it relates to the Spheres. (As an aside, IRL I am an academic in the field of Biochem. So, haha, probably part of the Technocracy.)

    Life Sphere: I'm a bit unclear on what criteria the developers were using to delineate between the categories "Simple" and "Complex" with regards to living things.

    In M20, pp.516-517 "Simple organisms – viruses, mollusks, insects, plants, etc..he can cause flowers to bloom or wither, help trees bear fruit, and so on" and "..on any complex organism – people, dogs, horses, and so forth." It appears, from reading this, that the authors' definition of complex is "mammal"...or possibly "vertebrate". The thing is, in terms of cellular architecture, any multicellular eukaryote (e.g. a plant, a mollusk (sic), arthropod) is just as evolved as a human. Sure, they may not be sentient in the same way we are, but I imagine that that particular trait would be covered by the Sphere of Mind. I accept that viruses, bacteria, protists and other extremely simple multicellulars (e.g. Porifera) can be covered by Rank 2. I'd argue that the rest of the multicellulars would be covered by the higher ranks (3+) of Life. Thoughts?

    What's the non-metagame justification for a player not using Life 3 to change ever present bacteria into a strain of MRSA? Or rhinovirus (not in a human yet, as that would require a higher rank) into Ebola?

    Other Spheres: It seems that "Science" itself, doesn't work when lined up against the Spheres. The demarcated 9 areas of magical influence are odd, when considered under the lens of Science. This might seem a pointless query to some, but I think it might be pertinent - particularly if trying to understand things from antagonist/protagonist Technocrat points of view. Consider Entropy: according to Thermodynamics, Entropy and Energy are tied together. We can talk about their relationship through Gibbs' Free Energy and the Laws of Thermodynamics. Does a Technocrat character understand the Spheres as written (as implied by "Dimensional Science"/"Primal Utility"/"Data") or is it better to explain things under the auspices of Science (i.e. Physics, Chemistry etc)? If the latter is true, how does it line up?

    I'm keen to know what others' thoughts are.

      There are a number of other places to draw the line between simple and complex life. Here are two: One of the distinctions in multicellular life is the difference between a diploblast and a triploblast. The actual definition of the two is actually based on specific stages of embryonic development, but the lay explanation is that triploblasts have a complex internal structure; a third place cells can be (inside, outside, and within). There is an argument in the biology community that the vertebrate neural crest counts as a fourth 'layer' and that vertebrates count as quadroblasts.

      As far as the various factions understandings, the Spheres (in game) are more of a way of describing a Mage's capabilities rather than a way of understanding the world. Technocracts will almost certainly think in terms of scientific principles first and 'spheres' only in their after action reports.

                      Keep in mind that in Mage, the authors are trying to explain what you can do in a world where people have many different views of life. The metaphors don't always work well.

                      Eukaryotes are more complex than prokaryotes, but I think the big reason Life 2 and Life 3 divide on complex organisms is so that your mage can't study just a little Life and start killing humans with Life magic or creating life. Forces in Mage 1e had a similarly strange split--gravity and nuclear forces required Forces 4 but you could do light and sound and make your mage invisible with Forces 2 (usually had to through in another sphere instead of flat Forces 2).

                      The Spheres are thus an in-game codification for gameplay, but every mage is going to see them operating differently. Ontogeny does not recapitulate phylogeny (Hackel's Law), but we do know you can see a lot of cool things about how life works from studying development. The same is true for physics and chemistry--some things that look really simple are in fact complex processes which depend on time and location to work out a certain way.

                      As for science, not every player of Mage will have a science degree, not every player of Mage will have paid attention in pre-college science courses and I imagine some players have religious views that make them disbelief some current scientific trends. So, ultimately, since our modern scientific method is a paradigm, not everyone will be applying this. Think of your fellow players and the Storyteller when going too sciency on them.

                      I remember trying to explain the Lotka-Volterra predator prey model in a history course to explain how one particular group of First Peoples (Canadian term for Native Americans) were the worst impacted by the British Empire. My explanation went over everyone's head and there were some pretty smart people in the room. They just didn't think about ecology in that way. . .

                          So Mary is a Physicist, and Anne is a Mystic. Mary knows that the Earth is flying through space at high speed. Anne rejected education at a young age and thus has no conception of the Earth moving.

                          Mary tries to travel forwards in time, 3 hours, using her prototype Time Machine. She succeeds, and appears in the same spot (having no Correspondence). She knows that she isn't in the same "place" in the universe, but she isn't going to appear in the midst of space with the earth having left her behind in its flight. She can explain this with technobabble; my calculations adjusted for it, the time machine's pattern is tethered to the equivalent location by a magnetic resonance field, yada yada.

                          Anne has nothing to explain away. Of COURSE she lands in the same place.

                          So basically we have the same rules being applied to people with vastly different conceptions of reality. Mary might reasonably appear mid-space if she Botched very badly, but we can almost always glaze over the scientific issues with time travel. This applies to every Sphere. Turning Carbon into a Diamond is the same Sphere effect for everyone; Anne has NO idea that Diamonds are Carbon (unless she has investigated with Matter 1), while Mary can get a Coincidental effect going here; this machine crushes Carbon into a Diamond, it's a prototype, but everyone can believe this working. Despite Anne thinking she is performing a grand transformation (from worthless coal to a perfect gem, a huge distinction in her paradigm), and Mary thinking her change is the SIMPLEST physical change in a chemical sense, they both have the same mechanics for the effect. This is a good thing, IMO; Paradigm should influence Coincidence and be effected by Reality Zones, but certain things shouldn't be much harder in some paradigms than others. If Mary thinks that these "simple" organisms are actually quite complicated, she can explain the gap by thinking about psychodynamic or quantum protections that "higher" organisms might have an advantage in. The crab isn't willing itself to stay the same shape, while the Sleeper is.

                              Originally posted by Cerulean Scream View Post
                              What's the non-metagame justification for a player not using Life 3 to change ever present bacteria into a strain of MRSA? Or rhinovirus (not in a human yet, as that would require a higher rank) into Ebola?
                              Aside from the moral and pragmatic reluctance a character might have to subject a person to such a disease (and risk causing an outbreak of infections)?

                              Well first, not everyone with Life knows what those organisms are, aside from in passing. The layman doesn't know how Ebola works. Many don't even know what it does, other than being vaguely deadly. (See also that one scene from The Simpsons where a peasant woman calls out in the streets that she's dying of Cholera, despite the fact an actual sufferer wouldn't be anywhere but in a bed, literally shitting themselves to death). If you don't know what you're trying to create, how can you create it?

                              This is why M20 presents the optional rule for Minimum Abilities, reflecting a need for a base understanding of a subject before you can use it to do magick. In our example, a character needs a number of dots in Science before they can start turning lesser microorganisms into deadly ones.

                              Further, all that assumes a character could make MRSA or Ebola at all, even if they were familiar with them from a biological standpoint. Not every Mage can. Their Focus limits the practical extent of their abilities. Even if, say, a Dreamspeaker believed that single celled organisms and viruses existed, and could cause disease, they wouldn't necessarily be able to work with them. A Shaman's training is based on practices thousands of years old, working on a theoretical framework for reality that doesn't line up with Science. What spirits does he contact to turn these tiny creatures into different, deadly ones? How does he even begin to understand what will make those spirits cooperative enough to do it for him?

                              Same with a Hermetic wizard. Which pentacle does she draw to channel MRSA? Which planet and zodiac signs govern Ebola? What words in Enochian correspond to them? What materials have sympathetic relevance that can be exploited to work with the diseases? Is Ebola higher or lower than the Common Cold on the medieval hierarchy of being?

                              Even if they personally believe the microorganisms exist, neither of these characters will necessarily have the tools to work with them. It's outside their mystical training, because tiny organisms that live in the bloodstream are a relatively recent Paradigmatic idea. At best, Life users from these Traditions would be working with what they know to afflict targets with symptoms similar to MRSA or Ebola, but which may or may not be them if a blood test is done after the fact. The Shaman contacts spirits of illness, the Hermetic tries to screw around with the target's humour balance, and neither could work extensively with the organisms outside the context of them being in a human, afflicting that human.

                              Which doesn't mean a mystic mage had to stay that way. Mages haven't been idle in the centuries up to this point. They take the changes in the Consensus and try to adapt to the shifting "meta". They experiment, do research, make educated guesses, cross-educate themselves on the subject, and talk to new spirits that arise in response to new developments. But a traditionally trained mystic is going to be lost, until they make those breakthroughs. And some don't want to.

                                      1. Spheres are a game mechanic to reflect that someone just starting to manipulate an aspect of reality can't transform things that someone who's really studied that aspect. This does calcify reality somewhat. If you really wanted to change things about a little, well I've been in a Vampire game where the first rank of Protean was claws instead of the vision and that's not necessarily game breaking. Although the spheres and the ranks are imperfect, they are good guidelines for what a person who can bend reality can and cannot do. If you have only the simplest understanding of how minds work, you should not be able to affect minds with magick because you cannot conceive of how to change someone's mind.

                                      Minimum abilities does add a layer of complexity to the game, but it means that characters who are really good at things can channel that will. It's something I like but would probably be loose on --if you can't really picture what a car looks like it will be much harder to create a working one. If you don't get the shamanic rites of Pacific Northwest First Peoples, then it's going to be hard doing something the way they do it. That being said, there are many examples of folk taxonomy working as well as more scientific taxonomy such as cladistics and Linneanism. I might define a bird differently than a Linnean by taking a cladistic perspective (to me it's part of the dinosaur line) and from one of the persons interviewed by UC Berkeley anthropologists in the early twentieth century, but we'd probably agree on certain common features and see connections to other life. I like the idea that mages are erudite, even if they know things I don't know.

                                      I deal with the fact that each mage's paradigm is not Truth by seeing Paradox as the universe's balance of counterveiling wills.

                                      2. I don't know what each group of developers interpreted. Mage started as a game which the authors made clear had some connections to Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance. I have degrees in accounting, business, biology and history, so I tend to see reality as having both objective and subjective elements. While we can argue about the subjective elements and objectivity, in the end, those discussions are not very fruitful except for getting us to think. In a game, as in life, you have to make decisions with the information you have, the beliefs you have and with some idea but not a complete ideas of potential outcomes. So for Mage, I go with the rules that have been playtested and written and try and be fair. The tone of the game matters a lot. If you've gone with high adventure, mages should be able to get away with more spectacular effects than if you make it so that most magick is vulgar and mages are in constant fear of paradox.

                                      Two of Mage's greatest strengths, its flexible spellcasting system and its definition of reality, are also its greatest weaknesses.
