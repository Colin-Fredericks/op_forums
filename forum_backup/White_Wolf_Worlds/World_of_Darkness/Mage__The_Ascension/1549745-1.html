    Fair enough, with one caveat: the Yearning means that every sorcerer sought it out. What you're pointing out is the possibility of a dabbler who's as dedicated to studying magic as sorcerers are. In game terms, this would be a character whose concept is "a dedicated student of magic," but is built using HH2/KF/GH Paths rather than Sorcerer Paths.

    But the reason I ended up using the terminology I did is because the descriptions given in HH2/KF/GH are of people who aren't primarily interested in the magic; they're only interested in it as a tool for hunting (in the case of HH2 and GH or participating in the world of their shapeshifting kin (in the case of KF). There may be exceptions to this; but they are exceptions. And as a result, the Paths in those books are described as something cobbled together by people stumbling around in the dark.

    By contrast, every edition of Sorcerer describes them as members of societies dedicated to the study and use of magic, and the Paths are something you learn from teachers who are already well-versed in them. Self-taught sorcerers are vanishingly rare.

    My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

        This presents one of the paradoxes of magic. You can play with magic your whole life and never truly step on the path. And you can just as easily tread the path without realizing it.

        In many ways, the Black Art is a destiny, not a choice. You don’t choose magic — it chooses you.
        This one makes clear that the character doesn't have to currently be that invested. They don't have a choice anymore, but that's less about dedication, continued drive or even pressure to have magic as your main calling in life, and more the pragmatic need to deal with supernatural entities becoming a fixture of your life. So, a character that "stumbled" on the Path soon in their life and dedicate most of their time and efforts to other endeavors would still be a Sorcerer, but not a dedicated student.

        I mean, sure, as presented it does indicate most of those people will be exactly like you said. But not necessarily so, not as a consequence of being a dabbler or a dedicated student. And the books don't do much to further link one to the other, which could be done. It could be the case, for example, that dedication is what causes the Yearning. Or that without it magic causes disinterest through a Mists-like effect as is common through the WoD, so that hunters can force themselves to learn something, but only the Yearning lets you keep focus and interest. It could be that the organizations Sorcerers form are what keep it like that, both denying development to those who didn't reach the Otherworld and coercing those who did to keep at it regardless.

        Heck, even the followers of Mages, which can hardly be deemed dabblers, end up using either Hedge-Magic rules or at best a couple simplified rotes, falling way behind Sorcerers.

        A lot could be done to make this about the difference between ignorant dabblers and informed dedicated students, but as is I feel they tried, but it isn't really that. Instead what we got is a new splat of magic-users that's exactly that, a new splat, if a minor one. I don't personally see it as a relevant addition to the WoD, regardless of how their rules are similar or distinct of others, or how good they are (I'm not necessarily against using their rules for every human magic-user).

        My personal headcanon is that all those are just different sets of rules to represent the same general phenomenon, and you chose which one fits best the current game.

        But all that said, I'm not dead-set on dismissing Sorcerers as a splat. Being unconvinced that they fill the role of human magic-users doesn't mean I'm not open to arguments on what make them an interesting splat on their own. I don't know them enough to already know many of those arguments, so I'm even curious.

                    Also... since I'm not paying to get a book for a thread on the Internet, what I can find that's freely available basically amounts to there not being a scholarly consensus on where to draw the line between a "sorcerer" and a "witch," or even if such a distinction is academically valid.                             I don't find anything weird about "conspicuous consumption." Anthropologists and psychologists have well established how credibility enhancing displays function as social currency by creating the appearance (real or not) of conviction in one's beliefs since only someone that really believes in something would give up valuable items for it; which is something humans tend to value as social organisms. That even applies to the wealthy (or those caught up in trying to fake it) flaunting their wealth and then reinforcing the social hierarchy that puts the rich on the top of the ladder. These constructs extend to many other animals in the form of mating behaviors that fall under the good gene hypothesis from biology. There's a deference between what feels intuitively logical on the scale of individuals, and what actually works in larger social structures and thus gets replicated and prioritized.

                            Some anthropologists trying to generate a universal definition of "witch" isn't weird because it doesn't abide by common Western conceptualizations of witches (which isn't even much of thing since that has plenty of its own variations, making it weird to assume there is one universal Western "witch" category). It's a long challenged concept because it doesn't accurately map to anything in the data. A universal definition that doesn't actually end up describing what more than a small percentage of what people mean within their cultural contexts is a useless taxonomy in anthropology.

                            The fact that it's hard to remove outdated concepts from text books is an entirely separate issue in academia; if somewhat relevant when we're told to read a text book first published in the 70s by a scholar that stayed in the field for almost three decades after that (including new editions of said text) and was strongly incentivized by the system to not overthrow his own positions despite significant push back from others in the field.

                                                        It doesn't "violates" ordinary categories more than any other term turned into technical parlance in academy. Anthropologists have a history of using terms like Sorcerer, Witch and Shaman precisely because they wanted to derive a universal theory of social roles where those things existed in every culture, just under other names.

                                                        But while this practice hasn't completely faded, it faces ever more criticism because it has no confirmation on the actual data. On top of the proponents of those categories being unable to agree on key aspects of their definitions, and their categorization being completely arbitrary instead of really based on any consistent use of the terms, the fact is that their categories fail to add any meaningful information to the actual observations. They don't survive even a decent exploration of the ways Europeans used those very terms in the last 15 centuries or so.

                                                        Other than that, they're exactly European cultural norms (that aren't even that normative in Europe) being extended to everyone else.

                                                        Conspicuous Consumption is a well attested phenomena and no culture overtly recognizes it as such as they interpret it under cultural lenses, yet it exists everywhere, including modern "Western" culture. Rich people do that all the fucking time in every country, and most people below them try to get their shot at it as well as they can.

                                                        Not to say the very concept of "Western world/culture/whatever" is far from well established or accepted, with too many critics and defenders, and a lot of dissenting on how exactly it is defined.​
