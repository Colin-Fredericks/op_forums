      This would have little meaningful impact on the modern Chorus. The big reason in that in order for the Tradition for form and unify a plethora of mages with distinct faith-systems into one group, they had do adopt a stance that basically amounts to no Sleeper Faith being "true" and just imperfect reflections of the "truth" of The One. While this might allow Mithraism to survive in the Chorus far longer than it did in the real world (frankly the idea of their being enough of them to mention in the Dark Ages is pretty far-fetched as it is, as Christians actively and rather successfully fought to wipe out the Cults of Mithras as Christianity ascended to power and the Messianic Voices are otherwise presented as far more dedicated to the Abrahamic Faiths and Zoroastrianism than makes sense of them to let cults worshiping a "pagan idol" in), it would also mean that being overly dedicated to a specific faith instead of religion being the foundation to build a better understand of the one would be very antithetical to Chorus members after the Tradition settled into its new normal.

      Since Mithrasism was a small group within the Voices, it's not some massive shock to the Chorus that some mages got duped by a powerful vampire if anyone them actually knew that happened (which there's no evidence to suggest).

      That specific side-bar is also massively out of step with both VtM presentations of Mithras-the-vampire (who you'd think would have done something with a cult of mages worshiping him even if that never comes up on the VtM side of things), and Mage outside of the Dark Ages material. However of an intrigue little plot hook to explore if a group was inspired, absolutely nothing in the rest of the books seems to acknowledge this concept as being an issue (which isn't that surprising, DA Mage was fairly late in the original WoD run, and M20 seems inclined to ignore it for the most part).

          Probably very few Choristers know about Mithras-the-Vampire, if any, and it is unlikely that they're Mithraics themselves.

          Even for those who know that means little. Mithras isn't the only Vampire (much less supernatural person in general) claiming to be a god, and that hardly matters. But outside a specific plot, it's unlikely that any Mage would particularly care about Vampire Mithras calling himself a god. They would probably care about a Methuselah of such age existing and doing stuff regardless, but if not, it isn't his name what will change anything.

                          There are many differences between the Iranian Mithra and the Roman Mithras, so many that the scholar consensus is that it is unlikely that there was any real continuity between them except in the (misguided) perception of the Romans. Those versions of Mithas didn't only have very different dispositions, they had distinct portfolios, iconography, rituals, everything. And Mithras-the-Vampire has much more to do with the Roman god than the Iranian. The Vedic Mitra is far more similar to (and probably has continuity with) the Iranian than the Roman.

                          So I personally think Mithras probably took his name from the god as worshiped in his living culture, where it was worshiped since at least a century before he was even born, but he never really thought of this cult as being about him, they "got everything wrong". He only really started his own cult (successfully, at least) in Rome, and effectively had nothing to do with the Iranian god except by being Iranian himself and the Romans knowing where he came from.

                          Mages that follow the Roman Mithras would be, indeed, inheritors of this vampire-led cult (regardless if their particular cell is linked to the vampire or not). But those who revere Mithra through Zoroastrianism have nothing to do with him.

                              And it's important to note that the Messianic Voices that were supposedly drawn to Mithras-the-vampire were Persian not European. Arsalan, the full mage, rejected the "Mithras" he found in the British isles.                                   By the way, one thing that should be clear, as it pertain to the real world:

                                  While the Roman Mithraism was completely extinguished IRL with the rise of Christianity, the Iranian Mithra is still worshiped today by some Zoroastrians. And it isn't a revival, either. While we can't say for sure how much its practices and beliefs changed through millennia, it's still a full continuity of the ancient cult.

                                  The Vedic Mitra too still exists, although his influence decline a lot within Hinduism, but he's still included in many rituals and festivals related to the sun or the morning.

                                  So, when we talk about the Roman Mithras represented within the setting by Mithras-the-Vampire, we're talking about a mystery cult that existed in Rome no earlier than the 1st century BCE and endured only up to the early 5th century at most, then got erased from popular knowledge for about 15 centuries. When we talk about the Indo-Iranian god, we're talking about a deity with mostly continuing worship from at least the 15th century BCE to today, always as part of mainstream religions in the area. They're very different beasts to use in the setting.
