                          I remember playing a superhero Champions back in the day who the ST and I created to die and have that sort of thing happen. Why? Because that sort of thing happens in comics and it can happen and be some of the best story-telling ever (or it can be complete gratuitous crap), but the way Champions is typically set up, it's not going to happen to a normally built character.                           I've had a mage character in one of my games die horribly and suddenly the whole game changed over to being about saving his soul from the Hell it was locked in and bringing him back to life. No one telling them that it was (mostly) impossible for them to do so or that, if they were to try it, it would be HARD and there would be serious consequences had ANY effect. It was glorious. Even though their result was a bit cobbled together (e.g. they couldn't bring him back in his own body, so they had to find a body without a soul, and so on).

                          In another game, the NPC who was the impetus to get the group together ended up being a 15 year old Widderslainte. Though the cabal didn't know it, her dark destiny was the reason for her kidnapping in the first session and the group gelled around rescuing her. When she finally turned bad, it was heartbreakingly tragic. Even she really didn't want to take that leap into the Abyss, she just couldn't help herself. A PC died trying to give her the choice he desperately wanted her to have and it changed nothing. Not that the group wasn't still trying to find a way to bring her back despite that until that game fizzled out.                                   Some flaws are exactly what they say they are. I'll point out "Dark Fate" for example, which is a five point flaw, not a seven point flaw. And the rules are very clear about what that means. Why would a seven point flaw be any better?

                                  That's the cost inherent with taking flaws. You are giving the storyteller license to use those flaws to make things very difficult for your character. And in some cases, that's going to mean a bad ending. And a player accepts that when they take these (completely optional) flaws.

                                  Communication is important, of course. And if a player and storyteller agree that they want to do a story of a not completely screwed Widderslainte, that's one thing. But if someone is just taking this flaw because they want the points, or because it sounds like a cool story, then they should be prepared to play THAT story. And, at least if you're following the source materials, that's not going to be a happy one.

                                                          Well, if you ask my opinion, it is previous setting lore that jumped the shark by implying Widderslainte status was irreversible and its carriers doomed to be evil, and this Flaw's writeup is merly attempting to jump back. If Nephandic corruption is so much more powerful and lasting across reincarnations than Awakened self-determination and whatever empowers Avatars and mages, and the Awakened can only deploy Gilgul as a destructive stopgap counter (maybe; the same setting lore implies perhaps soul-killing is not so effective or final as supposed), why haven't the Nephandi and their masters conquered the Tellurian and destroyed it or remolded it in their dark image long ago? The sect has been around for millennia and if one assumes lore about widderslainte is correct it should be steadily expanding its ranks every generation between what we are told is a significant number of newly-corrupted mages and all the reincarnating members that can only Awaken into ultimate evil, barring Gilgul. And it seems other mages aren't that effective or thorough in capturing Nephandi and sending them through the soul-killing ritual. This piece of setting lore seems nothing but yet another example of old WoD's unfortunate taste for angst for angst's sake tainting a game whose themes and playstyle were anything but doom-laden.

                                                          Anyway, I reread the Flaw's wording. Broadly summarizing, it says being Widderslainte is one possible cause of having a Taint of Corruption but there are several possible others, such as demonic possession, the aftermath of a bad Quiet or Seeking, corrupt Resonance, deals with malignant Umbrood, a curse, a vile past life or wicked karma, Nephandic heritage, poor life choices, the influence of the Wyrm, ownership of tainted texts or artifacts, and so on. It tends to taint one's practices, instruments, spells, aura, dreams, and impulses towards corruption. In game terms it gives a bonus to wicked magick and a similar penalty to 'good' one. However it also explictly says it does not force or doom the character to do evil - one can resist its bad influence - and it is possible to defeat the curse, although it won't be an easy fight. An epic quest may allow to purge the taint (and buy off the Flaw) although it would be easier to go with the flow. Entities that may recognize such soul-stains tend to regard the character as a mortal enemy (e.g. werewolves), an object of pity and potential salvation (the nicer kind of mages), or possilby both.

                                                          So having Taint of Corruption does not striclty equate being Widderslainte but the latter is one possible case for the Flaw's occurrence, and its writeup explictly says it does not force one to be evil - although it would be the easier course - and it may be potentially resisted and removed. Since this rule covers a more general case including various non-Nephandic possibilities, it does not say ToC is everything there is in M20 about being Widderslainte, but as far as it is concerned, such a status can be potentially resisted and overcome.

                                                          As a matter of fact, if one reads ToC rules (and the ones for similar Psychic Vampire Flaw) together with the general ones for Resonance in the same book, these Flaws (and the Spark of Life Merit) seem notable cases of especially strong and entrenched resonances more than anything else.
