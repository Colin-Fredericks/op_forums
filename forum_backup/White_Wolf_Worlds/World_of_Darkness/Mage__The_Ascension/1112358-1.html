            Originally posted by The3obaFett View Post
            Also, why is the M20 book over 700 pages?!?!!? What did they add to it to make that happen?
            Some of it has to do with the long-winded and informal writing style of the book's lead writer, Phil Bruccato. He's been writing for the game since 2nd edition, and was most enthusiastic to helm the 20th anniversary book.

            But the other part is simply because they tried to cram in as much material as they felt was relevant to the game. Which meant drawing upon dozens of details from dozens of books published between the start and end of MtAs. Conditioning rules from Guide to the Technocracy, overviews of the Umbra's various realms and how to travel them, rules for Certamon challenges from the Order of Hermes, write-ups for three Alternate Spheres (Data, Primal Utility, and Dimensional Science) as written in the Revised Technocracy Convention books, detailed overviews of the various Sects that are major sides in the Ascension War (including turning the various Crafts into a semi-formal Sect, where none had existed before), both primary and secondary Abilities (the latter including stuff like Animal Ken, Flying, Energy Weapons, and Do), etc. Not to mention brand new material, like the aforementioned Focus section; sure, we'd had lists of potential Instruments ("Foci") before, but not broad descriptions of Paradigm or Practice, which had only been talked about piecemeal. While the game always told us what tools a mage could use for their magic (Instruments), M20 goes more into detail into how those tools are used (Practice), and why the mage uses them that way (Paradigm).

            While the book could probably be more concise (and could use an editor to trim some of the fat), it IS still a dense read, trying to include a lot of material that previously filled out other books. And believe it or not, this isn't even all the material they planned to include. M20 had to be chopped up into two or three other books, only one of which (How Do You Do That?, a primer one Sphere mechanics that should be taken with a grain of salt) has yet been released. The core book, though, contains the essentials, and reading through the 2nd and Revised core books should fill out anything that M20 misses, so long as you're willing to integrate.

                    Originally posted by The3obaFett View Post
                    Also, why is the M20 book over 700 pages?!?!!? What did they add to it to make that happen?
                    Two words: Meta Plot.

                    M20 had the burden of having to go over pretty much everything that had been brought up in every book that came before it. This includes cliff notes of all the meta narratives, the transformation of the technocracy, the rogue council, the avatar storm, information on every possible faction inlcuding all the traditions, crafts, Technocratic conventions, and a (in my opinion, pretty poor) overview on the nephandi and marauders. Oh, and there's also all the little rules and systems that cropped up over the years, like the arts of Do, True Faith, Alternative history options, and combat rules, oh so many combat rules, and tables! About 6 pages worth of tables, and that's not even getting into the magic side of things.
                    That being said, it still hasn't got everything. It very deliberately does not include any information about the unawakened Sorcerers, for example.

                    Also, Mage books have a horrible habit of explaining things at least twice (sometimes three times) in different ways, often with each part omitting something mentioned somewhere else. Usually the first time is the narrative bit which talks about things in context to the world, while the later explanation are more mechanics based. But not always, as many important mechanics will be mentioned in the narrative bit but completely omitted from later mentions. M20 does this in spades, some things requiring you to jump all over the book before you get the complete picture.

                    It's a terrible introduction to Mage, but as a compendium, it's not too bad.

                        The beauty of Drive Thru RPG is that you can go both ways. You can buy just a PDF, get a book printed on demand, or even have them both. Of course, M20 being such a large book means that printing it will be expensive. So do what makes the most sense for your budget, keeping in mind that the PDF is the cheapest (and fastest, since you won't need to wait for the book to be delivered in the mail).                                         Other than that, a character's skill stats and the like are just that - mundane skills. And they should reflect a character's past life and current state after CG. 2 is average for many stats and skills for normal people. 3 is 'good / professional'. 4 is 'master'. 5 is pretty much legendary. And unlike D&D, WoD does not have character 'levels', the requirement for planning ahead is very, very minimal compared to your previous game.
                                        Seriously. Try to get that into their heads. An average game session gives around 1-2 XP per RL session for long campaigns..more if it is supposed to be a fast-paced game or if long skips of time are involved. As in, ingame-years. The books have a good writeup about the XP distribution. And that XP can be immediately invested or saved up, but the increase in attributes, skills or magick spheres should *reflect what the character did use, train or is practicing in-game*

                                        The real power fireworks come from the selection of spheres. Mind that the max sphere level out of CG matches a character's Arete trait - which can have a max of 3. While it is possible to pull off powerful characters right out of chargen compared to a normal mortal, they are nothing compared to higher end Mages - unless they get to fire off their magick without consequence and requirements.

                                        Also: They cannot raise that Arete level post-chargen with just XP The character needs to go through a Seeking - a metaphysical trial and riddle, in a way - that is supposed to bring them enlightenment and realize a new step along their path to Ascension. An intricate roleplayed event happening either in a mindscape or reality. If they fail the seeking - tough taco. No increased Arete,.
                                        And a seeking happens at times the storyteller decides on, usually when the character had events happen that would push them towards that realization.

                                        So, even powergamers can't willy-nilly just pump their Arete up - you control the pace for that.

                                        The problem comes from powergamers trying to ignore their character's paradigm and required tools, and pulling off magickal feats with the spheres that simply would not fit the kind of magickal training and belief that the character has. While trying to do it by merely snipping their fingers in-game.
                                        Essentially, the writeups of what each level of each sphere can do is the *whole* of the magickal toolbox - but not every character can pull all of it off simply because of their mindset of how magic works.
                                        Mage relies heavily on a dynamic of player self-restraint, and the ST keeping an eye on the same. It comes with the territory of a magic system that isn't made of fixed, pre-defined spells that you can buy, and a lack of mana bars.

                                                    Yeah, powergaming could be a problem in this game. But there are ways to mitigate this somewhat.

                                                    First, most of the really broken Sphere abilities are also the most Vulgar. So if you dole out Paradox points as normal and call for backlashes regularly (like whenever it seems most dramatic or just), the players will learn not to abuse them as much. That is, indeed, part of the reason Paradox exists as a game mechanic. Some Vulgar powers could be made coincidental under certain circumstances (turn invisible in a dark room, and it can't be proved to have happened by a Hypothetical Average Observer), but these tend to require some set-up or conditions being just right. As such, the amount of work or luck they need to do so can serve as their own moderating influences.

                                                    Second, the most Vulgar, game-breaking Effects also tend to attract attention. And there's an entire sect - the Technocratic Union - for whom policing overt supernatural power is their entire job description. If you start turning cars into slag in the middle of Main Street, you're going to have MIBs and cyborgs knocking on your door.

                                                    Indeed, this brings us to an important Third: Actions have Consequences. No use of magick occurs in a bubble; the Awakened arts have an effect on the world around them. More subtle uses of power will largely go unnoticed, but more dramatic alterations of Reality create larger ripples in the pond. So when a player pulls off an Effect, ask yourself what the possible ramifications of this action could result. If a player transforms into a tiger and runs around attacking people, it's going to make the evening news, and bring in organizations like Animal Control to search for the wayward big cat. If they use Forces 2 to change the traffic signals in their favor, it could potentially cause an accident. If they whip up a storm, it could devastate the area and potentially harm local infrastructure, depending on how intense the weather was.

                                                    And if they go around killing a bunch of people, there WILL be police investigations. The weirder the deaths, the more intense the scrutiny. In DnD, many areas that adventurers go to are outside the rule of law (which usually gets concentrated around settlements and cities), and when the law is present, forensic science in the pseudo-medieval setting is less than stellar. In the World of Darkness, police are a thing that exists, and their methods are much more advanced. Even if the PCs kill in a manner that doesn't leave immediately damning evidence against them, there's going to be officers going around, asking for witnesses and checking alibis. Security footage will be reviewed where possible - remember also that modern people carry cameras with them everywhere, and are not afraid to document weird goings-on if they see it (making avoiding witnesses all the more essential). Not only police, but journalists will go around asking questions...as will the friends of anyone the PCs kill, as they understandably want answers and potentially revenge. Hunter groups form all the time from normal people who had loved ones fall victim to supernatural creatures, of which mages qualify. PCs that are too sloppy with their murder-hobo tendencies could become the targets of angry witch-hunters.

                                                    Fourth, keep in mind also that forces in the material world aren't the only ones that affect - and are affected by - that material world. Spirits existing in the Umbra can take an interest in the activities of mages that abuse their magick. Elemental Effects might attract elemental spirits. Ghosts feed on emotions, and so manipulating them in the living could attract the dead. Whether the spirits dislike how the mages are upsetting the natural order - or whether they like it, and want to be around them at all times - either way can lead to problems for the mages. And the bigger the Effects, the more they attract spirits, for good and ill.
