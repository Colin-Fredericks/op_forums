                            Just a few brief thoughts:

                            First, MotA doesn't say that Archmasters are trapped by their pursuit of Archspheres; that's Exemplars. Archmasters are trapped by their pursuit of a single paradigm. Bear in mind that this was written during the transition from 2e to Revised, long before M20 came along with its clarification about the distinction between Paradigm and Practice. In terms of what Kharnov is talking about, M20's Practices are the "How", and its Paradigms are the "Why".

                            And the idea to take away from MotA is that Archmasters get trapped by focusing too heavily on their Practices. The business about "once you develop Archspheres, Ascension is no longer possible" would be downstream from that, with the idea being that Archspheres can only be accessed through a Practice, or by becoming an Exemplar. If you want to maintain the idea that Archmasters can't Ascend, the way to argue it is to say that they have to focus on the "How" to the detriment of the "Why" in order to access the upper reaches of what the Spheres can do.

                            On the subject of Marauders Ascending: I categorically reject the notion that a Marauder falling so far into Quiet that they completely disassociate from reality and fall out of it bears anything more than a surface level resemblance to Ascension. If Marauders can Ascend, they do so despite their Quiet, not because of it.

                            Ditto with Nephandic "Ascension", but moreso: in MotA terms, Nephandi don't seek Ascensioon; they seek apotheosis, or they seek Oblivion. But actual Ascension? Becoming a god is a distraction from that. And Oblivion is arguably the polar opposite of Ascension.

                            Finally, I think that one of the things that defines the Traditions is that each of them attempts to provide a vision of personal Ascension for their members to pursue. This is as opposed to the Technocracy and the Crafts: I'm not saying that a Black Hat or a Sister of Hypollyta can't Ascend; but for the most part, they do so without much help from their Convention or Craft. But one of the reasons the Traditions exist is to help their members Ascend; and in that regard, it would be interesting to walk through the Traditions and get a sense of what each one's vision of Ascension is.

                            For example, the Book of Ether speaks of the Walls of Troy, a metaphor for reality. In this metaphor, everything is possible, even contradictory things. The Walls of Troy contain those things that have been accepted as possible, with everything outside the Walls being considered impossible. In this view, Ascension consists of tearing down the walls so that the Scientist truly accepts the infinite possibilities of reality. That's how an Etherite views Ascension: developing such a profound understanding of Ether Science that all things become possible.

                            This is where the Revised Tradition Books come in handy. In particular, I would look at each one's take on the Tenth Sphere to get a sense of what sorts of concepts that Tradition would use in formulating its vision of Ascension.

                            My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

                                        To my taste, Ascension is a distraction and a trap. A character can get so focused on some ideal, impossible or otherwise, and it leads them down the wrong path.

                                        In one of the Star Wars animated shows, a Jedi character gets obsessed with the idea of force visions and destined heroes saving the galaxy. The resolution of that plotline is essentially that it is an understandable yet foolish distraction from the personal story being told, the story of him and his band of rebel friends, and the planet they want to liberate.

                                        Whatever the Storyteller's opinion of Ascension is, my OOC opinion, and the baseline most of my characters would tend to have IC unless I want them to seem like they're deeply influenced by propaganda (e.g. a Hermetic who has been raised in the order), is that it is meaningless and irrelevant. The absolute enlightenment of an individual means nothing without justice, and justice is interpersonal, it is worldly. Seek wisdom, but seek it as a means to guide your decision-making on worldly matters, to guide those of others, and to temper draws towards injustice and abuse of power.

                                        Basically, in a MTA game I'm more interested in saving the city from an oppressive reality zone than I am in Ascension, in WTA I'm more interested in saving an ecosystem from total destruction than I am in reaching Summer Country (or whatever 'win condition' you can think of), in VTM I'm more interested in unravelling the Primogen's plot than I am in Golconda, and so on.

                                        CTD is interesting because Siochain is a playable and plot-relevant state of being. A Siochain is not only compatible with a typical party, albeit one years into a chronicle, it's arguably an outright enhancement. What does it mean for a party if one of the PCs bears the Mantle of The Rogue With A Heart Of Gold, that they've become a supernatural being who truly sums up and IS the concept which weaves itself through millions of stories throughout human history? What does it mean for the plot if the PCs help a Thallain to stop being a Thallain, and instead become a Siochain, by taking up the Mantle of The Redeemed Villain or The Antihero? More to the point, reaching the state of Siochain involves the other PCs by default, since to get there you have to go through narrative arcs with a number of different characters.

                                        Basically unless I'm playing C20, I don't care about my PC 'ascending' as it were, and I would actively encourage my ST and fellow players to avoid it as well. A PC obsessed with their Mage Ascending sounds like a nightmare to play with.

As old-fashioned ceremonial magicians might put it, you're more interested in "Low Magic" than "High Magic."

                                            I think Dataweaver may be correct that the best option for you would be to read through some of the Tradition books and get a better understanding of what Ascension looks like from their perspectives.

                                                        Originally posted by Lord Revan View Post
                                                        Kharnov We do seem to be in different wave lengths. Would you care to explain your notion of "Why" with a Paradigm example?
                                                        I'll elaborate a bit on three of the examples that I've already provided.

                                                        First of all, let's talk about the "traditional/default" Hermetic Paradigm. Based on a wiki-overview of real-world Hermetic philosophy, the standard Hermetic Paradigm (in M20 terms) would probably be "Bring Back the Golden Age," with some "Divine Order, Earthly Chaos" and "Mechanistic Cosmos" thrown in. Humans used to be perfect divine entities, given rulership over Creation by The One, but ended up somehow becoming trapped within it, becoming mundane creatures bound by the laws of material existence.

                                                        The "How" of the Hermetic Focus, as described, is that humans still retain some of that divine spark, and they can draw upon ancient wisdom to figure out how to use that power to influence Creation.

                                                        The "Why" of the Hermetic Focus is the ultimate end-goal that power is supposed to be used to achieve: transcending the imperfections of the material universe and become a perfect divine being once more.

                                                        This process of becoming a perfect divine being is not just about becoming *powerful*, but also overcoming personal flaws and vices, as well as attachments to this mundane material existence. Magic can be helpful in this endeavour if you are using it in a way that helps you understand your limitations and overcome them, but not if you are just using it as a weapon in the pursuit of temporal power. And even if you intend to use your magical knowledge for the purposes of self-improvement, you have to engage with the wider material world to seek out that knowledge; Mages don't get to just pluck Sphere knowledge out of thin air. Thus every effort you make to seek out power and knowledge to help your Ascension comes with the risk of creating situations which drag you back into the muck of material existence, in the form of enemies you must fight or allies you must appease, whether you like it or not. Thus your pursuit of magical knowledge has to be careful weighed in terms of cost versus benefit towards your ultimate goal of spiritual enlightenment.

                                                        This leads up back to Porthos. While he mastered the magical knowledge found within the Practices of the Order of Hermes, the "How", he failed to use that knowledge to try and heal his wounded soul, resolving his guilt around the death of his family back in the Dark Ages, which would stand in the way of the spiritual perfection demanded by the "Why" of Hermetic philosophy. Despite his mostly-good intentions, he ultimately remained a flawed person, and thus could not achieve a Hermetic Ascension, becoming a perfect divine being once more.

                                                        Let's talk about the Chakravanti/Euthanatos now. According to the M20 section on Paradigms, the typical Chakravanti position is something along the lines of "Creation is Divine and Alive." They believe that their is a cosmic order (Destiny, Karma, etc.) that binds all of reality together, and gives a purpose to everything within it. Those who join the Chakravanti have a destined role to uphold and maintain this cosmic order, and they have access to great power in order to fulfill that function.

                                                        The "How" of the Chakravanti is that they are permitted to draw upon the power of the gods in order to serve their destined role of keeping Fate on its proper course.

                                                        The "Why" of the Chakravanti is using their powers to fulfill their role *correctly*, successfully keeping people on the course of their Dharma instead of impeding them or leading them astray. By fulfilling their own destined role, understanding their place in the cosmic order, they can eventually achieve Moksa, joining the ranks of the gods or becoming one with the universe.

                                                        While the Chakravanti draw their power from the function that they are intended to serve, it is clear that they do not always do so successfully. Some members of the Tradition are criticized for being too willing to resort to the "Good Death" when other gentler methods might better serve the needs of Dharma. Magic itself may even be completely unnecessary in serving their function, if thoughtful advice is more than enough to steer an individual back onto the proper course. Thus pursuing greater magical knowledge only helps a Chakravanti in reaching Ascension inasmuch as it helps them to properly fulfill their destined purpose, and seeking power while ignoring their responsibilities would lead them away from Ascension, not towards it.

                                                        Which brings us to Voormas. Voormas believed himself wise enough to judge the Wheel of Ages itself, thinking he had the right to try and force the fate of the entire universe to follow the path he had chosen. Underneath this, he also feared his own Karma, the consequences he was destined to face for the violence he embraced throughout his long life. He chose to reject his own fate, and rebel against the cosmic order that he was supposed to serve. Until he chose to return to the path of his Dharma, and accepts the Karma that he has brought upon himself, Voormas could not achieve Ascension as the Chakravanti understand it.

                                                        You've argued before that either Porthos or Voormas may not follow the "default" Paradigm of their Traditions. But so what? They are both still clearly gripped by personal imperfections, lacking in philosophical or spiritual enlightenment, regardless of their formidable magic power. What path *could* they follow where those failings would not be a hindrance to their efforts at Ascension, unless Ascension is nothing more than "being really good at doing magic"?

                                                        Let's wrap things up with the example that I gave for a Chaos Magician. Their Paradigm, of course, is that "Everything is Chaos - You Only Think It Makes Sense." Unlike the Hermetic or the Chakravanti, they don't believe in a higher order, divine truth, whatever. There is no prior meaning of life or purpose for existence, no fate but what we make for ourselves.

                                                        The "How" of Chaos Magic is that "belief is a tool," that you can learn techniques to direct its power in order to manipulate the Chaos of the universe towards the results that you desire.

                                                        The "Why" is a bit trickier in this case because "Everything is Chaos" rejects the notion of a universal meaning of life, divine plan, or "higher" form of existence. Unlike the previous two examples, it doesn't contain within it a predefined "end" that the Mage *should* be aimed towards achieving. This was why I think "Transcend Your Limits" is a good addition to a Chaos Mage's Paradigm, because it provides more direction in that regard than "Everything is Chaos" does on its own; the goal is to overcome your limitations, to self-actualize.

                                                        You referred to my previous idea for the Chaos Mage version of Ascension as "identity change," but I think that's a bit of an oversimplification. It's more about "identity discovery," uncovering your "True Will" hidden beneath the beliefs that have been imposed upon you by your experiences, finding out what remains when all of the assumptions about who you "are" or "should" be have been stripped away. If "Everything is Chaos," then so are You. You are One and You are Many, at the same time, contradiction be damned. A lot of this relies on the notion, supported by many of the originators of Chaos Magic philosophy, that we are all connected by the Kia/Collective Unconscious/Deep Mind, and the idea that we exist entirely as individuals, divided from everyone else, is just another belief, not an absolute truth. Our independent identity is just as much a construct as any other belief, and we should only adhere to it inasmuch as it proves itself useful. Eventually, all limitations are surpassed; not just on what can be done with magic, but on how the Chaos Mage understands themself and the infinity of Chaos, and Ascension is achieved.

                                                        This is just one course that a Chaos Mage could try to follow towards Ascension, however. Compared to most other magical traditions, Chaos Magic is more pragmatic and less ideological. Different Chaos Mages can decide for themselves what "liberation" or "self-actualization" looks like. But, as I mentioned previously, I thoroughly reject the notion that Ascension is simply a matter of "getting really good at magic," understanding the "How" of magic without ever embracing some notion of "Why," having some notion of how You or Reality is made more perfect by the path that you have chosen.

                                                        On a slightly separate note, it is worth mentioning that the writers chose to be vague about the nature of Ascension on purpose. They wanted it to be mysterious and ineffable. But also, they wanted to give Storytellers the room to decide for themselves what Ascension is supposed to mean at *their* table. If none of this really jives with you, there's no reason you can't handle it your own way as a Storyteller, or see if you can get the Storyteller on board with your interpretation if you are a player.

                                                            On the topic of the writers not elucidating much about the nature of Ascension, I think that something relatively obvious that has been overlooked is that Mage was meant to model miracle-workers as a whole (with more standard occultists being covered by Sorcerer). I think that the vagueness was intentional, and from a pragmatic perspective, just good as a way to account for the different and often mutually exclusive models of liberation present in various religions. It seems like the developers tried to get that across by helping secular people grok mysticism more generally, but there is no need to necessarily reify what they said, particular when some models of liberation do necessarily involve omnipotence. We Are All (Gods) In Disguise stands as just one example of a paradigm from Book of Secrets that involves power, even if knowledge of the Spheres does not really assist one with enlightenment.
