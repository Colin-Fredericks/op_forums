                            I like the system in BoS. Essentially you can have a Grimoire either grant cheaper purchase of Abilities, or Spheres, or Arete - or a mixture of each, with each of those effects (or multiple of the same) giving that +1 increase in Grimoire level, and requiring +3 more successes on the Arete roll on creation. I am not sure how page 143 implies that a Grimoire can be lower than the rank of spheres it teaches..it says quite the opposite in the Spheres section.


                            Also, an important on page 151:
                            " a player does not have to spend experience and/or Background points to obtain a Wonder that the mage herself has created."

                            As for the background cost of Wonders in general, it caused some confusion before:
                            A wonder usually costs 1-3 XP/Freebies per background dots it is rated at, according to the table in the M20 corebook. Plus whatever XP/Freebie cost the Wonder's flaws or features add/subtract from that cost, as BoS page 157 shows.
                            The example primer 'Oracle' has 4 background(!) dots - notice the small printed dots next to the name.
                            It costs 8 XP or freebies to get. That's what the 'background cost X pts' next to each example is. It should have been labeled XP/Freebie cost.

                                Much as I expected, I found most of BoS of high quality and rather interesting and useful as the de facto second volume of the M20 corebook, especially the updated compilation of Merits and Flaws and the expanded foci chapter which got most of my attention and interest. The main issue I found with BoS is a continuation of the author's unfortunate tendency to use M20 books as a soapbox to preach about his pet peeves and twist setting and rules to fulfill them, which sometimes grows rather annoying for people with their own reasons to disagree, including yours truly.

                                I'm generally very favorable to de-emphasized splats and freeform character development, so the way M20 corebook and BoS give ample leeway to custom-build all kinds of personal foci system from the bottom up, besides and beyond any group cultural stereotypes, found me enthusiastically agreeing. Although the M20 setting provides plenty of reasons for the Traditions, Technocracy, and Nephandi to keep existing as separate and hostile factions, I think this new system refreshingly blurs boundaries and de-emphasizes stuffy and rigid cultural stereotypes that separate groups within the same faction. A little experimentation at foci-building with the combination of M20 and BoS options showed me it is extremely easy to create plenty of characters that would fit fairly well in an handful different Traditions (or for that matter 2-3 Conventions) at once. This is a real character-customization godsend to me and I think should grow to be reflected in the Traditions structure to some serious degree; e.g. the Council should include a sizable number of quasi-Orphan 'hybrid' mages that do not easily fit in any given Tradition niche in ideological or membership terms, but rather span and settle the ground between multiple groups.

                                As a rule, additional foci options in BoS look just as interesting and useful as the ones already provided in the corebook. However, the freeform foci rules make me seriously question the value of Dual Affiliation. Since you can easily create a personal foci system for any given character that gives free access to the better part of any two or three Traditions' practices and instruments, this Merit seems rather superfluous and outrageously overpriced. On second thoughts, however, I assume the residual value of Dual Affiliation may be strictly found at getting a second Affinity Sphere, and so the Merit effectively becomes an alternative variant of similarily-priced Sphere Natural (on the other hand, I wonder if that Merit is overpriced, too). Unfortunately BoS says nothing about how its new foci options typically align with established Traditions, which tends to reduce their usefulness, esp. when they are exclusive or dominant in a character concept.

                                As it concerns the Merits and Flaws section, I find it another veritable treasuretrove of character development ideas that works very well to customize the character concept even much beyond what the other Traits and the foci rules can do. The options are so abundant and interesting I'm favorable to seriously loose limits about the number of Merits and Flaws a character may get at creation, and even more so be permissive about buying them during play with any barely decent story justification.

                                I only found a few issues that leave me puzzled: e.g. the Beast Within Flaw makes a lot of sense conceptwise, but the rule about checking for frenzy is nonsensical. The RAW makes it so it gets more difficult for a mage to resist frenzy with an higher Willpower. Becoming less able to control one's rage the more more willful you get makes no sense and is nothing like the way all other WoD characters with impulse-control issues make such checks. So I assume the rule needs to be changed so that the player rolls say Avatar vs. a 10-half Willpower difficulty. Or alternatively rolling Willpower vs. Avatar x2 difficulty.

                                Moreover, the Enchanting/Repulsive Merit/Flaw seems to assume any character is going to have only one such special feature. But one can easily think of several cases when it makes sense for a character to have multiple features, e.g. a mage that has an enchanting gaze or a seductive or commanding voice, or for that matter having an 'evil eye' and being very graceful. So I assume it should be possible to buy multiple versions of the M/F, or for that matter combine the Merit and the Flaw, as long as they concern different physical features.

                                I'm usually little interested in playing characters heavily invested in magical items, so I paid scarce attention so far at the Wonders chapter, and I think this game has too many Secondary Traits already. The Resonance and Synergy rules look very interesting as a concept but somewhat confusing in application, so I need to further ponder and experiment on them. The Justice and Influence chapter was a very mixed issue for me, sometimes looking interesting: e.g. the way Branding became kind of non-lethal standard sentence for serious crimes in the Traditions, although I wonder what keeps powerful mages from using their Spheres to remove a Brand before end of sentence.

                                Sometimes it made me shake my head: e.g. if you ask my opinion, the author went too far out of PC political bias in making Indenture a taboo in several cases, since mage society doesn't have many other feasible options to make Awakened offenders do restitution (and it makes no sense for the Choristers to shun it). The 'SJW' Disparates and their draconian 'justice' system that makes routine use of mutilation and torture, dear Universe. The Technocracy are totalitarian monsters, but how the heck this bunch of zealots is supposed to be a better alternative? The Certamen system is a welcome comeback and seems OK to me.

                                    "Considering that the Avatar could be seen as your inner
                                    Beast, a character with the Pattern Essence cannot take this
                                    Flaw. It’s best suited for Dynamic Essences, although a Quest-
                                    ing or Primordial Avatar could attain frightening rages too."

                                    Now add to that the fact that Beast Within is a supernatural(!) flaw as opposed to a mental one, plus the fact that in this particular berserk state the Mage is very able and downright urged to use True Magick instead of mindless physical violence..well. It makes it clear that the beast going on a rampage is not a human mind, but the Avatar itself. And the reason for the Willpower being the difficulty becomes more clear;
                                    The Avatar's symbiosis with the Mage (regardless of speculations of what the Avatar actualy *is*) and their True Magick gets fueled by sheer will. Willpower. The more potent in will the Mage is, the stronger the connection to their Avatar, the more easily the Avatar can influence the Mage (or vice versa). It is a little ironic and illogical sounding at first, given how we usually associate strong will with the ability to *resist* external or sudden changes...but the Avatar is anything but external.
                                    After all, it is the most strong willed Mages that become the most devoted to the paradigm and Magick their Avatar drives them towards.

                                    Now, the Avatar's *own* willpower could be seen as its Avatar rating - the stronger the avatar in essence, the more in control of itself it is. And thus to resist the frenzy, the amount of dice you get is that rating (+1), or the resonance, whichever is higher.

                                    I think the whole thing makes for a particularly interesting (if tough) flaw.

                                        I see your argument and the gist of it sure makes sense conceptwise (make no mistake this Flaw's fluff works nicely and the Flaw is excellent for certain concepts) but the RAW still makes no sense and your argument fails to justify it. Sure, in this case the Avatar is what fuels the mage's rage and it is an integral, 'internal' part of the mage. But we have several examples in the WoD of supernatural splats that face very similar circumstances and in no case their triggers for frenzy work remotely like this. And it outlandish to argue that a vampire's Beast is any less integral and 'internal' to the character than a mage's Avatar. In all other cases, a character's Willpower is more or less what they use to resist their violent urges, not what empowers them. Sure, if the Avatar is what fuels the mage's rage, it makes sense to roll its score to check for frenzy. And the mage's Awakened Will empowers their feats of True Magick. But barring extreme special cases (e.g. Marauders) the Will of the mage is always channeled and controlled by the character's consciousness, not the Avatar.

                                        So it makes no sense if the raging Avatar rolls against Willpower to unleash a frenzy, and it occurs if the roll fails. At the very least it should be the Avatar rolls against Willpower (adding a +1 or substituting it with Resonance if higher makes no particular problem) and frenzy occurs if the roll succeeds. The alternative would make exactly as much (non)sense as vampires or werewolves being more prone to frenzy the more willful and self-driven they are. Again, with this Flaw the Avatar essentally gets to play the role of a vampire's Beast, so it only makes sense they would behave in a very similar way. Invert the outcome of the roll, and the rule can make sense for the fluff; the Beast-like Avatar wants to rage when presented with the right stimulus, but the mage's self can use their own Will to try and keep it under control. The more powerful the Avatar, or the less developed the mage's Will, the more difficult stopping frenzy. The less strong the Avatar, or the more willful the mage, the easier is to suppress frenzy. This way, Willpower can be used as the main tool for the mage to remain in control of themselves, just like it happens for all other WoD characters that face an impulse control problem, regardless of whether it is fuelled by psychological or supernatural circumstances. Willpower acting against the mage's wish is contradiction and nonsense. Of course the case may occur when the mage wants frenzy to occur, just like a vampire riding the wave, esp. because a character may use magick during it.

                                                    Really? It's pretty uncontroversial stuff. He uses the FAQ to respond to common questions he faces, and he responds to them in pretty moderate ways which I feel are at the core of Mage.

                                                    I think most of the concern comes from his response to "Why is Mage so liberal", but he adequately answers this by pointing to negative traits in every Tradition, Craft etc, in-universe conservatives, and conservative ideas at the very core of Mage (many paradigms hark back to a Pre-Lapsarian concept where everything USED to be perfect, which is about as conservative as you can get really), whilst also justifying the liberal concepts (the basic idea of the game is changing reality to become better than it is right now, in your own opinion, which is going to lead to a lot of progressive elements). "Everything should be the way it is right now, basically" isn't a Paradigm.                                                     I simply enjoyed reading it because he clearly understands that written work is heavily tied to the person who writes it, and he doesn't apologize for being a person with opinions and beliefs which not every reader will agree with. Perhaps I only see it as reasoned and relatively neutral because it agrees with my own beliefs, but I would like to point out that "hey, maybe colonialism was a bad thing and some Mages would be pissed about its aftereffects and the destruction of their cultures" is about as uncontroversial a "progressive" statement as a person could possibly make, and practically every so-called liberal element falls into a camp of "hey, obviously bad things are bad, and obviously good things are good".
