                Regarding magic vs. science... I made both my Tradition and Technocrat players deeply uncomfortable when a time traveler from 1000 years in the future “accidentally” mentioned that the dominant magical organization of the future was the “New World Order of Hermes.”

                I tend to fall into the “Mage is the lightest shaded section of the World of Darkness” camp. It’s not meant to play like Vampire or Werewolf and a big chunk of its appeal is that it doesn’t.

                If every game played and felt just like D&D, why bother with anything but D&D. If you want bleak and hopeless you play Vampire*. If you want bleak and hopeless with a side of ridiculously large dice pools and depictions of violence you play Werewolf. If you you want downright nihilism there’s Wraith.

                Mage is what you play because you want to experience what I call “The Dark Fantastic.” The world is dark and dangerous... but at the same time full of wonder and a chance to ‘earn your happy ending.’

                In that respect, the mixed (but mostly okay) bag that is the Traditions makes sense. It has problems, but there enough there that you can generally root for the faction. Particularly the younger generations the PCs are intended to be members of.

                Yes, you can tell a heroic tale of the Technocracy too, but that’s mainly because the rank and file aren’t the same as the leadership and there are far darker villains in the setting for them to fight (i.e. it’s okay to root for Magneto when he’s fighting the Red Skull).

                Ultimately, I see the future of Mage not as some Armageddon (the term foisted on it by Revised to bring it line with Gehenna/Apocalypse), but, as I hinted at in my open, the less extreme members of the Traditions and Technocracy finding a middle ground that balances safety vs. wonder.

                Or as I’ve joked about the eventual peace treaty... “Okay, we’ll give you dragons, but they’re limited to dog size and eat only fish. In exchange we get our cybernetic wi-fi access and cold fusion technology.”

                * Vampire is kinda weird for me on the bleakness scale because I’ve always considered the “Wormwood” ending in Gehenna as the canonical one for the line.

                Further, the main time I ran it I threw in the “dying old homeless man outside the sanctuary” option just before the final sunrise (and since the old church had no power, no one had any charged phones after 40 days). As should shock no one... only those PCs willing to sacrifice all their struggles to survive by going out to help someone the world didn’t care about and would be dead soon either way... only those actually passed the test.

                Only one did... the others saluted his sacrifice and were still just inside the doors when the whole place blew apart and exposed everyone to the sunrise. Cue surprise as the player who thought he was getting a heroic sacrifice instead becomes human and everyone else burned to ash.

                Throw in the final blessing (in this case by the old man who then disappeared without a trace) on the now human PC and his descendants and the PC and the Dhampir** girl (the only other survivor) walking off into the sunrise and it’s hard not to feel like the reason for the Darkness isn’t nihilism, but contrast.

                ** also contrasting the darkness... the last generations produced mortal children. Children who could have children of their own.

                    Well, in Troper terms, Mage's issue is that it's heavily into the Fantastic Aesop (where the Aesop is valid, but only within the setting and thus struggles to be applied to real life) instead of the Space Whale Aesop (where the Aesop is valid, but relies on possible actions with highly improbable from the perspective of 'now' consequences that it gets weird).

                    While we know that Star Trek VI's message is pro-environmentalism and specifically preserving endangered species, saving the whales because in the distant future aliens thinking that whales are the form of life on Earth worth talking to will show up on kill humanity because they don't have whales to talk to is... a bit of a reach.

                    Stamping out mysticism is bad because mystics can cure cancer isn't much of a reach, but is a problem because mystics can only cure cancer in the fantasy rules of Mage.

                    Mage's morals can feel very disconnected, but a lot of that is also that progressive-libertarianism isn't terribly common as a political ideology (or at least openly identified with). Most real world progressives have a stronger collectivist bend that's at odds with the Tradition's view of how to achieve pluralism through individualism. Most real world libertarians have a strong conservative or at least laissez faire feeling towards even liking the idea of pluralism, putting them at odds with the Traditions' progressive streak despite promoting a more individualist stance.

                                            To play sorcerer's advocate for a moment.

                                            Assuming that in the pre-Order of Reason World of Darkness there was still diseases and illnesses propagated in the way that conventional medical science understands, which given Consensual Reality is something that could be debated.

                                            In pre-OoR society, they recognized that people from time to time would grow sick and suffer and perhaps even die, so they would do their level best to treat them. Whether through trying to expel spirits, using folk medicine, meditation, prayer, alchemical cleansing, arcane ritual, or whatever zaniness your local medical doctor etherite has cooked up.

                                            The important thing to note is that in the context of the World of Darkness and the paradigm prior to the Order of Reason, these medical practices aren't entirely limited to Willworkers. Certainly the Awakened excelled but when Consensual Reality called for spirit medicine or alchemical purification or sympathetic prayer; those were the tools of the day. Doctors could heal through appeasing spirits, cleaning the body, or calling for divine intercession.

                                            The doctors of that time might not have called things what we call them today, but they recognized when people suffered and would become sick and if they recognized the nature of the suffering they would use what had been sometime prior been found to work in healing it.

                                            Of course, as people have noted before, taking the implications of Consensual Reality into account leads to some bizarre and unhelpful allegories.
