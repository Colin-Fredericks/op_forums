    I'd be inclined to keep the Curia distinct from the Traditions until much later: even in the 20th century, the influence of the Church was a force to be reckoned with, and I wouldn't see the Curia admitting defeat and joining a power bloc until the middle to late 20th century at the earliest. Even then, the Traditions would need to be considerably less gnostic for the Curia to join with them: you'd need to re-envision the Traditions more along the lines of M20's Disparate Alliance, where there is no overall “we're all doing the same thing in different ways” glue holding them together, before the Curia would be willing to join: they'd be more inclined to side with Technocrats than with pagans. Whether the Technocrats would reciprocate is a different story.

            I'm not sure the Curia would ever "admit defeat" any more than the Church itself would because they are largely intertwined with it. Most likely they are part of the Church in a way not dissimilar to Franciscans or Jesuits are, wielding political power within it but ultimately subservient to it. Factions within the Curia might rise and fall and even schism as the Church does, but if the group itself is playing the role of orthodox Catholic mages, that position will be around as long as the Church is.

            Also, because I suspect that they would be a diverse lot within the bounds of that orthodoxy, they wouldn't change from Inquisitors to ecumenists as a group. Rather there would be both within the faction with one or the other (or neither) in ascendency at various times. I mean, its not like the Inquisition itself is even gone, it just changed its methods and its name. It's currently the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith and it is quite active.

            All that said, they would still be willing to work with other factions depending on who, why, and how that work is handled. I think Dataweaver has the right of it. They are much more likely to be a part of a Disparate alliance than under a Council of Traditions.


            Going back to the 1400s, lets say that the idea of the Consensus is still discovered at around the same time. If we are diverging from Mage history here are three important questions: Who discovers it? Who is interested in using it? How fast do they move?

                I actually think the Church might be the ones who would fire the opening shots of the Ascension War, and I think they'd move quick. Understand that throughout history, most people have been terribly interested in ensuring correct orthodoxy.                     As an addendum, if there really is better worldwide communication between mages, I would see something like the Disparate Alliance emerging earlier than the Traditions. Why? Because the Traditions are primarily a reaction to the Technocracy specifically while I've always interpreted the Disparates as emerging as a reaction to imperialism. In real life, there wasn't really a widespread anti-imperial alliance, but that was partly due to political divisions and lack of communication and transport. The latter isn't an issue for mages, and the former is less of an issue because mages are less attached to existing political institutions. It would struggle to get off the ground at first, but eventually people are going to notice that the Curia is bulldozing their way through much of the world and doing the occasional genocide. Combining forces is going to seem like the only realistic option to a lot of people.

                            Originally posted by Magus Maximus View Post
                            I think I agree. Not until the middle-late 20th century would they do it. AND there would be a rebel faction that would go rogue and hidden (everybody goes underground to plot revenge centuries later, I'm absolutely loving this lol)
                            Actually, I've already posited a “rebel faction” going rogue. But not hidden; and not splintering off in the 1900s. There's a reason why “Protestant” includes the word “protest”.

                            As Ramnesis said, the Curia wouldn't “admit defeat”; nor would it ever join a Council of Magick that's pushing the idea that pagans, spiritualists, necromancers, and the like are all ultimately doing the same thing that they are. That's why They'd only join something more along the lines of the Disparate Alliance, where there's no pressure to be ecumenical. They'd rather be part of the Technocratic Union; and the main reason they aren't is because the Union doesn't want them. In many ways, the Curia is very similar to the Gabrielites, save only that they weren't destroyed when they were kicked out of the nascent Union: they have enough of a power base in the various organized Churches to withstand efforts by the Technocrats to destroy them. They're a Craft, albeit a very powerful one.

                            The Protestant breakaway faction, on the other hand, needs an ecumenical philosophy to hold together as a coherent faction. Whether or not they'd be willing to extend that to non-Christian factions to the extend needed to join the Traditions is a separate issue; but I could see them being more open to joining the Traditions than the Curia would be, even if they make an effort to not get too sucked into what the Traditions are pushing. “Be in the world, but not of the world.”

                            Let's say that that's this setting's version of the Celestial Chorus. Mostly-Protestant Christian ecumenists rather than pseudo-Theosophists, who have joined the Traditions but don't feel terribly comfortable with their membership. In part because they fear that their membership will eventually lead to heresies springing up amongst them. Which has, in fact, happened: there are fringes of the Chorus who in fact have bought into Gnosticism and who resemble the published Celestial Chorus with its “we are all fragments of the One, and just need to be reminded of that” mindset. It's not a popular opinion amongst this Chorus, and those Choristers who hold such a view tend not to voice it too loudly; but it's there.

                            Conceptually, you'll mainly find bishops, priests, and nuns among the Curia. You mostly won't find them among the Celestial Chorus, though there are exceptions; instead, the Chorus tends to be decentralized (often with each congregation having no Earthly authority over it), and mainly features ministers and deacons. Given their lack of a central authority, it's a minor miracle that the Chorus has remained as harmonious as it has.

                                                            I was actually thinking, given the Traditions European bias, that they might be a consequence of the Curia/Technocracy attempting to suppress political and religious dissidents in Europe. The Technocracy has a habit of purging large parts of its organization pretty often, and often these groups survive in some form and join the Technocracy's enemies. In this case, they would have a falling out with various occult groups after the Disparate Alliance had already formed. The occultists are widespread and powerful enough that they're able to flee and regroup, but the Disparate Alliance isn't comfortable with them joining because not too long ago the occultists were pretty happy to be wearing the boot stamping on their faces.

                                                            Fast forward a couple of decades, and another group gets purged. These ones end up joining up with the occultists. Eventually you have a large and surprisingly powerful organization made up of the people ejected from the Curia/Technocracy/whatever it's called that it failed to kill. They're in an uneasy alliance of convenience with the Disparates that probably grows closer over time. Some of the groups we know and love won't be in there--I doubt the Kha'vadi or the Verbena would end up joining--but they might get additional defectors from the Technocracy. Look in the history of science and religion for approaches that lost out at the time; these could be the seeds of entirely new traditions. The Levellers, Humboldtian science, some of the earlier utopians before notions of eternal growth really took off; all of these are potential sources.
