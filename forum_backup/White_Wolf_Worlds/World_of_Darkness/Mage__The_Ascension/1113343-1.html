    Ok, so the PCs are ultimately fighting the Nephandi. I'd say start with the idea of 7 if you know the aspects of the demons and you can always cut down to 5 or 6 by having one of the other demons destroy the other one. Demons don't play nice.

Clearly there will need to be some ancient writings to decipher and weird signs left by demons who have minions but don't really get how this world works.             Well, if it's a big bad, remember you don't need to give it stats until it is time to fight it. Goals and how the minions are recruited and what they can do are more significant than stats for a mastermind villain fought in the end.

            One great thing about the Kingdoms of Kalamar campaign setting, a D&D campaign setting, is that most of the major adversaries in the world have a little history and you know their power base, but not their stats. The pseudo-Roman Emperor, Kabori, is a military genius trying to rebuild a falling empire. He's also capable of ordering his soldiers to great acts of brutality, such as burning whole villages that protest his rule. Is Kabori evil, is he good, is he lawful, is he chaotic--those questions are left up to the GM.

            If you go with the elements, then your minions could be devotees to darker versions of those elements. Make the fire minions pyromaniacs, water minions to be found of drowning people or giving everyone boating a personal introduction to ravenous sharks, the earth folks into earthquakes and live burials, and so forth. Some of those minions could be spirits and others could be people.

            If you go with the sins, then the minions are exemplars of those sins, or at least enjoy pretending one sin over others. Jodi Blake could easily do Lust, as that's one of her major Nephandic methods to get control of people.

                                                To make it a perfect Rock Paper Scissors scenario, you need each sin/element to have three they are strong against, and three they are weak against.

                                                Alternatively, you could do what fire emblem did in later versions with their magic. Have three elements have a Rock Paper Scissors relationship (for example, Water beats Fire beats Earth beats Water. Have all those things be be weak to something else (for example, Time), have that thing only weak to one other thing (for example, Space) and have that beatable by the original RPS, any of them.

                                                So it would look like this. ((Fire -> Earth -> Water -> Fire)) These are the Elements

                                                Time -> Elements -> Space -> Time

                                                This is just an example, you can mess around with this as much as you want to make the thematic components line up.

                                                If you want your game to have a more biblical bent, then you could always arranges the sins in hierarchical order as written in Dante's Inferno.

                                                The order, from least offensive to most offensive, goes as follows:

                                                Sloth
                                                Lust
                                                Gluttony
                                                Greed
                                                Wrath
                                                Envy
                                                Pride

                                                If you wanted to fill out the sphere list, you could add a few more from Dante's Inferno, such as Heresy, Treachery, Fraud, or Violence.
                                                There's a tonne of good stuff out there that can be adapted in whatever mould fits your story.

                                                    Seeing the new paradigms, practices, and foci material in the Mage 20 Book of Secrets and knowing that the Mage background material is pretty difficult to grasp as a new player, I'd suggest being a bit loose on paradigms at the beginning. Let the players get how their characters do magick over time. I know when I first played, my character was pretty lame on paradigm. In fact, Gerd Zauberei, due to being a vague PC and a series of poor rolls, became the Adept of Ineptitude. Now that I've spent years with the game, run a few campaigns and borrowed Mage ideas for other games, I'm a lot better on making characters with clear paradigms.

                                                    It may not be how everyone plays the game, but if you want to get immersed and not play mages throwing spheres around like superheroes, it helps to develop how the character's magick works. This requires time.

                                                    One rule I find useful for any tabletop RPG is to assume anything said at the table, unless incredibly tangential to the game, is said by the character a person is playing. This helps remind players not to communicate out-of-character and that if they make asides or nonsequitirs, the game will move on. In a game like Mage, where reality is being defined by how people perceive it, almost any odd remark could have been said in character. I couple this 'what's said at the table is said in character' rule with a reminder when we start the game and when we are on breaks. So, if we stop and eat, it's not an in-character conversation.

                                                    Oh, and I don't know if you have others at the game site or dropping by, but definitely consider what they will hear when you play and whether they will disrupt the game. The best game I played in was a year-long Vampire/Mage game. We basically were in character for all 6 hours or so we played each week, and even though my friends were pretty cool with people dropping by, on game night people who wanted to visit knew to just go to the computer or the fridge unless someone was on a break.

                                                            Originally posted by shadryx View Post
                                                            What do you think of these pairings? Fire/wrath, air/envy, water/lust, earth/sloth, spirit/pride, time/greed, and space/gluttony.
                                                            The latter two give me interesting ideas.

                                                            I like the idea of a demon that is constantly busy and abhors redundant statements and empty words, because he is literally a miser of time. Time is money for him, literally. The type of demon who will give orders to his infernalist follows that are straight and to the point, and who will become angry if he has to repeat himself (because they wasted his time by not listening well enough). His goals revolve around swindling people out of their personal time - and I mean in the sense that when he gives them something, he takes actual time as payment. The poor mortal blinks and finds that forty hours just passed they can't remember, because they didn't exist during that time. At the height of the demon's power, he was altering history by grabbing up people's pasts - hours at a time - in order to add to his coffers, changing how events played out by the person's absence.

                                                            I also like the idea of a demon that eats space. He's mostly a huge, gaping maw, in which vast quantities of empty space are contained. The kind of creature that doesn't travel by moving, but by consuming the space in front of him, until his destination is right there. Of course, because of the bottomless space he's consumed, he's terribly hungry also for things to fill it. So naturally, he eats everything else in front of him. I'm imagining an enormous worm, burrowing through the earth by eating it. And maybe the PCs get swallowed, and end up in a mass of matter floating in space. Lumps of raw earth, with heaps of concrete, boulders, cars and subway trains, and peasant houses sitting on it. And towering above it all is a medieval castle, which once belonged to a wizard until said magus gained the demon's ire. The PCs can raid the wizard's study for books and magical items, if they want. But they're also guarded by whatever magical defenses the wizard put in place. The island as a whole is also populated by the emaciated, half-mad cultists who failed the demon, and who want nothing more than to hunt the PCs down for their delicious meat.

                                                            Because of course a demon of gluttony would have starving cultists trapped in his gullet. That's just thematically appropriate.

                                                            Edit: Come to think of it, the wizard's study might hold the answer for how to escape the demon's stomach. The PCs reach the study, only to find the skeleton of the wizard slumped in his chair, having died of hunger, thirst, or simple old age, centuries ago (assuming he had the means to conjure food and water, so he persisted until his natural death). And on the work desk before him, his bony fingers rest on a journal, containing the instructions for a Ritual that could be used to escape, but that the wizard couldn't enact before his death. So, the PCs have to perform the ritual...all while most crazed cultists are on their way, intent on eating them.
