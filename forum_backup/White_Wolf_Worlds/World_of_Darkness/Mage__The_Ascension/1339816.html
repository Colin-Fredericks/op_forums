    Inhuman(6+) traits?

    10-06-2019, 09:15 AM
    Hello everyone! So how should inhuman traits work?

    We all know superhuman Abilities/Attributes do exist, and in theory mages can wield them through EXP and up to 10, but there are few mechanics given.

    Do they have some special roles or just add more dices to related pools? (To be honest the latter is very boring...)

    Resources I know:
    1. Revised has Legendary Attributes merit, which also tells you Stamina 6 has some additional functions(soak agg damage)
    2. Mummy Revised lists all Lv.6-8 Attributes
    3. Masters of the Art allows you to have 6 dots if you achieve archmagedom
    4. Some Dark Age books have brief description of 6 dots

                  Boring, but practical (esp. when pair with Magic Enhancing Abilities). Also... well.. boring is good to me here. I'd rather mages spend their XP on things besides 6+ traits. What makes mages fun and cool to play are all the cool things they do with magic, not pumping up their stats to levels generally reserved for immortal (not just supernatural) characters.

                  Originally posted by Rock113 View Post
                  And generally speaking 3 dots in traits are excellent to most people, 4 dots are extremely rare, 5 dots is the peak of WHOLE humankind/splat, 6+ are inhuman.
                  While not exactly uncommon... this is a pretty drastic overstatement of things.

                  3 dots is above average, not excellent. They're ratings for people that are good enough to stand out in the crowd so they could find employment in a field that relates to that trait. Physical Attributes at 3 and Athletics 3 aren't going to get you into Major League baseball, but you could easily get work in the minors, helping during spring training, or running serious training camps for high-school and college athletes.

                  4 dots aren't "extremely" rare. Keep in mind that almost all starting level supernaturals start with at least one four in an Attribute unless they take a Flaw to the contrary. This is not stated as some sort of inherent boost from becoming a supernatural (though they did do that in the CofD), but that such aptitude goes hand-in-hand with supernatural existence. As well, 4s in Knowledges are given a more concrete benchmark, that means every college professor that's qualified for their job has at least on 4 on their sheet. Every licensed doctor, lawyer that passed the bar, etc. all have 4s in their fields. While taking some fairly large assumptions here, I think it's fair to say around 1 out of every 15 adults has at least one Ability at 4+ based on how the games define these things. It just feels more rare when you spread out all those 4s between all the different Abilities.

                  5 is, indeed, "peak" but nothing in the book describes this as all that rare, esp. because one trait at 5 alone doesn't make you the peak of humanity. A scientist with Intelligence 2 and Science 5 isn't mechanically better than a Scientist with Intelligence 3 and Science 4. The peak of humanity is the Scientist with Intelligence 5 and Science 5 (and lots of additional side traits). And that's for something nice and easy where the game abstracts things out enough that one Attribute and one Ability is enough. If you want to be peak human as playing soccer, you need a lot of Attributes at 5 on top of your Athletics at 5.

                  Ultimately though, the PCs have ready access to 4s and 5s, because the game wants them to; gatekeeping it behind perceived notions of rarity isn't really the spirit of things.

                  Btw to be honest WW authorities do not obey their own rules, especially for vampires.
                  Sure... though there are no official creation rules for NPCs. The NPCs in the books are just stated however the authors felt they should be, because creation rules are for the PCs to keep the group on roughly the same page regarding what's appropriate at start.

                      Personally, I'm not sure why abilities (i.e. skills/talents/knowledges, not Attributes!) above 5 were always behind some supernatural power barrier (Vampire Generation, etc.) instead of the more simple and realistic mechanic of....age. Pure age.
                      We can presume 5 is as good as a human can get in something in their normal lifetime; Some do it a bit faster, some take most of their life to reach that level. But if you've practiced something for 200 years, gained knowledge in person about a topic for several centuries or simply have performed certain tasks for that long of a time, without the debilitating effects that normally come with very old age, it stands to reason that 6+ is simply a natural progression of you being around that long for it.

                      It would make so much sense, and also give any Unaging merit or immortality effects more mechanical flavor than just roleplaying related possibilities. Of course the ST would have to consider if 6+ 'd be logical for a character - a 300 years old Mage that started woodworking 5 years ago won't push to 6+ in a short time.

                          Realism-wise, while impossible to prove in humans, education research tends to favor the idea that learning plateaus; that there isn't an infinite potential to continue growing if you had more time to dedicate to something. This is well demonstrated in the physical realm of activity. Genetic and early developmental conditions are going to set your maximum sprint. If I had 1,000 years to hone the perfect sprinting technique without worrying about bodily decline, I'd still never come close to Usain Bolt. I can't train my ratio of muscle group types to get better sprinter muscles. I can't train my frame to be lankier, so I have a naturally slimmer build with a long stride to increase how efficiently I can turn muscle power into speed. Etc.

                          A lot of what we perceive as growth after a certain point is horizontal rather than vertical. In game terms, branching out into different Abilities rather than ever increasing one. I could master playing the guitar, but at some point I'll plateau in how far I can ever go in my specific aptitude for performing as a musician (maxing out Performance). The perception of growth is going to come in as I start improving my song writing abilities (Expression), study musical history and theory (Academics), and develop a better understanding of the science of music, acoustics, and how to apply them via sound engineering (Science and Technology). So I would be growing as an artist, even if my guitar playing skills aren't technically getting better.

                          A whole other issue is that skills require upkeep (which is a plausible reason why we don't see continued vertical growth). The more you try to improve your skills, the more time you dedicate to practicing and keeping them up to date. At a certain point, there wouldn't be enough time in the day to practice all your training and you'd slowly start to lose skills you don't focus on. The world's best blacksmith can't just stop smithing for a decade and come back and expect to be at the same level they used to be at. They have t constantly practice their skills just to maintain where they are, and then devote even more time in any hopes of improvement, which would preclude also having time to master lots of other stuff.

                          From a simplicity perspective, setting everything at 5 is vastly easier than trying to set plateaus in a fair but more natural/realistic fashion. Having a system to set a character's personal plateaus in every trait is a giant pain in the ass for little positive benefit to playing the game.

                              Yeah, but the question then is, what abilities over 5 even *mean*. Especially in knowledges. If higher values in knowledges are not explainable by supernatural age allowing you to gain more of it, what *do* they represent?

                              But, on the other topic, regarding the problem of levels of 4-5 being supposedly quite rare in the population...
                              The core issue is, that there is a disparity between what those dots technically mean vs. the gameplay-mechanics effect they have at the average table.
                              Even having a 4 instead of a 3 in an attribute or ability is supposed to, as-written, put you leaps and bounds over the other. Heck, I showed that off in my little signature'd thread about what the individual strength dots actually mean.
                              Game-mechanics wise though, all you get is simply a single additional die, which doesn't even translate to an additional success on average with standard difficulty. And the standard difficulty of 6 is not an 'everyday stuff' difficulty, but more an 'out of the norm for you' difficulty, as-written.
                              It's that disconnect between the meaning of the higher level vs. the actual mechanics result that give a feeling of 'More dots aren't a big deal' or 'You really need high stats to pull most things off well.'

                              Now, I personally think part of that also mostly stems from some STs having fallen into a trap of not giving enough individual value to the resulting successes of a roll, and rarely if ever going below difficulty 6 for even basic tasks. I've been at tables where the outcome of rolls was "Ah, only two successes. Not enough, sorry, you fail!", while the books actually stress that if you get successes on a roll in the first place, you in some way, shape or form partially succeeded, and that three successes should give you a full blown just-perfectly-as-you-wanted-it result.
                              Anything above is moving into territory of "Holy shit that was amazing!" and standing ovations.

                              TL;DR: Difficulty is supposed to be the dial for controlling how hard something is - especially *lower* difficulties than 6, which get often neglected by STs - not an arbitrary amount of required successes. Except for non-attribute things like Arete rolls of course. And giving more weight to individual successes in the first place also makes rarity of high dots in attributes/abilities more sensible. If you need to spam high amounts of successes to perform basic actions, everybody needs high stats to function.

                                      I don't know about modern era Mage but in DA:Mage I have a system for permanent supernatural stats (but I also require players to spend XP as normal). Three dots in your Fundation and 3 dots in 2 of your relevant Pillars allow you to permanently raise one of your attributes to 6 dots by casting an ongoning extended spell. You need 60 successes gained over 10 minutes intervals. As per the usual rules you also need to spend 1 point of quintessence for success (so 60 quintesence) then another 15 quintessence and 2 willpower points to seal the spell.

                                      For example an hermetic with Modus 3, Anima 3 and Primus 3 can attempt the ritual above to permanently increase one of his own physical attributes to 6 (provided said attribute was at 5 dots already AND the correct amount of XP was avaiable), while using Corona 3 instead of Anima allows to increase mental attributes and using Forces does the same with social attributes.

                                      It's costy, but during the DA this does not cause backlash unless you botch. The system is also meant to allow for higher increases (4 dots in fundation, 4 dots in one pillar 3 in the next allow you to attempt a ritual to increase an attribute to 7 and 4 dots in fundation and 4 in 2 relevant pillars allow for an increase to 8 dots, 5 dots in fundation, 5 dots in one pillar and 4 dots in another one and you can try to get one attribute to 9 and finally you need 5 in fundation and 5 in 2 pillars to get 10 permanent dots in one attribute. Obviously the quintessence and willpower expense increases as well, following the rules of ongoning spells).

                                      I've never considered 6+ abilities raised on a permanent basis but I suppose one could adapt the method above in order to get the desired result.

                                                  In the sister game Awakening, mages can raise Attributes and Abilities as high as 10 (or a limit of 5 or Gnosis, whichever is higher). Different rules and different there, true. But looking at it objectively as a bunch of descriptive numbers, six to ten is not really *that* superhuman. I have the same issue with old D&D and most superhero games. Allow me to use superheros to illustrate my point. Batman and Captain America, both at peak human potential (say Strength 5). In comparison, what Strength would Superman or the Hulk have? 10? Only twice as strong? I understand thematically it represents much more than that. But in the math mechanics, a Str 10 punch does the same damage as a Str 5 guy with a sword. Superhuman yeah, but just barely.

                                                  Which is to say that, in the role of a st, I would have no issue with a PC raising stats above 5 if they spend the xp and have a plausible excuse. I would not invoke pattern bleeding, as it just seems to be an artificial meta-game barrier.

                                                          Watch out with this--the special powers or abilities listed for Lvls. 6-8 are incredibly specific to the sorts of powers Mummies (and specifically the Amenti--mummies made using the non-corrupted/actually functioning life equation/immortality spell/Horus magic thing). You can house rule it and say that some mages can, or whatever (you can house rule anything obviously,) but just be cognizant that, as the game exists right now, those powers for attributes above 5 that are listed in Mummy: The Resurrection have a very limited scope and availability.
