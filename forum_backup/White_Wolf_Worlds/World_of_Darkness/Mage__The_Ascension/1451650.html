    1940s Hypertech

    06-11-2021, 09:33 AM
    Hi!

    As usual, I come here looking for ideas and inspiration.

    Im running a historical mage story set in the 1940s and it has been difficult for me to imagine the kind of gadgets and hypertech the Technocracy might have had back then. Grasping steampunk-like/victorian stuff seems too out of date, while anything digital seems too futuristic (I wouldn't like to break the historical mood that much).

    Any ideas would be greatly appreciated.

          Am I right that this is more about aesthetics than about capabilities?

          A general rule of thumb when figuring out Technocracy aesthetics is that it is a mix of what technology looks like at the time with what people (sleeper and Technocrat) think it will look like in 20-50 years. You can get an idea of what people of the 40's thought the future would look like by searching for 40's scifi cover art.

          When new things are introduced they often start out looking like old things. The earliest cars looked a lot like carriages. The earliest plastic phones were made to look like traditional wood and metal ones. Only after the new thing becomes normal do people begin experimenting with new shapes and appearances.

          I'll add one other suggestion and that is that the early 40s is part of WWII so expect things to have a certain utilitarian/surplus aesthetic. On the other hand the late 40s was the start of the cold war and paranoid espionage. Expect tech from then to be subtle and hidden.

              Originally posted by Dataweaver View Post
              Check out the writings of the Golden Age of Science Fiction: Robert Heinlein, Isaac Asimov, Arthur Clark, and their like.
              More information: the genres are retroactively labeled dieselpunk and atompunk, because naturally every SFF sub-genre has to be extraneously called something entirely different and suffixed with "-punk" regardless how, what, why, content, context, or theme.

              As much as I loathe to call it out, thematically the Fallout games would be a good start, minus the post-apocalyptic nature of the setting. The cumbersome, bulky, ostentatious powered armors that go hand-in-hand with the setting would be entirely in It-X's and the VE's wheelhouse. But beyond that, the smart move would be for things to be digital and powered by transistors and microprocessors; the rule of thumb with hypertech in the post-industrial era, is for it to function equivalently to technology fifty years ahead of its period, or a semi-reasonable analog in non-historical settings but with a mishmash of that era's aesthetic and ideas of what technology would be.

              So, a good example of a 1940's hypertech device would be a...cell phone. Just a cell phone, no bells or whistles, certainly no Technocractic intranet connectivity but maybe agents with a high degree of technical expertise could send SMS. Except, one of the early- to mid-'90s satchel phones, and instead of having a nickel-cadmium battery, being powered by a miniature radiothermal generator. And of course, because cell towers don't exist yet, it would instead use a Correspondence effect to connect to real-life operators -- likely Extraordinary Citizens -- who would patch agents into the existing telephone system.

                  The biggest influence is probably going to be the "Golden Age" of Science Fiction, generally centered around John W. Campbell and his editorship of Astounding Science Fiction magazine. He tended to prefer "hard" science fiction stories, based on various scientific theories and technologies of the day, and the celebration of scientific progress and what said progress would enable humanity to accomplish. (The flip side of this is the same period's dystopian works influenced by what was going on in the Soviet Union, Germany, Italy, and Spain. This includes Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1936), George Orwell's 1984 (1948), and Alexander Korda's adaption of HG Wells's Things to Come (1936), as well as earlier works like Yevegeny Zamyatin's We (1920) and Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927).)

                  From an aesthetic perspective, this is the period when Art Deco had transitioned into Streamline Moderne and is now (after the War) developing Mid-Century Modern and Googie styles. (Googie is especially interesting as it was heavily influenced by the period's development of car culture, jets, and the Atomic Age.) Again, the flip side of this are the Brutalist, Stalinist, and PWA Moderne. If you plug any of those terms into Google Images (or its equivalent) along with "design" or "architecture", you can get a good idea of a lot of the overall design style.

                  As far as technology, everything is Analog. This is a world of transistors, vacuum tubes, and slide rules, as well as new synthetic materials (such as nylon and plexiglass) and computing machines that took up entire rooms (if not entire buildings). This will continue to be a thing for a while, even in science fiction. If you've ever seen the earliest appearances of Iron Man in the early 60s, all his technology for his suit is based entirely on the "magic" of transistors and magnets.

                  The single signature device for Technocracy agents throughout the 30s and 40s (and even into the 60s, to be honest) is the Dick Tracy Two-Way Wrist Radio. How you have this item work is up to you. Correspondence is one option, as is Mind, or possibly even Forces. It may also be an almost mundane device constructed by taking advantage of Matter to make the parts small enough to actually work and Forces or Prime for a power source.
                  Other ideas I'd suggest would be to borrow heavily from other 30s and 40s pulp works, including radio and serials. This includes Green Hornet's gas pistol and his Black Beauty car (it's main feature being that it's faster than anything else on the road, but you could easily upgrade it to being fairly damage resistance via Matter). Bullet-resistant suits or coats, especially for MiBs, is probably fitting. Ray guns may be a little too vulgar for the Union's tastes, but are probably still an option during emergencies. It's probably a little too soon for power armor, as that isn't really a thing until the late 50s. The idea of replacing body parts with artificial ones is an idea from the time. Electric brain stimulators. Emergency medical regeneration tanks. Microsurgery tools. And various miracle drugs that can (temporarily) eliminate the need to eat, sleep, or go to the bathroom, or put you into a hibernating sleep state.

                      Comic books and comic strips of the era (and derivative media like movie serials) would likely provide a good example of advanced technology that would be available as how it looks. You can find three Flash Gordon movie serials in the public domain that show all sorts of advance science and technology as imagined by people at the time. It's a good example to see what they though the future would be like, and how much they completely missed what it would be like.

                      Another easy solution would be to take distinctive jet age technology from the 1960s and have it be in the 1940s. The 1960s was still very much an analog technology age. Simple transistors appeared to be miracle that could do anything. The early Iron Man comics of Stan Lee always used the term "transistor" to explain Iron Man's power like it was magic.

                                          As far as the Progenitors go, eugenics will still be something of an influence. While it's already starting to be socially discredited because of the German National Socialists (with, for example, HG Wells renouncing his previous support of the concept by 1940), there is always that certain type of enlightened expert who will refuse to abandon ideas or movements they've based much of their professional career on. On the flip side, this period, from the 30s to the very early 50s, is when a lot of our current understanding of DNA and how it worked were first developed, including some of the early science fiction ideas about genetic engineering. And as Astromancer mentions, this is the era of "wonder drugs", most especially the real world development and mass production of penicillin and sulfa drugs.

                                          And as Dataweaver mentions, the Void Engineers, especially those operating beyond the Gauntlet, probably go the full Buck Rogers route, complete with laser guns and rocket packs. And if you've never seen it, I do recommend the Colonel Weird material from Jeff Lemire's Black Hammer comics, especially the Cosmagog miniseries, as potential period Void Engineer inspiration.

                                          Regarding psychic abilities and parapsychology, this is just after the era of places like Stanford and Duke Universities trying to study things like ESP and psychokinesis in a laboratory setting, and getting what could best be described as mixed results. Scientific interest in psychic phenomenon fell off a bit during the War, but started to pick up again in the 50s. To a certain degree, the Progenitors are likely to continue studying it, especially once they can try to genetically engineer potential subjects. However, this is probably sort of a back burner project at least until the 50s. (On the other hand, the 30s pulp style of psychical abilities is certainly still very much a thing for the period Sons of Ether.)
