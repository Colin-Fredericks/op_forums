          Think of the game you want to run; the emotions you wanna evoke, the pacing, the mood, the tone. Now make a small list of themes--maybe in the form of key words or maybe just images you could stick to a virtual image board or copy/paste into a word document (I often have the names of movies, characters from fiction, scenes from specific movies, or sometimes just clever ads or album covers). Maybe even prepare a short plain statement describing how you would want your players to describe your game, elevator pitch style.

          Now, whenever you run into a snag in the game--something that requires that you shoot from the hip or cobble together a scene, character, problem, or solution on the fly... look at your word doc/image board/mission statement/whatever. This always helps to re-focus me onto the core milieu of my game, and (it is my belief that) it helps keep whatever rando decision I make stay tonally consistent. That said, over time, after the game has developed its own personality, informed not only by your creative vision, but the players' as well, this reference tool may become less useful (though I always reference mine when planning the next major story arc).

          Hope this helps! Good luck and have fun!!!

                  Make sure the players make characters who can and will work together. They can have personal plots, but there should be a reason the players are at the table at the same time. This is useful in most games, of course, but it's particularly pertinent in games where the characters can have wildly divergent worldviews and agendas. Likewise, find out what the players want to do, and what their characters want as well, which may be very different things!

                  Introduce the setting bits at a time. Get a hang of the Traditions and Technocracy, and how they operate unseen in the mundane world, before bringing in Marauders, Nephandi, spirit realms and zeppelins. Similarly, don't feel the need to include everything - a low-key game about a casual cabal who meet at a coffee shop in San Francisco might not be the best place for those zeppelins to show up.

                  There aren't many published adventures available. The free M20 quickstart includes a cabal of characters with plot hooks built in, rather than a ready-to-run adventure.

                  There are also adventures for first edition: the intro adventure Loom Of Fate, another shorter intro adventure in White Wolf Magazine 37 and the famously over-the-top Werewolf: The Apocalypse crossover Chaos Factor - you can get Loom and Chaos in this collection.

                  And for Revised there's the Vampire: The Masquerade crossover Blood Treachery, and the "end of the world" book Ascension containing five adventures featuring different ways to end the setting.

                  That said, Mages could investigate the strange happenings in many modern horror adventures, including ones for the World Of Darkness and Chronicles Of Darkness lines as well as other RPGs - as long as you're prepared to adjust statistics as required, and consider their power level compared to "average joe" monster hunters as they face their enemies with arcane knowledge and mystical powers rather than blind faith and shotguns.

                  Craig Oxbrow
                  The Trinity Continuum freelancer

                          Awakenings are very personal. They need to be experiences, like Seekings, where mages realize that reality is not what it is. They need to be transformative experiences.

                          If we go by the groups, a Chorister is primarily empowered by their faith in the One. So, some religious revelation would be in order. Hollow Ones are a particular brand of 'orphan,' and tend to tie into the gothic culture in Mage lore. So, for the Hollow One, they'd probably be thumbing their nose at authority or having some ironic realization. The Ecstatic is from a tradition that's about experiences that are mind-blowing and assuming they take Time as their affinity sphere, you'd want some experience where their sense of time is jarred.

                          The Hermetic is the most interesting and difficult case. See, the Order of Hermes comes into Mage from Ars Magica, so you have many different little groups and practices blending into western occultism and studious learners of magic. A Hermetic will typically awaken after years of study, moving from just going through magical rituals to performing minor magics ala a sorcerer to Awakening. However, depending on the House, this could be through doing magic tied to the fae, magic tied to sacred numbers, and so many other things. The Order of Hermes is one of my two favorite traditions (the other being the Sons of Ether) because there are a lot of common beliefs but the Hermetic can have many different connections due to their houses.

                          The Initiates of the Arts book from Mage Revised might provide some further ideas on Awakenings.

                          If there are four players, perhaps you could have them work with you in pairs on their Awakenings if the character concepts mesh.

                                              Mage 1e was much more open-ended and you could do more powerful magic without having Paradox eat you for lunch. Also, since it was closer to the time Ars Magica was published, you got more of a flavor of the Hermetic history. The Order of Hermes in Ars Magica, published by Lion Rampant and later White Wolf, ends up being important to early Mage because you get the mages. You also see what Tremere was in the Order of Hermes in Ars Magica and how it becomes the clan in Vampire.

                                              Mage 2e, especially the Guide to the Technocracy, is furthering the idea in Mage 1e but running it a bit more cleanly and with some better information on the factions. If you want the Technocracy as decent people, the Guide to the Technocracy was great at making Technocrats playable as heroes or more believable as antagonists. The Technocracy was humanized.

                                              Mage Revised, especially the Avatar Storm and nastier Paradox, restrict player options. For the Order of Hermes, several Houses are gone or weakened, because the fall of Doisstep and Horizon and other chantries cuts off the masters from everyone else. So, if one is playing a Hermetic, which history you use matters a lot.

                                              As for the Mage Revised books many things about them are good, but they are far too declensionist for my tastes. Everything is moving towards the Apocalypse in both the fiction and the rules, creating a more grimdark World of Darkness.

                                              I've owned most of the material for Mage in digital form or print form over the years and recently picked up Mage 20. I like the blending of the good ideas from the various editions, as well as the sidebar options for how to handle the metaplot.

                                              For The OP and anyone else running a chronicle, unless your group is going to completely follow a metaplot or is rules-restricted like the Camarilla is, it is best to figure out what you want and take the seasonings of Mage that work for that. Rule 0 of any game should be the person running the game is always right, with the entailed duty to do right by those other participants in the game by taking into account their tastes and being fair.

                                                  There's a plot structure in urban fantasy that has become pretty standard: a bad thing manifests itself and threatens the status quo; the protagonists use occult lore to research the thing, locate the thing, and then eliminate the thing, restoring the status quo. That is fine if you are running a Technocracy game--for the most part the Technocrats are statusing the hell out of the quo. But mystic mages are an aberration. Their mere existence represents upheaval to reality as we know it. Tradition games and Orphan games are better done when the protagonists are proactive rather than reactive, remaking the world in their image.

                                                  There is also a practical reason for attempting to go beyond the urban fantasy mystery plot: the magic rules in Mage make it really easy for players to uncover mysteries. Finding the McGuffin becomes pretty easy with Correspondence. Finding out What Happened is not that hard when you have Time 2.

                                                  Edit: To head off another iteration of the same debates--yes, the Technocracy creates and facilitates a lot of change. But a Technocracy story is more likely to center around stopping a thing. Not always, of course. There are disagreements and is dynamism among Technocrats, just like sometimes thing-stoppage makes sense for mystic mages. I'm speaking in generalities here.

                                                      Instead of the status quo, think about a new equilibrium point if you want the game to be dynamic. Sure, big bad thing comes out of the night and the PCs have to stop it, but just going back to the status quo is more like an episodic TV show like Star Trek. If you go with a new equilibrium point, the PCs stopped the big bad thing and are changed by that. Maybe there's a subtle change to society and maybe all of the changes are in the mages, but it's more fun to have a game change.

                                                      You can mix proactive and reactive scenes in any game. My preference is to have my various factions and antagonists developed enough so that they act regardless of whether the PCs act. That means a Nephandi cabal planning on bringing more hate to the city would keep working on their dastardly plans to empower hate groups. If the Nephandi's plans were thwarted, they would have plans to resolve their failures. So, let's say the PCs get one of the mouthpiece racists the Nephandi use captured. The Nephandi cabal would have another minion they could promote, or recruit another.

                                                      Also, most mages are going to know to take precautions to make it harder to do magic. Correspondence and Time are powerful, but subtle mages tend to make it harder to find them. For mages who use cameras as part of their Correspondence effect, foes who know how to avoid the camera are going to be hard to see. The mage might have to use a more vulgar effect or give up the chase.

                                                      For the OP, what are your big bads and what do they want?
