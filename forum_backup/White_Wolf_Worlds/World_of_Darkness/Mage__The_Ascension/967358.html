    Magick question: "What happens when the consensus is WRONG?"

    09-11-2016, 12:21 PM
    Background on me: I love Mage, or at least the idea of it... but back when I started WoD, my troupe found the 2E magick system too complex and with too many unanswered (IMPORTANT) questions to ever practically play it. So we never did. Now, 2 editions later, I'm seeing people here intelligently debate and quote books on topics that had everyone walking away in a huff before... so I thought I'd start asking. (I'll divide them into separate threads to try and avoid making this a mess)

    The actual question:

    Consensual reality and the notion of "Would an onlooker see this as impossible?" seem to be the major players in what's Coincidental and Vulgar in Mage.... so what happens when:

    1) The effect is totally plausible, but there's no way any onlooker (hypothetical or otherwise) would have the knowledge/omniscience to KNOW it's plausible. Sudden aneurysms, a bare wire somewhere buried in the walls making something elsewhere electrified, or any effect where a background in science or intense googling would be required to know it's actually a thing that happens. There might be a perfectly scientific explanation (or method used) for Mark Patsy to suddenly drop dead while walking down the street... but does that make it coincidental? If so, or if that's accepted as a thing that CAN happen, does the Mage even NEED a convoluted method or explanation to kill that way?

    2) The effect is actually scientifically possible, but due to poor education or common misconception most people would think that the opposite, or something completely different would happen. Like INCREASING the concentration of a flammable gas so it snuffs out its own flame (due to lack of oxygen mix), making water pure so it won't conduct electricity, or using concrete as a conduit path of your own electrical attack.

    3) Something is impossible, but due to poor education or common misconception most people think that's the way it happens. Best example is the belief a penny dropped off the Empire State can kill someone or embed itself inches into concrete, rather than not even being able to leave a bruise. (Of course the Mythbusters probably had an impact on this one, but until then I think most folks accepted it as fact)

                  As stated above, Science as we know it in Real Life is, in MtAs, just one of many equally valid Paradigms for Reality. Indeed, "Science" is kind of a loose term, and what's seen as scientific fact has changed radically over millennia. The ancient Greek natural philosophers were convinced of the primacy of the four classical elements, and that unicorns really existed (it wasn't the myth-makers that wrote about them, but the naturalists). Medieval doctors and researchers talked at length about humorism, and performed long calculations to track the paths of planets, assuming a geocentric model. As late as the nineteenth century, substances like the Luminiferous Ether, Miasma, and Phlogiston were ardently defended by the scientific consensus. It would be arrogant of us modern thinkers to presume that we now have a completely "correct" grasp of how the world works, and that subsequent years won't see long-held "facts" overturned (and then seen by future men and women as quaint concepts).

                  Now imagine that no aspect of Science is set in stone, but is based entirely on the ability of thinkers to convince other people of a theory's viability. Imagine that things like Darwinian Evolution became exploitable facts (for Technomancers), simply because it became massively popular in the public consciousness.

                  When it comes to Mage and coincidence, the important question is not "does Science support this?", but rather "does popular belief support this?". If enough people in a given area believe that glass is actually a very slow-moving liquid (rather than a solid crystal), a Mage could possibly manipulate glass like a fluid and have it be coincidental. On the other hand, most people don't realize that flammable gases need a certain concentration of oxygen to ignite; they assume that if it's flammable, that's all that's needed for a combustion reaction.

                                                  Originally posted by Lord Revan View Post

                                                  This brings the question how would the world (physics) be if there were no humand beings to create consensus?

                                                  Personally, I think not everything is resultof consensus, so no "more breast cancer" because people start believing in it.

                                                  About the questions of OP, I'd say every of them is coincident. To be vulgar it must be a "WTF?!?" situation.
                                                  The fundamental problem with attributing cancer to earthly foundations is that cancer is a disease that only makes sense in a medical paradigm that includes genetics and cellular structures. Generic wasting diseases could exist in a paradigm revolving around evil spirits or humors, but their behavior (and in some cases intelligence) would be so different they can't possibly be the same thing.

                                                  This is actually a major problem with Earthly Foundations, and why I rely on them as little as possible.

                                                  Classic Mage Questions: Was the earth flat before the Ancient Greeks proved it was round? Did the sun revolve around the earth until Copernicus and Galileo brought their maths to everyone's attention? And in general the idea is that, yes, the consensus really can shape the world to that degree. At one point, heavier things did fall faster than slower things. At one point the planets were pushed around by angels.

                                                  But what does that mean for the concept of gravity? On a flat world gravity is a generic unidirectional tendency in one direction. On a geocentric one, it is a universal pull towards a central point (one that only affects earth, given that angels need to push the planets around). In Newton's model it's a general attraction of mass to mass. In one I'm always falling towards earth, unless I get beneath it, in which case I'm falling away. In one I'm falling towards the center of the universe, but not everything is. In one everything behaves the same way, but I can be pulled in any direction depending on where I am.

                                                  An Earthly Foundation of "Things are affected by a downward force" seems a pretty arbitrary universal rule when we can completely change the definition of "down," "force," and "things."

                                                  I prefer to think of it this way. We live for so long in an environment where things fall downward that it is deeply written into our expectations, in a way that things like cars and gunpowder and germs just aren't. We learn to account for gravity and light and matter before we even learn to speak. They are consequently next to impossible to convince the consensus to fully abandon in any kind of straightforward way. It's not an arbitrary set of universal constants at work, its just a massive amount of conceptual inertia.

                                                      I would contest this, actually, depending on how 'basic' you mean by 'basic behavior'. While "things fall down" is certainly a constant, and the behavior of most things near the Earth's surface is as well (and reinforced by direct human experience), the actual behavior away from the most commonly encountered situations are... highly variable, and not just in 'what causes this'.

                                                      For instance, Newtonian and Einsteinian gravity models both describe the gravitational force such that in some places, there is no down for things to fall. Places far away from Earth's surface, sure, but places nonetheless - where the forces balance out to exactly zero. That means that the force can in a sense 'not exist' in places. And these places are different for the different theories and models. Likewise, how much fall things undergo is likewise variable.

                                                      Also, they define a zero-gravity-point in the center of the Earth, so it's not quite as... solely unEarthly as might be expected.

                                                      So, if both those models, and many others - respecting the 'things which are heavyish on Earth act this way' constraint - can be true based on Consensus, then Consensus can actually get very close to altering the 'basic' behavior of these constants in a lot of ways, so long as certain particular global constraints are respected.

                                                      These global constraints are probably the true constants, unlike the possibly-deceptive labels of "gravity" and so on.
