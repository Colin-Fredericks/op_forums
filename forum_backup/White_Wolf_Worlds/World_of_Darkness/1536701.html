                  Different answer:

                  Humans.

                  While the books tend to lay the blame on the Weaver, they also tend to do so from the unreliable narrator perspective when touching on such matters. Though this is also something that evolved as the game did. In early WtA the Weaver wasn't treated nearly as seriously as a threat, but that changed as the 90s chugged along a the idea that the Weaver wasn't ascendant didn't really reflect "our world but darker" well. Hence the books started to treat "the Weaver is to blame"as more of the default because at a minimum it would be very hard for the characters in the setting to see it as anything other than this.

                  But that somewhat ignores what caused the Weaver to suddenly lose sight of its place in the Triat. The Weaver might have struck the first blow in the Triat falling out of balance, but that just shifts the question the OP asked: what could have possibly caused the Weaver to become corrupted from its purpose? "The Weaver just went overboard with its duties, after untold eons of doing it right," isn't really much of an answer there.

                  The books also offer plenty on my differing answer. The rise of humanity is pretty much directly linked to everything going wrong in the WtA cosmology. Humans being out of touch with spirituality and true natural wilderness has always been a core of the game. Humans, even normal ignorant ones, have an immensely outsized influence on the animistic spirits of the setting. Every other animal naturally exists within the (not very pleasant) natural order equated with Gaia herself; existing in a state of homeostatic balance that the Triat stems from instead of the other way around. Humans are (possibly inherently) the one that embody the Triat first, and honor Gaia second, instead of the other way around. Humans created imbalance in the Triat by giving the Triat something to actually want to fight over. There was finally something in the Tellurian that could give one member of the Triat power over the others. The whole concept of the "anthropocene epoch" very neatly aligns with, well, everything going wrong; including the Fera going at each other and the Wars of Rage starting.

                      It also depends on the Triat's relationship to Gaia. Sometimes Gaia is presented as the creator of the Triat, sometimes the Triat is presented as a universal element while Gaia is local to the Earth and associated Umbra. In the latter case, there probably isn't an understandable explanation for how the Weaver and Wyrm corrupted unless it is only a local corruption. That said, the idea that Werewolves have even the slightest hope of fighting the Wyrm or restoring its balance implies that the problem is more local (if you can call the entire Earth and associated Umbra local).

                      If Gaia created them, it is important to remember that she is not depicted as perfect and she certainly does not create perfect, unchanging beings. Her creations are flawed and adaptive. If she creates a balance it is not one of a perfectly balanced scale, but of a tightrope walker who is moment by moment adjusting to maintain that balance; a constant series of destabilizations and momentary equilibriums. . Nothing pushes towards balance, everything (including her "guardians") actually pushes towards imbalance, just in different directions. Usually this ends up pushing the world back towards some new balance, though there is no certainty it always will.

                      If the Triat are her creations they would be the same: their metaphysical balance would be created not by some ideal harmony but by constant competition. If that is the case, nothing happened to the Weaver to make her fight the Wyrm, she was always doing that anyways. What was different was her assault was particularly effective.

                                              The biome equilibrium analogy really doesn't work on a number of levels (also, biome stability is not something you can measure year-to-year, that's like conflating weather and climate).

                                              The biggest issue is that wolves and deer don't intentionally work towards a balance for a number of reasons, including that balance being beyond their ability to comprehend. Gaia created beings with the ability to perceive and comprehend the state of balance in the system, and then actively get involved. The WtA is not built on a system of mindless actors. Gaia, the Triat, the Celestines, the Incarnae and all their brood, the shifters... they're all conscious intelligent actors on the system. Regardless of how one wants to position Gaia vs. the Triat in hierarchy, Gaia wanted intentional actors in the system. The WoD is an animist world where the wolves and the deer are being steered by forces they don't recognize, but are not just the natural processes of real world evolution either.

                                              "The Weaver just landed a particularly effective attack," again, doesn't really explain anything. It just takes it as an axiomatic truth that this was always possible and thus over along enough period of time one of the Triat would destabilize the system irrevocably. But the only evidence of that is that it happened, creating a circular argument at best. We know stable-tension systems exist. Tension systems are not inherently subject to probabilistic ends where every moment is an extremely low probability event that the system breaks and it's just when we finally get that one in a vastly huge number chance event. Most tension systems will fail eventually, but also predictably rather than on probability (even if we, as imperfect beings, need to rely on statistics to estimate when that will be). IRL, we know that baring an external event disrupting things, the Earth's orbit around the sun is stable enough that it will last until the sun starts to increase in size and then swallows the Earth. That is the knowable end result of the gravity-tensions of the solar system we live in. It's not a matter of "when will one gravity-body disrupt the system first." We can't measure things precisely enough to give an exact date to when this will happen, but we can make a very good prediction considering the time scales involved.

                                              It is, of course, possible for something to change that equilibrium that's not in the current equation. Any number of celestial events could knock Earth out of orbit, or just generally mess everything up. And we can't really predict any of them reasonably (we know our galaxy is on a collision course with another galaxy, but galaxies are so big and mostly empty, we have no idea how disruptive that will be "locally").

                                              Since I see no compelling reason to assume the tension based stabilize of the Triat is inherently probabilistic in its internal equilibrium, the best answer to why things change is always going to be the introduction of something new to the system. And the books provide a direct answer to that: humans.
