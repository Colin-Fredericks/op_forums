    Originally posted by No One of Consequence View Post
    In my head canon, I tend to think of the proto-Adepts as being parts of the Medieval Craftmasons and Subtle Ones, as well as some of the Byzantine Artificers, with a lot of them being monks, scholars, merchants, alchemists, and physics. The development of the printing press is probably what caused them to start to coalesce into a functional Lodge/Craft by the late Renaissance/early Enlightenment. The Sectors Guild would have been full of mapmakers, explorers, astronomers, clockmakers, and tinkerers in addition to natural philosophers, salon intellectuals, and revolutionaries.
    It shows how bounded we can become in just following the canon lore, I had largely discounted the Adepts for our historic campaign as Awakened Hackers.

    Having them as archivists, historians, alchemists and explorers puts a completely new spin on them and for say a Victorian campaign presents a subtle less esoteric group to the SOE.

    Searching for the True History of the world is something I really like, for our campaign it fits with the fluctuating reality of the 19th Century.

                                        Setting the Wayback Machine to 1993, and an alternate World of Darkness. Mage: The Ascension is the new hotness this year. After the core book and the Storytellers Screen/ Secrets of Magic, there was Horizon: Stronghold of Hope (in this universe, detailing the signature city of Seattle and its surrounding area, as well as the newly founded mega-chantry of Horizon, meant to take the place of the recently fallen Doissetep as the meeting place for the Council of Nine, and as a beacon of hope to the current generation of Tradition mages) and Witchhunters. And now ...

                                        Modern Magic, a guide to modern mysticism

                                        Chapter One: Modern Magi
                                        The Modern World vs The Awakened
                                        Starting with a look at the personal ties younger Mages still have with the mundane world, including friends and family, dating and marriage, children, employment and/or school, and various social organizations or groups (churches, hobbies, clubs, etc.). This would touch on when and how these might qualify as Allies, Contacts, or Followers.
                                        Secondly, the potential damage the Awakening and mystic life can do to the above. Besides the strains caused by a potentially dangerous lifestyle, there's also the issues of trying to lead a double life (including keeping your mystical and mundane lives separate from each other), what happens if you decide to leave the mundane world behind, the issues caused by things like Arcane, and what rules the various traditions have about protecting mundane bystanders from the Ascension War and other mystical dangers. Also the ins and outs of trying to bring people from your mundane life (any of the people discussed above) into the mystical world as acolytes, apprentices, or other roles.

                                        The Modern Leviathan
                                        A look at the tangled web of bureaucracy and the law within the modern Western world and the impact it can have on the Awakened. This includes state identification (social security numbers, driver's licenses, passports, etc.) and how they may work as True Names (and why it's a good idea for Mages to adapt shadow name aliases), issues of personal finance, property ownership, health care, and travel (especially internationally), and ways the law may limit or control one's ownership or purchase of certain items (including firearms and other weapons, antiques, pharmaceuticals and other drugs, animals, certain vehicles, and the like).
                                        Also, just what all is involved in living "off the grid", just what it takes to do so, and the pros and cons of that path verses the above efforts of attempting to live a mundane life as long as possible.

                                        Chapter Two: Modern Superstition
                                        An in depth look at the various nooks and crannies Awakened Magic is able to operate within the modern world while (mostly) avoiding the dangers of Paradox and disbelief. (A lot of Verbena material refers to these things as mythic threads, but the concept applies just as well to all Mages.) This includes things like cultural traditions and folklore, religion, faith and spirituality, urban legends and conspiracy theories, Fortean phenomenon and paranormal events, and advanced technology and the miracles of modern science. Also the time-honored tradition of bluffing and misdirection.
                                        In addition, this would also talk about the subconscious effect the World of Darkness has on its mundane population. Even if people don't admit it, they are - in the back of their minds - somewhat aware of the presence of supernatural predators and uncanny powers at play. This is why various ethnic and cultural enclaves (including things like gated communities or LGBT+ neighborhoods) are wary of strangers and often fall back on many of the above beliefs.

                                        Chapter Three: Modern War
                                        The Ascension War and the struggle for the destiny of the human race. This chapter looks at the goals and theories of the War, as well as the magic players such as the Technocracy (the dominant force), the Traditions (the resistance), the Nephandi (the rot within), the Marauders (wild cards), and the Orphans and Crafts (potential X-factors). In addition, some discussion about how the Traditions often tend to spend a lot of time arguing with each other.
                                        The tactics and weapons of the Ascension War, including hit and run attacks, sabotage and attrition, the importance of choosing your battles and of enjoying small victories, and the even greater importance of the individual in the War and the destiny of human kind.
                                        And the battlefields of the War, including the Counterculture vs Mainstream/Corporate Culture, the various cracks that exist within the modern Western way of life, the ancient power that can be found in the past, a look at the Ascension War outside the First World, the mysteries of the Invisible World and the Otherworlds, and the growing power of the Digital Web.

                                        Chapter Four: Modern Tools
                                        Modern Foci, and the swapping out of ancient symbols for modern counterparts.
                                        A look at the new hotness that is Technomancy.
                                        Various grimoires of the young and modern mages, including works by Dante, X-Cel, Bryce Grimm, Hesha Morningglade, and others.
                                        A collection of modern Talismans such as X-Ray Spex, the Rough Trade Jacket, the Endless Change Purse, Lightning Blades, EMP grenades, Go-Juice, Parking Karma, the Spirit Vac, and Not So Silly Puddy.
                                        Modern Bygones such as The Hook, Bigfoot, Chupacabra, Bloody Mary, the Mothman, and Sewer Alligators.
                                        Oddities of the modern world, including the Digital Web, the Midnight Express, and the 13th Floor.

                                        Appendix One: Modern Gear
                                        A brief collection of modern firearms, survival gear, computers and communications equipment, medical gear, a vehicles.

                                        Appendix Two: The Witching Hour
                                        A short chronicle involving high tech start ups, technomancy, the Technocracy, and the 13th Floor.


                                        (This would be followed by Technocracy: New World Order - an antagonist sourcebook - and the Mage Players Guide, which in addition to further fleshing out the Traditions and Hollow Ones, would fully introduce the Crafts, go into more detail about magic (including specific types such as technomancy, Spirit-Talking, Ars Cupidea, Skinchanging, and others), go into more detail about attributes and abilities, and looking at creating cabals and chantries. And that would've finished out Mage's first year of releases.

                                        Year two would've been the Vampire crossover book Blood Magic, The Digital Web, Technocracy: Iteration X, the who's who book Seekers of Ascension, the Storytellers Handbook, the Technomancers' Toolbox, the Year of the Hunter book The Arcanum, and Halls of Power: Washington DC.)

                                            For context, you might want to review the publication history of Mage. In particular, 1993 only saw two books: the first edition (in August) and the Storyteller's Screen/The Angel of Mercy. The proper supplements didn't start coming out until 1994, with the Book of Chantries released in January. Ten books were released in 1994, the last one being the Book of Madness, in December. 1995 saw the final four books of the first edition era published, and ended with the second edition and Destiny's Price being published in December.

                                            Horizon: Stronghold of Hope wasn't published until July 1996. By then, the Mage line consisted of 19 supplements, including four Technocracy books and six Tradition Books. Interestingly, the Book of Crafts wouldn't be published for another two or three months. Horizon was the first supplement, if you discount Tradition Books, Technocracy Books, the Storyteller's Screen and the Hidden Lore book packaged with it, and the entire run of first edition books.

                                            Basically, your alternate scenario for Mage publication strikes me as wildly implausible, with the game appearing fully formed like Athena emerging from Zeus's skull: you have ideas in your Modern Magic book that hadn't actually gelled until years later in reality. Recast it as an alternative history of second edition Mage publications, in 1996, and it makes much more sense.

                                                            Those would be great, but some of these cultures are extremely difficult to cover sensibly in the magic department. The question then becomes how ancient you want your magic, because in cultures with oral history you have to rely on archaeological evidence and what little has survived as implications in stories or song. The Norse are a good example- there are a few sentences in the Sagas that refer to different branches of magic, but almost nothing is left of the actual practice. The best we have are books from the 17th century, which I would hardly qualify as ancient. Much is muddied by re-imaginations that have entered popular culture: anything concrete you can find on Seiðr​ for example is made up, because there are no primary sources.
