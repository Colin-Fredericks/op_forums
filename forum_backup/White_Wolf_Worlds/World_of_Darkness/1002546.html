    Fixing Combat

    11-13-2016, 12:39 PM
    I was wondering what fixes that people use for making the combat rules for WoD actually work. For example, in my games, I make combat a static difficulty '6' and, instead of decreasing or increasing difficulty, I increase or decrease die pools (if a technique would have a base difficulty below '6' or a base difficulty above '6', I give a die pool bonus or penalty equal to the difference). I separate defensive and offensive die pools, so characters roll their entire defensive die pool (minus one per previous attack defended against during the same turn), which allows players to take risks and reduces the imbalance creation by Celerity, Rage, etc (Celerity, Rage, etc allows players to take extra attacks and extra movements, rather than extra actions). Normal movement occurs during the attack phase and, with each extra attack, a player can take extra movement.

      I think there needs to be a set idea of what you consider wrong with the system before you can just jump in and change stuff and make it work. Mechanically speaking, combat in the WoD (though there are differences between the editions and the game lines even in the 20th era, so pinning any of that down is hard) does actually work. How it works just isn't satisfying to a lot of people; but they don't all have the same issues.

      Part of the issue is that a lot of things I think could improve things don't necessarily work well for every game-line.

      For example, I would love to see WtA move to a popcorn style initiative system because it's a massive improvement in the realms of pack-tactics over traditional initiative (with or without the declaration phase). However, I don't feel nearly as strongly about popcorn initiative for the other games (if anything, I'd love to move away from the traditional + declare model for something more like Exalted 3e's traditional + Initiative damage system).

      Of course, both of those would be massive overhauls compared to things like you've suggested.

      Some smaller (or at least less of a need to rewrite wide swathes of the game) stuff I'd do:

      1) I think DAV20 was on the right track with one attack per round and have Celerity be a mix of reducing multiple action penalties and increasing your number of attacks cap. It simplifies extra action powers significantly and makes them easier to counter without neutering them.

      2) Instead of having an offense and defense pool to split up as you want, I'd favor moving towards static defenses (so soak as well) with the option to roll for "special" defenses. So you have some base Defense Value if you're just trying avoid the hit, but if you want to parry with the option to disarm or riposte you roll and it cuts into your main action dice-pool (possibly with penalties to your DV for the rest of the round as well).

      3) Take an ax to the massive maneuver list. The number of maneuvers are insane, and frequently redundant. The differences between a punch and a kick are not worth having two maneuvers. Put all that word count into a better guide towards adjudicating how to apply bonuses/penalties to the intent of an action so players can say, "I want to knockdown my opponent," without having to figure out which of the multiple maneuvers that can result in this is the best for what they're after. Basically I see this as a big source of decision paralysis that adds little to the game.

      4) Dump the weapon and armor lists. Take a few pages from Ex3 and other games and just have a few basic templates that you modify with a tag system. Less to remember, less hunting and pecking for the optimal weapon, less hassle adding weapons that the books don't cover, etc. Again, cut down on play decision making that doesn't really add much. Also take the chance to make armor less mediocre. Also abstract ammo instead of carrying about clip sizes.

      5) Use the DA Revised multiple action order system instead of the weird, normal action first, Celerity/Rage/etc. actions at the end (though less of an issue if you do #1).

      6) Abstract range bands over nebulous distances. CofD's Hurt Locker is going to include a whole section on doing this and how to work in the extant movement rates of that game just like you'd need to do with WoD.

              I eliminate many rolls. Especially in combat.

              Take this example:
              Ben Brujah dances circles around his opponent, he feints left, then right and throws himself in a spinning hurricane kick.
              Walther Wendigo saw it coming and manages to catch the kick. But the force knocks his hand back right in his face.
              He snarls and throws Ben towards the next wall.

              Now count the dice rolls of this incomplete turn with two participants.

              Even if we just take the first attack and the block we get FOUR.
              Now consider positioning, feints and the rest of the Players all with multiple Actions and you get something like 8 rolls per second game time.
              Bullet Time is a newborn cub compared to this.

              The declare actions step slows the crawl further.

              How are you supposed to be able to TELL a action filled, immersive STORY with these clunky mechanics?

              So I cut the soak for starters, the action declaring step was an instant dislike anyway.

                                  Minor nit-pick (since I generally agree with that approach): the odds of net successes is the same. The odds of failure, the odds of botching, and the odds of lucky big hits change significantly.

                                  8 dice vs 4 dice in a roll has low odds of botching, low odds of outright failure before the defender even rolls (which can impact things like parry and rebound damage), and theoretically could score up to 16 successes (all 10s with a specialty).

                                  4 dice dramatically increases the odds of outright failure/botch, and your maximum possible successes is cut to 8.

                                  The increase in fail/botch rates can make combat drag because the average damage output goes down; resulting in more rolls because it takes more turns. There's also the issue of a character having high enough defenses that they can reduce attacks to 0, which is an automatic failure as well which can stalemate or drag on fights as well.

                                      Increasing the threshold of successes required tends to deal with both of those issues as, even if someone fails to hit the threshold, they will still generate successes and will not botch. For example, if you had two characters, each with 10 dice to attack and defend, you would end up with 0 dice in attacks on both sides if you subtract defense pools from attack rolls, giving a 0% chance of success on both sides. If, however, defense die pools instead modify thresholds, then each would roll 10 dice against a threshold of 6, giving around a 30% of success with each attack. The same thing would apply with soak rolls, subtraction would result in 0 dice for damage while increasing thresholds would give around a 30% chance of one health level of damage.

                                          -Two rolls, one for the aggressor and one for the defender.
                                          -Standard action is to avoid damage if attacked (spend WP to ignore instinct and attack, knowing full well you'll take damage, otherwise abort stated action to defend).
                                          -Multiple actions are standard (seriously, you dont throw one punch every 3 seconds unless all out swing, you have a flurry of strikes and blocks from both parties in that timeframe).
