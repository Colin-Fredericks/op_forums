      IIRC there are rules for vehicular impacts in some of the books, and I really wouldn't consider that an "environmental hazard."

      Having something large and heavy land on someone is... extremely hard to model (even before taking supernatural toughness into account). The issue really isn't the impact itself, but the crush damage. I'd probably model it like falling damage (with the mass of the rock replacing the distance of the fall), that results in a clinch that keeps inflicting that damage while the poor person under is pinned. I'm not going to get into things like crush syndrome.

      Speaking of crush syndrome, I really don't value modelling the assorted medical situations of, "you're dead, your body just hasn't caught up with that fact yet." Allow some narrative liberties for some last moments and all that, but trying to get too mechanically invested in that is a massive design black hole.

      While I might tweak the numbers a bit, I generally don't muck around with the rules too much. Messing with the fire rules completely changes the value of numerous supernatural powers that assume you're using the base-rules and so on.

      This also goes back to the difficulties of abstracting things that kill you, but take significant time between the injury being inflicted, and when someone actually expires. A human that's covered head-to-toe in napalm and burned for 10 seconds in reality would still be alive... but a wrecked mass of horrifically damaged tissue enduring unimaginable pain until something finally gives and they die. Putting rules to that is... a lot of work for very little pay off. Yes, I do get that a lot of people would like to say, "but what about magically healing someone that shouldn't be 'dead' yet!" but that requires a strict definition of when someone actually dies in the first place. Most fatal gunshots are not immediately fatal, but take minutes to hours to finally cause death. How long does a magically healer get to save someone? Any answer is going to be arbitrary and ultimately unrealistic.

              Keep in mind that when doctors and firefighters talk about what kills you in a fire, they aren't talking about what happens when you get hit with military grade incendiaries meant to destroy everything from small villages, to tanks, to whole damned cities. They're mostly talking about what kills people who are near fire (such as people caught in a burning building).

              The horrific reason napalm was invented, is that fire isn't sticky. They same reason someone invent "Greek fire" and later people used pitch and burning oil in siege warfare. The human body is mostly tissue that doesn't ignite (water and minerals). So war has led to the repeated invention of ways to stick fire to people so it keeps burning them instead of short duration touching of flames.

              On top of that, there are some major differences. Most modern homes are constructed out of materials that burn between ~1000 to ~1500 F at maximum heat. Napalm burns at ~5000 F. As you can image that equates to a massive change in how dangerous it is just to be standing near a fire in a home made of wood, carpet fibers, and so on, vs. high end accelerants.

              The reason why asphyxiation is the most common thing that kills people in fires, is because house fires aren't hot enough to cause immediate physical injury unless you come into direct contact with the flames or the super-heated air around them. But the fire will suck up all the oxygen in the room leaving you nothing to inhale but dangerous chemicals (CO, CO2, various noxious byproduct of modern life burning, etc.), and you can't stay conscious long in those conditions, which will eventually lead to you suffocating long before the flames do you in.

              Napalm is a different beast. That's enough heat to start boiling the water inside of a human if they get too close. Breathing in lit napalm means sucking fire into your lungs that will burn your respiratory system to a crisp. Napalm eliminates water from the air around it completely and will start sucking the water out of your body as you try to sweat to cool off faster than you can hydrate. Etc. But, thankfully (?) we don't have good data on what tends to kill people first regarding napalm because it hasn't been used on people in control conditions or in significant enough instances to gather that kind of medical information.

              ------------

              As for "approximately accurate." It's not worth it. You basically have two choices: go narrative and do what feels good for the story, or go arbitrary so there's some mechanics to hang stuff off of. Remotely close to accurate fire rules would be pages and pages long.

                      I have a strange brain and a set of life experience that left me with a lot of very detailed knowledge on things that happen to align well with stuff we chat about on RPG forums. I assure you there's plenty of topics I just quietly sit back and don't add to because I don't know shit about what people are saying.

                      Narrative doesn't have to be so cut and dry or simplistic. It's just a priority: what's best for the story? Who is in control of the story in that moment? Most narrative focused games share story responsibilities more than the WoD does where that's more traditionally on the ST to handle. A good example might be looking at Trinity Continuum and the Dramatic Editing rules that all Talents get access to. Players have limited resources to make Dramatic Edits, and the bigger the change the more it takes, but it puts things in a player's hands to spend those resources to decide, "This character will just die in the fire," or "this person will be beyond modern medicine to save but will be alive in theory despite the severe burns," to, "They're suffer severe injury to be out of the fight, but nothing a trip to the hospital can't bandage up, then some bed rest and their back on their feet." A whole spectrum of options is available, it's just not the traditional "fire does damage, either you survive and get healed, or die," method. It doesn't have to be overly simply even if it helps avoid being overly complex.

                      This even expands to normal combat rules if you kick it in after you have dealt enough damage to defeat an opponent. Who's willing to invest in what kind of outcome of defeating the enemy? Combat resolves who "wins" in a normal traditional way, but narrative mechanics can kick in to expand on what winning looks like.

                                  So... this is why I said remotely accurate fire rules would take a lot of rules. Fire is surprisingly complicating. Just as a basic outline of major (not exhaustive) factors assuming you're in air (not water or the vacuum of space):

                                  1) The base temperature of the fire itself.

                                  2) The ambient humidity (remember why we use water as a cooking medium, water absorbs a lot of heat before it transfers heat to other things, but once it's heated up it distributes heat fairly evenly, so very dry air transmits the heat of a fire to other objects faster, but is more subject to other factors, while very humid air gives you more time before the heat of a fire spreads, but once the air is heated the humid air is effectively boiling you on its own too).

                                  3) The ambient temperature (directly impacts the impacts of humidity in both the direct effect of heat transfer from the fire into the air, and on things like how effective wicking is); obviously the colder it is, all things being equal, the more work the fire has to do to heat the air around it to a dangerous level.

                                  4) Airflow (like humidity this is kind of a mixed situation, if the air is moving a lot around the fire it brings more oxygen to the fire and spreads the heat through the air faster but less consistently; still air is going to be more intense as it heats). This also interacts heavily with the next point:

                                  5) Heat shedding into external environments. This should be pretty intuitive: if you're in a sealed and insulated room where heat can't effectively escape into other parts of the building, the air of the rest of the atmosphere, etc. the fire will be worse than if you're sitting around a campfire surrounded by the open air.

                                  6) The ignition point of objects around the fire. This is one of the hardest things to abstract because ignition points are hard to calculate for most things in the real world. The basic idea though, is how hot does something need to get before it starts burning. Obviously, when a simple candle's flame is still 1,000 C, you can see why touching flame tends to ignite them (or at least start to cause burns), but when you get into things like ambient heat over prolonged periods of time, you can get ignition without any direct contact between flame and object. The most famous ignition point is probably 451 F/233 C (aka Fahrenheit 451, the title of the Bradbury novel), which is the ignition point of paper in standard laboratory conditions (in real word conditions paper can ignite over a significant range depending on all the factors here).

                                  7) Secondary chemical products of the fire (either the source or objects around it burning). This can inhibit or exacerbate fires. The most basic fire fighting techniques take advantage of most fires requiring oxygen as a consumed part of the reaction, so you cut out new oxygen and the chemical products of the flames will snuff out the fire on their own. Conversely, wood carbonization (the process that turns wood into charcoal) can make a fire even stronger as wood turns into better fuel if exposed to heat under the right conditions. This is also where dangers like asphyxiation and toxic chemical exposures start to come into play. The

                                  8) How direct and how prolonged exposure to heat is. Also pretty obvious, but generally what you'll see RPG rules focus on the most (hence I bracketed the list with this and base heat).

                                  The source nature of the flames is actually not that big of a factor outside of the potential ranges of points 1 and 8. Napalm isn't just fluid, as I said above, it's sticky, which is purposefully designed to maximize the impact of 8. Flamethrowers in WWI and WWII tended to use gasoline-based fires (also liquids) but without being "sticky" the flames just slide off things, so they don't expose the target to heat for as long. What you're burning (or things that are naturally extremely hot) obviously dictates how much heat they can spread into the local environment and what it takes to sustain the flames, but what separates different fuel sources of the same heat is generally stuff like what secondary chemicals come into play, or what can reduce the impact of the heat (you can pick up solid sources of heat with a decent pair of fire tongs and move them somewhere else, which is not easy to do for liquid or plasma flames).

                                  A fun little bit of physics that really illustrates how weird this can all get in combination is the Leidenfrost effect. What happens is that the heat vaporizes a liquid (usually water) and the vapor acts as an insulator between the heat source and whatever was covered in the liquid that keeps the heat from transferring (at least temporarily). An extremely dangerous, but very real, stunt is that you can dunk your hand in water, and then rapidly dip your hand in molten metal and pull your hand back out without suffering any injury. If you're fast (again, do not try this just because it can be done), the water vapor from your wet hand will create a protective barrier for a brief moment that keeps the molten metal from touching your hand at all and keeps you safe from serious burns.

                                                      The worst hazard for a vampire is the one they aren't prepared for. Vampires that live in earthquake prone areas are going to build their havens to account for that fact; just like normal humans adjust how they build their homes to mitigate the impact of earthquakes; avoiding the issues of being crushed into long-term torpor. A vampire that's in an earthquake prone area that hasn't seen a cyclone of appropriate type for the location in 200 years probably is in far more trouble if a freak occurrence of one happens. And the same is true in the either direction. A vampire that lives in a tornado prone area is going to be ready for tornadoes, but possibly not ready for earthquakes; obviously some places mean having to do the best you can being ready for more than one thing.

                                                      That said, I'd imagine most vampires would rather risk long term torpor over final death. Being buried under more rubble than they can escape from at least means they might endure. Winds that rip the roof off of their haven and leave them exposed to the sun once the storm passes, or fires than burn them to a crisp while they slumber aren't really things they can hope to recover from.
