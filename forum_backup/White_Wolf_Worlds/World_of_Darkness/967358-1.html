        I generally have used science as the foundation reality and magic as an overlay that temporarily alters reality. The spiritual planes have their own form of science as the foundational reality and, in order to change their reality, magic must be used to overlay their reality. The Consensus is nothing more than the deluded hubris of Mages who are trying to permanently change the laws of reality in vain to avoid having to use magic. Linear magic is explicitly scientific, even if human science is incapable of explaining it, because it can be used without altering reality. Coincidental magic involves subtle alterations in reality while vulgar magic involves obvious alterations in reality.

                        Originally posted by Ramnesis View Post

                        The fundamental problem with attributing cancer to earthly foundations is that cancer is a disease that only makes sense in a medical paradigm that includes genetics and cellular structures. Generic wasting diseases could exist in a paradigm revolving around evil spirits or humors, but their behavior (and in some cases intelligence) would be so different they can't possibly be the same thing.

                        This is actually a major problem with Earthly Foundations, and why I rely on them as little as possible.

                        Classic Mage Questions: Was the earth flat before the Ancient Greeks proved it was round? Did the sun revolve around the earth until Copernicus and Galileo brought their maths to everyone's attention? And in general the idea is that, yes, the consensus really can shape the world to that degree. At one point, heavier things did fall faster than slower things. At one point the planets were pushed around by angels.

                        But what does that mean for the concept of gravity? On a flat world gravity is a generic unidirectional tendency in one direction. On a geocentric one, it is a universal pull towards a central point (one that only affects earth, given that angels need to push the planets around). In Newton's model it's a general attraction of mass to mass. In one I'm always falling towards earth, unless I get beneath it, in which case I'm falling away. In one I'm falling towards the center of the universe, but not everything is. In one everything behaves the same way, but I can be pulled in any direction depending on where I am.

                        An Earthly Foundation of &quot;Things are affected by a downward force&quot; seems a pretty arbitrary universal rule when we can completely change the definition of &quot;down,&quot; &quot;force,&quot; and &quot;things.&quot;

                        I prefer to think of it this way. We live for so long in an environment where things fall downward that it is deeply written into our expectations, in a way that things like cars and gunpowder and germs just aren't. We learn to account for gravity and light and matter before we even learn to speak. They are consequently next to impossible to convince the consensus to fully abandon in any kind of straightforward way. It's not an arbitrary set of universal constants at work, its just a massive amount of conceptual inertia.
                        I strongly disagree entitely with what you posted. And I believe there is no where in the books to base your statrments, if there are, I'm sorry and I'd like to see it.

                        As I understand the consensus isn't reality per se. Consensus is what most people in an area believe exist/is possible, making those things doable without reality hitting you back. This doesn't mean consensus shape the universe.

                        To me, the universe has his own laws, people believing that you can reverse gravity just makes it easy yo reverse, not make it reverse. People believing in breast cancer make it possible for someone with Life to give breast cancer to someone without getting paradox, it doesn't mean that it wouldn't exist if they didn't believe.

                        Saying that the earth was flat before people believed it was round is extreme. Changing the shape of a planet is a planetary feat that would have lots of consequences.

                                    I think that before we had science and explanations for why things are the way they are, there wasn't really a why. When you swim in water, you can go up and down because you're surrounded by water - a thing you can drink, hold up, touch, etc. When you're in air, there's nothing to "touch" in the same way or meaningfully interact with - so it's not "solid" enough to contain you. You are near to the ground because that's what makes sense intuitively. In essence, there was no gravity, just an expectation of staying near the ground.

                                    What would life be like if it was more "narrative" rather than objective? Maybe a journey to find your new homeland would be a hard journey, fraught with cliffs and hills and barren wastes, not because you happen to be traversing a geographic area with past tectonic activity and an arid climate, but because you expect a journey to uproot your life and change everything about it *should* be hard? How would things change when someone comes up with the idea that the difficulty of a journey depends not on why you're taking the journey but the nature of the path you're taking?

                                    Part of the modern world is this air of superiority when we look at history - how amazed we are that they lived how they lived, unaware of the scientific reality all around them. I think Mage is more fun when we explicitly discard that perspective. No, your laptop doesn't work in the deep Amazonian jungle. Yes, there were dragons long ago that needed to be slain. No, Dark Ages Europeans aren't stupid for not inventing gunpowder like the Chinese; it wasn't possible to make it there and then. When things were invented, they were *created*.
