    Hm. I wonder if it would be fun, in this thread, to really try to analyze what did and did not work in C:tD, what flavor elements it evoked well and which its mechanics hindered, and discuss ways to re-evaluate the mechanics in a broad sense to better capture that feel. (Admittedly, this is a mental exercise I like to do with most games, so...I'm biased.)

    For me, the biggest flaw in the mechanical execution of C:tD is that, in order for it to feel like you're playing a supernatural creature tragically trapped, rather than merely a delusional mortal with whom other mortals with similar delusions play SCA events, you have to have high levels in all the Realms, and a ton of Glamour. Because Glamour is ludicrously hard to recover, you need a large pool (and thus to be a Childling) to really have any freedom to be a supernatural creature even weekly.

    There also was little reason to play an older Changeling. A Wilder or a Grump got next to nothing but downside. Banality was not the double-edged sword it was apparently meant to be, and the war between the two sides (human and fae) to try to keep your sanity as both tended to leave little reward for risking falling into madness on the fae side (losing touch with mortality). There just wasn't enough oomph in being a beautiful mad faerie to make it tempting.

    There was also a very seductive, but unfortunately flavor-violating, tendency to treat Banality - as used by the antagonists - as an almost "dark magic" compared to the "good" magic (in relative, subjective-to-the-splat terms) of Glamour. By this, I mean that, while Banality for player characters tended to just be a bad thing, or to be something they could occasionally use to shake off the imaginary-pretend-games effects of Changeling magical duels (because, again, the division between worlds was TOO strong, so it felt like it really was more playing imaginary games than being a supernatural creature in the world of darkness)...while Banality was just bad stuff and hurt-yourself-for-armor for PCs, for NPCs it fueled actual supernatural effects.

    I think it would have been better served if Banality were something that enabled Fae to overcome their Echos-style weaknesses, to use those weaknesses in a mortal fashion against each other, and, where used as a "dark magic," it looks to the practitioner like just being...banal. Mundane. The "banality as dark magic" portrayal can work from the perspective of those given over to the madness of the Fae; it can look to them like a deep mystery. But it shouldn't feel that way to the practitioner...and it unfortunately still kind-of looked like it.

    I think a tug-of-war of useful, tempting capabilities drawn from both halves of their nature - Banality for their humanity and Glamour for their fae selves - would have evoked the game's tone more powerfully.

    To achieve this, Banality would have to be more than just a "nope" effect when used positively. The idea that Grumps have more of it than any other Changeling (while remaining, well, Changelings) should have given them interesting ways to bring mortal protections to bear in the Dreaming. It should be something that lets them strike the balance, at least in appearance, more readily. Perhaps starting Grumps actually are more mortal than fae, as they are so mired in Banality with so little experience with Glamour that they do face the actual struggle. But being more worldly, more Banal, should also be its own advantage.

    Grumps should have to struggle to NOT use these advantages in order to advance in Glamour.

    Childlings, on the other hand, should actually be in the most danger of losing themselves to their faerie nature entirely. Less connected to the world, they should find that the Echoes really do bind them strongly. The more they draw on their Faerie nature, the more Fae they should become to mortal eyes, as well: Remember, that little boy houes the ancient soul of a Nocker mastercraftsman. If he's come too strongly into his Fae nature, it's not with the eyes of a human child that he looks upon the mortal world, but the eyes of an ancient being that views the world through an alien lens.

    Childlings too swept up in Glamour and not rooted in Banality should come across as, well, fey. They're too old and wise for their years, too obsessed with the mania of their fae archetype (the Nocker, for instance, may already be working on adult crafts such as instrument-making, automobile repair, or artistry of their own), and to almost paradoxically be obsessed with childish taboos like not stepping on cracks or pretending the floor is lava or never, EVER letting their clothes be inside-out.

    Banality, when it hits a Childling, actually helps them be a child. At the same time, it helps them grow up. It protects them from the monsters even as it makes them more afraid of them. (The Troll Dame in the Chlldling body fears no monster in the dark...but the little girl when reminded that she's just a child who sees monsters under her bed might doubt her faerie powers, and find refuge in Daddy's assurances that there are no such things as monsters. Even if she doesn't really believe it.)

    Grumps can threaten to disbelieve, wielding their Banality as a two-edged sword and struggling to accept their Glamour-ridden sight. Willfully ACCEPTING the reality of the Dreaming renders them vulnerable in ways they prefer not to be, but lets them work with it and gain power over it. For the Grump, it's a question of whether they tap this power of faerie magic at the risk of growing madness.

    In a sense, I suppose what I propose is that the ability to use Glamour in the real world come with Banality, if only because it's what one needs to perceive the world as it is and recognize that it is the Dreaming that is an imposition. Meanwhile, power within the Dreaming comes with more Glamour and less Banality.

    Maybe it should honestly be a shared bar: gaining Banality means losing Glamour, and vice-versa.


    The other major issue is just the difficulty of recovering Glamour, but this post is long enough as it is.

        Thinking further, maybe it would be interesting if each Kith had at least one of their traits - the strange obsessive behaviors they have, like Pooka lying and Redcap blood-seeking - be given an active way to express it that generates Glamour. Pooka who get somebody to swallow a fib whole might gain one point (or part of one point?) of Glamour; Redcaps who soak their cap in fresh blood might do similarly. Something a little more active than the Nature/Demeanor willpower-recovery mechanics, but in the same vein.

        They could also have an echo-like taboo that they can break fairly easily, but at the cost of some amount of Glamour (maybe fractional). The higher their Banality rating, the lower the cost in Glamour for violating it. Pooka telling the whole truth, Sidhe breaking a promise/oath, Sluaugh sharing secrets without getting bigger ones in return.

        Tying Echoes to Glamour retention, recovery, and loss would probably be a good design choice all around, honestly.

                    I also think the game would benefit by showing that Changelings are not necessarily the only Fae creatures out there, they are just one type. Integrate the various other Fae more strongly into Changeling society and have them have important roles. Demonstrate that in the core book. Then show why the Changeling's position among the Fae - half faerie and half mortal - is important to that society's survival. It should generate more obvious adventures and interactions since only Changelings can interact with the banal world on an ongoing basis.

                    Instead, the books seem to indicate that Changelings are the core type of Fae and dominant the Fae government and population. It would be better if other Fae dominated - at least since the Resurgence - or at least made up a huge bulk of Changeling society. That would make Changelings precious and important to the survival of the Fae. Changelings would have the great task of finding new sources of Glamor and awakening them within mortals.

                                Personally, I always felt that the Realms system could use a little work. I know that it was intended to show the chaotic nature of changelings' powers, but it did so in a way that seemed entirely too structured. For example, if you see a changeling use a power on a computer, it is safe to assume that the character could also use the same power on a knife or a cloak. If one uses a power on a friend, they may or may not be able to use it on an enemy as well, but if they use it on an enemy, you know they can use it on a friend. I think the Realms could keep a lot of the same flavor if they removed the 5-dot progression, and made it so that a player chose a facet of the Realm with each dot gained. For example, if two changelings both had Actor 2, one of them might only be able to use their powers on people that they felt strongly about one way or another (True Friend and Dire Enemy), while the other might try to avoid forging too many connections with people in order to maintain the effectiveness of their cantrips (Familiar Face and Complete Stranger). This would allow a player to have the levels of the Realms they want to use more easily, and would leave their powers more unpredictable even to other changelings.

                                        I admit that, having only ever owned and read the second edition of C:tD, bunks never really made sense to me. There are (possibly due to poor editing) oblique references to "decks," which I am given to understand refers to a 1e thing where a random "bunk" was pulled from a deck for each Cantrip. Bunks are, as well, basically the "verbal and somatic components" of the "spell" that makes up the Cantrip's activation, I believe. But it took forever for me to realize that this was supposed to represent a stylistic way that the Changeling did something to make his magic happen.

                                        He reached into the pocket of the shadow of the person he was pickpocketing with Gimmix. He performed a riverdance where the jumps got higher and higher to perform that prodigious leap.

                                        The problem is, it seems to be really ill-described. It would probably have benefitted to have sample bunks for each Cantrip and each Realm, with ideas for mixing them for specific uses. As-is, it comes off more silly than thematic, at least to first and even second reading.

                                                For me the greatest problems of CtD were and are Glamour economics, nearly everything costs Glamour and you only have a small pool of it that is rather difficult to fill, and the Realms which were poorly structured. Banality could have used clearer and more structured rules, and such clear and strict rules would only be thematically appropriate for Banality, and a bit less ageism the last of which is rather easy to correct.

                                                Some quick fixes would be to make Glamour Pool dependent upon Permanent Glamour but much larger, a bit like with Wyrd/Glamour division in CtL, and make gathering Glamour a bit easier like the DtF option of exposing a mortal to your true self and picking up Faith from it. Realms should perhaps be more about the closeness of something to the story at large, that is it is easier to affect something that you have strong ties to, your sworn enemy and true friend alike are close and thus the first things you learn to affect while some passer by or other extra only loosely tied to your story is harder to affect. etc. Also strip away the less needed Glamour cost like from most, if not all, Birthrights.

                                                Note that those are only patchwork solutions not actual solutions which would require a bit more work than 5 minutes of fast thinking.

                                                As for DA: Fae. I love the game but it is a different game than CtD. Both have their virtues and weaknesses. I love DA:F magic system that though extremely strong and flexible does have it's risks as well. I dislike the Echo system, well I like the idea behind it but not the execution, that mostly has the effect of driving the PC's to exclusive work amongst the Fae, or other supernaturals, instead of interacting with mortals and it drives that point home a bit too effectively. That is to say it's a bit much that each encounter with a mortal you have has a chance to inflict you with a new Echo. I much more liked the idea of Echoes being tied to your power, more powerful you are the more Echoes you have as enforced by the fact that you have an Echo for each Greater Feature. I love the Kiths of CtD so the lack of them is a minus for me, though it is something I could live with if only the Greater Features would be closer to mechanical strength of Birthrights than they currently are, that is to say while DA:F magic is extremely strong the Greater Features are mechanically much weaker than the Birthrights of CtD which is a shame as it leaves them as small side quirks instead of defining features they are in CtD. I do not mind customisable Kiths but if going for that let the Birthrights keep their oomph.
