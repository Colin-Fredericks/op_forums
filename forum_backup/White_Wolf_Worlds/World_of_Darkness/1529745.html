    Mage the Ascention as Personal Horror game.

    01-31-2024, 05:42 AM
    Hey I'm trying to construct a campaign that will be more in line atmospherically with other splats like Vampire, Wraith, Werewolf or Demon in that Mages will be "The monsters".
    Currently Mage is a Horror about 1984 totalitarian state or about well-meaning but flawed people or maybe about dangerous and unforeseen consequences of using magic.
    The other splats draw the PERSONAL-horror from making the player a monster himself. I'd like mages to be a little bit scary.
    Every splat has some kind of "Beast".

    Vampires have hunger for blood, immortality and "The Beast" that not only slowly chips away their humanity, but also makes them wild predators.
    In werewolf from what I understand "The Beast" is the inner Rage that is difficult to control and their mission, tribe and conviction makes the Rage "justifiable" but tragic. (I didn't play it, so sorry if that is off the mark.)
    In Demon you have torment from eons past inflicted by your family on you. It is hate and rebellious ire for the Creator Himself which in turn spoils your very essence.
    In Wraith you have a dark half of yourself that turns your passions and character into some kind of abomination.

    In all of those cases it is undeniable that you are the monster. Killing, controlling, self-righteous monster and it is easy to be one because you are compelled by your supernatural nature.
    With mage it is a lot more difficult. Even if a mage has huge flaws they mostly are well meaning. The whole setting is centered around fighting for freedom or fighting monsters. (Nephandi, marouders)
    Mages do have huge power but that does not make them automatically pricks, which is very much the case in other splats. If I made mages pricks without their very nature compelling them to I'd feel like there would be something wrong with them even without powers. It feels unrealistic to make most of them or all of them corrupt because they have magic. It's not in the same league of compulsion as "The Beast".

    I had this idea that I will make the Avatar the compulsion of Mage. The immortal otherworldly spirit that has it's own goals. It is mage's higher self in a sense so it will push him towards personal ascension but will have no regard for anything that is outside it's personal "paradigm". It itself believes that world works in some certain mystic way and it acts like it's true. It also might imprint those principles on the world.
    With that kind of compulsion I can easily justify why mages do monstrous stuff without making it look like I have a cringe'y cynical views on human nature and morality.

    Any other ideas ? Do you have a proposition for this Avatar-compulsion system ?
    Do you make your Mages monstrous ? How do you achieve that ?

      The key to personal horror in Mage for me is remembering that the road to hell is paved with good intentions. Mages are monsters because they have ridiculous amounts of power and almost zero reasons not to use it to maximum effect. What's worse, they can bend reality, including morality, to make their actions just. Mages are at their worst when their acting like any human would if granted reality warping superpowers.

      I really don't like the idea that you need a "Beast" or equivalent thereof. Rage in Werewolf is not the same as a vampire's Beast, a wraith's Shadow, or a demon's Torment. Each is unique and different as tailored to the themes of those games. Changelings, hunters, mummies, and Orpheus agents have plenty of horror to deal with without such codified issues to content with as well.

      If you really want to add something, I would steal from Mage the Awakening 2e: each mage in that version of the game is irrevocably obsessed in an unhealthy way. They have to poke a little more, prod a little harder, bend the rules just a bit more, to sate whatever their current desire is to sate their obsessions. This keeps to their very human monstrosity, by focusing on keeping their motives personal and relatable, but always tainted by the fact that their power feels like it's meant to be used, and there's always something to use it to do; even if they shouldn't from the audience perspective.

              To develop what Heavy Arms said, the biggest source of personal horror comes from the fact that mages are human and humans are monsters tempered only by their lack of power and the consequences of their actions. Mages, given power and shielded from the consequences of their actions, are primed to become monsters without ever realizing it.

              As you say, though, there's not really a system to bring that into focus. It's easy to see what horrors other mages do, but not the players.

              The Avatar, as an otherworldly force in a Mage's life works, but unless you shift focus so that all Avatars are working towards alien and ultimately horrible goals they're going to be a push towards strangeness, not horror.

              Instead, the system I would tweak for personal horror is Paradox. Paradox is already set up to be a consequence of a mage's actions and one of the suggestions for how to handle it is to consider it a reflection of the fears or motivations of the characters. So focus on that. Tweak Paradox so that it has as much of a caustic effect on local reality as it does on the Mage or so that they can choose between taking a backlash or letting it permeate the world like toxic waste. Make backlashes a reflection of the mage's goals. Give them what they want but sometimes, and only sometimes, in the way they least want it. That might get you the personal horror element you want.

                  What you're looking for is Quiet. That's the Mage equivalent of frenzy in Vampire, death-rage in Werewolf, being taken over by your Shadow in Wraith, and going monstrous in Demon.

                  The concept might be revised or expanded on: for instance, you might more rigorously define how and when one enters Quiet; and you might revise the state of Quiet to take away a bit more player agency. After all, that's the whole point of the various systems you pointed to in the other game, to force the character to act in monstrous ways, whether the player wants him to or not.

                  I might consider revising Morbidity to something more general — something that's not necessarily about death in particular, but rather about some primordial element of reality. Death and decay is just one possibility among many; but the idea is that whatever the primordial concept that you're drawn to, you tend to become more like it the deeper your Quiet becomes, and the more that concept manifests around you. The Marauder equivalent would be someone who has become a manifestation of the force of nature in question: a bit like the Exemplars from Masters of the Art, but not tied specifically to Spheres (unless you want to do that) and reconceptualized to apply to regular mages instead of being an Archmaster thing.

                                      "Monkey Paw logic" is the desired outcome being 'twisted' in some fashion that makes it bad when it otherwise should have been good.

                                      What Heavy Arms is getting at is a bit more subtle: the idea of getting what you want, and not realizing that, over time, "what you want" has extended far beyond your original expectations, and out of the bounds of decency or morality. You are 100% correct that it is harder to set up, and takes a lot longer. And that is a consequence of how Mage was designed in comparison to the other game-lines.

                                      Part of that is that things like Vampires and Werewolves, in pop culture, have obvious and explicit "monstrosity." They are The Other in ways that are obvious, fundamental, and dangerous. The pop-culture notion of a "mage/wizard/etc." just doesn't have a lot of that hardwired into it. Sure, we have evil witches, demon-summoners, and such, but the concept of Magic itself being evil and dangerous is mostly found within viewpoints defined by religious extremism, at least in my experience. Magic, like science, tends to default to "ideologically neutral" in fiction. Magic is less evil in itself, and more a means of enabling evil (or good).

                                      Therefore, the monstrosity in Mage has to come from the choices that the Mage makes in how to use their power. You can be heavy-handed with stuff like Nephandi and Marauders, but horror with "regular" Mages pretty much has to be subtle.

                                      I do think that there is one fundamental problem with your assessment:

                                      So the majority of session there is little to be unsettled about, because at heart mages are well meaning to humanity with a few exceptions.
                                      This is largely wrong, or at least missing a mountain of nuance. The "average" Mage cares as much about humanity as the average Joe. Which is how we end up with an Orwellian conspiracy manipulating humanity "for our own good" as one of the major factions in Mage, one that quickly joined the ranks of playable characters as soon as people started to push back against the one-dimensional portrayal of said faction. If you really want to find the low-hanging fruit for Horror in Mage, you just need to start exploring the implications of Power when it is combined with the idea that the people with Power are convinced that they are using it for the "Greater Good." The Traditions have absolutely the same amount of potential for personal horror as the abuses of the Technocracy. You just need to find the situation where they would justify something "monstrous" by our standards which fits *their* conception of the "Greater Good."

                                      This is really easy to apply to NPCs. The Traditions are overflowing with opportunities for their ideals to be twisted, abused, or even just applied in a way that can mess with the norms and mores established by our society. Everyone's favourite example in the Traditions is the Chakravanti/Euthanatos. You know, the folks who will sometimes kill a person with no explanation other than "Karma said so." If you dig a bit more into the Sahajiya/Cult of Ecstasy, you're going to find that there's a split between the folks who believe that "opening someone's mind" should only be done with (somewhat) informed consent, and the ones who are willing to mind-rape someone for "their own good." There is a ton of potential for monstrous behaviour in Mages.

                                      The tricky part is dragging this horror into the realm of the Player Characters. Part of this has to do with the extensive planning involved. But the other part has to do with the Players, and their intentions. Among more nuanced and experienced roleplayers, you can find folks who are interested in exploring moral complexities and unusual beliefs, but with many people you will find them reductively wanting to be either "good" or "bad." If your Players want to be Good, then the Players will mostly avoid moral complexity in favour of their own personal conception of 'goodness' and will refuse to participate in anything that obviously conflicts with the moral code they have decided to follow. If your Players want to be "bad," then you're going to have a very hard time managing to create the crisis involved in Personal Horror because they have committed to monstrosity and are probably not going to be spooked out of it unless you go out of your way to invoke genuine feelings of Yuck.

                                      Creating a Personal Horror situation for a Vampire or Werewolf is easy, because they have powerful impulses that are threatening to conventional human existence. Creating a Personal Horror situation with Mage is more difficult because you can't lean on any built-in sources of horror: you have to create the horror yourself. My favourite go-to is the Mind Sphere, because I used to be the kind of person who didn't understand the horror of devastating the freedom and identity of another sentient being as a method of avoiding something we consider more obviously evil like murder. It's easy enough to look at something like the Jedi Mind Trick in Episode VI of Star Wars and think of it as relatively innocent, just sneaking past a security checkpoint. But then look at Episode II where Obi-Wan brainwashes someone so that they "go home and rethink their life." Sure, that's arguably for their benefit in some sense, but that's also a fundamental violation of their personal autonomy: sure, telling someone to think really hard about their decision to become a drug-dealer is a good thing to do, but when you literally FORCE THEM to reconsider that choice, it is a violation of their autonomy regardless of whether or not they are happier with the outcome.

                                      So, if you want to bring Personal Horror to Mage without necessarily hardwiring it into the mechanics, you need to design your stories in such a way that your characters are forced to question "the Greater Good" that they are (presumably) trying to serve (if you are trying to tell a story about Personal Horror: again, it's really hard to do this if your Players are just committed to being assholes). You need to create situations where your Players will be tempted to overlook idealistic principles for pragmatism, and keep slowly building that up, until you get them to the point where you can suddenly Wham them with the fact that they are (again, using the Mind Sphere as an example) using their powers to manipulate everyone around them "for their own good," with bonus points if you can slap them in the face with how the stuff they believed was for the right reasons was actually just serving their own agenda.

                                      That kind of stuff is complicated.

                                      Mage just isn't built for the kind of horror that you find in Vampire or Werewolf. It's the kind of horror that you find in the story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" by Ursula K. Le Guin. You can't really introduce horror into the setup of Mage without being willing to explore it in a subtle and cumulative fashion, or you have to do more to hardwire it into the mechanics. And the former option is extremely difficult if you don't want to make any kind of "authoritative" statement on the values involved in the discussion. I haven't had the opportunity to use it before now, but one of the expressions that I've come up with when imagining how to explain Mage to people who haven't been exposed before is "(full-contact) philosophical combat." You can't really add more horror to what's already there without being able to decide for yourself what "horror" means.

                                          The problem with "personal horror" in general is that regardless of mechanics, players really have to buy into the concept for it to work. Lots of people play dark super heroes with fangs/Katanas and Trenchcoats with VtM and don't give a shit about the personal horror side of the game.

                                          If your players buy into personal horror and mage, they simply need to buy into the ideas that (a) humans aren't inherently morally good, and (b) power shows you for you really are.

                                          Magic is the ultimate temptation. It's the allegorical ring of invisibility. Magic is, at its core in Mage, cheating. Once you start justifying the small cheats, you're on the road to justifying more and more cheating. You have your personal codes and morals that say some kinds of cheating are bad, but others are acceptable, but that acceptable sandbox is always going to be more than permissive enough to be horrible.

                                                  Originally posted by SirResnick View Post

Hunger and Anger are more disturbing because it feels like it's YOU, like it's your very essence that did the deed. Psychosis comes and goes and it feels more like a brain-fart.
                                                  If you review the write-ups of what the various levels of Quiet mean, you'll notice that the lowest levels, of Denial at least, aren't any kind of psychosis. Denial 1 manifests as stubbornness and mild projection (attributing your own faults to others); level 2 Denial involves selective perceptions (a.k.a., only seeing what you want to see; at this stage, figuratively) and hypocritical behavior. I'd go so far as to remove the Rose-colored Mirrorshades Flaw, which was introduced before ClarityDenial was added to the game, and replace it with a dot or two of a suitable Denial. It isn't until level 3 Denial that it enters the realm of psychotic behavior (irrational behavior, and literal blindness to denied subjects). Someone could easily walk around for years with Denial 1 and be perceived by others merely as an opinionated jerk. Madness becomes problematic much earlier, with even Madness 1 featuring minor hallucinations — though I personally play that less as hallucinations and more as delusions — believing things that are generally accepted to be false, as opposed to Denial which starts with denying things that are generally accepted to be true. If you shift it around that way, the first two levels of Madness aren't particularly psychotic, either. And Morbidity; well, like I said before, I think that's a bit too one-note for Mage as written. As I'd rewrite and expand it, the first couple of levels would involve something closer to an obsession with a subject.

                                                  This is appropriate, considering that Quiet operates on a 5-point scale. The first dot should be subtle; the second, noticable; the third, strong; the fourth, very strong; and the fifth, overwhelming. Go beyond 5, and you become a Marauder.

                                                  The other reason to broaden Morbidity would be to bring it in line with the other two: both Denial and Madness implicitly require you to pick a subject. With Madness, it's what sorts of delusions you have; with Denial, it's what you reject. And with the broadened replacement for Morbidity, it's what you obsess over.


                                                  But since you mentioned psychosis, let's look that up: “Psychosis refers to a collection of symptoms that affect the mind, where there has been some loss of contact with reality.” — National Institute for Mental Health. “Some loss of contact with reality” strikes me as something very appropriate for a game where the protagonists figuratively fight against reality whenever they use their powers: “I reject your reality and substitute my own.” Conceptually, I would argue that psychosis could be to Mage as Hunger is to Vampire and Anger is to Werewolf.
