    How to separate yourself from your character

    10-16-2014, 06:32 PM
    Hi there.

    First of all, I'd like to say two things:

    1] English isn't my native language so at times I may sound strange or my message may not be very clear. I apologize in advance for that.

    2] Do not write anything if you intend on mocking me. I am mocking myself enough, I really don't need your "support" in that case. Unless mocking someone who you've never met over the Internet makes you feel very good about yourself, then who am I to deny you that.


    My question is... How do you separate yourself from your character when playing WoD? And is it really an issue for you at all, or am I just... weird?

    The thing is... some of my best friends play WoD regularly, and they are really nice, mature people who are great to play with. They don't seem to mind me, and I respect and like them a lot, so it would be nice to spend some time together when playing.

    I also like the setting very much - the closeness between the world of the game and the real world gives it this... sort of a "real-ness" feeling, makes you very engaged in the play, makes you feel very... strong emotions, as it is much easier to relate to the events that take place in the world that is mostly like ours, only with some "additions". And that's great, I think. Especially for people who, like me, play mainly for the narrative.



    But that's... kind of a problem. For me at least. Whenever I try to play WoD, I just end up creating a supernatural version of myself. Or - in the least - the idealized, supernatural version of who I'd like to be but am not. And that... well, to me, that sucks. 'Cause when playing, I am... sort of constantly looking at myself from the side. And I don't really... like the things I see. Suffice to say, at the moment I don't... have a high opinion of myself. Worse still, I feel... exposed. Like... I am worried that the other people see what I see what I look at my character - which really, more often than not is just me - and see what I see. And then think less of me.


    I said that I tend to create either clones of myself, or idealized versions of myself. The latter is only partially true. The thing is... I can't seem to be able to roleplay a character I'd REALLY want to be like in real life, just because... well... I am not like this. Example: I, in real life, am a very quiet, shy person. And I find it impossible to roleplay as an outgoing, boisterous guy 'cause even if I wanted to, I don't really know how those people act. I think it would seem... artificial. And pathetic. But then, so is my own image - at least in my own eyes - and that's what I'm stuck with.


    Does anyone have this kind of problem as well? What did you do with it? (Also, as a half-serious note: do you feel stupid as well when your GM sets the base of the campaign on some historical events/seemingly common knowledge about the world in general, and you find out that you don't really know much about any of these things?)

      Hi there. First of all, I can reliably say this: No, you're not the only one who has this "issue" (if you want to call it that).

      In fact, in my experience, most people, when they start getting into roleplaying, do one of two things:

      1. Create characters very like themself, most often idealized versions

      2. Create characters completely different from themselves, often with characteristics they wished they had

      Neither of those is wrong in any way, of course, roleplaying is an art, and not everyone takes to it equally fast, starting out from familiar ground, so to speak is actually a pretty good idea. However I must admit I do grow a bit tired when people refuse to go outside those initial boundaries, and essentially just create the same character over and over.

      Personally, I fit into category #1 as well when I started out, the strong, silent type was my go-to character (not that I was particularly strong, that would be the "idealized" part of it).

      So, what did I do with it ? Well, to be frank, I forced myself, with the aid of some very good STs, to play outside my "safe zone" of the strong, silent "warrior" type. I still remember my first ever W:tA chronicle, where the ST decided I was to play a Ragabash (the players were all complete noobs to the game, so the ST simply decided for us). Of course, this necessitates a complete level of trust within the group, and a high tolerance for people "not playing out their role" (I do so hate that expression).

      Finally, I have just one piece of advice: Roleplaying is about having fun, together with friends. So what if you play a charismatic character as a bit of a stereotype ? The only way it would be "pathetic" is if someone in your group made it out to be that way, and if they are indeed your friends, they wouldn't do that. I'm sure my first charismatic characters were total cartoons or b-movie heroes, but who cares? I got to play out a side of myself I pretty much never showed any other time, and in the long run, I do believe it helped me become a more charismatic person as a whole. After all, practice makes perfect, in any field of life. If you want to be strong, you have to hit those weights, if you want to be more charismatic, you have to try that, too. And what better arena than with people you (hopefully) trust not to take a piss on your expense? That is the beauty of roleplaying, and one of the main reasons I love this hobby.

              I think that the best way to get out of the mold you seem to be in (and forgive me if I misinterpreted your post, since it seems like you do) is to pick a fictional character. Preferably one that you have seen on a long running show or anime or what have you or else someone who has been in a series of movies. Pick them and try to play them. Even if all you did was to copy them and change a name. For example, if you wanted to play a cocky arrogant genius billionaire who has black hair and a goatee but named him Loni Dark, then go for it! It helps you to ease your way out of the rut and to try different things. If your ST and your group are your friends (and they should be since you spend a few nights with them for hours on end), then they may give you some friendly joking but no one will (or should) begrudge you trying it out.
              When you do this a few times, then you will be able to make new characters who are not copies of others and not the same that you have been playing. One day, you will be able to make characters wholly from your mind and be able to slip into them easily. I tend to make new characters be nothing like the previous characters I have played because I enjoy the mental exercise, however my first character ever was a human fighter in AD&D. When he died, I made up a series of characters just like him until I broke the mold with a Paladin who had a different personality.
              Ease yourself out of the rut by borrowing or even stealing other personalities or characters from other media and then use those training wheels to help you with making up brand new people.

                      I love making characters, I do it a lot.

                      What you describe isn't a "problem" so much as it is the beginning of a wonderful boon. What follows is my personal process, so please take it with a grain of salt.



                      Instead of doing an idealized you (or a diametric opposite), try to instead find two personality traits (preferably not ones that work well together) from your own psyche, and then combine them into the mind of a new psyche. Meaning... make these two ideas the character's foundation.

                      So for example of a recent character, I wanted to combine the concept of an outsider (loner type) with the concept of someone who was driven to help people (at great risk to himself).

                      What I like to do is take these two things (I choose two because its an easy foundation, 1 or 3 or 15 could work depending on complexity), which are obviously some part of my own mind (probably not the most dominate parts) and just sit on them for a day.

                      It's also sometimes VERY helpful to consider a possible flawed concept... like in the previous example, I chose to make this character a racist (supernatural wise).

                      Go about your normal daily business, and try to imagine having MET a person who was fundamentally driven by those concepts. Throw out ideas that dont seem right and let your mind wander over all the people you see. Their clothing, their mannerisms, their little foibles that most people dont see - all with an eye not of ridicule or judgement, but personality theft!

                      Usually by the end of the day I have a character who has some of it's own personality beginning to sort of form. Through the "character creation" process of slapping down dots or stats or LARP notes or whatever it is you use as a record, you will flesh them out and get some more details.



                      The reason I like using this method is because I find it leads to characters (NPCs and PCs) that grow, learn, change, and are not me. They all have their true roots in my psyche, enough that I can explore the aspects without losing myself in the process.

                              "Me, but with supernatural powers" is how a lot of people start RPing. It's easy, it's fun, and it doesn't require a lot of thought. Nothing wrong with it.

                              If you want to do something else, you'll just need practice. Essentially, you need to make up a character with passions, motivations, and a history you don't share, and while you're playing the game, you ask yourself "what would make sense for a person with these traits to do?"

                              Here's an example. Suppose my character is in a bar with some guy who was harassed my character's friends.

                              If my character were based on me, they would try to talk it out. They'd try to figure out the source of the guy's beef, and resolve it. Failing that, they might try using stern language or veiled threats to convey that a fight might be coming if the guy didn't lay off. They would probably call for support if the guy actually looked like he'd be willing to fight. They would mostly be concerned about the long-term consequences of any conflict.

                              If my character were a hot-blooded warrior from a culture with a strong honor code and a fierce devotion to protecting his friends, they would be a lot more hostile from the word go. They might punch the guy in the face and demand a duel without even asking for the guy's name. They would mostly be concerned about immediate threats to their friends and upholding their honor.

                              It could be some time before you're ready to do this automatically, but improvised roleplaying is almost entirely a matter of asking "What would (Character) do?" and then doing that thing.

                                  Originally posted by Sarah RPs View Post

                                  This is good advice. Don't necessarily make characters you hate, but build your character with story hooks based on failures. If you build a character with the assumption that they will succeed, then it's harder to deal with it when they don't. If you build a character with the assumption that they will fail in all the worst ways, then you can really get into trying to save them from it--and still enjoy the story of them failing anyway.

                                  Most WoD books (or any rpgs lately, really) have a bunch of questions in character generation that you can ask yourself to flesh out your character. Add to these questions: How could this character die heroically, how could this character be corrupted and turned into someone they hate, how could you see this character dying like a coward.

                                              Everyone who's new to gaming has their Mary Sue/Marty Stu phase. That's fine... really it is... Just learn to recognize the Cream-filled behavior when others call you out on it and gracefully take the criticism to dial it down a bit. What makes memorable and good characters is that they are flawed, they have weaknesses, make mistakes and they are not always perfect. Same goes for the horribly flawed characters, remember that they have virtues to build on as well. I turned out better than my own character. It took me a while, a few years of playing, but when I had grown beyond this character and needing her to think on and learn to cope with life's challenges, I moved on to new characters that I could separate myself from much easier.

                                              I think it's perfectly normal and fine to have this phase of gaming, so long as your learn more about yourself through the experience. Once you see the parts of your personality or behaviors that are less than ideal, you can start to change yourself.
