        - **Description**: Ancient Chinese religion primarily consisted of shamanistic practices, ancestor worship, and the veneration of nature spirits.         - **Time Period**: Emerged around the 4th century BCE and has remained an influential spiritual and philosophical tradition in China.
        - **Description**: Based on the teachings of Laozi and Zhuangzi, Daoism emphasizes living in harmony with the Tao (the Way), natural simplicity, and inner cultivation. It encompasses both philosophical teachings and religious practices, including rituals and alchemy.

        - **Time Period**: Continuous from ancient times to present
        - **Description**: A diverse collection of local practices, including ancestor worship, the veneration of local deities, and the use of divination and ritual. Chinese folk religion has often coexisted with other major religious traditions, blending elements from Confucianism, Daoism, and Buddhism.

        6. **Christianity**
        - **Time Period**: First introduced in the 7th century (Nestorian Christianity during the Tang Dynasty), but more significantly during the Ming Dynasty (1368–1644) and Qing Dynasty (1644–1912) with the arrival of Catholic and Protestant missionaries.
        - **Description**: Christianity in China has experienced periods of growth and suppression. While not as widespread as traditional Chinese religions, it has a presence, particularly among certain communities.

        7. **Islam**
        - **Time Period**: Introduced during the Tang Dynasty (around the 7th century) and has been present in China, particularly among ethnic minorities like the Hui and Uighur people.
        - **Description**: Islam in China has developed unique cultural expressions and has been practiced continuously, especially in regions like Xinjiang and Ningxia.

        8. **Manichaeism and Zoroastrianism**
        - **Time Period**: Manichaeism and Zoroastrianism were present during the Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE) due to cultural exchanges along the Silk Road.
        - **Description**: These religions had limited influence and eventually declined, but they left an imprint on Chinese religious diversity.

        9. **Neo-Confucianism**
        - **Time Period**: Developed during the Song Dynasty (960–1279 CE) and became dominant in the Ming (1368–1644 CE) and Qing (1644–1912 CE) Dynasties.
        - **Description**: A revival and reinterpretation of Confucianism incorporating elements from Daoism and Buddhism. Neo-Confucianism emphasized rationalism, morality, and the importance of education.

                Originally posted by ChatGPT
                **Manichaeism** and **Zoroastrianism** are both ancient religions with some overlapping concepts, but they have distinct origins, teachings, and theological structures.

                ### **Origins and Founders**

                - **Manichaeism**:
                - **Founder**: Mani, a Persian prophet who lived in the 3rd century CE.
                - **Geographic Origin**: Mesopotamia (modern-day Iraq and Iran).
                - **Time Period**: Founded in the 3rd century CE.

                - **Zoroastrianism**:
                - **Founder**: Traditionally attributed to the prophet Zoroaster (also known as Zarathustra).
                - **Geographic Origin**: Ancient Persia (modern-day Iran).
                - **Time Period**: Likely founded between the 10th and 6th centuries BCE.

                ### **Theological Differences**

                - **Manichaeism**:
                - **Dualism**: A strict dualistic belief in the eternal struggle between the forces of Light (good) and Darkness (evil). The material world is seen as the creation of the evil principle.
                - **Cosmology**: The universe is a battleground for the conflict between Light and Darkness. The soul, part of the Light, is trapped in the material body, which is part of Darkness.
                - **Prophets and Syncretism**: Mani presented himself as the last in a line of prophets, including Zoroaster, Buddha, and Jesus, aiming to synthesize their teachings into a universal religion.
                - **Scriptures**: Manichaeism had its own scriptures written by Mani and his followers.

                - **Zoroastrianism**:
                - **Dualism**: Also dualistic but less strictly so. It emphasizes the struggle between Ahura Mazda (the supreme god of light and wisdom) and Angra Mainyu (the destructive spirit).
                - **Cosmology**: The world is created by Ahura Mazda as inherently good, with evil as an opposing force that corrupts it. Humans have free will to choose between good (asha) and evil (druj).
                - **Monotheistic Elements**: Zoroastrianism is often considered the first monotheistic religion, with Ahura Mazda as the supreme deity. However, it also acknowledges other divine beings and spirits.
                - **Scriptures**: The primary sacred text is the Avesta, which includes hymns, rituals, and religious teachings attributed to Zoroaster.

                ### **Ethical and Moral Teachings**

                - **Manichaeism**:
                - Focuses on the salvation of the soul by rejecting the material world and living a life of asceticism. It teaches that the soul must be liberated from the material world of Darkness through knowledge and spiritual practices.

                - **Zoroastrianism**:
                - Emphasizes the moral choice between good and evil, with a focus on good thoughts, good words, and good deeds. It teaches that humans should promote the order of asha (truth and righteousness) and fight against druj (falsehood and chaos).

                ### **Religious Practices**

                - **Manichaeism**:
                - **Asceticism**: Encourages a strict ascetic lifestyle, especially among the elect, who are the spiritual elite.
                - **Rituals and Scriptures**: Rituals include prayers, fasting, and the study of Manichaean scriptures.

                - **Zoroastrianism**:
                - **Rituals**: Includes fire worship (as fire is seen as a symbol of purity and Ahura Mazda), rituals of purification, and recitation of the Gathas (hymns attributed to Zoroaster).
                - **Ethical Living**: Emphasizes living an ethical life and participating in community rituals and festivals.

                ### **Historical Influence and Legacy**

                - **Manichaeism**:
                - Spread widely across the Roman Empire, Persia, and even into China, but faced persecution and declined over time. Its influence persisted in various dualistic traditions and sects.

                - **Zoroastrianism**:
                - Dominant in the Persian empires and influenced later religious and philosophical systems, including Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Although much reduced in number today, Zoroastrian communities continue to exist, primarily in Iran and India (where they are known as Parsis).

                In summary, while both Manichaeism and Zoroastrianism involve dualistic elements and originate from Persia, they differ significantly in their origins, theological details, and religious practices. Manichaeism's strict dualism and syncretic nature contrast with Zoroastrianism's emphasis on a single supreme deity and a more nuanced understanding of good and evil.

Elements of Zoroastrianism continued to be present in Persian literature, folklore, and practices.
                - **Diaspora and the Parsis**: A significant group of Zoroastrians migrated to India between the 8th and 10th centuries, where they became known as the Parsis. By the 14th century, the Parsi community was well-established in India, preserving Zoroastrian traditions and contributing to the cultural and economic life of the region.

                ### **Summary**

                In the 14th century, **Manichaeism** was largely in decline, with limited influence and only a few surviving communities. **Zoroastrianism**, while no longer the dominant religion in Persia, continued to exist as a minority faith and had a lasting cultural impact in Persia and among the Parsi community in India. Both religions, though diminished in direct influence, contributed to the religious and cultural landscape of the regions where they had once thrived.​

                                                            I am by no means as well read on South Asian history, theology, or mysticism as I’d like to be so this will probably just be surface level brushes, but here are some thoughts that come to me when thinking about how irl interactions between European polities and the polities of South Asia might effect the development of the traditions.

                                                            European, particularly British, imperial penetration of India was a major factor in development of 19th century occult philosophy in the European world (here taken to include euro-settler colonies outside of Europe itself). Perhaps most famous is the case of Madame Helena Blavatsky who is considered to be one of the earliest and most popular occultists to introduce concepts from “Further Asia” into the milieu of Western Esotericism (prior to her, Occult Orientalism was more likely to focus on West Asia/North Africa: Persia, Turkey, Egypt, etc.). As one occult historian I follow put it in an interesting fashion, she was one of the first who could claim some firsthand experience with Indian mysticism and Buddhism, in an era where one couldn’t necessarily double-check her claims via a copy of the Vedas from a local library. Therefore her peculiar idiosyncratic (and dubious understood) interpretations of Buddhism and Indian spirituality are brought into Europe and, with the phenomenal success of Theosophy, become nigh-inseparable from all later western occultism. She’s kinda like Crowley in that, even if you aren’t a Crowleyan, if you’re in western esotericism after Crowley you’re still influenced by his work; same with Blavatsky.

                                                            Mage canon actually has some good examples of this: for one, the Akashic Brotherhood are a direct reference to the theosophical concept of the Akashic record. Blavatsky’s fascination with Tibet is also clearly reflected in the White Wolf’s writing (it’s not just because of how huge Tibet was in the 90s). Plus with Crowley downstream from Blavatsky you have his massive influence over the Order of Hermes depiction and Mage in general (and downstream of Crowley you have Robert Graves and his influence over the conception of the Verbena), the list could go on.

                                                            A project such as this definitely deserves a serious consideration of the myriad pre-colonization Awakened orders of South Asia, which is something I’m not sufficiently familiar with to touch on too much, but there’s also definitely the proliferation of adopted and appropriated materials to consider for the development of western schools of Awakened.
