    Originally posted by Rabbit Pooka View Post

    I can't argue with you there.



    I think system does matter, but you don't need a system for everything. Especially things that either don't really matter, or that will easily lead to more focus on the mechanics themselves rather than the fiction being created at the table. For example, crafting chimera. It really doesn't need a full-blown system. From the fluff, any Changeling having a bad night can accidentally create a chimera. And artisans can intentionally craft their dreams into chimerical reality. What more do we need in terms of nailed down mechanics? Charts and tables, modifiers and ingredients? I don't think so.

    Take this as an example, a Nocker is tasked with making a chimerical sword for the duke. Okay. But the duke wants something special... say a sword crafted from the moonlight reflected off the fountain in Armstrong Park in New Orleans. But he's the Duke of New York. You can handle that as a quest to get the stuff and once that's successfully gathered up, you just craft the sword... or you can mechanize that and force a roll to actually make the thing. Which could fail. Is that the type of mundane banal reality that needs to be reflected in the mechanics? I don't think so.

    After a quest like that, crafting the sword should be a gimme. The fun and adventure and excitement of the quest itself is the exciting part...not in making a roll after all that, especially a roll that could be failed. This is a storytelling game. It's supposed to be about telling great stories at the table through play and later on about the play. The story you tell for years to come is about how you were chased out of Armstrong Park by a garou and had to use the pooka as bait to lure it away long enough to harvest the moonlight, not: "Yep, rolled one success to craft the sword."

    I can hear people saying they're not mutually exclusive. I think they are, for the simple reason that having to make that roll after the quest undercuts the thing entirely. It focuses things in the wrong direction. It focuses play on the mechanics (whether you rolled well enough to craft the sword) instead of on the story about how you gathered up all the stuff on an epic road trip. It's the whiff factor, x100.

    The other drawback is munchkining the mechanics. Take crafting in most iterations of D&D, you have charts and charts of weapons, crafting times, costs, etc. Most players and GMs think nothing of it till they get that one player to games the system. They spot that the cost of 100 arrows is about 100x the cost of crafting 100 arrows. So they start thinking of small businesses, franchise rights, and saving themselves a few coin on starting equipment. The Changeling version is skipping the Chimera background because the character's a Nocker and can just make their own. It doesn't sound like a big deal, but is that the kind of banal mundanity that should be reflected in the mechanics of C20? I don't think so.


    You know what's banal? Another quest. I've quested in a thousand games. Gathering loot, gathering stupid little materials. Its boring. Its trite. You know who are true legends? Wayland Smith. Hatori Hanzo. Hephaestus. Emiya Shiro. The quest to become a legendary swordsmith is just as worthy of the Dreaming as the quest to forge a legendary sword, and to the Nocker player more so. To craft the stuff of Dreams, to craft the things of Legend these are his story and it is one worth say. You want to take his story and throw it to the hands of some murder hobo who can steal the right item.

    There's nothing more banal than spitting in the face of an entire Kith's Dream saying its "Boring" and pointless. Unless its Bogans cause their dream is "barter Economy"(J/k)

                I would say it's a matter of balance. It's not always suitable to stage an epic quest every time a character wants to forge a new chimerical sword. In those instances, use the mechanics. But where the crafting of the sword itself tells an interesting story, then turn it into a quest and roleplay it.

                Remember that one of the key things to know as a Storyteller is when to call for a roll or not. If the characters have completed that quest magnificently, it'd be a shame to say they fail because of a bad roll. If the quest was roleplayed well I won't call for a roll at all.

                But there's such a thing as downtime. Sometimes you don't have time for roleplaying how everyone accounts for every item in their possession. So just make some rolls, spend some XP and add a brief description to your blue book or character sheet. Then it's done.

                Take, as an example, the hunting rules of VTM. If a vampire seduces a human in a club and drinks their blood, you don't need to make a hunting roll as well. But when you need to handwave the process, the hunting roll is a good way to do so fairly.

                The other thing to consider is balance. I.e., is it fair if the only rules in the book are for combat? Most Storyteller Dramatic Systems chapters relate to combat, car chases and cinematic explosions. It's a legacy from wargames, but it suggests an emphasis on combat to some players. In recent years OPP (like the rest of the RPG industry) has come to recognise this imbalance and has worked to better balance those systems.

                Not every player is equally good at roleplaying social encounters, for example. In a way, forcing people to roleplay social interaction, or giving such rules short thrift, disadvantages some types of player and discourages some character builds. So those players might want a system for settling social combat. Likewise, forging treasures. Not every player will be expert at handling that as a roleplaying thing. Nor should they need to.

                Besides, all these subsystems will always be options. They're there if you need them and you're encouraged to use them only when appropriate. It's better to have them just in case. You're not forced to use them, but they might come in useful to someone. As Lian pointed out, people don't always want to (or can) create systems on the fly for every eventuality. Better that the rulebook give you a helping hand--after all, that's why we're paying for it.
