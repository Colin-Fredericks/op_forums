    Sell Me on Changeling: The Dreaming 20th Anniversary Edition

    12-10-2015, 12:33 PM
    Back when the Wraith 20th KS came out, Hawthorn, created a 'Sell me Wraith' as he had done before for M20 and during that expressed that he wouldn't do it again for C20. I, however, said that I would because of the five original game lines, CtD was the only one that I never touched.

    So I ask you Changeling fans, sell me and those like me on C20, sell me on Changeling: the Dream. Don't bring up Dark Ages: Fae or Changeling: the Lost, CtD should stand up on its own merits, not those of others. Consider me someone that has never touched any of the Changeling/Fae games of WW/OPP.

    Why should I, someone with no experience with the line, back C20?

    Homo sapiens. What an inventive, invincible species. It's only a few million years since they crawled up out of the mud and learned to walk. Puny, defenceless bipeds. They've survived flood, famine and plague. They've survived cosmic wars and holocausts. And now, here they are, out among the stars, waiting to begin a new life. Ready to outsit eternity. They're indomitable. Indomitable.

      I'm supposed to be at work, so excuse the stream of consciousness here. So...

      All of White Wolfs games offer up a lot of feels. To me though Changeling is a game that offers a very specific feeling, the feeling of holding onto your childhood. Keeping it safe and warm from all the slings and arrows of life. I think we as role players do this every day more than just about any other group, and it's rare to find a game that so wonderfully touches on those themes. I think anyone who loves our hobby can love Changeling the Dreaming, and if they get into it, it will truly touch them on some primal level virtually no other game can. Just food for thought.

          I just think it's the best game for running urban fantasy like Neil Gaiman, Charles de Lint and others. It can be dark, but it doesn't need to be (like Mage). It is set in the World of Darkness, but has its own fantasy world to explore with its own politics and rewards (like Wraith). It allows you to have raging fantasy battles on the streets without breaching the Masquerade or gaining Paradox (so long as it's entirely chimerical). It's just, generally, a very versatile game, and allows for archetypal quests, politics and intrigue, and wonder or despair all at once.

                  For my view, bare in mind I haven't had an opportunity to play it, Changeling is a game about Hope, Wonder, Despair, and Normalcy. Broadly speaking the first two are better than the latter two, but it's not strictly the case. CtD is about balancing the fact you're an ordinary person with the fact that you're a reincarnated fairy, and the two don't always align, but they don't always clash. It's about losing yourself in wonder, but keeping a link to the mundane so that you don't go crazy.

                  Bare in mind that this isn't explained very well in the mechanics, but it's useful to have some Banality so you don't just saunter off into the Dreaming and become crazy as your Glamour grows, but it's also a good idea not to have too much, or else you'll become a normal human.

                  At least, that's why, as somebody who has never proper experienced it, would back CtD20 if I wasn't a poor student. As it is I'll be buying the cheapest physical rulebook I can when they come out.

                              One of the nice things about Changeling: the Dreaming is that it lets you play with concepts and locations that just don't work anywhere else, and that would radically change the World of Darkness if allowed to spread to the other lines. Ice castles and talking polar bears? Check. Underground cities populated by man-eating dark elves? Check. Baseball gnomes? Check.

                              "The Dreaming" is a spirit realm built out of humanity's collective subconscious - anything humanity's ever fantasized about, wrote about, or woken screaming from a nightmare over, exists somewhere in the Dreaming. And unlike the Shadowlands or the Umbra, the Dreaming still directly touches the Earth. It overlays everything, pooling into coherent fantasy realms that exist simultaneously with more mundane elements of reality.

                              Players take on the role of spirit beings from the Dreaming who've merged themselves with humanity to survive in the modern era. Exactly what they were before is left ambiguous, but changelings* (the default cultural block) believe they were the fairies of European myth. The Seelie and Unseelie Courts, high elves, goblins and dwarves, giants and ogres . . . They all live on, wrapped in human flesh and shielded by the soul they've merged with.

                              Left to their own devices, modern changelings tend to default to an Arthurian, vaguely Celtic feel. But in places where the local Dreaming has a strong theme of its own, long term residents adopt roles within the local mythos. New York City is steampunk, for example, while the changelings of Boston are busy reenacting the American Revolution. Changelings are born of the Dreaming, after all, and they instinctively play the parts humanity has dreamt for them.

                              The game line debuted at a lull in the metaplot. For centuries, the only sidhe (high elves) on Earth were House Scathach and the occasional Lost One - both rare enough that many changelings believed the sidhe were gone or even extinct. But in the wake of the moon landing, when the imagination of virtually every human was aflame, five entire Houses of sidhe rode out of the heart of the Dreaming . . . and promptly plunged the changeling world into civil war, as they tried to reassert control.

                              In modern days, the rise of High King David (a sidhe with strong ties to the "commoner" changelings) has resulted in an uneasy peace . . . a peace that may have arrived too late to save the changelings. Winter is coming. Humanity continues to turn from the wonder of imagination, from the Dreaming itself, slowly starving the changelings of the Glamour they need to survive.

                              And yet they fight on. As the world grows darker, vampires whisper of Gehenna and werewolves fear the Apocalypse, the changelings ready for war. The smithies of nockers ring with song as they beat dragon scale into armor, and forge moonlight into blades. If this is to be the end of the world, the changelings intend their last stand to be one worthy of legend. As the darkness rises, the children of dreams make ready to fight alongside the humans who breathed life into them.

                              (And yet many sidhe are haunted by a nagging sense that they've forgotten something important . . . Why were so many of them wounded, when they first arrived on Earth? Why are their dreams haunted by vague memories of palaces in flames, and desperate war councils?)



                              * their Native American counterparts (the nunnehi), or Asian (the hsien), tell very different stories about their origins. But "overlapping" groups like the menehune imply that (fundamentally) they're the same thing.

                              Originally posted by Nin View Post
                              CtD is a game abou geeks/nerds.
                              Personally, I think I would've compared it to LARPing. Even more so than the werewolves, changelings (et al) are dual-natured beings. They exist simultaneously as humans and faerie creatures, they are continually aware of both mundane Earth and the Dreaming . . . both are real for them.

                              And yet the humans around them are blind and ignorant to the Dreaming side of things. Thus changelings dealing with "chimerical' beings or other problems within the Dreaming often seem to be play acting or miming to humans. Coupled with "interesting" fashion choices, a group of changelings often look like refugees from an SCA reenactment engaging in LARP. And those are the lucky ones. Unlucky ones often get institutionalized as being mentally ill - ranting and raving about beings the humans can't see, afflicted with problems that aren't "real". A chimerically broken leg is still broken . . . for a changeling, but it won't show up on an X-ray.

                              Now, changelings are free to ignore the Dreaming, deny that its real . . . but that has consequences, too. The faerie half of their soul starts to fall asleep, starts to forget who and what it is. Deny the Dreaming too long, and you may not be able to find your way back to it. Are you still a LARPer (or geek/nerd) if you never game, never go to the cons, never read another fantasy novel or watched another sci-fi show? That's the problem changelings face. Pretend too hard, and the lie becomes real.

                                                      Changeling splats are known as kiths, and correspond to various types of faerie creatures. Born of different dreams and fantasies, each kith has been blessed by humanity with a particular gift, and a certain style:
                                                      • The sidhe are the Fair Folk, the Beautiful Ones. Knights and aristocrats, the sidhe are born of the dreams of nobility. The heroic champion in shining armor, the foul usurper who oppresses the masses, the rightful heir who returns to set things right . . . The sidhe find themselves exiled from the deathless lands of Arcadia, stranded in a world that believes the time for such heroics long passed.
                                                      • Travelers from afar, the eshu come bearing tales of distant wonders and epic deeds in exotic lands. Wandering storytellers and bards, an eshu always walks the path to adventure . . . whether they want to or not.
                                                      • Whisperers in the dark, the pale sluagh disturb even their (few) friends. Bend your ear close, and listen to their secrets . . . its better than having them seek you out. For a sluagh can always get to you, twisting and contorting into the most unnatural shapes to crawl under the door, or up through the pipes.
                                                      • Drawn from the myths of dwarves and goblins, the oft-surly nockers are the wondersmiths of the fae. An endless stream of treasures tumble from their forges: Thor's hammer Mjolnir or Freya's necklace, the sword Excalibur . . . with the right tools, a nocker can build anything from a mechanical dragon to steampunk mecha
                                                      Lusty satyrs and hardworking boggans, skin-changing selkies and mighty trolls . . . there are a wide variety of kiths to chose from. Throw in the Inanimae (dryads, mannequin people, and more), along with the Native American nunnehi and Asian hsien . . . players can take on the role of pretty much any creature from Eastern or Western mythology they want to.

                                                      Why bother materializing anything? Between the memory-distorting Mists, and the sheer variety of chimera, the Dreaming practically begs for guerilla tactics. Enchant your foe, and presto - they're not in Kansas anymore.

                                                      There's no reason in the world the changelings of Roswell can't stage an "alien abduction" (anal probing optional). Or drag their target off to "Area 51", to be interrogated by "real" men-in-black. And you have to feel bad for the poor Prince who's retainers keep getting mugged by "Robin Hood and his Merry Men".
