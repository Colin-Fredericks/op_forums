    Ask a Question, Changeling Edition

    01-09-2024, 05:48 AM
    I had a question about the game, and while I feel it is a waste to create a new thread for a single question. If I opened it up for anyone else to ask a question, that feel more worthwhile.

    My ridiculous, one-note question is this; what the rationale behind the Willpower restriction for House Balor's Frailty/Flaw? I don't understand how being a member of this House means that one cannot be determined to such an extent. Being adopted into the House will even lower new members Willpower ratings if they are above 6. And I can't think of a reason why, beyond maybe impulse control...which honestly could be covered by the Unseelie code, rather than Willpower.

    Thoughts ripple out, birthing others

      If there are any official statements that elaborate on the Willpower restriction beyond it being "due to their savage Formorian blood" (that's how it's phrased in C20 at least), then I'm not aware of them. I agree that attributing the Willpower cap to their Formorian origin in and of itself isn't really convincing: Thallain ties to the Formorians are at least as strong and they aren't limited in this way. If you're going by older editions of Changeling, it might make more sense to justify this part of the House Flaw in terms of the fact that half of their Seelie Legacies, arguably half of themselves, are so thoroughly shackled and silenced. An alternative explanation that could work for C20 and older editions would be that the cost of acquiring the House resistance to cold iron is a deadening of the self that makes it impossible to muster the sort of passion, commitment, and belief required to reach the highest levels of Willpower, but I'd generally expect that sort of thing to be expressed in terms of Banality.

      Or keeping the Formorian angle, maybe isn't so much that they have Formorian blood but that it wars against the fact that they are Kithain, splitting their focus even though they strongly favor their Formorian natures. Of course, this explanation wouldn't really work for a hypothetical Thallain member of the House; I know that they were being recruited in past editions, but it isn't clear if that means that any of them are members of the House proper as opposed to just oath-bound muscle.

      Honestly, I don't think it's a very good fit for this House made up of militaristic fanatics who have, for millennia, maintained a sense of identity and purpose that is actively antithetical to Kithain society as a whole despite being part of it at the same time, so I replaced it with something else. I don't recall if pre-C20 Changeling allowed violating a primary Legacy's Ban to be justified in terms of characteristics and tendencies associated with a secondary Legacy, but for C20 at least I think making it so that a Seelie Legacy can't ever be invoked to justify the violation of an Unseelie Legacy's Ban works as a fitting replacement for that part of the House Flaw; if there are Thallain members, then the arrangement would be that they can't justify violating the Taboos of their Nightmare Legacies in terms of their secondary Legacy, regardless of whether it's Unseelie or Seelie (though this obviously only works with C20 Thallain rules).

                              There are hints in C20 that DA:Fae might be the backstory for modern Changeling; and the final Dark Ages supplement Darkening Sky has a section for DA:Fae that triggers the Shattering. So I wouldn't go so far as to say that there's no canonical connection between the two.

                              One of the writers of C20 also wrote a Storyteller's Vault supplement called Harbingers of Winter. It has a whole chapter (Chapter Five: Waking Nightmares) talking about the Fomorians and their Courts (Green, Red, and White). The Fomorians themselves are presented as gods, and beyond the scope of what protagonists can confront directly, much like Rank 6+ spirits in Werewolf. But their Courts are populated by Thallain and Dark-Kin. Meanwhile, the Green, Red, and White Courts are presented as stepping stones between the Black Court and Kithain society. In effect, the Shadow Court is the other end of the progression. (The Black Court and the Shadow Court are talked about in Chapter Four.) Balor is described as a member of the Green Court.

                              Harbingers of Winter also ties into the same author's Storyteller's Vault supplement for Dark Ages: Fae called Past is Prologue which details the Fomorians in that game's terms by adding a new Origin (Dark) and a Dominion of Nightmare, and to the same author's unofficial C5, which operates on the premise that Arcadia has fallen, the Fomorians (now known as the Elder Dark) have returned, the Endless Winter has begun, and the Dreaming is now hostile to changelings.

                                                          Dark Ages: Fae addresses the first part: in Chapter Six, it explores a series of options as to what the true nature of the fae might be; and on pages 197–198, the option it addresses is “Dreams Incarnate”. It outlined the possibility that the Fae and things associated with them, including the otherworldly realms they often inhabit, may in fact be created by human imagination, and that they just don't realize it. Though some suspect.

                                                          The events of the Fae chapter of Darkening Skies describes what changes that. The story takes place, as do all of the stories in the book, during the eclipse of AD 1230, at the end of the Oath-Truce.
                                                          The story centers around a scheme to free a caravan of baptized changelings from the effects of baptism, allowing them to regain their full fae nature once again. At least, that's how it starts. In truth, the changelings in question are wearing Treasures that will kill them and release a new phenomen on the world, which is described in the book as a sort of “anti-magic” field. Rumors of these Treasures spread throughout fae society, and the more warlike of the Fae start using them as weapons to devestate their enemies. What's really happening is that each baptized changeling's death creates a bit of Banality in the world


                                                          The following is a direct quote from Darkening Skies, describing the long-term aftermath of the story:
                                                          Many fae who wish to rekindle the War of Seasons attempt to recreate it, causing more Unleashings all over and seeking out baptized changelings to cripple their enemies. More and more fae become Lost, and the Mists react violently to the abuse to the point that Unleashing becomes nearly impossible in some places, especially human cities. The firstborn find it more difficult to hide in the Mists and have to find other means to conceal themselves, if not retreat into faerie realms completely. Changelings feel the sting of this less keenly, as they can more naturally walk among humanity.

                                                          In an effort to adapt, some fae try to use cantrips for the same effect of the Friar's Cross, especially changelings. With the imposition of order and structure, the fae themselves become less varied, taking on Features similar to one another that are shaped by the dreams and nightmares of humanity. This gives rise to different kiths of changelings.

                                                          When the Oath-Truce falls, the Shattering begins.


                                                          It's not particularly elegant, and many fans of both Dark Ages: Fae and Changeling: the Dreaming have criticized it for how kludgey it is. But one thing that nobody denies is that it's a deliberate effort to build a bridge from the former to the latter.

                                                          C20 then picks up the tale (pp.35–36):
                                                          In 1230 an eclipse covered much of the Autumn world, but while that would pass unnoticed by far, this one also blotted out all light in the Dreaming. Panic over further human growth slowly spread in the courts. To protect themselves, many fae moved their holdings into the Dreaming to avoid contact with humanity, and the process acted to further sever bonds between the dream and the flesh.

                                                          …Over the course of the next two centuries, trods and freeholds vanished, were destroyed, or were otherwise lost to the fae. …

                                                          …In the early 14th century, the Autumn world entered a new era that would eventually transition into the Renaissance. …

                                                          The text then goes on to describe how the fae reacted, with some sealing themselves off in their freeholds and becoming the Lost Ones, others fleeing to what they now referred to as Arcadia, and the rest subjecting themselves to the newly discovered Changeling Way.

                                                          By the end of the Shattering in the fifteenth century, the transition from Fae to Changeling metaphysics was complete.

                                                          Which, incidentally, means that during the time of Mage: the Sorcerers Crusade, the world of the fae was smack dab in the most chaotic phase of the Shattering. At the start of the period, the Courts, Houses, and Kiths had already emerged, but the underlying metaphysics were likely still more like Fae than Changeling. By the end of it, all but two Houses had retreated from the Autumn world, and those who remained either became Lost Ones or Changelings; and the last of the true fae vanished from the face of the Earth.
