    Changeling newbie here, so forgive me if I come across as ignorant of the game and its themes or mechanics, I'm still new to Dreaming.

    From what I have read, Changeling: The Dreaming is outwardly lighthearted but inwardly hopelessly dark. But is there a way to make Changeling not hopeless? Does Winter have to be endless? Can it not be finite? I want to get into Changeling and maybe even ST it, but I am afraid that Changeling may be too dark despite its bright and colorful exterior. If you were going to "fix" Changeling without making it hopelessly grimdark, how would you do it? From what I have read of the gameline, it had a unique flaw in that it was both too dark and too light at the same time and in lopsided ways.

    Forgive me if I am missing the point, I just want to figure out this game that I really want to get into, and see what I need to tweak and what I need to keep. After all, I'm the kind of Storyteller who focuses more on the action-adventure aspects of World of Darkness, and I am wondering if Changeling can pull this off. I have done it with Werewolf, Mage (well, I planned out a Mage game, never got to run it), and even Vampire, and I am hoping I can run some adventurous chronicles with Changeling: The Dreaming

            Changeling: the Dreaming is about hope vs. despair, imagination vs. reality, and innocence vs. experience.

            On the surface, its stories are a combination of medieval-ish fantasy-adventure/fantasy-politics with a struggle to keep this extra life going in the face of real-world concerns. Or, it's a game about being normal people with an extraordinary secret and secret powers to go with it, and trying to keep the fun and excitement without letting it get in the way of your normal life.

            If you want to focus on action-adventure, Changeling's Dreaming-side is going to be a good home for you. Focus on the intrigue and adventure of dealing with chimerical threats and fantasy-adventure. The mundane world becomes a place you're protecting from these threats which most people can't even see. If you interact with werewolves, mages, or vampires, they are, to you, just strange chimerical threats which CAN be seen by the real world (but don't want to be). Bring your glamour and magics with you, and cultivate your dross so that you can remain potent; mages don't need "juice" to work, and vampires feed far more easily than you do. Werewolves...are terrifyingly potent, even without their Gifts.

            Like all WoD splats, you're living a double life as a Changeling. It's just more literal for you than most, because you're straddling two worlds, and it takes effort to bring one into the other. But both are real to you, and the politics of other Kinain inevitably impact your life in both worlds, because they, too, exist in both.


            As for whether Winter need be eternal...

            When I ran a Changeling game, I had the villain be a Sidhe youth that the party had some passing attachment to who, during a ceremony that he'd tried to avoid but which greatly offended his ancient Sidhe soul, erupted into his Unseelie mein. The Lord of Winter is a long-haired pretty-boy of a Sidhe who has worked for centuries, across multiple lifetimes, ever since the Shattering, to bring about Winter. Not because he's suicidal, but because he believes that this prolonged Autumn is killing the Fae, just as an eternal fall would lead to the death of plant and animal life alike. Winter, he believes, is not to be feared, but endured, or even embraced as a time of rest. For, after Winter, does not the Spring always come?

            So the question of its necessary permanence is a good one! Answer it how you will, as ST, to make your game more interesting and enjoyable.

                        Originally posted by Pilodermann View Post
                        Unseelies are trying to live in the human world. So even if the dreaming is lost, it is not the end of faeries.
                        That's not really hope though.

                        The humans are not only killing the dream by making art mainstream, they are also doing it by being less and less mystical and superstitious. However, the humans are also the reason why the dream exists because they can not explain everything.
                        Again though, this sort of nonsensical approach of how humanity and the Dreaming interact completely undercuts the darkness of the game. Why is mainstream art bad? Mainstream art inspires thousands of people to dream of being stars, and trying art for themselves. Just like the moon landing, it offers a contradictory message that doing big impressive and inspiring stuff is awesome, but all the hard work of getting it there is horrible and evil. That's not dark, it's just kind of stupid.

                        And the idea that humanity is somehow becoming less mystical and superstitious isn't really true either. It both romanticizes the state of such things in the past, and completely cherry picks who gets to count in the present.

                        To avoid any major controversies, I'm going to stick to a bit of IRL experience with this. I spent some time working as an assistant for one of the members a hospital's alternative medicine committee. Basically, the committee decided if a given form of alternative medicine would fall into four categories. (1) It's scientifically valid, even if not the recommended treatment, so it will be practiced with the hospital's approval, (2) there's no scientific basis, but it isn't disruptive to accepted treatments/harmful to the patients, and the patients to can it on their own if they want, (3) it isn't harmful to the patient, but is disruptive to other patients and/or staff, and will not be allowed, and (4) it's harmful and hell no.

                        When you start looking up what people are willing to do when medical science says, "Sorry, we don't have anything we can do but make you comfortable," we can think we're living in a highly rational and reasoned society, but people will make up almost anything to convince themselves that if they do it, they can do what medicine can't. Which is pretty much invented on the spot superstition.

                        What it really comes down to, is that humanity hasn't really changed in terms of why and when we experience the need for such things. It's that we try as hard as possible to avoid the causes. And the causes actually make for a much darker game. Comfortable humans, are humans that don't reach out to mystical and superstitious thinking. Those modes of thinking are part of our natural responses to living in highly stressful conditions.

                        So, when you look at it from the idea that sucky lives (and the WOD is full of those) is what inspires humans to be less rational minded, and more superstitious, it makes for a much darker game where the protagonists want people to be more superstitious.

                            Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
                            Humanity can't be killing the Dream if humanity is responsible for the Dream existing. This sort of nuance lacking concept of Banality ruins the potential darker aspects of the Changeling metaphysics. It never reconciles things like how on earth the moon landing, which is supposed to have been one of the greatest releases of Glamour in the modern age, emerges from Banality 10 Dream murdering laboratories that made the technology that allowed travel to the moon possible. . .
                            Humanity is not killing the Dreaming. As a tangible manifestation of humanity's dreams and beliefs, that's probably not possible without killing every human on the planet (or maybe turning them all into mindless drones). Instead, humanity is sterilizing the Dreaming. Paving over the Smurf Village to put in another strip mall. Capturing all the unicorns, and offering 2-for-1 rides on their backs (infants ride free!). Dumping trash all over the sunken city of Atlantis, and (accidentally) wiping out the mermaids with dragnet fishing.

                            The conflict between Glamour and Banality is basically the difference between wild possibility and boring predictability. Glamour is raw potential, wild magic made manifest. Anything is possible with it, from rainbow fairies dancing in the moonlight, to an albino tentacle horror exploding from the ground and eating out your eyes. Good, evil, or just insane, Glamour only cares whether something is amazing and wondrous. Its the stuff of dreams, nightmares, and raw inspiration.

                            Banality, on the other hand, is boring and repetitive. Today will be like yesterday, and tomorrow will be like today. Banality has no room for rainbow fairies . . . or albino tentacle horrors. Sure, it may be dull, and toxic to the fae, but its also safe. And in the World of Darkness, safe is quite attractive. Stay in your box, and nothing from outside will come in and threaten you.

                            That's why the moon landing was such a big deal. The Technocracy may promote Banality (although they might disagree with you on that), but going to the moon was a BIG DEAL for humanity. Suddenly, the idea of going to another world was real. Moving to another planet, meeting aliens . . . or having aliens come here. The moon landing showed the whole world that it was possible. It knocked a billion people out of their repetitive lines, and mindless repetition. Questions had to be asked, and radical new possibilities considered. It was a huge, huge win for Glamour, and a massive defeat for the pull of Banality.

                                                    It is a matter of emphasis and presentation. The core rulebook presents a kind of high fantasy setting hidden behind our own world (technically the World of Darkness, although it doesn't really do a good job of creating the Gothic-Punk milieu for that setting).

                                                    Later supplements ranged from urban fantasy to dark fantasy, although, like Wraith, much of the setting in the books seems to take place away from the World of Darkness somewhat in its own fantasy realm (freeholds, chimerical reality, the Dreaming), even where the two overlap.

                                                    I like the breadth of possibilities in the Changeling setting. But I think the core rulebook should work better at deciding which angle it's going for, and to present it clearly. Or rather, to make clear the distinct possibilities for the game in terms of mood, so that the different styles of play are evident without having to read multiple sourcebooks.

                                                    I imagine if it had received the Revised Edition treatment, we'd have seen gritty urban fantasy presented in the core book, rather like Mage, with a greater emphasis on the external threats of the Denizens, Dauntain and Fomorians. As it was, Changeling 2e seems to focus primarily on changeling politics and society, with Banality as an indistinct behemoth in the background, hence the feeling that it's kind of detached from the World of Darkness and existing in a sort of high fantasy alternate world all on its own.

                                                    One way to do it would be to break the book down in a way similar to Unknown Armies 2e. Start off with the street-level stuff, the gritty stories, the everyday changeling stuff. Then go onto the courtly stuff and the international politics. Then, finally, take on the cosmic (or cosmological) stuff. M20 is probably a good model too.

                                                    I think that would allow players to see the different styles of game, and the different ways they can tailor it to fit the kinds of moods they want to play.

                                                            Some Banality is needed so that Glamour can have shape and meaning. But this means, as you pointed out earlier, I think, that there should be some tangible benefit for Banality too, so that it's not always a cartoon evil.
