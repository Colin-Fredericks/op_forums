                                        Originally posted by Daniel Knight View Post
                                        Irish Privileges Engaged

                                        The Irish kith is the drunkard because that's what leprechauns are. If you expect to see stereotypes everywhere then you will and gods help us if you're charged with deciding when lethal force is applicable.
                                        1) Thankfully I wouldn't have to decide that. In my view, lethal force is never applicable. If you kill a criminal, they can't stand trial. If you kill an innocent person, you're a murderer. Everyone is innocent until proven guilty, so shooting anyone without a trial is murder.

                                        2) You may have replied to my comment immediately, in which case you missed that I immediately updated that post to clarify that I meant drunken fighter to be clear (I wrote drunkard but it was more the violence that came through in the text). He's clearly a fighter. He's wearing an Irish flag on his T-shirt with blood flecks on him. His Birthright is starting fights. That is an Irish stereotype. Calling Irish privilege back at you, though, when I say this.

                                        Also, the book literally namedrops Black Lives Matters to make a point about the Shadow Court. But when we critically engage with the book using a core argument of groups like BLM, that's just looking for stereotypes? Nah. It's not an original idea of mine that stereotypes dehumanise, and that that makes it easier to kill minorities.

                                        To be fair, it's not just a C20 thing, and others have had this discussion elsewhere. All the 90s stuff had similar issues (the Brujah kith image was usually Theo Bell, a black bruiser; the whole concept of the Settites as black drug dealer serpent dudes; the Assamites; the Ravnos; etc). It's a legacy carried over in our nostalgia products. I'm not really blaming anyone, and it wasn't me who raised the initial point, but we also shouldn't pretend it's not there.

                                        As someone else pointed out in the thread, these are simple art issues that can be fixed by inviting POC to the decision-making process. I edit magazines all the time, and I manage one for a charity whose remit is diversity, so I literally have to go through and make sure that every race is featured, as well as the full spectrum of LGBT identities, disabled people, etc. I tell the designers this for my art notes, and they still don't always get it right the first time, so I send them back and they do it again until they get it right. In this case, we're using photography, and we have lots of photos to work with, but the basic framework is needed. I always pinpoint articles that might be contentious (domestic violence, mental health, cyberbullying), and then make notes to the designers to specifically avoid placing problematic images alongside them.

                                        Anyway, rant over. It's not enough to ruin the book for me--it's gorgeous. It's just a fair thing for us to point out, so that next time the next book can be even better.

                                            What about the fact, that changelings are born of stereotypes? Good and bad.. Should we ignore that part, too? Get rid of all kiths as well? Listen.

                                            Their very race and strengths and weaknesses are typecasted by collective subconsciousness of humanity before they even born.

                                            I agree, that stereotypes dehumanize. That ideologies turn people's brains into mush, making them a living cogs, serving great machines of Great Ideas.

                                            Many people, and Changelings, I presume, embrace their stereotypes, are proud of them. Football clubs, universities, faculties, classes, nationalities, occupations, political parties, family clans, guilds, religions, cults, corporations, communities, and ideologies. Tribes. Some of are happy to live, work and die for their tribe and embrace the stereotypes. They embrace mocking-titles thrown at them and make them part of their identity.

                                            I'd prefer, that people not do that. That they would not stereotype others, not see the world in black and white and not turn themselves into a hivemind sockpuppets.

                                            But we live in a world, where they have a choice to do that.

                                            Troll stereotype of an honorable servant is no different from a leprechaun fighter or Sidhe ruler or Satyr party-goat. Or Boggan Hermit. Or Pooka Fool. Leprechauns of today are a stereotype of Irish people in 19th century. But nobody sees Leprechauns of modern media, as Irish people. They're Leprechauns. Faries. Spirits. Either malicious or benevolent, they are their own individual thing. Some embrace stereotypes, others - reject them furiously. Looking and acting like a particular stereotype no less representative, than not looking like it.

                                            Changeling: the Dreaming is a game about living embodiments of stereotypes. I think, its players can handle the idea of individuality and use this game as a tool to explore the riddles of human condition, and if they can't - their friends will help them. Have some trust in people.
