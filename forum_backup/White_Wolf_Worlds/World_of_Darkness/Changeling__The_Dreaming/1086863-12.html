    Argh! Apologies, I didn't realise there was also a Gift of Pan debate on this thread - I went and derailed the errata thread instead. I won't repeat myself on that point, then.

    I will say that I quite like the new setup of the Shadow Court and the Black Court. I found the relationship between the two very clear and didn't think it was delegitimising the concerns of the Kithain involved (or the human protest movements that inspired their tactics). It reminded me of the recent reporting that Russia attempted to manipulate Sanders' supporters into voting for Trump by targeting them for anti-Clinton spam. A grassroots movement grounded in ethical concerns about justice and fairness manipulated (well, attempted anyway) by the clandestine and powerful. I think it's an interesting dynamic.

    I also don't think it's inherently inappropriate to reference Black Lives Matter, Occupy Wall Street or the Arab Spring. These are disparate political movements bound more by form than function, and - in keeping with C20's focus on Kithain society mirroring humanity's throughout history - it makes sense that mass grassroots protest would also characterise modern Kithain political conflict. I don't feel it's inappropriately laying claim to the seriousness of, for instance, the murder of young black men. It's more acknowledging that the Dream of protest and political activism is affected by the times - by the dominant imagery on our screens.

    I can see why it might be seen that way, but then, does the Week of Nightmares retcon - now associated with September 11 - trivialise those deaths? Is it in poor taste to tie the Accordance War to the Vietnam War, and all its attendant atrocities? If not, is it a matter of time? Is it too soon? That's a serious question. But it does raise another - when has it been long enough? How should a game set in a version of our contemporary world deal with current events?

    On the other hand I think the kith art is definitely questionable. I understand wanting to avoid tokenistic eshu but as others have noted, doing so by giving the only major kith associated with a non-Western myth white representation while portraying the violent and domestic fae as black is a very poor choice.

Well, it means that now people know that *they* can make and change history and they believed in a better future instead of a fantasy past (see Reinhard Kosseleck and his "horizon of expectation").

                                    So labelling the Feench Revolution *as a whole* as a moment of despair is so wrong -- unless we consider that the Autumn People have won the way we look at history.

                                            Originally posted by PookaKnight View Post
                                            My apologies for the poor word choice. The mention of the French Revolution was intended to reference the situation leading up to it and the Reign of Terror, not the final outcome.
                                            PookaKnight,

                                            The "Reign of Terror" is an expression which was first coined by very conservative historians who wanted to discredit Robespierre by saying that he had "reigned" over France, which is very, very wrong. Robespierre, as many historians have now shown convincingly, was a rather centrist-like figure who eliminated opponents from the left (Hebertists) and right (Dantonists) because he felt that they both threatened the stability of the newly-established French Republic.

                                            Also, Robespierre never reigned over France. He was just one of the twelve members of the Comity of Public Safety who constantly fought among themselves, and who were accountable to the National Convention and its 700+ elected members (elected with universal suffrage for the first time in history!). France was surely a dictatorship at this moment (suppression of liberties) in order to fight almost all of Europe in order to survive, but it was a legislative dictatorship, not an executive one.

                                            The war in the Vendée was most certainly a moment of Dark Glamour.

                                            Finally, the immediate outcome of the French Revolution was bleak: Napoléon Bonaparte took power with a coup on 9 November 1799 and then set up an executive dictatorship to prepare for his conquest of almost the whole of Europe. This conquest and the Napoleonic Wars were certainly also a moment of Dark Glamour, though it is very ambiguous: many peoples resisted against Napoléon in the name of the very ideals of the French Revolution that he claimed to bring to them. And for some (the Irish for instance), Napoléon was a liberator.

                                                        Originally posted by Firkraag View Post
                                                        How exactly historians have shown convincingly?..
                                                        Well, because they have done their jobs and have been honest about it: they have quoted their sources, they have been very open about why they interpret them the way they do, and, perhaps more importantly, they have reached now a pretty agreed-on consensus on who was Robespierre.

                                                        The perception of Robespierre that has endured was forged immediately his fall and execution by those who were very close to him and who used him as a scapegoat in order to wash themselves of the crimes they had participated in. And since the enemies of the Revolution were using Robespierre as their scapegoat to sell to their own people their war against a democratic republican France, the two discourses coalesced and took hold on many, many minds. Then, it was used by very conservative historians (like Thomas Carlyle) who in turn were used by conservative novelists (Charles Dickens's Tale of Two Cities comes to mind) who then inspired many movies (the French Revolution was the historical topic most used in cinema after the Vietnam War until recently -- I'm thinking about Batman: The Dark Knight here, which is a direct evocation of the French Revolution, inspired by a Tale of Two Cities). In other words, when people think about it today, they think about a myth, not about what it really was, and what it did for France (but that's true for many other historic periods, and this is where historians have an important job to do).
