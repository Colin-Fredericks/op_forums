    Changeling is not a "light" game.

    11-09-2015, 05:09 AM
    The criticism of Changeling that I keep seeing is that it is too light to fit in the World of Darkness. It's true that anything that isn't Vampire deviates from the World of Darkness aesthetic that was created specifically for Vampire. Each game line is a separate entity with a separate approach--but each presents a different facet of a broken world. Vampire shows vulnerable people being preyed on with no one noticing, and the loss of humanity that comes from practicing violence even when it's necessary to survive. Werewolf's world is one that is literally dying. Mage's World of Darkness is an apparently bright and clean one, but that hides Orwellian puppet masters that define reality itself. Wraith in its roundabout way portrays death as the final oblivion we fear it to be.

    Changeling is so pessimistic that it portrays merely growing up as a kind of death. There is the solipsism of childhood and the narcissism of youth and then... nothing. Nothing that really matters. The World of Darkness is such a grim place that once the reality of an ordinary life foists itself upon a person, the spark within them is extinguished. Even in a relatively vampire-dense city you're probably not going to fall prey to one. But most people grow up. Most people live fairly long lives nowadays; the majority of one's life, then, one is transfixed like an insect on a display board, by banality. This may not be a fair representation of real life. But it seems like a suitable fate in the World of Darkness.

          The common complaint isn't really that CtD is too "light" of a game. It's that the CtD books do a horrible job of selling it as a dark game (compounded heavily by plenty of groups that play it lighter than the total package of the books paint). I think missing this distinction generally harms attempts to discuss CtD with its detractors.

          And it is important to note that even though there are plenty of dark parts of the core books, they suffer significantly from making the dark parts of the game either feel minor, or no fun to deal with. Banality isn't presented as a nuanced force that makes the darkness in CtD interesting. It's presented extremely inconsistently and as something to avoid rather than engage with.

              How long the game runs, and what age group the characters play as, matters a lot. A short (one or two session) game, where all the PCs are childlings (pre-teens) . . . that can be very lighthearted. Anything is possible, from talking stuffed animals, to having tea with a dragon, to dancing with pixies under the moon light.

              Alternately, that same game can go very, very dark. Borrowing an idea from the Immortal Eyes chronicle (and movies like Sucker Punch), imagine a bunch of childlings committed to a snakepit of an mental hospital by well-meaning parents. Nightmarish chimera spawned by the other patients, Autumn People trying to "help" them with electroshock therapy and powerful psychoactive drugs . . .

              Meanwhile, a long-term grump (adult changeling) campaign is going to be big on the depressing/pessimistic elements. They've already got a lot of Banality weighing them down, eating at their memories . . .and the world is always happy to give 'em even more. Depending on the Storyteller, in such a campaign, you're more likely to lose your PC to Banality and permanent amnesia, than to death. Which might hurt more, actually.



              Edit: There are also the complications from trying to be both fae and human at the same time. Seanan McGuire explored some of them in Rosemary and Rue, the first of her "October Daye" novels (which read suspiciously like a variant Changeling campaign). One quest gone wrong, and the protagonist spends fifteen years trapped in the Dreaming. Fifteen years where her daughter and husband believed she ran out on them. . . and there's no way to explain to the bitter, angry, and (very heartbroken) duo what really happened.

                      Something I've said in the past, at least regarding the art of CtD, is that I don't think it needs to be more overtly dark. I think that misses the point. It needs to be more subtly dark; and consistently so.

                      To give an example:

                      The friendly balloon bear. As is, he's a problem with being too lighthearted. He's presence says, "The Dreaming is an awesome fun fantasy land that is amazing and cool." It never communicates the potential darkness lurking in the Dreaming.

                      Now imagine if the art had a piece where a balloon gets away, and the bear seems incredibly distraught. And another piece shows that inside each balloon is a dream of its own. Suddenly... each lost balloon is a dream lost into the Dreaming; maybe never to return. Now the Dreaming, no matter how saccharine it looks, is a realm of strange symbolism, and consequences completely out of proportion with their normal associated values.

                      That little bit of art change, makes things a ton darker. It isn't hitting you over the head by turning balloon bear into pedobear, or some such nonsense. It's letting the art guide people to seeing that even in the bright shiny light of the happiest parts of the Dreaming, things are not good.

                                              Here are my three points of contention on this issue, and know up front that *I* know that the following is completely unfair and may not actually reflect my own feelings. That said, also know that I only bring these up because they are mentioned above, which caused me to think of the reasons Changeling: the Dreaming is NOT a dark game, if not a proper 'light' game.

                                              1. The game as written hinges on an impossibly idealised image of childhood.

                                              This is a core concept all over the core book (both editions), and that very notion of 'grown up equals bad' and 'childhood equals good' sets a contrast that isn't allowed much nuance in the core books. Oh sure, there are options found elsewhere (please see below), but that borderline infantilism vibe is off-putting for many of us more familiar with other iterations of the World of Darkness. Yeah, the 'loss of innocence' bit is tragic, but it's also part of growing up in the Real World. That makes it a little hard for some to take seriously as a 'horror' or 'dark' game. So, the WORST thing that can happen to a changeling is to fall to banality, which makes that character a *gasp* human being just like me! Oh, the dark and oh, the horror...

                                              2. Bright colours cannot exist in the World of Darkness.

                                              All of the art in the core book (and yeah, I keep hitting this unfairly, but I'll get to that point) is full colour, bears and clowns and sad 'monsters' in full daylight. Hell, even the Sluagh and Redcaps don't come across as terribly dark in that book, despite us being told their emo and savage, respectively. The system of gaining Glamour doesn't hurt anyone or anything, and the notion of their 'fallen world' is, well, OUR world rather than a worse-than-our-world presented in, say, Werewolf where the whole world is ending, Vampire where the whole world is ending, or Mage where, um... The whole world is ending? Maybe that gets old, but in Changeling it comes down to far more personal stakes, something that isn't seen as so much 'dark' by some of us.

                                              3. The core book doesn't encompass the entire line in terms of theme, mood, or overall milieu.

                                              THIS is why I'm so unfair above (and ultimately why I'm right chuffed for Changeling 20). Vampire and Werewolf and all through the rest of the classic World of Darkness lines present a vision of the game that is expanded upon and 'focused' by supplements growing from the initial book's presentation. Changeling: the Dreaming did this quite differently, which was actually brilliant in its own way of 'drawing out the darkness' form. However, it also made it terribly difficult for a 'casual' observer (read: one who only looked at the core book) to see many nuances.

                                              ...So yeah, I fall closer to the OP's point of view on this than the opposite, but the above does show how I saw the game at one time. My HOPE is that the Twentieth Anniversary Edition/Revised version will address these by giving a more holistic view of the Changeling milieu.

                                                  The difference in Changeling is that players play hope.

                                                  Vampires scheme against each others in the darkness.
                                                  Wolves have already perpetrated a genocide against others change-forms.
                                                  Technomages have eradicated mysticism and are making magic disappear.

                                                  Changeling have to fight for their world, but to do so, they had to leave it, maybe for ever.
                                                  They were fae, now they are just half of it.
                                                  They forgot who they were, the only fragments of memories they keep, come through greymare.
                                                  If they spend too much time in fae form, they get mad.
                                                  In Changeling, the shadow court and the nightmares are monsters that kill people and changeling alike.
                                                  But people are the one that kill the Dream.
                                                  By making reality shows, reusing the same songs over and over, making everything mainstream.

                                                  However, they can fight, they can make people think about what is true or not.
                                                  They have to make people dream.

                                                  So yeah, Changeling is the spark in the shadows of the world of darkness, but there is nothing light about it.

                                                      I think C20 will be a hard one to do right, because the core mechanics of CtD were...insufficient...at best, to dealing with the nature of the setting they seem to want to get across. (And that's leaving out the literally incomplete mechanics that exist in a few places.)

                                                      The biggest thing - and I've said it before - that I would do, if I were writing the rules for a CtD system, is have glamour and banality both be resources. Banality would offer genuine "dark gifts" for embracing your humanity, your mortality, and the harsh realities of the world. Being lost to Banality is horrible, yes, but I would re-emphasize the risks of being lost to the Dreaming, as well. Being unable to recall your mortality, being bound too strongly by the sorts of things reflected in the extant system's Echoes flaw.

                                                      But at the same time, gaining glamour would be a little easier; obeying the strictures of the fae, doing things which ignore reality's conveniences for the fantastic limitations of the Dreaming, these would give you tokens of glamour. Dross would be more prevalent as a way to bolster your personal reserves. Because as-is, using Cantrips was...painfully difficult and costly, and so I found that you more often played a mortal who was in a shared game of "let's pretend" with other fae, rather than an actual magical being in a mundane world.
