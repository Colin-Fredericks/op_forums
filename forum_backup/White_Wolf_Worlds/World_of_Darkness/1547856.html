    Maybe we should split the Metaplot.

    01-20-2025, 03:31 PM
    The five main lines of the WoD don't belong together. At least not anymore. I once joked that V:tM, W:tA, and W:tO, were the Damned, the Doomed, and the Lost. But that's literal. Vampires are mainly "Damned if they do and damned if they don't." Werewolves are bound by fate, their doom, and unable to change that, and Wraiths are very much lost souls.

    Mages and Changelings are profoundly different. They are striving to be the heroes of their own tales creating and rewriting their destinies as they go.

    The first three can fit together and so can the second, but all five lead to incoherence. Vampire, Werewolf, and Wraith, are like Macbeth, Hamlet, King Lear, and Othello, tragedies. Mage and Changeling combine As You Like It, Much Ado about Nothing, The Tempest, and Alls Well That Ends Well. With Changeling pulling more from the first two and Mage pulling more from the last two.

    I think it would be creatively good for there to be one metaplot for the tragic trio and another for the comic duo.

    Other game lines (Hunter, Demon, Mummy, and Kindred of the East) could be placed where they fit best.

    Note: All types of Supernaturals would be in each Metaplot. But the supernaturals that don't fit that particular Metaplot would be sidelined.

    Reactions? Refinements? Questions?

      So reactions:

      1) Is there even a point to caring any more? WoD5 is basically an entirely different thing now, and the pre-WoD5 continuity is no longer officially supported. There are no new WoD books coming out with the old takes on the games, so which games get along with which is ultimately down to table preference for people still playing the pre-WoD5 games.

      2) The rationale here is so incredibly surface that I'm kinda shocked you're posting it given you're generally more in-depth takes on things. Calling Mage and Changeling Shakespearean comedies feel like a complete lack of understanding of either side of that statement. All of the core initial five WoD game lines are tragedies, and it's not hard to line them up to Shakespeare more directly: Vampire is Othello and Titus, where political maneuvering and plays for power bring out the most brutal and viscous aspects of human nature. Werewolf is Macbeth and Hamlet, where honored warriors are pressed to seek even greater station in life instead of pursuit of what they know is a more moral path to their own destruction. Wraith is Romeo and Juliet, and Antony and Cleopatra, stories of deep passions driving characters to both the greatest highs and lowest lows, but ultimately futile as it all comes to naught. Mage is Timon of Athens where a well meaning man of wealth is incautious with his power and only brings sorrow where he thought he planted joy, and Coriolanus where pride and shifting allegiances turn all against the self-righteous hero. Changeling is Troilus and Cressida and King Lear, where duty and folly mix to the verge of comedy before falling into pain and violence.

      3) The idea that the ease of making a metaplot work is based on the tone of the individual games is misguided. A lot of the metaplot debates (to be polite) over the past 30 years have been around the metaphysical inconsistencies of the differing games. Vampire and Wraith are usually their own little islands that don't interact nicely with Werewolf, Mage, and Changeling who have more easily reconciled metaphysical outlooks. The tone of the games might be different, but putting the Garou and the Traditions in the same universe is not as difficult as trying to figure out how to make Wraiths relevant in a world with necromancers like the Giovani around.

      -------------

      So I guess, ultimately, what's the point? This is a very old debate, and one that's never really been well settled. Everyone has their own priorities, levels of tolerance for disparities between the games, and so on. The "metaplot" is dead (since there's not really any future metaplot to happen now to really consider it one), and the games' audiences aren't exactly thriving. The WoD fandom kinda needs to be one thing to endure, not five different things. The desire to get every game line to "play together" well in one more unified shared universe is what drives people to use the CofD and is a bit draw of Cursborne as well. The idiosyncratic oddities of the shared WoD universe are basically what keeps people coming back to it.

      Or to put it another way... how does it make things any better to try to "settle" the argument of which games work well together as some sort of objective thing to cluster folks around?

          In general, I just never liked the doomsday metaplots. I liked the ideas behind Y2K, and that behind the mundane events of IRL January 1, 2000 - the entire supernatural world went through a giant storm that kinda-sorta soft-reset things.

          Which... is kinda exactly what happens in the 20th Anniversary editions (?)

          Take the Prophecy of the Phoenix, combine it with the Avatar Storm... And then have an aftermath where there's room for everyone to breathe again. The setting stops being depressing, and instead gives the players the opportunity to rebuild Nodes and Caerns from the ashes - to be reborn, in a sense (like the Phoenix prophecy). It opens up and becomes more sandbox-like.

          The stories are now more slice-of-life, rags-to-riches, adventurous, and so-on.

          Here, there be a world worth residing in.

          I mean... the whole point of the comic in W20 is to show the reader "Hey! We're still here! We're still alive! There's still HOPE!"
          And that to me, is by far WAY more appealing than whatever outright nonsense Hunter: the Reckoning was supposed to be (outside of an Antagonist "Sabbat but Hunters" splat that bamboozles me that it's supposed to be a player splat instead of an oddly detailed Antagonist faction - on top of the fact that there are people who worship the ground of hot garbage that are the H:tR books), or just the load of disappointment that was the Time of Judgement on a general level.

          TL : Dr
          Pretend the metaplot doesn't exist beyond "an event in the past" and just run your games as you see fit, with the overarching theme being the concept of a Phoenix and rebuilding. That's all I can really offer. Because what the pre-9/11 lore of oWoD offers is hot garbage.

          Alternatively, go ahead and read bits and pieces of it off of whatever wiki and form a picture in your mind what it could've been, in the case your games have an NPC that survived some aspect of it. More fun that way.

          Imagine a series of stories for Werewolf, where your players are rediscovering the Totems of the Tribes, and you're trying to rebuild the Garou nation. That'd be cool!
          Imagine a Mage Game where the players are able to picture the world as large shards of a broken mirror, and must push them together and make an art-piece out of it.
          Imagine a Changeling game, where even though the Great Shattering has happened, you can still encourage humanity to dream again.

          Or, my personal favorite: A Mummy game where you're going around post-Y2K, being the supernatural therapist to everyone else (and sometimes cracking a few skulls, because not everyone wants to be healed).

          And so-on.

          I'm also just now imagining Big D from Hunter: the Parenting, going around after the Y2K, Anthelios, and Avatar Storm events, helping out stragglers and giving therapy sessions to survivors like they're shiny Pokémon.

                          Third part is the most important.

                          Originally posted by DaFrontlineSpecter View Post
                          For me, the metaplot has always been this bizzare testament to people's anxieties about Y2k and the belief that the world was going to end in Jan 1 of 2000. Which... I honestly have no idea how to react or engage with that.
                          While I mostly agree with Heavy Arms in this regard, there is something I want to add that makes a big difference:

                          The 90's were abnormal in how much eschatology gained terrain in the public's mind. Especially in Christian.

                          As he said, eschatology is an old thing among humans. While it isn't true that it's a feature of every religion as some think, it has been around since before writing. It's a common anxiety among humans the idea of a catastrophe that will endanger the very existence of our people.

                          But Christianity specifically is a very Eschatological religion. It is, fundamentally, about the Time of Judgement. Even if you ignore the Book of Revelations, the foundation of the whole religion is the Second Coming of Jesus and the Judgement that comes with it. You're not expected to follow the rules because this will bring immediate benefits to you or others (even though it may), but because this will decide the fate of your eternal soul in the final battle between the two sides of reality itself.

                          For anyone with a Christian upbringing, even if very weak, this is an underlying assumption of existence and everything even tangentially linked to the religion. Regardless if you believe or not it's a constant presence, and if you're a religious person to any degree this is an ever-present thought.

                          In the 90's, on top of Christianity being even more widespread than now and people in general even more religious all around the globe, a few things compounded to bring this to a boil: Climate and environment fears were starting to leave the academic niches and really get public attention (hence we had the Earth Summit of 1992). The Cold War just ended, which was in fact an Earth-Shattering event like none since. Apparent political, military and economic stability in the world-stage, give or take local occurrences, gave us a collective ennui that ended up nicknamed "the end of history" by philosophers, an impression that we've reached the pinnacle of social development for better or worse and nothing else of significance would ever happen again. And above all that, playing directly to Christian eschatological anxiety, the Millenium was coming, and that was a very powerful symbol. We do have evidence of a similar uptick in eschatological fears a thousand years before.

                          So, what that means? First, while it became famous later as the only real Millenium threat, Y2K wasn't really the cause of such anxieties, it was just one of many random things that got caught up in the very real anxiety and got overblown out of proportion by some people. Second, eschatological fears didn't just became a widespread anxiety through the decade, this greatly affected art and media at the time. Third, it also resulted in the growth of the very real things that people do based on eschatological beliefs: doomsday cults attracted more followers and grew, which by itself leaves a trail of broken families and people, they also got bolder in their actions, that included mass suicides for some and terrorist attacks for others (yes, terrorism gained mainstream attention back in the 90's because of Christian terrorists), here and there individuals were doing those things on their own, too, sometimes taking the lives of their families before their own, or just doing things like sell all their possessions and other life-destroying decisions based on the belief that they were living the last day of their lives.

                          Nothing here is an absolute, of course, just a small fraction of everything happening at the time, but the cultural impact was intense. And the World of Darkness was born and further developed against this background. In fact I'm personally of the opinion that a big part of WoD's success was due to how much it spoke to the zeitgeist of the time right out of the bat. Even if note, it's undeniably a satire on it, a response to the social dynamics that both birthed the 90's and that developed through it.

                          Originally posted by DaFrontlineSpecter View Post
                          What do you do, when a world's end that was foretold in prophecy, arrives... But the world itself doesn't end? All of Creation was supposed to die, and it didn't.
                          Now what?
                          Everything goes on as normal, including the fears and prophecies of the end. It's not as common nowadays, but widespread apocalyptic prophecies popped out sometimes several times a year during the 90's and their failures never changed anything. And they didn't change now, they just became quieter as the mainstream attention wandered elsewhere. Doomsday cults still exist, there are still people talking about the end or throwing away their lives (figuratively or literally) because of it, the end is still a popular artistic theme, Christianity is as eschatological as ever and growing in power again (it's in fact more politically powerful now than in the 90's), people still fear a lot of things out of proportion in a manner that is in many ways an apocalyptic belief (social media, AI, climate change, industrialization, space race, cold war, economic collapse, dictatorships, religious war, national isolation, pandemics, mental health problems, anti-tech movements, communism, environmentalism, de-industrialization, space threats, new alliances, anarcho-capitalism, weak governments, the woke, globalization, vaccines, mental health worries, Nazism...), with the compounding problem that many of those are legitimate fears that do have a chance at ending society even at reasonable proportions. Conspiracy theories are in many ways a new form of doomsday cult, by the way.

                          Literally none of those ceased to exist, they just changed a little and became less obvious, or get less mainstream attention.

                          Despite how the metaplot ended, as a core theme eschatology in the game has never been about a factual risk of it happening (although this risk being real helps set the mood), but about the things people do because of it. And people do a lot of things because of it. Just look at how the sects are shaped by it, the Sabbat preparing for the final battle against an existential threat of world-ending proportions, the Camarilla being defined not by indifference to the prophecies, but a policy of active denial, at the same time that a lot of it's actual culture is an ever-present fear and acts of destruction against prophetic signs in the hopes that this will keep the cataclysm at bay.

                          Nothing here really requires for the prophecy to be real. And just as in human society, there's no real date to it, nothing really says that it happens shortly after 2004. It has been expected for thousands of years to start at any time now, great upheavals happened dozens of times that were taken as the end finally coming and then passed, this is just one more weird period in the long history of the Kindred where the world seemed to be on the brink, it happened many times before, it will happen many times again.

                              I'd quibble back a bit on one point:

                              The eschatology fervor was abnormally high in the global zeitgeist of the 90s. For US Christianity... it was fairly normal if a bit louder.

                              Ever since the Second Great Awakening in the 1820s, the Protestant majority of the US also turned heavily into an Evangelical plurality and with that came a strong adoption of rapture-theology as doctrine or dogma among the different mainly US-specific Christian movements. As the Catholic Churches, the Orthodox Churches, the Church of England and thus most Anglicans, the traditional European Lutherans and most Lutherans in the rest of the world, all reject rapture eschatology as doctrine or dogma (though there's diversity in tolerance of belief in it as tradition or preference), most of the Christian world doesn't have the same level of base-line eschatology-obsession that the US has. It correlates highly to how abnormally religious the US is based on factors that predict religiosity from the psychological study of religion.

                              When you consider that for the last 150 years or so, 30-40% of the people in the US were raised on the specific eschatology of rapture theology as doctrine, it shouldn't be surprising that we have a significant ebb and flow of eschatology hype that tends to mirror the political climate of the era. Pretty much every sense of major social change or upheaval was seen by at least someone as a sign of the Tribulation starting. Civil War? Jim Crow entrenchment? Great Depression? WWI? WWII? The Civil Rights Movement? The push back against the successful expansion of civil rights? The quasi-Awakening movement now called the Silent Majority (which is still active in US even if that name is more associated with a few decades ago)? All of those came with major eschatology movements as well.

                              I have no studies I can point to on this, but in my experience non-US Christians that know what Christian Millenialism is tend to know it isn't actually linked to the years that are multiples of 1000, while US Christians tend to be more drawn in by the symmetry of that (though they don't all append it to the calendar... Young Earth Creationist sects sometimes try to base it on 6000 years since Creation, with 7000 starting to creep in a bit as time moves forward) without realizing they're unrelated.

                                  Now that you mention, I really noticed how much Rapture is common on US arts and religious discourse. Around here it's far from a standard, although I'm under the impression that it's becoming more common.

                                  It's important though to observe that there's an important distinction between knowing your theology and having an emotional response to something, especially when it's a common fear.

                                  People instinctively react to those symmetries. Probably a byproduct of our constant search for patterns and meaning, the idea of the turn of the millennium felt powerful regardless of actual beliefs all by itself. While that alone doesn't mean much, for a people that do have a concept of what the most important event ever will be and tacitly expect it to happen any day "without warning" (even if not really most of the time), the association between the two becomes inevitable, if just as a "hunch".

                                  Most Christians I knew at the time (which were almost everyone I knew, period) had at least this unease. In fact, even agnostics frequently had it. It didn't always correlate to any real belief that it would happen, most people didn't, but the tension was there.

                                                          I'll agree with Astromancer on one point: Mage is different from nearly every other World of Darkness gameline in that it never really had a doom scenario of its own. Just look at the titles of the final books (or chapters in Time of Judgment, for those gamelines that ended there) and you can see a difference: Vampire got Gehenna; Werewolf got Apocalypse; Wraith, which had died five years earlier in Ends of Empire, got a last hurrah of sorts in Orpheus, which ended with End Game. Changeling ended with Eternal Winter; Demon ended with Agents of the Apocalypse; Kindred of the East ended with Interesting Times (a Chinese curse); and Mummy ended with Armageddon.

                                                          Mage? It ended with Ascension. The only other gameline that didn't end on a downer title was Hunter, which got to Inherit the Earth (that is, it ended with the monsters being vanquished, and humanity picking up the pieces and rebuilding in the post-Darkness world). Note that Mage could have ended with Descension as its title, had the authors wanted to emphasize a more nihilistic view of the end of the line; and that would have been in keeping with how nearly all other gamelines ended. But they didn't. Instead, they chose to end on a high note.

                                                          And to emphasize further how different Mage is from the other gamelines: the concept of Ascension in Mage isn't inherently an "end times" thing. Mass Ascension can be argued to be an "end times" thing; but by default, "Ascension" in Mage refers to something more personal: the triumphant end of one mage's story. In my experience, Mage's metaplot never had the same weight that the metaplot had for the other gamelines: even before the Revised Edition split the fan base with the rise of the Avatar Storm, most Mage fans were more inclined to complain about how those metaplot elements that existed were getting in the way of the stories that they wanted to tell. Rather than conveying a sense of a living, growing world, Mage's metaplot was viewed by most as an inconvenience.

                                                          To me, it's not even a matter of Mage's metaplot following a different trajectory than the other gamelines; it's that its metaplot simply didn't carry the same relevance as the other gamelines' metaplots carried.
