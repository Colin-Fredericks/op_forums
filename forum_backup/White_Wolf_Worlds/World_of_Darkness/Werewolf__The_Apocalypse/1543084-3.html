                    Umbra? Not yet. Storytelling!

                    The book gives assurances that the ST isn't a dictator. That seems like a very 90s assurance.

                    We're introduced to Chronicle Tenets! They get more consideration than Litany Tenets! And depending on which ones your ST chooses, you may be facing more or fewer incidences of Hauglosk, because going against those tenets will cause the accumulation of the Feel Bad Trackers. That seems like it's trying to to seem more open with play options than the book actually sets down.

                    Themes come next. A lot of them are hitting some of the points that have already been driven into the ground, and some of which don't even feel like they fit because a lot of them don't apply in a setting where Garou can just bone among their own and not have to deal with the dance around the former first tenet of the litany. While a lot of the world might be against them, it is, in fact, possible to have a birth family among pack members. Much of the tension there just deflated from where it was down to normal levels. This kind of derails the emphasis on the pack that the other themes try to bang into the ground.

                    Then there's the section entitled "The Real Problem With the Garou". As noted, I can't help but think that at least some of this is spillover targeted at legacy players. It really bangs on the "Garou are really good at violence!" drum, presenting them as ineffectual by other means. Well, I suppose if you cut many of those out, they don't quite do as well, but even then, there are a vast number of Garou that have access to Animal Magnetism to boost general social rolls (not just because it's in a number of common splats, but also because there aren't really many Gifts to get). The spirit world is also treated as a weight around their neck rather than a vital piece of their existence and potential place to find allies and means to deal with problems.

                    Some basic story structuring and game advice, like session 0 and goals, follows. There's a note that the game can get gory, but with this iteration, I'm not completely seeing it, since it's waiting with a watch to take away Crinos... and all of your shapeshifting and other powers, most likely. Much is made about various sources of conflict, which the game notes as having the potential to cause strain with the players if it goes on for too long, but it gives no advice about how to deal with that if you trip over it.

                    Then it comes to the Umbra. The most significant portion of this chapter is the sidebar that notes that animism is a real world belief system, which is broadly true (it tends to be an anthropological waste basket of classification, if I'm not mistaken, where anyone considered to be "pre-modern" gets described as "animistic"). Critically, it doesn't let W:tA lay down its vision of animism as true for the game setting. That's the beginning of the problems, where any proximity to the Umbra that the Garou had was basically scrubbed away for this edition; they're not considered to be natives of it, and the game takes the time to kick you again by having spirits blaming the Garou for everything instead of being ready to help them do something.

                    In "Appeal to the Senses", we get one of the few times that the book tries to get into the heads of beings with wolf senses. Not the wolf-born, though; if that were the case, it would note something important in addition to the phrase "the Garou are no longer *just* human...". There's a slight bit more attention given to spirits, and how their state and activity level should indicate something about the Umbra. I guess if you can't just go check for yourself, that would be more important.

                    There's some call for letting consequences be the concern for actions. That tracks with old stuff regarding bad decisions made by Garou, but it lacks the larger scale that those things can happen on. Antagonists are given an "alignment" of sorts, with the axes of Individual to Systemic and Active to Passive; I probably would have changed the latter with Enemy to Rival, personally, denoting whether something is part of your society and a nominal ally that just doesn't like you or, well, BSDs (don't believe their lies!).

                    The game kind of goes into its own ass about Three Act Structure. As a counterpoint, I'll just post this link. And also this one.

                    XP and rewards, straight out of the 90s!

                    Problems Werewolves Face - if you thought that the book was done kicking you for maybe liking the game or the characters, buckle up. It's going to get bumpy, here.

                    The first point, that Garou are imperfect guardians with an ancient purpose to defend the realm of Gaia, seems innocent enough. That Garou are a bit isolated could be, too, though the descriptions used prompt one to also think of the X-Men - guardians of a world that hates and fears you!

                    Then we hit "The Wyrm is winning because it has adapted", and a lot of things go sideways. It's knocking Garou back to where they were even before the first edition core. Forget W5 being a less good version of Revised, it's also a terrible version of 1e and 2e. Also, I'd like to point out that per the old lore, the Wyrm didn't adapt; it can just easily corrupt and suborn the structures that the Weaver produces. But who knows if we'll ever get lengthy discussion of Triatic Wyrms and the psychological break of a cosmic entity. As usual, I suspect not; I'd say not anytime soon, but Shattered Nation previews don't leave me very hopeful.

                    We get that they're creatures of Rage and spirit, wolf and human, and could have fucking fooled me, book. You talk like them as if they're not part spirit with that whole "foreign to the Umbra, hated by the spirits" crap.

                    There's a somewhat confused and garbled paragraph on the importance of Renown among the "fractured, isolated" Garou and their broken society. Setting aside that Renown is only really possible in a more cohesive setup, it's trying to pump something back up (tale-telling and deeds) after it's been trashing it the whole book.

                    Werewolf society walks a perilous line? Well, yes, I suppose if you delete or ignore the half of the Litany that stabilizes it, it would. This deserves a wanking gesture. (Don't worry, it's just a Berserk '97 outtake.)

                    The Werewolf instinct is to Rage and destroy - yet another point trying to hammer you down about Garou frequently harming those around them when they try to do something, and a cry to not do anything, because doing something would be bad. You monster. I think this book needs to take some advice from the X-Men '97 cartoon (or real life) about the continual and constant effects of outrage and callouts. Also, apparently the Glory and Honor tribes kick sand in the face of the Wisdom tribes, that bunch of spirit-nerds.

                    Umbra, next time.

                                When it comes to ST-advice, we gotta bring up this bit from the Frenzy-section:

                                The werewolf cannot themselves use Gifts, however, and must keep acting out frenzy, or the player must yield control of the character to the Storyteller.
                                I couldn't believe my eyes the first time I saw that during the previews. GMs who do this kind of thing are material for rpg-horror-stories-threads. In this day and age where a more collaborative process is favored, it's insane to put something like this in your corebook.

                                And of course, a player could potentially become frustrated with Frenzy in this game. If your PC has entered Frenzy and then after killing the cause for the Frenzy fails the WP-test, Frenzy can turn into this lengthy scene where you have to play out how this PC is gonna try killing everything in sight.

                                As for the actual Storytelling-chapter... It's no wonder that once you get past all the complaints about how bad Garou are, what is actually left? Playing traditionalist Garou. Caring abour Renown, fighting the Wyrm, protecting caerns... And that's why it doesn't work: All these complaints are about WtA but not addressed within W5. The only thing W5 has going for it as a big change to how it plays is this giant heap of punitive mechanics. Being a Garou is less fun than in previous editions. Alone the fact that W5 is a game with a doom-spiral in the form of Harano/Hauglosk, an inescapable downward spiral that will render your character "unplayable" according to the game. I think this is the only WOD-game that is this extreme. You can't lose dots in Harano/Hauglosk, you can only ever accrue more of them as your character gets closer and closer to "death". But then at the same time, as I mentioned at the start here: You're still just doing the same shit. The only way I can make sense of this is that this edition was written with the idea of "Garou suck and we're gonna make sure you get the message.".

                                One particular mechanic in how it's presented also doesn't work: Chronicle Tenets. I don't think there's enough emphasis in the description that Chronicle Tenets are linked to Hauglosk. Chronicle Tenets aren't just this general "preference for genre emulation or dramatic irony, elevate a moral direction, or plant a flag for personal taste or real-life player concerns" as the book puts it.

                                Take Apocalypse Radicals:
                                • Do not cling to the old ways
                                • Never kill an outsider
                                • Vengeance must be served​
                                If you were to take these Tenets, the game would be saying that the moment these PCs do something that would be considered more traditional, they would be going down the path of Hauglosk. How does that make sense in the way Hauglosk is described? The other problem is when you have these Tenets that overlap. "Never kill and outsider" and "Vengeance must be served" might well apply to a scenario at the same time. And what do you do then? The mechanic is "Here's a line in the sand and if you cross it, do a Hauglosk-check.". Narratively you can play a lot with conflicting PC values and principles within a lot of games, of course, but this particular system becomes muddled and unworkable when you do that here. And an even worse example of exactly that is the suggested example in the written out description:

                                Rural Gothic:
                                • Punish the guilty​
                                • Everyone deserves a second chance
                                • Never harm the innocent
                                When things get muddled like this, it all comes back once again to ST fiat. And that's another thing that comes up time and time again in the W5 corebook: This blind trust that the ST will simply work it all out in the end.

                                        Now, for the sum of all fears, the Umbra and Spirits chapter.

                                        Right from the start, it keeps calling it the Spirit Wilds and mentions that it's the lens that they see it as. So, some fool has attempted to make this game work like Mage, complete with the vidare concept. Thanks, I hate it!

                                        Much like Mage, this section is agonizingly non-committal about what it's talking about. The Umbra could be this, or it could be that; Gaia could be the spirit of the Earth, or could be larger than that; the Triat could be a metaphysical trinity, or it could be the Triat that we're more familiar with. From listening to Mage the Podcast, I've learned that this is something that's all over Mage books about cosmology; the hosts even noted that Werewolf books were refreshingly direct about their details. So, we're falling and falling further from what makes Werewolf Werewolf, here.

                                        Treating Garou as more like Mage also extends to the forced introduction of its attempts to expel or kill them, and this is also noted as a thing about the supposed era of the Apocalypse. How it's framed, however, also makes it sound like it was meant to be a change in a continuation rather than from a reboot. It exists right down to the claim of there being no universal truths. Great. Now we have to play in the Mage Umbra.

                                        This is so up in the air that I'm not even sure that reading these pages is productive in any sense of the word. Whatever you find is now up to heavy interpretation, which I don't really feel makes it matter at all. There's also this tidbit.

                                        The Umbra cannot be mapped...
                                        (Looks over to the Corax book and its "Umbral Maps" Background)

                                        Sure, Jan.

                                        This "it depends..." attitude gets repetitive in a hurry, and ends everything with a ritual statement of non-commitment like it's MatPat talking about FNAF. Some details that were present in prior editions exist in the margins of its wild flailing to avoid seeming biased to anything at all. There's a contrasting comparison of how a building might appear in the Umbra (or not) that could fit in fairly well with said older material.

                                        Getting into the Umbra is still called Stepping Sideways. How this happens is also made to be up to interpretation, with a very Mage-like explanation of one possible reason why going in as a pack is easier being "more will causes reality to yield!" The rules for what happens inside are described by a text box as "impressionistic", which seems like they're just trying to cover up for how much material this book doesn't have in it and how they want to not answer any questions like in a Mage book. Also, instead of having spiritual energies within, the text describes surviving in the Umbra as a matter of being sustained by will. Thought and emotion casting a metaphysical shadow that is the Umbra are an assumption that is mostly default; forget about this being the soul of the world that exists beyond you but containing you, it's all about the impressions of living beings.

                                        The Hierarchy of Spirits is about the same... except that spirits might apparently understand it differently, somehow. Again, hilariously noncommittal. Gaia is named primarily as a Celestine, so the game is probably just copying a lot of 1e with way too much Mage 1e thrown in. Some spirits are noted as communicating in weird ways, and the game is determined not to let Theurges know how, because it took out Spirit Speech.

                                        And that's the end. No discussion of Near Realms, the Deep Umbra, or Zones, or hint that they'll be a Thing. Simply put, this half-complete chapter doesn't belong in a Werewolf book, and insisting that this is the way that spirit matters now work means that they're probably going to give that part of the game over to Mage above everyone else. Assuming that ever gets released, anyway. I could kind of see this coming ten years ago with the "One World of Darkness" BS that got played down until it became the overriding mandate, but didn't quite factor in how much the group that PDX gave the WoD was going to single out W:tA as what it was going to cut out to make everything work.
