                Still baffled by Justin's design-philosophy... I get what he's trying to do. I always got the sense what he wants to do is some sort of story-first game with a blank-space setting and the game is mainly setting up a framework for people at the table to create the setting through collaborative storytelling. The problem is that at the time he signed up to WOD5E V5 had already set up a different paradigm for what WOD5E looks like. Not to mention the expectations that are summoned by invoking all the old WOD-lines like HtR and WtA. If it were all up to him, he would've run in the opposite direction as quickly as possible... away from the lore, the mechanics, the legacy... all of it. But alas, the situation with WOD5E is quite different and so we got H5 (with its hard anti-hunter-org-stance while abandoning the original premise of HtR) and W5 (with its hard aversion to "lore" of any kind to the point that it doesn't even want to touch Garou-history with a ten-foot-pole).

                I guess, maybe it wasn't surprising he ended up quitting prematurely.

                    The design philosophy works fine when you're making a new gameline (such as when White Wolf rolled out CofD). It doesn't work nearly as well when you take an existing gameline with decades of lore that fans and players have been following and you throw it all in the trash. Wizards of the Coast attempted to do this with 4th edition D&D, throwing much of the lore of the Forgotten Realms in the trash because they felt it was too difficult for new players to get into the setting. They ended up losing many of the old players who were invested in the setting but did not gain the number of new players to balance out the loss. During that time period D&D was dethroned as the best selling RPG on the market, and when 5th edition rolled around they immediately began reverting most of those lore changes and started bringing back the material that the long-time players enjoyed.

                    I suspect you'll see something similar with W5, where it will be considered less successful than W20, especially as word of mouth gets around to fans about the significant retcons and changes they've made to the setting the lore - that this is so different it's less of a new edition building on what existed and more of a game taking pieces of the lore but being set in a parallel universe. Rather than being V5, which is a direct continuation to the gameline, W5 is a bit closer in many respects to H5 - it's a very different game that just uses some of the old ideas and names to pull in fans of the previous work but it isn't meant to be a continuation of the old game.

                                        Originally posted by Eric Ericson View Post

                                        It feels backwards to me too. It's like the idea is that it's easier for STs, especially new STs, to create a deep and enticing setting filled with lore, than it is to simply ignore the parts of the setting or lore that you don't like. As an ST I've never had any issue with ignoring parts of the setting I don't care for and replacing pieces of it with my own. But coming up with all (or most) of the lore and the setting for a gameline is incredibly daunting. That's why I prefer games with already existent settings and lore that I can modify than games where you get the mechanics but little to no useable lore.

                                        Ultimately I believe it's better for games to include a section that says, "Your game, your rules. Everything in here are ideas and tools for you to use, but you can, and should, ignore, change or modify any part of the game, settings or mechanics that you don't like. You have our permission and any players who are unhappy with that should find a new ST." I think that's better than dropping the burden of the creative process into the lap of the ST, especially new STs who are still trying to get a handle on the game.

                                                I don't really understand how he even can claim that this was his design-philosophy and that he saw it through with this game. But since it's still a game called WtA, he instead got saddled with "The Litany". And as a setting-element it's probably way too detailed for his tastes, so what gets focused on instead how it can be circumvented/ignored in the game in order to not "take control away from the people at the table" or that's how it seems he looks at something like that.

                                                And despite the disclaimer this book still has a lot of "continuation" in its DNA but instead of trying to get away from that by coming up with new stuff, the book is just constantly trying to undermine all the legacy-stuff that is still very present in the book.
