        An Aside: The Founding Five

        While doing all of this, I've been going back and reading through a lot of the older sourcebooks for assorted tidbits, and one of the things I came across that I'd long forgotten about was PENTEX's origins as described in the original Book of the Wyrm. To wit, in 1913, the Wyrm spirit/entity that worked through Premium Oil was apparently approached/confronted by four other Wyrm entities that also worked through businesses and forced into a partnership with them. Hence, the name PENTEX, as there were five founding companies. So this led me to wondering who, besides Premium (now Endron), were the other four founding companies?

        Good House is listed as PENTEX's "second oldest" company, so it might be one of them. I'm just not sure how they'd be connected to a major Wyrm entity.

        I'm guessing that Harold & Harold Mining is probably one of them, just because the mining industry (especially the early coal mining companies, several of which apparently also worked in steel and railroads) was huge throughout the later half of the 19th century and much of the 20th. The Lehigh Coal and Navigation Company, for example, went back as far as 1792, withTennessee Coal Iron and Railroad formed in 1852, St. Joseph Lead Company in 1864, Union Pacific Coal Company in 1874, and Homestake Mining in 1877. Besides which, this would dovetail nicely into my earlier mention that the company may be built around the cult of some long buried Wyrm "god" similar to Tak from Stephen King's Desperation or the red lirium in Dragon Age.

        Young & Smith are, I presume, meant to be Procter & Gamble, who were founded in 1837, and Johnson & Johnson, founded in 1887. Because I find the urban legend about P&G's logo being a Satanic symbol to be incredibly funny, I presume that Young & Smith have a similar logo that's full of all sorts of obscure Wyrm signs, and if they were founded by some sort of cult of Wyrm-witches, they might also be a good candidate for a founder.

        Circinus Brands (the tobacco company) likely goes back to the 19th century as well, as R.J. Reynolds goes back to 1875, Brown & Williamson to 1894, Philip Morris to 1902 and Lorillard went all the way back to 1760. Perhaps they serve Lord Choke, Elemental Maeljin of Smog.

        I'd kind of like to think that a meat packing company was one of the founders, just because it seems so fitting somehow (not least of which because of ties to The Jungle).

        If anyone else has any thoughts on the matter, I'd enjoy hearing them.

                RAINBOW INCORPERATED (Plastics)

                ​I want to say one word to you. Just one word. Are you listening? Plastics. There's a great future in plastics. Think about it. Will you think about it?
                - Mr. McGuire, The Graduate (1967)

                So, plastics are, quite honestly, the corner stone of much of the modern world. And, from an environmental standpoint, kind of suck. They don't biodegrade very well (if at all, depending on circumstances) and depending on the type is often a pain to recycle properly (if at all). And Rainbow makes plastics. Lots n lots of plastics.

                Rainbow never got much mention in detail, just that they make plastics and rubber goods, were apparently founded in the 1950s, and ... that's about it. I'd initially thought their closest real world counterpart would be the likes of DuPont and Dow until I saw where BlackFox had pointed out Rubbermaid. So, Rubbermaid grew out of the 1933 patent for a rubber kitchen dustpan, and in the 50s started working with plastics. And in 1999, were bought out by Newell Brands. In addition to Rubbermaid stuff, Newell also makes/owns Sharpie, PaperMate, Uniball, Liquid Paper, Krazy Glue, Crock-Pot, Coleman, Mr. Coffee, Jostens (the high school yearbook people), Yankee Candle, First Alert smoke detectors, Bicycle Playing Cards, and a lot of other things (including a lot of outdoor and fishing gear).

                If you want a primer on plastics, Modern Marvels has done an episode on the subject (season six, episode six), as well as one on rubber (season 11, episode 22). Book wise, there is Susan Freinkel's Plastic: A Toxic Love Story, Jeffery Meikle's American Plastic: A Cultural History, and Stephen Fenichell's Plastic: The Making of a Synthetic Century. (The last two are annoyingly out of print, but available in ebook form.) There's also a number of "documentaries" about the evils of plastics that one can find on Netflix and other platforms. Several of them talk about the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, which weirdly enough always struck me as a possible PENTEX experiment in trying to create a new landmass that they could claim as their own sovereign nation (which is no less insane than some of their plots).

                Certain plastic polymer molecules are apparently small enough that they can be inhaled and allegedly even pass through the skin, which would make for a potential vector for Fomori infection/creation. I have this image in my head of Rainbow created fomori whose entire bodies have turned into plastic polymers, possibly making them easily mistaken for Drones by Garou who've never seen them before. Perhaps developing powers similar to that of DC's Plastic Man. Maybe call them Plastics (after the clique in Mean Girls), created by rampant consumerism for disposable plastic goods, creating a breed that looks human, in a vapid, superficial and shallow way (like evil giant Barbie dolls).

                As far as the rubber side of things, Stephen Lang's A World History of Rubber is an overview. Joe Jackson's The Thief at the End of the World is a rather interesting historical account of how a British agent smuggled rubber tree seeds out of Brazil in the Victorian Era, which may be useful for period historical games. Likewise Greg Grandon's Fordlandia, about Henry Ford's efforts to establish a rubber plantation the size of Delaware in Brazil back in the 1920s. (Most rubber nowadays is synthetic, to my understanding.)

                If one wants something totally ludicrous for their Garou to fight, see the film Rubber (2010), about a killer tire with psychic powers. (Yes, you read that correctly.)

                Added: An interesting bit of apocalyptic sci-fi is the novel Ill Wind by Kevin J. Anderson and Doug Beason, about an attempt to clean up a massive oil spill using a petroleum eating microbe results in an air born contagion that eats away and dissolves everything made from petroleum, including plastics, gasoline, synthetic fabrics and other things. One might consider this as a potential chronicle plot with a mentally unstable Glasswalker or Kinfolk scientist trying to tear down modern civilization.

                                Avalon Incorporated

                                So, toys.

                                Avalon is one of the companies covered in Subsidiaries: A Guide to PENTEX and, in all honesty, I've always felt it was the weakest chapter of the book. (It comes across as an attempt to try to force fit a Changeling/Mage crossover into a Werewolf book. The example toys in the first edition of Book of the Wyrm are way creepier.) That aside, the toy industry is at times a rather fascinating and outright weird place. Hasbro and Mattel are two of the more famous American companies. A few books on the subject include G. Wayne Miller's Toy Wars: The Epic Struggle Between G.I. Joe, Barbie and the Companies That Make Them, Jerry Oppenheimer's Toy Monster: The Big Bad World of Mattel, Sydney Stern and Ted Schoenhaus's Toyland: The High-Stakes Game of the Toy Industry, Eric Clark's The Real Toy Story: Inside the Ruthless Battle for America's Youngest Consumers, Roger Sweet's Mastering the Universe: He-Man and the Rise and Fall of a Billion Dollar Idea1, Robin Gerber's Barbie and Ruth: The Story of the World's Most Famous Doll and the Woman who Created Her, and Orly Lobel's You Don't Own Me: How Mattel v MGA Entertainment Exposed Barbie's Dark Side.

                                Modern Marvels has done an episode on Toys (season 10, episode 85). Also, the documentary Plastic Galaxy: The Story of Star Wars Toys (2014), and the Netflix series The Toys That Made Us (2017; only 4 episodes, unfortunately). You can also find a number of toy-themed channels on YouTube made by hardcore fans of various lines and franchises. From a more fictional angle, there's the 1992 Robin Williams film Toys, which is not all that good but may offer a few ideas. Also, Small Soldiers (1998) and the "Lisa vs Malibu Stacy" episode of The Simpsons (which is were the clip I posted earlier in the thread came from).

                                When it comes to the idea of toys - especially dolls - coming to life and attacking people, there's a long and often enjoyable history of the subject. The Twilight Zone's "Living Doll" episode (season five, episode six) is a classic. Stephen King did a variation on the theme with his short story "Battleground". And, of course, the Child's Play series. (The second film is especially interesting for Avalon purposes as the opening shows both the manufacturing process for the Good Guy dolls and the company executives in full CYA mode in the wake of the first film.) One suspects that Avalon has long made dolls that talk to children and encourage them to do bad things.

                                Magic kits are a long time favorite of mine (not that I was ever able to get any of the tricks to work right). Harry Blackstone Jr. was the big name when I was a kid. Chris Angel appears to be selling his own kits now. Such things offer the possibility of introducing kids to Wyrm-craft or possible possession of the psychic banes that create Odyssey subjects.

                                Boardgames are a big part of the toy industry, and Tristan Donovan's It's All A Game: The History of Board Games from Monopoly to Settlers of Catan is a good overview of them. Parker Brothers and Milton-Bradley were two of the major companies until they were bought out by Hasbro. I suspect that Avalon likes to make board games that encourages the worst aspects of society, including ones where you win by backstabbing and betraying others. I'm sure they also like ones that encourage rampant consumerism. I grew up in the 80s, and one I remember was called Go For It, with the objective of trying to accumulate as much extravagant trappings of wealth and status as possible (in the form of sports cars, high end electronics, huge houses, and the like).

                                McFarlane Toys, the money printing machine created by former comic artist Todd McFarlane to fund his sports memorabilia collecting obsession, is slightly infamous for it's horror figures. Their stuff is really aimed more at adults, but I suspect that most of Avalon's action figure lines look like the stuff McFarlane makes. (Thanks to Fat Larry for reminding me about them.)

                                I can remember the Collectable Card Game boom of the mid-90s, including stories of people getting mugged for boxes of Magic: The Gathering Cards. One can easily imagine banes feeding on and encouraging such behavior. Likewise using Cute & Fuzzy Cockfighting Seizure Monsters to encourage kids to trap small animals and force them to fight to the death for their amusement

                                I may as well mention Mighty Morphin Power Rangers: The Movie (1995), with Ivan Ooze's mind controlling ooze as a potential angle for the toy slime that seems so popular with kids (it's allure always escaped me, for some reason). Kids play with it and it ends up taking over their parents, Invasion of the Body Snatchers style.

                                And, finally, I've previously mentioned my love for Grant Morrison's short bit "New Toys" from the Vertigo miniseries Weird War Tales DC Comics did nearly 20 years ago.
                                The reveal at the end is that kids toys are being replaced by insectoid like aliens for malevolent purposes. In my own personal WoD, I like to insinuate that Avalon is secretly run by Ramsey Campbell's Insects from Shaggai.


                                ADDED: I'd nearly forgotten about one of my favorite action figure parodies, The Kronies, teaching kids the importance of all the crony corporate pseudo-capitalism that big-business/big-government collusion can engineer.

                                Also, I added a bit about the 1976 remake of King Kong under the original Endron post.

                                And let's all have a moment of silence for the passing of Toys R Us.


                                (Hope to have a bit about the meat packing industry done for the weekend. Then probably video games/computers, cigarettes, and Project: Odyssey in some order.)

                                            The Meat Industry

                                            A rather large business that I'm honestly a little surprised was never mentioned anywhere in 1st, 2nd or revised edition. The main part of the industry is meat packing. This is the slaughterhouses, processing/butchery and packaging. Besides meat, it also produces a lot of byproducts such as hides, fats and the like. Tyson Foods, most famous for chicken processing, is actually also one of the top US beef and pork packers as well, producing such major brands as Jimmy Dean, Ball Park, Hillshire Farms and others. They've known for a pretty shoddy environmental record, as well as price fixing and other issues.

                                            Modern Marvels (again) has a number of episodes on the subject, including Cattle Ranches (8-22), The Butcher (12-6), Leather (13-9), The Pig (14-40), Cold Cuts (14-43), and The Turkey (15-35). At least one of these (Cattle Ranches, IIRC) shows a device designed to strip the hide off a cattle carcass in the blink of an eye, which made me imagine it being used as a torture device on captured Garou.

                                            Obviously, the classic work on the meat packing industry as it was 100 years ago is Upton Sinclair's The Jungle. It's a work of fiction, but the portrayal of the industry's conditions did raise enough of a public outcry to institute meat and food inspections (something the major meat packing companies were all in favor of, as it was far easier for them to adapt to the new standards and it helped drive small companies out of business). Christopher Leonard's The Meat Racket is a look at the modern business, including the fact that now four companies (Tysons being one of them) produce almost all of the meat sold in the US, while Ted Genoways's The Chain focuses on the pork portion of the industry and Maryn McKenna's Big Chicken concentrates on the use of antibiotics in chicken farming. David Kirby's Animal Factory focuses on animal agriculture as a whole, including dairy, and it's environmental impact. Also interesting is Maurine Ogle's In Meat We Trust, a look at the history of meat eating in the United States. The film Temple Grandin (2010) is an interesting look at the real life woman who helped revolutionize certain aspects of the industry.

                                            On the fictional side of things, Roland Dahl's short story "Pig" is a good one. And, as mentioned under O'Tolley's, Soylent Green (1973) can provide some ideas involving the idea of industrialized cannibalism. If you have an interest in your pack going up against a herd of man-eating sheep, see Black Sheep (2006), which I'm sure could be adapted to cows, chickens, goats, and other farm animals. And for growth-hormones/antibiotics got out of control, see Food of the Gods (1976), which is loosely based on an H.G. Welles story of the same name. And volume 5 of Garth Ennis and Steve Dillon's series Preacher introduces the rather off-kilter meat plant owner Odin Quincannon, while season one of the tv series presents a different take on him. (One interesting bit from the tv version is that the company collects cow manure as a means of generating methane to provide power for the town. Also, Quincannon's mention of "The God of Meat", which made me think that if PENTEX had a meat packing company1 and it was one of the five founders, then said God of Meat would be an interesting Wyrm entity for it to serve.)
                                            I've never seen the French movie Raw (2016), but the premise sounds pretty interesting as a potential side effect of eating PENTEX produced meat.

                                                ​CIRCINUS BRANDS (Tobacco)

                                                ​"No matter how much money the anti-smoking groups spend, the cigarette companies are there to fill out children's heads with lies and propaganda that make them want to smoke!"
                                                "Yeah! It - it's like the tobacco companies have control of my mind! No! Must fight it. Tobacco companies making me want to smoke! Ah!"
                                                - South Park, "Butt Out"

                                                So, cigarettes are bad. And the companies that make cigarettes have a not-unfairly gained reputation for being pretty scummy. And probably about ever bad thing you've ever heard about cigarette or other tobacco companies applies to Circinus.

                                                Modern Marvels once again has an episode on the subject, Tobacco (13-51), which includes a description of most of the chemical additives put into cigarettes.

                                                In the realm of non-fiction books, Iain Gately's Tobacco: A Cultural History of How an Exotic Plant Seduced Civilization is a pretty comprehensive coverage of the subject. Also Chris Harrald and Fletcher Watkins's The Cigarette Book: The History and Culture of Smoking offers some interesting socio-historical details.
                                                As for the industry itself, there's Tara Parker-Pope's Cigarettes: Anatomy of an Industry from Seed to Smoke. Also Allan Brandt's The Cigarette Century: The Rise, Fall and Deadly Persistence of the Product that Defined America and Richard Kluger's Ashes to Ashes: America's Hundred Year Cigarette War, the Public Heath, and the Unabashed Triumph of Philip Morris.
                                                For those who are really really into factual data, there's The Cigarette Papers, the collection of internal tobacco company memos and documents that revealed most of their practices.

                                                Fictionally, there's Christopher Buckley's Thank You For Smoking, a satire of tobacco industry lobbying. It was made into a movie in 2006, though with some of the bite taken out in places.

                                                The Insider (1999) is based on the real life story of whistle blower Jeffery Wigand and his relationship with 60 Minutes as outlined in Marie Brenner's Vanity Fair article "The Man Who Knew Too Much". It's one of those films that, if you have any interest at all PENTEX, is well worth watching.

                                                From a Wyrm perspective, the first two editions of Book of the Wyrm mention Lord Choke, the Elemental Wyrm of Smog and tainted air, as being connected to cigarettes and smoking. He/it is likely the driving spiritual force behind Circinus, and banes that serve him probably infest their products and turn heavy smokers into fomori (Wheezers?). The Cigarette Smoking Man from The X-Files might act as a possible template for such a breed.

                                                Finally, I'll toss in a mention of Stephen King's short story "Quitters Inc.", a rather amusing bit about an organization that helps people break their bad habits, including smoking. It was adapted into one of the parts of the movie Cat's Eye (1985).

                                                ADDED: As Tazmaniacs mentions below, Robert Proctor's Golden Holocaust: Origins of the Cigarette Catastrophe and the Case for Abolition. (My own libertarian leaning nature favors informed consumer choices over bans, but the more information the better when making decisions.) Also, Philip J. Hilts's Smokescreen: The Truth Behind the Tobacco Industry Cover-Up, David Kessler's A Question of Intent: A Great American Battle with a Deadly Industry (about how and why the FDA finally decided to start looking into the tobacco industry), and Richard White's Smoke Screens: The Truth About Tobacco. On the less anti-tobacco side, there's Peter Benson's Tobacco Capitalism: Growers, Migrant Workers and the Changing Face of a Global Industry.

                                                More historically, Robert Proctor's The Nazi War on Cancer is a weird intersection of his research on both cancer and on Nazi medical freakishness.

                                                And if you're doing anything with the Beast Courts, The Walter H. Shorenstein Asia-Pacific Research Center's Poisonous Pandas: Chinese Cigarette Manufacturing tin Critical Historical Perspectives (which Robert Proctor helped edit/compile) is a look at the subject of tobacco use and manufacturing in China throughout the Communist era.

                                                (I honestly remain a little surprised that we never had a slate of big Hollywood movies with Evil Tobacco Company Conspiracies as the villain.)

Something very seriously thought upon by the US Atomic Energy Commission & the California department of highways, who published in 1963 a report about blowing up the Bristol Mountains in order to make room for a highway (google "Bristol Mountains" & nuclear). Similar projects were envisioned in other countries (France, USSR, etc.)
                                                    • Nuclear proliferation & Cold War. Today, nuclear proliferation is (mostly, if not exclusively) a "rogue state" issue. Before, it used to be a deliberate, if non-official, strategy. For instance, Israel & South Africa both had help from France, who was encouraged by Washington (which prefers to have a proxy do the work). You might also want to check out the Eurodif WP page (a franco-iranian project which blew up after 1979, some say literally, alleging that several 80's bombings in Paris were a sort of blackmail of France by Iran to get its money back... Conspiracy theory or not, definitely real stuff in WoD).
                                                    Wood-pulp & paper mills: check out https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urugua...p_mill_dispute. Paper industry can be very polluting... Sidney Lumet's Fail-Safe (kind of like Kubrick's Dr. Strangelove, but less comedy-style) would also a good picture on the theme "the Weaver is scarier than the Wyrm" or "Weaver & Wyrm alliance"...

                                                            SUNBURST ENTERPRISES INTERNATIONAL (Computers)

                                                            Wait, you may ask, why is he doing a defunct company that's been bought out by another? [At least that's the way it was in Subsidiaries; I've not read any WW20 material, so I've no idea if that has changed/been retconned/evolved in some way.] Well, much as with Atlas and Endron, the computer industry and the video game industry are two separate animals, and because there's such a massive amount of stuff involving all things PENTEX computer related, it's just easier for me to organize it this way. And by "this way", I mean quasi-chronologically, first with the development of computers (Sunburst), then of the video game industry (Tellus), and then of the Internet and related things (ICS).

                                                            The two major names in the world of computers has always been I.B.M. and Apple. Also of interest, historically, are Commodore, Tandy (ie Radio Shack), and Texas Instruments. IBM always heavily focused on business machines for large companies, while Apple, Commodore and Tandy were the ones who brought the personal computer idea into homes starting in the late 70s. While Sunburst is/was probably more like the latter three, there is some interesting weirdness and skeletons at IBM that are worth looking at for inspiration also. And, of course, Microsoft is constantly lurking in the background.

                                                            So, once again, I bring up Modern Marvels, as it has, not one, but three episodes on computers (4:5 Thinking Machines: The Creation of the Computer; 8:11 Computers; and 11:76 The Computer).

                                                            Historically, Michael Swain and Paul Frielberger's Fire in the Valley: The Making of the Personal Computer is a good recounting of the subject, including the roles of Steve Jobs and Bill Gates in the process. It was adapted into a film version, Pirates of Silicon Valley (1999), which makes for an interesting "how he was seen then vs how he is seen now" comparison with the 2015 film based on Walter Isaacson's Steve Jobs biography.
                                                            For another perspective, see Stephen J. Marshall's The Story of the Computer: A Technical and Business History. It's a good overview of both how computers evolved into what we know now and of the business side of things.

                                                            For IBM, see Emerson J. Pegh's Building IBM and Robert X. Cringely's The Decline and Fall of IBM. Then there's Edwin Black's IBM and the Holocaust, looking at one of the company's less than stunning periods. (Black's done a number of works on Nazi/Holocaust related subjects, including Nazi Nexus, about how the work of various progressive technocratic oriented companies and foundations in the 20s and 30s helped set up some of the underpinnings of the Third Reich. He also writes about oil/petroleum policies, and his book Internal Combustion is probably useful Endron reading.)

                                                            For Apple, there is Jim Carlton's Apple, Owen Linzmayer's Apple Confidential 2.0, Adam Lashinsky's Inside Apple, and Michael Malone's Infinite Loop.

                                                            For those interested in Apple's landmark Apple II computer, there's Stephen Weyhrich's Sophistication & Simplicity: The Life and Times of the Apple II Computer and David L. Craddock's Breakout: How the Apple II Launched the PC Gaming Revolution.

                                                            On the other side of the aisle, there's Jamie Lendino's Breakout: How Atari's 8-Bit Computers Defined a Generation and Boisy G. Petri and Bill Loguidice's CoCo: The Colorful History of Tandy's Underdog Computer.

                                                            And, for Commodore, Brian Bagnell's On The Edge: The Spectacular Rise and Fall of Commodore and Commodore: The Amiga Years, Jimmy Maher's The Future Was Here: The Commodore Amiga, and Robert Mason's A Commodore 64 Walkabout. There's also a recent (2017) documentary, 8-Bit Generation: The Commodore Wars, which I have never seen, but which gets good word of mouth.

                                                            From a fictional standpoint, it's only recently that computer companies seem to be "sexy" enough for Hollywood. But, things like the movie TRON (1982) and Michael Crichton's Disclosure offer some interesting period ideas, as does the tv series Halt and Catch Fire (2014-2017).

                                                            I strongly suspect that, during the 90s, Sunburst's products were designed to intentionally provoke computer rage as much as possible, perhaps in the hope of provoking workplace or domestic violence, and/or to attract Bitter Rages (or some similar Bane) to create some sort of Fomori with perpetual anger management issues.
