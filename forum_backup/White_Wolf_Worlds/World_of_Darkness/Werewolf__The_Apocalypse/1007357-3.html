    (Decided to make this my first post here: why not, right?)

    I think Werewolf is one of those games where what's useful as inspiration depends largely on your chronicle: a story about a trip to the Arcadia Gateway is very different to a story about Bane-ridden child soldiers, and both of them are perfectly acceptable stories to tell. That being said, there's some good general inspiration out there: Princess Mononoke is the quintessential WTA film imo, bar none; but consider also film/TV like Wolf's Rain, Dog Soldiers, The Howling, American Horror Story, Stranger Things, The Twilight Zone, The X-Files, etc. Children's films can be surprisingly useful: I am thinking, in particular, of Song of the Sea, which is great for all your Fianna aesthetic needs.

    For literature I suggest reading a lot of traditional Gothic tales more than straight-up horror: Lovecraft of course, as long as you understand where the pervasive xenophobic terror in it comes from (making a deliberate subversion like the recent Lovecraft Country perhaps more useful than the parent text these days); early ghost stories like those of M.R. James; classic works of Southern Gothic like We Have Always Lived in the Castle or The Ballad of the Sad Cafe; magical realism like Allende, Borges, Calvino, Carter (The Company of Wolves, anyone?), Diaz, Marquez, Rushdie. I still maintain that American Gods is very much a Werewolf-y work. A couple of recent collections that walk the line between American Gothic and magical realism spring to mind: Falling in Love with Hominids and North American Lake Monsters, the latter of which includes a good gory werewolf tale.

    Music is probably the most subjective of all, but I often have stuff playing like Nick Cave, Timber Timbre, Tom Waits, Mount Eerie (esp. 'Between Two Mysteries' and 'Sighing'), The Handsome Family, etc. while I prep. The Handsome Family is particularly good for inspiration: check out tracks like 'The White Dog', 'Birds You Cannot See' (listen to that one and tell me it's not about your friendly local urban spirits), 'Down in the Ground', 'Whitehaven', 'Sleepy', 'The Forgotten Lake'. Lots of good Umbral stuff in there besides the general aesthetic creepiness. Dead Can Dance is also fantastic; I have particular praise for their album Spiritchaser, particularly 'Song of the Stars', which is 100% a song for the Umbra/spirits.

    I also have a lot of chronicle-specific stuff. If you play in a Rage Across Australia chronicle on Friday nights, look away, you pests:

    Australia makes for a very interesting chronicle setting; I started using it simply because one should always stick, at first, with what one knows, but it has a unique...sense of decay that also extends to Australian film and literature. Some extremely influential pieces upon my own chronicle design include Wolf Creek, Rabbit-Proof Fence, Picnic at Hanging Rock, The World Beneath, For the Term of His Natural Life, The Chant of Jimmie Blacksmith, Gould's Book of Fish, The Hunter, The Proposition (an Australian Western written by that master of Australian Gothic, Nick Cave of Bad Seeds fame; I cannot recommend it enough), and non-fiction like The Fatal Shore, The Future Eaters, A Secret Country, and Songlines, all for understanding Australia's tumultuous relationship with its own past and indigenous inhabitants. The children's fiction I grew up with, like The Nargun and the Stars and The Silver Brumby, are also good for providing a sense of place.

    A special shout-out has to be given here to The Hunter, for being one of the most WTA-ish books I have read in a long time: it concerns a man employed by a shady biotechnology company, who is sent to Tasmania to track down--and kill--the last surviving thylacine so that aforementioned shady company might use its blood to craft a bioweapon (I straight-up stole this idea for my own chronicle; players have not noticed). The thylacine, in this case, is not just a thylacine but also a metaphor for environmental destruction and the innocent past of the country; the main character stands for its present state of moral decay. Good stuff. There is also a film; I recommend checking it out.

    Well! That got long. I look forward to making many more equally long posts in future.

            One thing I meant to add up there is the surprising utility of folk songs! A lot of traditional folk, esp.                     Much of Robert E. Howard's body of work, as his stories (and poems) involve underlying themes of slowly but inevitably dying cultures and straight-forward violence vs 'civilized' corruption and dark magic. He was also friends with H. P. Lovecraft, so certain aspects of the Mythos creeps in from time to time. His Bran Mac Morn stories were very clearly a huge influence on the development of the White Howlers/Black Spiral Dancers, especially "Worms of the Earth".

                    As for the Fianna, Sharyn McCrumb's Ballad Series of novels, which draw heavily on the Celtic/Scots-Irish culture, folklore and music of the Appalachia region.
