                                I was planning on giving W5 a hard pass, but I had a friend purchase a copy for me for Christmas ("I saw they had a new edition of Werewolf and I remember you used to love the game. Merry Christmas!") I didn't have the heart to tell him I was planning to skip it. So I've been reading through it and I feel like W5 works pretty well for what it is - the game is intended to be a street level, short duration campaign. Unlike Vampire or even Hunter, I feel that the W5 play experience is intended to be wrapped up within a half-dozen sessions or so. It's not intended for chronicles to last years, the way Legacy editions or other WW games did.

                                When you look at the mechanics you have the Harano/Haglusk mechanic. This mechanic slowly builds up until a character turns into an NPC. That sort of system is anathema to any long-running game. It's not uncommon in games for PCs to turn into NPCs for a short period of time (maybe a scene or so) but permanently? That sucks. So avoiding building up points on those tracks is imperative if you're looking at still playing this character in a year or two. On the other hand, the mechanic comes with a system where you can fill up the slots to get a boost in power, and that only makes sense if the assumption is that the game won't go on for too long. Who cares if your character is going to turn into an NPC if the chronicle is likely going to be over before that happens.

                                That also dovetails with the lore. A lot of the lore is left as a mystery box. Nobody really knows what's happened in the ancient past, or even the more recent past. That's shitty worldbuilding, but it's fine for the intended play experience. Are the Silent Striders cursed? Who cursed them? How did it happen? Well it doesn't matter, it's irrelevant. Your PCs are going to be part of a street level, short duration chronicle. They aren't going to be instigating or solving the Silent Strider curse. That's well outside of the intended play experience. Did the War of Rage happen? It doesn't really matter. Your players aren't going to be fixing that issue or reconciling the Werewolves and other shifters. Maybe, if an ST wants to run with that, your group of PCs could make friends with some other shifters, but it's not going to be doing anything past that heal the past between Werewolves and Shifters.

                                Finally we get to the intended playloop, which is fairly small scale and repetitive. The game isn't really designed for players to pick up, and become hobo/crusaders travelling around fighting Pentex and trying to save the world (in fact the group that does do that is the bad guys). Your players are intended to hang out in one place, protecting their touchstones and dealing with local problems. Some issue rears its head, the PCs smash it, and rinse and repeat. There's only so many times you can do this before things start to feel a bit stale, which is about when you'd start thinking of ending the game, probably by having the PCs launch an assault against the big bad guy who's been pulling the strings.

                                This idea of localized gameplay is something that's part of the VtM play experience, but the difference is that faction politics are a big part of VtM, whether you're playing Anarchs of Camarilla, the idea is that you'll be spending a fair amount of the game focused on the local politics of the place you live. Not so with in W5. The game doesn't feel like it's intended that PCs are part of a large sept with a couple of dozen other members for PCs to engage in politics with. Rather it feels like they're intended to largely be on their own, which limits the kind of options that STs might have in their toolbox. Again though, if the game isn't intended to last beyond a half-dozen or sessions, then it's not a huge problem.

                                So overall, I think for what W5 intends to be - a fairly short duration game where players play as street level characters with a basic understanding of the world who smash stuff up when it causes problems in their territory, it's actually pretty good. In that regard, I don't really have any problem with it. On the other hand, if you try to run W5 the same way you would run earlier editions of Werewolf, you're quickly going to run into a bunch of walls, whether from mechanics or the setting. At the very least your players are going to be sweating the Harano/Haglusk mechanic because they'll likely be worried about having to hand their character sheet over.

                                                As much as I'd enjoy a 'there but by the grace of Gaia I go' faction from the CoF, the way they are set up and how other tribes are set up prevents that.

                                                To begin with, Hauglosk is a one way street. You can shift your dots to Harano, but they can never be fully removed. Once you hit Hauglosk 5, your character is unplayable.

                                                CoF are stated to all be under Hauglosk and the tribe is defined by it.

                                                You can redeem a BSD, but the CoF loresheet implies the character just tipped their toe into the cult and even that nearly tainted them beyond redemption.

                                                Even though CoF are meant to be the ones taking things too far, the writing doesn't actually support it. Instead of 'they do what we do but take things too far', the message in the core is more along the lines of 'don't do what they do.' Which then creates the passive image of the playable garou.

                                                And in Deepest Wounds, the CoF are basically just written as BSDs, robbing the tribe of their own unique flavour.

                                                In addition, as much as Hauglosk and therefore CoF are stated to be examples of extremism, they are not.

                                                For one, BSDs aren't suffering from Hauglosk OR Harano. They do what they do with no mechanical mental health issues. If BSDs aren't used as examples of being maddened into extremism, then Hauglosk can't just be extremism.

                                                What makes this clearer is that Red Talon example characters include the following; A maneater, someone who intentionally infects towns with deadly diseases and someone who wants to put all of humanity into a cage and is doing so by being a bounty hunter.

                                                If neither BSDs nor Red Talons who kill whole towns are not suffering from Hauglosk, despite taking more extreme measures than the CoF in the core, then what makes the CoF bad is not being extreme.

                                                The only thing I can think of, is that they are a Nordic tribe and that is used as a shorthand for fascism.
