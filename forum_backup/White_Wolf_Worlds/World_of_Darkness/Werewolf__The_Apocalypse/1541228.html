    W5 Shattered Nation - Review

    08-30-2024, 11:23 AM
    There’s a tagline on the product-page for Shattered Nation. It says “The society of Garou unveiled.”. In that sense it seems quite obvious what the book is trying to do. But what the book is actually doing is somewhat more complicated.

    Three strands show up in this effort to “unveil Garou society”. The first is to revise or expand information given by the W5 corebook. The most pronounced example of this is Chapter Five: Speaking With The Enemy. Rather than simply introducing new allies and antagonists or offer new story-hooks for existing ones, this chapter re-examines ALL the categories of beings in the corebook’s “Allies and Antagonists”-chapter and gives different or more nuanced takes on all these categories. The second strand that stands out is that this book is liberally borrowing from legacy-material. Sept-positions, howls, Rites that are new to W5, references to things like the Under The Blood Red Moon book, the Storm Eater, the Amazon war and of course a lot of stuff about Jonas Albrecht as an older (and broken) version of legacy Jonas Albrecht… It’s all legacy-material. The third strand is that the book’s proposed concept of a “fallen Garou Nation” can be summarized like this: “It’s a wolf-eats-wolf-world and it’s every Sept for themselves.”

    This is what is going on and there’s good stuff in this book. But before I get to a beat-by-beat review of this book, to finish this introductory summary there are three glaring issues that plague this book. The first comes with that that third strand I mentioned, the “every Sept for themselves”-aspect. So you get talk of how a PC relates to their Pack, how the Pack relates to the Sept, how Septs relate to each other and how Spirits figure into this. But there’s something big missing from this: The Tribal perspective. Hell, you get more of a perspective on Black Spiral Dancers, the Cult of Fenris and Stargazers in the aforementioned Chapter Five than on any of the other Tribes. Garou are mentioned and the Tribes they belong to always feel more incidental than anything. And other than that, it’s just this obvious stuff like Children of Gaia Garou being community-minded and longing for peace, Silent Striders being fans of travel and Silver Fangs being arrogant. But you never get any dedicated sections exploring how Tribes figure into this fallen-Garou-Nation-setting. The second issue is that while this book introduces all these references to legacy-material, it can’t completely leave the ahistorical “Who knows?”-mystification behind. So you get a reference like the Storm Eater and it’s this half-hearted summary because it has to come with an asterisk saying “Who knows what really happened?”. But if you so obviously borrow from legacy-material and you only present like 70% of the story then any person is just one google-search away from just looking this up on the White Wolf Wiki and getting the whole story anyway. So why even bother with this whole charade at that point? Since you’re 70% of the way to a whole copy-paste-affair already, why not go the whole nine yards? The third and last big issue with this book is that there’s quite a bit of in-character talk, lengthy quotes, dialogues, speeches… there are a lot of pages dedicated to this. And in theory I welcome that. It’s a great idea and provides an opportunity to give the W5 more flavor. But here’s the thing: The in-character writing in this book is terrible. Most of the characters are either annoying and/or deeply unlikeable. Honestly, I’d probably leave the Garou Nation as well if Garou are like this in W5. For example, old Jonas Albrecht in how he’s presented in this book is this cranky, self-important old man that according to this book, I shit you not, walks with a walking-stick that he beats people with if he feels that they don’t respect him enough. And the writing itself… oh god… there’s a scene where a Pack is summoning an Ancestor-Spirit… That one really tested my patience. It had lines like this (which is supposed to be part of the summoning): “Sweet flesh we hunted. TOCH AROUH. Our life. Humans call it “animism” or some shit. We call it tradition.” And since the Ancestor Spirit is an indigenous person while the Garou are not, phrases like “colonialist guilt” are thrown around. But then these phrases are embedded in these abrasive comments. It just feels tonally off.

    And speaking of bad writing…

    Introduction - Low Growl Speaks…

    The Introduction starts with a bit of in-character-writing. Low Growl is a wolf-born Silent Strider Philodox (she’s later featured in a character-section as well). It’s about this Garou making a speech at a Moot and trying to gather support for a mission. It starts like this:
    Last night, this moot opened with a grand tale of woe. A Galliard speaking with the voice of the most beautiful of songbirds – yes, you there , hi – told us of the final days of the Garou Nation. The Last Grand Convo… Conco…

    (Someone shouts: Concolation!) Yes! That. One of those really big moots where the important issues facing all Garou were decided. Bear with me, okay? I’m trying to talk fancy since this is a moot and you’re all so eloquent.

    (Laughter, not all derisive)

    The Last Concolation was where King Albrecht was supposed to have nominated a successor. Instead, his rule was challenged by the Shadow Lords, the Red Talons, the Galestalkers, even the Hart Wardens – to say nothing of the Septs and Packs clamoring for action. So many young Garou who’d never been to such a moot before eager to hear from their king.

    I wasn’t there and the first I heard of all this was yesterday. You-

    (Low Growl pointing at the Galliard in the audience)

    - Put this in sweeter words. But what I heard was that a bitter old man had nothing to offer those who were not even born in his glory days.

    Why did the Garou Nation fall? Why did that Concolation become the last time we’d come together?

    Don’t ask me! I was born a wolf, so all these stories from the youth of our elders are ancient history to me!
    You got a few key parts of this book right there. You’ve got information about this Last Grand Concolation where it all went south for the Garou Nation (there are other in-character quotes in the book that add more detail to what was going on like more than just the mentioned challenge, this was also the event where the Fenrir left) and most importantly you have the paradoxical relationship this book has with the fallen Garou Nation. The characters in this book and the attitudes represented all clamor for something like the Garou Nation, a state of things where unity, cohesion and cooperation are assured. Where it becomes paradoxical is that at the same time all the characters in this book (who are old enough to remember the Garou Nation) are convinced that the Garou Nation sucked and it was never all that useful.

    The sample of the writing is also an indicator what all in-character-writing is like in this book. The tone’s abrasive, irreverent and vulgar but it doesn’t come with the sort of wit or humor to make this bearable to read. There are some side-characters like Lil Sister who are different but they only feature as characters in dialogues. The main-voices you hear in this book are similar to how Low Growl speaks in this Introduction.

    Chapter One: Strength In The Pack

    This chapter is focused on Pack Types and gives advice on creating Packs and what sorts of complex actions Packs can undertake. Overall, a good chapter that both offers new options to players as well as offers some good advice for session-zero.

    It starts by talking about Pack-dynamics and it does that by offering four key-issues and then presents three options for how a Pack addresses that. First, you have how the Pack treats each other (you get “The Family”, “Team of Rivals” and “The Professionals”). Then you have the Pack’s relationship to the world of Spirits (you got “Cautious”, “Curious” and “Problem Solvers”). A concept that I think should’ve been covered in far more detail by the book is that the next issue is the Pack’s relationship to the Veil (you got “Veil Lowered”, “Veil Parted” and “Veil Lifted”). What this proposes is that in W5 you have approaches ranging from “We honor the Veil and keep things secret.” to “We regularly talk to our human allies and tell them everything.”. The last bit is the Pack’s relationship to the Sept (you got “The Linchpin”, “The Iconoclasts” and “The Outsiders”). All in all, it’s a good outline to go through with players about what kind of Pack they wanna play. Something that comes up quite often in this book, though, is that options are getting discussed here that are so broad and general that it doesn’t feel like it warrants the wordcount that it gets. Likewise here, because I think outside of the Veil-section, it’s pretty much all what you think it is. And yet all these options get these explanations with dozens of words each.

    Next we get to the introduction of the new Pack Type system. It’s good, although there are some issues that I think you’d immediately have noticed if you had playtested this. It’s similar to the Coterie-system by having its own set of attributes. You got Territory, Community and Spirit. Territory isn’t the amount of area you control but rather measures the health of the land. Community is about how closely the Pack is integrated into human society. And Spirit… well, that one’s the weakest one because of how vague it is. It isn’t the Pack’s knowledge of the Umbra and the local spirits but rather a kind of “gut instinct” the spirits have when they encounter the Pack. How or why this happens is left unexplained. You can use these ratings as a once per session dice bonus effect when it’s applicable. So far so good. What I found a bit mystifying is that the book is overly concerned right here about players stacking dice-boni and you get these exceptions for all three for what you can and can’t add as dice-boni at the same time. Like with Spirit, for example, the text is making clear that you can either use your Renown-bonus for a social roll while interacting with a spirit OR the Spirit-dice-bonus from the Pack-Type but not both at once. Honestly, who cares if a player decides to do both at the same time… As long as it makes sense, why not?

    Then it talks about pooled Backgrounds and Flaws which is also the moment where W5 introduces Patron-Spirits for Packs. The idea is that you use Spirit Pact as a pooled Background and at three dots you get a “Guiding Spirit” for the Pact. This is how the book explains it: “At the Storyteller’s discretion, Guiding Spirits at three dots or above may provide the pack with a minor version of a Favor and Ban for as long as the pact exists. The Favor is a one-die bonus to a single kind of test in the spirit’s purview once per session. The Ban is a separate action related to the opposite of the spirit’s purview that requires spending a Willpower point to perform it after invoking the Favor. For example, a flame spirit might grant a onedie bonus to withstanding moving through a burning environment but might require spending a Willpower point to put out any of those fires.” That still leaves a bit of work to the ST to make this work, of course. And I was surprised that the book never acknowledges how this is supposed to interact with the Rite of Patronage (pg 184) from the W5 corebook.

    Where it gets a bit silly is in how it explains the amount of dots you get. First, it says that with two players you get three dots per player, with three players two dots per player and with four and more you get one dot per player. I’m pretty sure there’s a simpler way to present this. Obviously, the standard is six dots and with five and more, you might want to raise it. Then it gets even sillier when it says that you have to put at least one dot in each of the three aforementioned Pack Type attributes (unless you play a nomadic Pack which has 0 in Territory). So, effectively, the actual amount of points you get to spend is three. Then you can take up to four dots in Flaws which gives you four extra dots to spend on Pack-stuff. This is relevant because nearly ALL the listed examples for Pack Types in this book take four dots in flaws. Even worse, in 90% of the cases, it’s four dots in either Enemies or Adversary. Personally, I would feel like something has gone wrong here conceptually when the listed examples make it feel like four dots in Enemies or Adversary is some sort of auto-take for this process.

    The list of listed Pack-Type is quite long with 15 given examples. On one hand, that’s good but when you’re gonna hear what they’re like, you’ll realize that most of them are quite bland conceptually. In my opinion, most players should be expected to come up with broad concepts like these on their own.

    The listed Pack-Types are: Activists (the Pack has attached themselves to a human activist cause), Avengers, Banishers, Caern Guard, Couriers, Diplomats, Evangelists for Luna, Hell Raisers (the Pack has attached themselves to a particular subculture movement), Monkeywrenchers, Occultists, Penitents, Reformers (the Pack wants to rebuild the Garou Nation in a reformed state), Scavengers, Veil Keepers and Watchers (Garou policing other Garou).

    To showcase what these Pack-Type descriptions are like (and show off the only truly interesting one), here’s what Evangelists for Luna is like:


    Evangelists for Luna
    Moon Cults dedicate themselves to the service of Luna, the spirit of the Moon and Gaia’s sister. They venerate her in the same way that other Garou do Gaia, and some even go as far as to declare that doing so is the only natural response if Gaia is indeed dead or dying. While Moon Cults aren’t considered fallen or actual enemies, to say that theirs is a controversial belief among Garou society is an understatement.
    Some septs outright forbid Moon Cults from visiting their caerns, let alone joining them. Still, Moon Cults are on the rise with younger Garou, and rumor has it at least one sept is made up entirely of Moon Cult packs. When the troupe chooses this pack type, every player must decide on three beliefs about Luna that the pack holds. This is their Moon Cult doctrine. This doctrine is not universal; while Moon Cults respect each other, they tend to have their own beliefs about Luna, her new role in the world with Gaia’s decline, and how Garou should best serve her.
    - Territory ••
    - Community •
    - Spirit •••
    - Spirit Pact (•••, a spirit related to the cult’s doctrine, Power 2, Host)
    - Spirit Pact Flaw: Pact Condition (•, adds one dot to the pack pool)
    - Adversaries (••, a rival Moon Cult or an orthodox pack that despises the pack’s doctrine, adds one dot to the pack pool)
    Possible Extras: Allies (Human adherents who don’t know the truth), Mentor, Resources


    It feels a bit like cheating that this book mentions Moon Cults a few times but as you can see here, the details are pretty much up to the people at the table to figure out. Also, if you’re gonna make them pick three Moon Cult doctrines, it should be mentioned that there’s another part of the game that also already is kind of about picking three doctrines: Chronicle Tenets. I think it would be reasonable to just do both at the same time in a situation like this one. The text also highlights the strange thing where the listed Pack Types seem to think that Flaws only give you half their value (rounded up) in extra dots to the Pack-Type-dot-pool which isn’t the case. Lastly, that “Possible Extras”-bit there never felt worthwhile because of how obvious it was. Similar to how a lot of the listed Pack-Types are way too broad to be particularly interesting as an example, these “Possible Extras” are also way too obvious. Most of the listed Pack Types in this book just lack flavor. They should’ve brought back Pack-Types from other supplements that feel more interesting (like the mentioned Pack-Types in Scent of Decay where everybody has the same Auspice, for example).

    Next we get to Pack Actions which starts normal but gets weird fast. It starts with “Organize an Ambush” and you think “Okay, I can see that. You’d do something like that as a Pack.”. Then you get “Reconnaissance” which is already a bit odd because the “Organize an Ambush”-section already talked about how you should scout out your enemy but okay, so it goes into more detail on that aspect. Next Pack Action: “Guard Local Human Environmental Activists”. Suddenly things have gotten VERY specific! Here’s a quote from the section: “They are Earth liberationists, anarcho-primitivists, and green anarchists fighting to stop deforestation, oil drilling, the coming of the end. When the work of human activist groups aligns with the goal of the Garou, it makes sense to protect them, to help them keep fighting. Politics can be a very useful Skill to understand the big picture. Awareness may alert of an incoming attack and Medicine help keep humans alive in its aftermath.” I get the part where when your human allies come under attack, you protect them – but how is that a distinct “Pack Action”? That’s obviously more of a reaction to what the bad guys are doing.

    But it gets crazier! Next up in the list of Pack Actions we have: “Kidnap a local politician”. I had a bit of a double-take reading that. “Kidnap a politician”…? This book thinks one way for Packs to take action is to “kidnap politicians”…? First of all, how is this even supposed to work? I can understand kidnapping somebody close to the politicians to blackmail/pressure/manipulate them. But if you want the politician to do something how is kidnapping them supposed to help? Obviously they can’t do anything for the Garou while they’re kidnapped. It makes sense to kidnap a higher-status person if all you’re after is a ransom. But if you want particular higher-status people to do a particular thing, kidnapping them isn’t going to help a lot (unless you have some supernatural means to make them keep their word). You can make them pinky-swear that they ought to do a particular thing and if I were the ST, the first thing that person would do upon release is call the cops instead. If it’s just for the sake of gaining information, kidnapping is a very drastic move (that also attracts a lot of attention). The writing in this section is particularly cringe-worthy as well (it reminded me of the Ahroun-section in the corebook) with lines like "Kidnapping public figures is risky business, no shit." or passages like "Congratulations. You have a fundamentalist politician strapped to a chair in your aunt’s Airbnb. Now it’s time to expose his corruption, state your demands, negotiate his release, and hope to Luna the vacation rental won’t get booked." (also, again, who are you stating your demands to...? You've already kidnapped the target of such a pressure-campaign...).

    Next we got “Blow It All Up” which is what it says… Again, seems a bit obvious. I don’t think this needed to get dozens of words and its own subsection. After that you have “Claim and secure your territory” which is way too broad to be useful. Then you got “Call a Moot” which wasn’t necessary because the book gets into that in more detail later on. This is followed by “Sabotage the Infrastructure of Entropy” which is just “Blow It All Up” again except this time the text acknowledges that you can do more than just literally blow things up if you want to take down Pentex-supported corporations. Then you got another weird one with “Form an (Unlikely) Alliance” because as I mentioned at the start, this book has an entire chapter later on about this very thing!

    After the ambush-bit and reconnaissance, this section on Pack Actions just kinda went off the rails. In my opinion, whoever wrote this section didn’t understand the assignment and what a section called “Pack Actions” should’ve been about. Besides the first two, everything veered between irrelevant, too broad, too specific and just plain odd here. This wasn’t helpful at all!

    Chapter Two: Among Wolves

    This chapter discusses Garou society and presents some example Garou and Septs. It starts by emphasizing the “every Sept for themselves”-aspect and you get a table with ten examples for why two Septs dislike each other. It’s not that useful because I think most STs could easily figure out why two Garou-Septs dedicated to the War against the Wyrm might’ve grown to dislike each other or run into some issues that aggrieved one or both parties.

    Then we get a section on Generations where the book doubles down on all the “Who knows”-mystification and tries to explain it. It still isn’t convincing in my opinion. The main-thrust of the argument relies on this notion: “The quick succession of generations means that a Garou in their thirties might already be considered a grizzled veteran, someone who survived when so many did not. At a typical sept, only a minority of the Garou make it past ten years as a Garou.” And this then gets cemented by a description of the Garou’s sense of time and history: “The Garou are creatures of the present, their traditions built on oral storytelling. This means that factual accuracy is often not considered particularly important as long as the broad outlines fit what the crowd at the moot wants to hear. The stories of Garou from the '80s and '90s are already ancient history, having happened before most Garou today were even born. Garou legends are notoriously difficult to date accurately. When was the War of Rage or the Impergium? In the Middle Ages? In the Stone Age? Who knows, and the spirits are no help either. Their sense of time is so different that it’s impossible to translate into human reckoning.” It’s the same issue the corebook had. Oral history is undervalued again, the context of modern human society gets ignored etc., etc. It’s still a bad explanation and it still doesn’t make sense. It’s even worse here in trying to double down. Somebody was honestly okay with putting the notion in this book that the 80s and 90s are “ancient history”. Come on!

    There’s a nice little sidebar that offers better options for playing more experienced starting characters which is a good addition. It distinguishes between Cliath, Fostern and Adrath… which are terms borrowed from legacy-material that have never been explained in W5 before (because Ranks were removed). Somebody probably just assumed that these are still established terms in W5…

    Then you get some more setting-information that’s rather bland as well as a list of howls and Sept positions which are copy-pastes from the W20 corebook. It’s not a straight up copy-past but you can clearly see how somebody looked at the W20 corebook and tried to present the same descriptions with different words.

    The Among Humans sections introduces Gaian Cults and honestly, I felt a bit underwhelmed. There’s nothing substantial to this idea. The idea is that these are either humans who work together with Garou that don’t care about the Veil or people who experienced Delirium but interpreted it as a moment of religious revelation. Their brush with the supernatural turns them into believers of some kind. And that’s pretty much it. Like I said, underwhelming.

    Now we get to the characters portion and I can once again talk about how I don’t like most of them. The biggest mistake this chapter has made is to make a human-born Silver Fang Ragabash named Dominique “Death-in-Word” Omar the main-voice in this chapter. That guy is such an asshole!

    Here’s part of the first quote you get from him:
    You know what I find annoying about being Garou? All the sincerity. It’s really the worst. I was at a moot with that Gaia First girl, Aino, you know, the one who’s suddenly become all the rage. I fail to see why. She kept berating us about working with humans to help Gaia, fighting the war on all fronts, equally in the Umbra, on the battlefield, in human politics, and in people’s minds. She was pleading!
    It was terrible. I mean, sure. I believe all those things, too. But why does she have to be so strident about it! It’s a moot – there’s a lot to put up with already. Red Talons stinking of wet fur. Bone Gnawers hitting me up for charity. Some godawful Children of Gaia striking up one of their hippie songs.
    Wake up, the Sixties ended over fifty years ago! We’re all about the end times now, the planet and it’s destruction.

    Very unlikeable character but you get this section with quotes from characters about the fall of the Garou Nation. And it’s this paradox I mentioned at the beginning: Everybody wants something like the Garou Nation – and everybody hated the Garou Nation when it was still around. But the kicker is that you get a taste at the end of how Jonas Albrecht is written in this book. This is how his quote starts: “We don't understand what we lost. My beautiful, perfect realm! The Garou Nation, the home for all of our kind.” I don’t know about you but the Jonas Albrecht that I knew from the legacy material would never exclaim “My beautiful, perfect realm!”. Urgh, like nails on a chalkboard. Even if you were to say that this is a different Jonas Albrecht and not the legacy-one, it still is such a sad sap of a character in how this book depicts him. It’s hard to believe that this character ever managed to be the king of the Garou Nation to begin with.

    Then there’s the Touchstones-section which we already saw in the previews (not anything more to it than what was shown in the previews) which then goes to the example Garou:
    • Little Sister: Children of Gaia pacifist. That’s it. Nothing more to it than that.
    • Ilona and Karola Korányi: Shadow Lord Ahroun, hate Silver Fangs, part of some evil baron’s scheme, good Ahrouns.
    • Dominique “Death-In-Word” Omar: Ragabash who was recruited by the Silver Fangs mostly because he’s wealthy. He fell in love with his role as a Ragabash. Translation: He’s an asshole who takes his Ragabash-duties way too far.
    • Shiro: A Hart Warden. Also a Galliard. And I bet you won’t expect what comes next: He’s a musician! Sigh
    • Low Growl: Young wolfborn Silent Strider Philodox who travels from Sept to Sept helping human-born Garou to get more in touch with their wolf-nature.

    The Building A Social Environment section is useful as it talks about scale and in particular presenting the number of Garou in the setting as four different levels you should choose between: Non-existent – There’s the PC-Pack and a few other Garou; Small – There’s the PC-Pack, a small Sept with various Garou Elders and a rival Pack; Regional – There’s a Sept with four or five Packs; The Nation In Ruins – The story involves multiple Septs with each having multiple Packs. The section would’ve been even more interesting if there had been some commentary on why so much of the previous W5 stuff operated on the “Non-existent”-scale-level. Because I’m curious if the “non-existent”-scale is supposed to be the standard according to the current writing-team. Or maybe after the release of this book, they will embrace the bigger Regional and Nation In Ruins scale…? I guess, we’ll see.

    Next you got a list of example Septs:
    • Sept of the Moon’s Blessing: A rolemodel for a good Sept, I guess. Not that interesting.
    • Sept of the Water’s Birth: Garou Sept attached to a human activist cause. Not sure what exactly the Garou are supposed to be doing here. It’s a human activist cause. It doesn’t really involve Garou-issues or -goals or needs the support of Garou-powers. The only thing I could picture here is doing either one of the two stereotypical W5 stories: Cult of Fenris needs to be stopped or you take the opposition to the activist cause and say “Oh, by the way, they’re Fomori.”.
    • Sept of the North County: This is Jonas Albrecht’s Sept where he lingers these days as this broken figure who has fallen to Harano. Besides getting Jonas out of Harano, the other story-hook is that he has adopted this Lebanese teenager from New York. He has given her a new name (which is weird) and she’s now called Mari (after one of Jonas’ Packmates). Also, she’s supposed to be his successor. And it suggests you could play this out by having one player play “Princess Mari” and have the other players be her “honor guard” as Mari is chasing her destiny of being the next leader of the Garou Nation.
    • Sept of the Bitter Frost: A Cult of Fenris Sept that is very welcoming and open-minded when outsiders are around and tries to get newcomers to join the Sept. But then behind closed doors the Cult show what they’re truly like and abuse/torture the newcomers until they join the Cult or they become a human sacrifice instead.

    There’s a new element introduced towards the end of this chapter called “Great Hunts” which is the term for any effort that involves multiple Septs to work together to achieve. I don’t know but the first time I heard this it reminded me of Werewolf: The Forsaken. “Great Hunt” just doesn’t quite feel right for what Garou would call this, I feel like. The section also lists “Great Hunts of the past” and it’s essentially just a long list of references to legacy-material. The only present-day newish Great Hunt that is mentioned is called “The Breath of Gaia” and it’s targeting the southern states of the US and the Mediterranean in Europe. It goes after places “where cheap, exploited labor is used in industrial farming, meatpacking plants, heavy industry and other sectors with a significant negative impact on both the environment and human communities.” Again, another thing where this just feels overly broad and vague. And it lacks Garou-specific flavor.

    Oh, and another famous Great Hunt is called “The Dead Purge” which starts like this: “Most Garou consider vampires to be servants of the Wyrm and agents of the enemy. While suspicion and hatred of the undead have always been part of Garou culture, it has escalated greatly in the last 10 years.” This gets continued in Chapter Five where vampires are discussed again but you read right, this book brings back the idea that most Garou think vampires are servants of the Wyrm and that they have an inherently antagonistic relationship with them.

    Chapter Three: Rites of the Moon

    Honestly it’s exactly what you’d expect: A list of new Rites you can use in your game. One that’s particularly interesting is the Rite of Renunciation as it’s the process of leaving your Tribe. But more than that, it introduces the concept of “Forsworn” (aka the Ronin from legacy-material) in a sidebar. Tribe-less Garou are a thing again.

    This chapter also offers a list of story-hooks but none of them seem that interesting in how bland and obvious they are. I bet most STs would be capable of coming up with stuff like this on their own. There’s nothing particularly insightful or Garou-specific offered here.

    Chapter Four: Saga of the Wolf

    This chapter is dedicated to Moots and it brings nothing new to the table. It essentially just re-introduces the W5 audience to the particulars of how a Moot works. And the examples are again a bit on the blander side of things. Again, I imagine that most STs could come up with these broad story-hooks on their own.

    Where the chapter gets interesting, though, is towards the end where “Legendary Rites” are discussed. These are reenactments of critical events in Garou history. The way it’s presented it comes off like an idea that obviously came from someone with a LARP-background and I do wonder how well this concept would even work if the players aren’t game for this bit of a high-concept-idea of playing characters who play other characters in this reenactment. Four examples are presented (Ross the She-Wolf Fights Union Bromide, The Tale of the Storm-Eater, The Quest for the Silver Crown, The Last Battle).

    Let’s look at “The Quest for the Silver Crown”. You have a Dramatis Personae where roles get distributed. This particular Legendary Rite calls for five main-roles and two groups of people being side-characters. Again, let’s just say that this feels very much like a LARP-thing.

    And then you get a sequence of scenes that look like this:

    1. The Attack: Black Spiral Dancers attack Queen Morningkill and Albrecht’s packmates. The battle ends with the death of Morningkill and the attackers fleeing. (This battle, like all the battles in this Legendary Rite, should be roleplayed with regular combat rules.)
    2. The King: Lord Arkady and Jonas Albrecht arrive at the murder scene, and Arkady proclaims himself king. Jonas Albrecht disputes this and takes his pack to look for the Silver Crown.
    3. The Search: Jonas Albrecht and his pack look for the crown everywhere but do not find it. (The characters should describe where in the world they go to look for the talisman. The pyramids? The rainforest? The North Pole? Rolls of Wits + Survival should be made, and on a result of two or fewer successes, characters take 2 Superficial Physical damage.)
    4. The Ambush: Black Spiral Dancers ambush the pack. Another combat ensues. (The Black Spiral Dancers get to start with surprise attacks.)

    And this goes up to 11. Also, everybody mentioned here are supposed to be Garou playing these roles (including the Black Spiral Dancers). It also is expected that these scenes are acted out. What is part of this concept is that the events aren’t fixed. So in this particular case you have divergent routes where Albrecht’s pack either gets captured or they win against the Black Spiral Dancers and get them to lead to Dagrack, the main-BSD-guy. And it can end in different ways, too. There’s one ending where Albrecht wins the day and there’s another ending where Arkady becomes the next king of the Garou Nation by defeating Albrecht.

    I like the idea of this but I can’t help but feel that this one’s solely for LARP-play. It’s hard to imagine how this would go well at the table.

    Chapter Five: Speaking With The Enemy

    As mentioned at the start, this chapter is a revision of the Allies/Antagonist-chapter from the W5 corebook. All the factions are re-examined under the auspice of under what circumstances an alliance might be possible. But as mentioned categories like vampires aren’t in this neutral zone like it was sometimes described during the release of the W5 corebook.

    This is the best chapter of the book, though, as it provides much better write-ups for the Cult of Fenris, Stargazers and pretty much all the categories of allies and antagonists. The book also adds Bear-Shifters and Rat-Shifters. It still doesn’t come close to the depth these shifters got in the legacy-material but for example, it’s heavily implied that the Bear-Shifters are still the same-old Gurahl from previous editions.

    There’s one exception, though, which are the Black Spiral Dancers. It’s different from the W5 corebook and personally I never ran BSD like how it’s described here. First, it’s stated that while the Fenrir are a Tribe fallen to Hauglosk, the BSD are a Tribe fallen to Harano. It says: “Some, inspired by how the Cult of Fenris rose into prominence, propose that the Black Spiral Dancers are a tribe in harano, albeit a kind that inverted the tribe’s fighting spirit as opposed to destroying it.”

    But this is a much more morally gray version of the BSD. This version of the BSD is still very much invested in their White Howler heritage and is still interested in stuff like honoring pacts the White Howlers made in the past (one story-hook that’s suggested in the book is that a BSD-pack might show up to an abandoned caern that the PCs are also targeting but the BSD-pack say it’s their right to claim it because it used to belong to the White Howlers). Also, the text describes how the BSD don’t want to get pushed around by Fomori and/or Pentex and if they feel slighted, they are willing to help Garou in order to teach their erstwhile allies a lesson. I always portrayed BSD as much more monstrous and psychopathic so the version described in this book is a bit different.


    Appendix: Systems

    Here you get new Merits and Flaws… Nothing noteworthy here. The one, though, that absolutely doesn’t work is the two-dot Flaw “Additional Ban”. It does what it says: Your character gets an additional Ban from another Tribe. Why? Who knows… Similarly to this version of the Flaw from the V5 Players Guide it comes with the lazy addendum saying “The Storyteller can prohibit this Flaw if the second Ban would cause problems for, or lack impact in, the chronicle.” If you think, it could cause problems or lack impact, just don’t put it in the book! Or at least put in the work of listing what combinations of Bans are good-to-go.

    The book adds one new Native Gift. Yep, just one. And it isn’t a particularly good one as well. It’s called The Howl Carries and it does this: “The Garou steels themselves and howl loudly. That howl is picked up and repeated by mundane wolves who travel some distance and then repeat the howl until the sun next rises or sets. Any Garou within a few miles recognizes the howl as unusual (including Garou unknown to the howler), as do some spirits. Humans, however, have no chance of intercepting the message.” So it’s a howl-power that’s reliant on mundane wolves… I would love to talk to whoever wrote this and direct them to pg 74 of a book called Shattered Nation which introduces this Rite called “Rite of the Borne Word” where you task a spirit with delivering a spoken message to a specific target. Yep, this very book has a Rite that does the same thing this Gift does – but better.

    Also… in contrast to the book I can think of a few ways humans might be able to intercept a howl that’s carried by mundane wolves…

    Appendix: Loresheets

    There’s something odd about these Loresheets. All seven of them are centered on one particular Garou. But what makes it odd that in all seven cases something very different is going on when you delve into the dot-ratings. Despite the names always being one particular Garou no Loresheet is actually about you being a close contact of this one Garou and that person giving your character stuff. So I will always first explain who the showcased Garou is and then I will explain what’s going on with the dots.

    Aino “Lil Gaia” Metsälä:
    • A Red Talon Ragabash who’s a Finnish activist and clearly inspired by Greta Thunberg. She has an NGO called “Gaia First” that “organize demonstrations, civil disobedience, and social media campaigns to inform the populace and to demand that governments and corporations act according to their aims”. Her detractors also accuse her of being a Stolen Moon.
    • What the dots describe is you becoming a member of “Gaia First” and gradually getting more access to that organization which can get you local intel, allies, safe houses and they’ll create demonstrations, civil unrest and whatnot on your behalf at higher ratings.

    Alejandro “Vaporfang” Mendoza:
    • A Bone Gnawer Galliard who’s a world-famous hacker who’s getting into secret databases from governments, corporations and rich oligarchs in order to leak those secrets to the public. This character is clearly inspired by Edward Snowden.
    • What makes this character odd, though, is the description notes that he’s currently in prison. So… how exactly would PCs come into contact with him…? Sure, I could envision scenarios to make this work but here’s a quicker solution: Just don’t make your Loresheet about a character in prison!
    • The Loresheet notes that you need at least two dots in Technology and that has something to do with what’s actually going on in the dots. As you gain dots, you actually become proficient in doing the same thing that Vaporfang is famous for.

    Jonas Albrecht:
    • Pretty self-explanatory who he is.
    • What’s going on in the dots is that as you gain more, you become a more ardent follower of Jonas Albrecht and a firm believer in restoring the Garou Nation. The mechanics are pretty interesting here as you gain bonus-dice from defending the ideals of the Garou Nation but also you can get further bonus-dice by taking dots of Harano (and later Hauglosk as well). And it all starts with the first dot being that once per session you can add your Harano-score to your dice-pool. You essentially play a zealot who’s burning themselves out in pursuit of rebuilding the Garou Nation.

    Liu “Returning Maiden” Aihan:
    • She grew up in Shanghai and is a taikonaut who had her First Change in space (which is a bit hard to believe). But the actual focus is that as she had her First Change in space she has no Auspice. She’s a mysterious figure because instead it seems like she’s all five Auspices at the same time. She’s supposed to be a Galestalker according to some but the way she’s acting makes a lot of Garou think that she’s a Stargazer instead.
    • The dots are about your character becoming a moon cultist and getting closer to Luna. First dot is about you being able to learn Gifts from other Auspices. Second Dot is about you being able to switch Auspices for the purpose of Rites and other ceremonial purposes. Third Dot gives you bonus die when interacting with Lunes and other spirits of Luna. Fourth dot allows you once per session to treat an attack with silver as superficial damage. And fifth dot… * drumroll * Moon-Path (aka Moon-Bridge). Yep, they’re back – sort-of. This fifth dot allows the character to create moon-bridges between two caerns.

    Oba “Trailblazer” Makinde:
    • A Glass Walker Theurge who’s an entrepeneuer and CEO of a surveillance-company called Omniscent. Here’s the thing about Omniscent: It’s a Pentex-front. And Trailblazer knows this. He often comes in conflict with other Garou (especially the aforementioned Lil Gaia and her “Gaia First”-organization) because his grand plan is to take out Pentex from within.
    • The dots describe the PC rising in the ranks of Omniscent and giving them access to information about what Pentex and the various corporations connected to it are up to. And you get dots in Resources.
    • It gets weirder towards the higher end as you get permanent dot of Harano or Hauglosk at four dots and at five dots it says: “You gain a level of Aggravated Willpower damage if you attempt to harm Omniscent, Trailblazer, or any of their business partners, including Pentex and its fronts.” That certainly makes it seem like that whole “taking Pentex down from within” doesn’t quite work how it’s pitched by Trailblazer…

    Ross the She-Wolf Jackson:
    • A Silent Strider Philodox and a proud gay man (I mention that because that’s what the Loresheet is about). He’s an environmental activist who has earned quite a bit of Renown fighting Union Bromide in Nagpur.
    • It’s a bit hard to pin down what exactly is going on here except that LGBTQ+, pollution and minority-groups are the focus… sort-of. First dot allows you to have a sixth sense for knowing when environmental pollution is occurring and how bad it is. Second dot gives you a bonus-die for the first action after you have shape-shifted. Third dot gives you a four-dice-bonus once per session if you’re trying to prevent environmental pollution. Fourth dot allows your character to change their sex every time they shapeshift (at the cost of a point of Willpower and an additional Rage-check). Fifth dot says this: “You are a member of a minority group. Once per story, If you spend a scene hanging out with members of this community, you recover all lost Willpower. As with Ross, you are expected to help and protect this group, and if you ever refuse a call for help from someone in this minority you sustain three points of Aggravated Willpower damage and lose this trait until you have provided said help.” I guess, this whole Loresheet works best if you’re part of a minority group from the very beginning and that’s what gets you to this Loresheet.

    Vandana “The Impresario” Puri:
    • A Hart Warden Galliard that’s well known for organizing Moots and Rites (like a Rite of Passage). She’s also very invested in hunting down Stolen Moons.
    • The dots in this case are nother example like Vaporblade where you essentially turn into a copy of this character and become proficient in what this character is famous for. You’re getting better at organizing events and you’re getting better at tracking down Stolen Moons and killing them.

    Conclusion:

    After going through the whole book, I think the thing that stuck most with me is how much of this book is just about bringing back legacy-material. Meanwhile, newer concepts that are W5-specific like Moon and Gaian Cults don’t get nearly enough coverage in this book. Rather, in contrast to the “re-imagination, not continuation”-slogan from the W5 corebook, this is a book that quite earnestly invested in being a continuation.

    On one hand, I think W5 is richer for having all these bits and pieces from the legacy-material back in the W5-setting. On the other hand, though, this book hasn’t managed to move past any of the big issues the W5 corebook had. Does the fall of the Garou Nation make any more sense now? Not really. Has the negative attitude towards oral history and this “Who knows”-attitude been addressed? No, instead it just doubled down and it still just sounds as absurd as when it came up in the W5 corebook. Does it offer an alternative route for Garou to aspire to? No, it just feels like everyone wants to bring the Garou Nation back with these ephemeral caveats that this time it will be completely different and much better. And it would’ve been things like the Moon and Gaian Cults (the idea of lifting the Veil and working more closely together with humans) which are actual genuine alternative visions. So it’s sad that it’s exactly in those areas where you don’t get enough information.

    And then I gotta wonder: Especially if you’re familiar with the legacy-material… what does this book actually add to the game? All the people who went online and asked about more information for moots, for example. Often in situations like that, they were told to go look up the older books for more information. So they did that. And this book is essentially the official equivalent of just that. The people who wrote those sections also just went back to the older books and gave you that same information. So how much is this book truly offering to its readers…?

    I think, the parts on Pack-Types and all the sections that had more of a toolbox-approach are worthy additions, though. Getting STs to consider the number of Garou in the setting and discussing the options or helping with session zero by giving general overviews in what terms you can define Packs. And personally, instead of spending all that wordcount on painstakingly writing back in all the legacy-material, I think everybody would just be happier by going for a toolbox-approach and letting everybody play the way they want. You want your legacy-stuff back, your 30-Garou Septs and epic adventures? Sure. You want your Age of Apocalypse, fallen Garou Nation and the setting consists of the PC Pack and only one or two other Garou? That’s fine as well. And for example, if you just focus on making Moon Cults a genuinely interesting thing, I’m sure there will be W20 groups who will try to bring that into their game. In the same way that there should be no hard feelings about anyone who likes a part of the legacy-material and wants to bring those into a W5 game.

    It all comes back to the W5 corebook. That’s one of the bigger issues W5 has. In trying to re-examine core-concepts from this book, they just came off as even more absurd. Like the fallen Garou Nation. It fell apart because Garou couldn’t work together but if you have these Septs who have these grievances and who won’t work together, then in the face of the Apocalypse and the war against the Wyrm, naturally you would wanna make it your goal to get them to work together. But the whole point of the fall of the Garou Nation is that these Garou couldn’t work together. So you need to introduce a reason for why this time around would be different. But you’re making the change too radical and you’re not playing WtA anymore. On the other hand, you don’t do that and you’d wonder why the story wouldn’t be just about history repeating itself. These issues are so fundamental that you would’ve needed here a different approach with different ideas from the very beginning. But instead with W5 you’ll just run into these contradictions again and again.

    All in all, as a book it’s a good addition but it can’t move beyond the shortcomings of this game’s setting. The only good sign here is that each future supplement that is similar to this one will be W5 getting one inch further away from the problems of the corebook.

    Oh and hopefully, we’ll get better in-character-writing in the future…

                  I don’t really follow or play 5th edition much so I appreciate threads like this and I’ll talk about some of the things you mentioned and how I might consider cribbing them for previous editions if that interests folks

                  Introduction

                  •In theory I do like the idea of using a sort of vulgar, abrasive, ‘dirty realism’ tone for Garou and Garou society but this is clearly a case of YMMV depending on taste and how well its done. I do think it can be a good fit when done well in both how it serves the (imperfect term) ‘savage horror’ element of the game and the aspect of the werewolf that’s essentially a frenzied murderer as well as the contrast that this tone can strike with the more pulpy Conan/Robert Howard elements of pageantry which Garou culture can also use

                  •While such a tone has use throughout I think it would be particularly useful for young turks and outsiders within the Nation as well as groups like the Bone Gnawers in general, who might talk like a Chuck Palahniuk or Charles Bukowski character

                  Chapter 1

                  •I like the pack dynamic dials for interpersonal relationship, spiritual relationship, & relationship with the veil although perhaps spiritual relationship could use further differentiation - the options mentioned sound kind of samey. The books have always played with the idea that some Garou are hostile and coercive with spirits while others are reverent and conciliatory (and others are in between) and that might have been an interesting dynamic to spotlight there

                  •I like the idea of pack-types and even the pack-systems mentioned but they could probably be tweaked somewhat. I do like Territory having to do with the health of the territory and familiarity with it but it could also have certain tags, maybe something like the stuff in WtF. I like Community too and it would be interesting to distinguish between human, kinfolk, & wolf/wild community relations. Spirit feels a little weird. Perhaps one could argue that the umbra of a pack’s protectorate ought to fit under Territory but even so Spirit feels like it ought to reflect a familiarity with the prevalent and important spirit’s of a pack’s protectorate and the pack’s relationship with them. I like the concepts here but they could use a few tweaks maybe

                  •I like the concept of pack types and it can be useful to have lists like these for inspiration and an exploration of archetypes and dynamics

                  •The Moon Cults idea is very cool imo and something is definitely be interested in. Garou society already functions as a nested network of cults to begin with and it could be a very rich vein to more thoroughly explore. I wish they’d offered a more concrete explanation of what the Moon Cults in general are like but still, credit where it’s due

                  •I like the idea of Pack Actions but it sounds like this section really went off the rails. This is where something like Blades in the Dark or related games could offer inspiration, because I do like the general concept of systems for pack-actions and their effects in play, but they would definitely need to be more archetypal and flexible rather then specific. Reconnoiter & Ambush both work fine but some of these are def too specific. Off the top of my head I’d say that Reconnoiter (gather information), Ambush, Hunt (WtF style chasing down a target), Patrol (surveying a territory or keeping certain territory routines upkept), Sabotage, Assault, Ritual, Howl, etc. would be more in line with what I’d be looking for in general systems for pack-actions

                  Chapter 2

                  •I feel like there’s a current of something in the idea of Generations as described and how most active Garou (like PCs) tend to live in the present but like others I disagree with its framing within the context of W5. Still, I do think there’s a nugget there, about how many Garou are like your pack of young PCs and are mostly concerned with the hectic, breakneck speed of Garou war life, and will be lucky to survive a decade of it. What I feel W5 leaves out though and what legacy can help with, particularly 1e/2e is the idea that the pedal-to-the-medal image of Garou was never the whole of the nation but rather its cadres of relatively young packs. The Garou nation in legacy also had Old Wolves (retired Garou who had yet to feel the need to go out Winter Wolf-style and stayed around to teach the young, be the audience at moots & help establish renown, teach rites, etc; generally being the crabby chorus that both criticize and guide the PCs), Guardians of the Nation (the workhorse Garou who were either sitting on their laurels with a steady position within a step providing local leadership or the Garou who preferred to dedicate their energy to Sept maintaince instead of roving around anruth style in search of adventure and Wyrms to slay), & Drop Outs (Garou who did not spend the majority of their time working for the Nation, whether at Sept or as Anruth, and instead did whatever else held their fancy but could possibly be called upon as allies for more active Garou), etc.
                  -Plus the whole attitude towards oral tradition isn’t a great look for W5 and while I can buy that Garou would tailor stories to fit the audience of every generation I don’t buy that there wouldn’t be competing cliques of both elders and spirits who kept alive certain versions of important historical narratives

I could buy some version of this concept in the right chronicle - a sort of quasi-apocalypse scenario where most Garou think they’ve failed to stop the Apocalypse and it’s too late to do anything and Jonas ends up as a washed-up could-have-been wallowing in angst and despairing for the world he lost and for what could have been, but it doesn’t really feel earned given the context of W5. This just feels kind of like they turned Albrecht into an updated Jacob Morningkill, not really into it

                  •The Cult Sept sounds like the most interesting one to me tbh
