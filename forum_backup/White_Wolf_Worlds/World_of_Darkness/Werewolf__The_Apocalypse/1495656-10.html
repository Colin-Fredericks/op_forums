            Tribe. at least in English, is a pretty loose term. Most groups called tribes do have a common ethnic, familial, and cultural lineage, but how much each aspect is stressed varies considerably. Many tribes have ways for people that are not born into the tribe to join (marriage being the most frequent, but not exclusively), and many tribes also have some sort of concept commonly referred to in modern terms as an "honorary member of the tribe," as a status granted to strong individual allies that are either ineligible or unwilling to fully join the tribe according to existing customs; but then granted greater privileges in tribal spaces than other outsiders would receive.

            While tribe was probably picked because it has a strong connotation to social organizations based on ancient principles/identifiers and/or predate exposure to European colonialism, that's not an inherently bad thing for WtA or WtF Tribes considering how old they are supposed to be. Tribes in formal anthropology and sociology are also frequently associated with communal land management instead of individual ownership as a cultural feature; something also very appropriate to WtA and WtF's use of the word as tribes orient themselves around caerns/loci as a primary local social focal point that everyone is responsible for tending to.

            For me, the big thing that's necessary is defining the internal cultural of the group that is significantly different from the majority cultural around them. This is much easier to construct as a fictional entity when you focus on bloodline lineages, but not impossible to do if you work on other ways your fictional tribal cultures establish strong faux-kin bonds between members rather than those built in with familial born expectations.

            Forsaken could go into more depth on this, but has established a number of cultural expressions that make each of its Tribes unique. If a few mixed-Tribe packs of Uratha get together, the books have created a few specific examples and a looser general idea of things that characters that share the same Tribe will want to do as a group apart from their normal pack associations. However formal or not, there are customs and traditions that they have that are meant for when they're alone with other members of their Tribes. Oddly this is even better defined for the Pure. Probably because the ST has to run the Pure and isn't as worried about the level of self expression the players want out of the Forsaken Tribes that has them with looser defined cultural traits.

            W5 hasn't really shown what it's doing to justify if Tribe is still a useful designation or just legacy terminology that doesn't really work in the new context (like Fianna). If W5 does enough to establish each Tribe having a unique cultural identity that can emerge in play in a meaningful fashion, I don't have a problem with it. Again, the example of a few mixed Tribe packs coming together resulting in members of each Tribe with multiple members in the gathering (a bit harder with 11 of them though) wanting to have some "tribe time" feeling like a natural extension of the presented material means its probably in a good place. Human cultures and geographic association were an easy way to fill that in for the older editions of the game, but W5 can fill them in in other ways. We just have to wait and see if they actually will.

                        My expectation on this at this point is that the BSDs will have some sort of ritual that's more similar to the Cauls from Mage than the way the Spiral worked in the old books. Honestly, I don't have a big problem with the idea of "dancing the spiral" having numerous variations on a ritualistic practice rather than a literal sojourn to a specific place on the Umbra. The important part is a "point of no return," where a character is fully dedicated to the service of the Wyrm. A ritual with hundreds of variants that all base around the idea of symbolically dancing down a black spiral or a literal journey to an actual spiritual realm doesn't seem nearly as important to the role the BSDs play in the setting.

                        If one of the strengths of WtA that a lot of us feel Justin is abandoning is that WtA is very scalable to different takes and levels of focus, the full induction method presented in the old books for the Black Spiral is not something that plays into that scalability. It's an element that strongly focuses on the "epic" side of the game as even the most basic "try to stop a Lost Cub from dancing the spiral before it's too late," scenario means the PCs have to get involved in some pretty heavy shit. Having the base-line ritual be something that can just be a pack interrupting a BSD pack mid-blasphemy, while the idea of inducted BSDs constantly seeking more potent ways to dance the spiral again, with greater risks, and greater blessings from the Wyrm for those that succeed, offers plenty of story hooks for the epic side of things. It feels to me like the game would be stronger with the idea that the BSDs aren't done dancing their spirals once they're Cliaths, and that the more they dance, the bigger threats they become (and helps give them a bit more depth as antagonists because the evil they're doing has a more concrete rationale to it), and one of the scariest thoughts a Gaian Garou can have is a renowned BSD finally finding a path to the true Black Spiral, and then successfully dancing it before returning a a full avatar of the Wyrm.

                        I don't expect any of that out of W5 mind you, but it feels like it would be a net positive for those of us that are currently leaning towards keeping the old lore and setting over W5's reboot to see some of the changes as things that can be mined for making the old stuff better.

                                                        What bugs me when people say "burdened with metaplot" and I just stare in confusion. Sure, Vampire was driven by metaplot. Mage got wrecked by metaplot in revised only to spend its last days completely revamping and reintroducing things the metaplot wrecked and opening the game to other modes of play.

                                                        However. What was the metaplot of Werewolf? There was the story of Albrecht and his crew, but it was a relatively small scale thing and wouldn't impact 99% of all the games. There was the fact that the European Garou were banding together through the efforts of Konietzko, and you could be a part of that... or not! Baba Yaga was supposed to be a big bad of Russia... but she got killed offscreen in another game and wasn't really relevant to anything. Silent Striders were going back to Egypt and needed help. You had the Ahadi coming into being which was a huge win for the setting allowing people to actually explore an underexplored part of the world and have a legit way of playing shifters. Plus all the prophecies and strange portents that could or could not influence your chronicle.

                                                        At the end of the day metaplot of Werewolf was pretty unobtrusive and mostly conductive to either getting rid of things that were problematic (Swords of Heimdall, hello!) or opening new venues, social constructs and ways of playing the game. To me, Revised was the golden age of Werewolf, and a few stinkers aside it defined an incredible setting with more freedom than any other WOD line at the time.
