            Originally posted by The Cat Came Back View Post
            All the dice in the world won't save you from a Difficulty 9 roll.

            Adding five dice to a Charisma + leadership roll to convince your sept of a wild, cockamemie plan, after having proved yourself to be a nincompoop, is really just ,more likely to give you botches than anything.
            This is factually, genuinely wrong. While sometimes extra dice does increase your risk of botching (1 vs 2 dice at difficulty 10 as an example), it reduces your risk of Failure in ALL situations; it's just that a higher % of the failures that do happen are Botches.

            More dice is ALWAYS good, even at difficulty 9. 8 dice vs 13 dice (good social character with PB 0 vs. good social character with PB 5) means a difference of 57.72 / 31.26 / 11.01 (1.8) vs 66.08 / 29.39 / 4.53 (2.4). This means the risk of botching is more than halved, for starters. Furthermore the odds of gaining 1 success or more increases by almost 10%. Your average successes increase by 0.6, which means a lot by aggregate.

            And this is at difficulty 9, the highest difficulty you'll face with most STs. You know what 8 vs. 13 (our Bone Gnawer vs. our Silver Fang with PB 5) dice means at difficulty 6?

            92.06 / 7.61 / 0.33 (3.5)

            97.15 / 2.84 / 0.01 (5.4)

            More than a 5% increase in chances to succeed. Chance to fail is reduced by almost 5%, and chance to botch is reduced from nearly a third of a % to almost zero. Average successes are up by 2.

            This is pretty huge, mechanically speaking. It's why Vampire's invest in Celerity 5, even if they don't tend to spend blood on it. It's why Mages have Cults and work together on big spells. Dice make a huge difference at any difficulty.

                    Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post

                    Except for the number of systems over the past 10 or so years that have actually put the effort into making social mechanics that avoid the binary results older games defaulted to, which caused the feeling of lack of player agency. There's plenty of systems around that have social mechanics players actually enjoy even at the risk of their character ending up doing something they didn't want, or having to incorporating a view on an NPC they didn't initially envision.

                    People can tolerate mundane social mechanics just fine, when designers put in the effort to make it rewarding to do so.

                    The whole set of principles are basically for when you're playing a game that doesn't bother making social mechanics fun to engage with. The WoD is certainly one of those games, but it's certainly not all games.
                    Read The Succubus Club; Dead Man's Party. Social rolls NEVER rob PCs or NPCs or free will.

                    Example:

                    James is a bit of a rogue in his Tribe, and he doesn't like playing in a team. He does, however, want to learn new Gifts, and being in good standing with the Tribe is a big part of that (with many Spirits being more friendly towards those who are working well with their Tribe). John has PB 5 and Charisma 5, and Expression 4. He states in plain view of other members of their Tribe that James has to be more of a team player, otherwise he can't expect help from anyone else when he needs it.

                    John rolls his 14 dice against a difficulty of 6 (or 4 if he has a Merit) and gets 6 successes. Others nod in agreement, people avoid making eye contact with James. James COULD storm off and ignore the result of the roll, but he would face social debuffs as a result. NPCs would be very slow to help him, train him etc. Next time James needs help he'll be rolling at +3 difficulty because he is at -3 favour as a result of John's roll.

                    So James still has full agency, there are just social problems arising from his behaviour and his failure to comply with John's successful roll. If John were to botch? He looks like a control freak and loses Favour, and James gets to keep his devil-may-care attitude for a while longer.

                    If James comes around and becomes a team player as a result, John might be in for an increase in Renown (I don't remember which three things Garou have off the top of my head, Wizdom maybe?) for getting one of his relatives to play their part better. This means social stats can actually be as potent as other stats, situationally.
