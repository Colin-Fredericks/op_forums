                                        I think the underlying issue here is, it would be an interesting thing to address... if the corebook didn't feel like it was doing a big sweep under the rug. To address both sides of this argument:

                                        1. Getting rid of ancestor spirits and how the Nation addresses its sins feels like it's removing a huge chunk of history, hubris, and reckoning that was central to the Nation in Legacy editions. It also opens up the territory to explore the War of Rage from multiple perspectives, and create the kind of scenario where proud images of the Wild West and Manifest Destiny and California elementary school projects on the mission system give way to the Apache Wars and the reservation schools. It feels like it opens the door for a more fractured Nation, where the Children of Gaia might have tried to find out what happened and actually gotten a better perspective from the Fera, and the more traditional tribes take the "that was millennia ago" approach, and some real reactionaries act like those folks on Twitter who respond to "Happy Indigenous Peoples Day" by saying "Yeah well the Aztecs ate people!"
                                        2. But, as the book stands, there is no room for shame or doubt or debate because the proud legacy of the Garou Nation is gone. Your characters don't know shit, and it's heavily implied their mentors don't know shit. The Impergium doesn't have a historical record beyond "it was bad, we think," the collapse of the Nation itself is hard to date, and some tribes have no idea of their foundational lore beyond conjecture. There are artifacts and spiritual pacts and debts, but you're a people without a clear history. It's Posadism with fewer dolphins. All you really know about what came before you is it didn't work, your ancestors were assholes, and you need to do something new.
                                        3. Which is where the Fera come in. There's a good possibility that you may need to make amends with these other shifters for sins past. But you don't know what those sins are. Part of that is historical erasure, but there's not really a counternarrative to push against, no sense that you'll be accused of "critical shifter theory" if you argue that, hey, maybe we should make up for that genocide we did.

                                        That's my issue with how the W5 corebook lays it out, though I acknowledge future books could correct it. It's ladling on the message of "Your ancestors were shit," but when it comes time to address the concrete sins that you need to correct, it often adopts an air of "Who knows, we're not telling." You can't talk to the Fera because they're snippy (for good reason) and you've got nothing to go on. It's not about providing the materials for redress by staring down your sins; rather, it feels like the boogeyman version of addressing racial injustice in the historical record, where some DEI scholar tells your kid, "Your great-grandpa was racist, and that stain passes on to you."

                                                Thinking about the discussions on history in Garou, I think the writing-team for the released W5 book didn’t work with any sort of timeline or consideration for what happens when. So any mention of time is more a coincidence than a sign of intent, I would say. Take the Garou-Nation section on pg 37. It starts to outline the contours of the Garou-Nation as unknowable. When did it start? Who knows. When did it fall? Who knows? How long did it last? A decade? A century? Millenia? Who knows. Somehow nobody can be sure about that.

                                                More importantly, in the next paragraph, though, suddenly a time-frame for the decline of the Garou-Nation is introduced: "The Wyrm’s growing strength manifested through numerous tools, and through agents who goaded many werewolves into participating in colonial conquest by imperial powers, which tapped the Garou’s inherent love for dominance. At times, these agents took advantage of the uncertainty brought about by the Renaissance, the Enlightenment, and the Industrial Revolution." So the time-frame it's talking about is roughly 15th century to 19th. That’s already a contradiction of the first paragraph. The section started with all these unknowable facts and then immediately afterward jumped into “Let me tell you about the age of colonization and what the Garou did during that period.”

                                                Two other interesting bits here: Why do we start with the Renaissance here? It's unclear why that particular time-frame is even picked. Maybe it's because of the "capitalocene era" bit from the first chapter but it's poor writing to not connect the dots here. If the writers were willing to throw around terms like "capitalocene", the least one could expect them to do is back that up. Sadly the W5 corebook underwhelms in that regard. It doesn't seem like a lot of thought was put into the worldbuilding here. And the second point is that it's telling how the description uses European history-periods for the description. The links between Tribes and human culture/ethnicity were erased, the Native American Tribes' identity was removed but look at that, it goes right back to the default of viewing history through the lens of Europe.

                                                Then the next paragraph offers examples of Garou's bad behavior, the invasion of America, the killing of that Sept of witch-wolves... So far so good, it fits within that time-frame of colonialism that was mentioned before... But here comes the kicker: The third event that is mentioned in the same paragraph is... the fall of the White Howlers and them turning into the Black Spiral Dancers.

                                                The weirdness of the White Howlers being mentioned in this context aside and what this means for the W5 chronology of events, think about this: It’s the age of colonialism, the bad deeds of Garou are the invasion of America and eradicating a Sept of witch-wolves in South-East Asia. I get that there are criticisms to be levied against past editions in terms of diverse representation but this feels like a step backwards. After all, what the Garou-Nation section in W5 outlines is a perspective firmly focused on white Europeans conquering the world. And the victims of these bad actions are merely victims/targets. At least, with previous editions the perspective of the Native American Tribes offered a different perspective. Now it’s just guilt over European Garou doing bad stuff.

                                                They’ve removed the Native American Tribes only to recenter the whole thing from a European colonialist perspective. Sure, it’s a perspective tinged with guilt and regret but it speaks volumes that the retrospective on colonialism as a bad thing gets representation while the representation of Indigenous voices reacting to it and/or suffering because of it was erased.

                                                I think James F. Sambrano was right in calling this game being about “white guilt”. There’s no global or historical perspective here. It’s just present-day white people awkwardly trying to wrangle with the bad stuff of the past and the present. It’s all about being angry at what has happened in the past and how ugly the present is. It’s bad. But then there’s no plan on how to make things better. Because "feeling bad for what was and how things are" is all you get from the limited horizons of the W5 corebook's writing-team. The book just continuously looks for affirmation about what is considered bad and ugly. The past of the Garou is bad and the W5 corebook will NEVER let you move on from that. No matter where you are in the book, at some point, you return to “But do you know that the Garou are bad…?”. And the book shows little interest in the question of “So now what…?”. Or as the "Drawing Distinctions"-sidebar dismissively talks about fighting companies that advance climate-change: "It's an emotional issue."

                                                Nobody expects the vampires of VtM to save the world but the Garou of WtA are on a holy mission by Gaia to prevent the Apocalypse. Since it’s the World Of Darkness the Garou aren’t very good at doing that. W5 doesn’t really improve the formula by stripping away the nuance and focusing on “Garou suck”. Then add corporate-mandated “street-level-only” and “less Umbra” and you get… this. Diversity got repressed, you got a game focused on feeling bad and tons of lore/worldbuilding got stripped away to be replaced by nothing.

                                                    Originally posted by Ana Mizuki View Post

                                                    Oh yeah, this is part of the erasure of nature in W5. For all the book yaps about being inclusive and considerate of Indigenous people, it gleefully discourages stewarding the land.

                                                    The wanted PCs are suburban or urban youths who have little ties to anything outside cities and who are disconnected from their cultures. This could be a great starting point, but the book treats this disconnect as a GOOD thing.

                                                    Actually doing something is either treated as insanity (CoF) or abstracted to the point that it is little more than a werewolfy HoA meeting.

                                                    For all the flaws in Legacy, at least it has the guts to say pollution, colonialism and hunting keytstone species are bad.
                                                    Yeah, I remember Sambrano saying in his article he wanted the Gale Stalkers to be a group of land protectors inspired by Indigenous practices of land stewardship without necessarily being all Indigenous. Like the Standing Rock protesters inviting white people in as long as they didn't showboat. But Muammar's counterargument was this just made them a tribe full of Ahrouns doing what Garou are already supposed to be doing.

                                                    In trying to divorce itself from inspiration in real-world cultural and spiritual practices out of a fear of fucking them up, the game has become culturally agnostic... and, as a result, a bit Western by default. It says not to fuck up any real world animist faiths but makes what the Garou have more of a system of patronage based on contracts and material gain (the idea of spirit Gifts in Legacy veered uncomfortably close to the idea that Native American spirituality has to have material effects to have meaning based on its "more liminal" nature, but it at least had a system of ancestor worship and a greater cosmology to interact with bound into it; now it feels like more of a vending machine). As mentioned by Knightingale, its attempts at addressing the sins of the past risk falling into a white guilt paradigm of the unquenchable sin that you must bear without necessarily knowing how to correct, like how some works of fiction act as if all natives stopped existing in the area past a certain year (which is unfortunately true in some cases, but not as many as you'd think).

                                                    Even the sidebars meant to offer an Indigenous perspective fall into this a bit. The first one talks about generational trauma and how Indigenous communities still deal with the sense of loss that comes from exterminationist campaigns, broken treaties, the reservation school system, and other attempts at genocide, both cultural and physical... and then it says "This might apply to the Garou as well." Problem 1: I can most likely go on a Dine reservation and still hear from somebody about Spider Grandmother and old bits of oral tradition, but I can't have a Silent Strider tell me why exactly their tribe is nomadic. Problem 2: This generational trauma stemmed from a campaign meant to either kill Indians or "kill the Indian, save the man"; what happened to the Garou to fuck up their sense of history even worse?

                                                    The second sidebar, on the other hand, is all about how Western culture is future-oriented and materialistic, which can cause people to lose touch with their ancestral roots... and then, again, applies this to all Garou, everywhere. No word on how it applies to Garou in cultures that may be more "past-oriented" (for those who haven't dipped a toe into anthropology, there's an idea that some cultures tend towards past orientation, or centering the values and ways of one ancestors; present orientation, focusing mainly on living in the moment; and future orientation, focused mainly on what comes next and building towards the future; this does not apply to all people within those cultures but denotes cultural trends, but God knows I've seen some missteps where nursing textbooks have "cultural considerations" sections that talk about making adjustments for "Indian time").

                                                    I still think there's potential to build this out down the line, like how Forsaken eventually incorporated local folklores in a way to show how the Uratha had roots there and how their creation tale didn't necessarily override human folklore so much as syncretize with it. But the corebook shows that, if you try to strip out all the cultural elements you think you're getting wrong, you accidentally default back to the idea of a "default" culture which, in the West, is gonna be pretty damn Western.
