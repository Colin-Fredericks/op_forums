    W5 Scent Of Decay - Review

    06-03-2024, 11:02 AM
    I had some time yesterday, so I bought the Scent of Decay book and dug into it.

    Scent of Decay presents itself as a chronicle book that takes starting-characters and introduces new STs and players to various elements of W5 through three adventures. And then there’s an appendix at the end with some smaller scenes that you supposedly can use to create moments that explain raises in Renown.

    But first, you have…

    (Also, spoiler-warning for the events in the particular adventures)

    Chapter One: Assembling The Pack

    This chapter tries to act like it will help with introducing the framework of the campaign and offers some tips and guidelines for a Session Zero. You get a section on Session Zero, Conflict In The Pack and Building Up Your Packmates.

    The biggest problem here is that it’s all very surface-level. And this wouldn’t be that big of a deal. After all, this is supposed to be a starter-chronicle. Since it could not just be an introduction for the players but a person’s first attempt at being the ST as well. But the problem is that the advice is so surface-level that there’s a lot of overlap with the W5 corebook. Even if we talk about an ST who’s just a beginner. The least you can expect is that this person has also read the W5 corebook.

    Another problem is that due to the rather surface-level approach, it misses a lot of the intricacies of the WtA-setting. For example, in the secton on “Conflict In The Pack” it offers this: “The ahroun is just about to rip apart the employee of an oil company when the philodox intervenes, arguing that killing defenseless people is not what the Garou are supposed to do.” And that’s it. It doesn’t really get into the roles the Auspices have and how that figures into the group-dynamic of the PC-Pack. The closest it comes when it later talks about Galliards: “One auspice excels above all others when it comes to building up their packmates. That one is obviously galliard. When your job is to tell the stories of your pack at the moot, you have a wonderful opportunity to build up your friends in ways that support their character concepts. It doesn’t have to be all that complicated: Simply make their successes sound as grand as possible, ideally in line with their character concepts.” But it just isn’t enough to offer anything of much use (especially in light of what you already get from the W5 corebook). And how Tribes play into these group-dynamics doesn’t get discussed, at all, for example. Like how does it figure into a group’s dynamic to have a Glass Walker and a Red Talon in one group? It would have been more worthwhile to delve into that instead of the very surface-level discussion of Session Zero and group dynamics that happens here.

    Then it has a list of “Unusual Packs” and with that it presents the idea of Packs where all the PCs have the same Auspice. So, you get five Pack-types here for each Auspice. The most interesting one, I think, is the All-Philodox-one which is called “Rebuilding the Nation” and it’s described like this: “The Garou Nation is in tatters, destroyed by the internal divisions among werewolves. Yet the dream of being united against the Wyrm still lives and is the purpose of this traveling pack consisting of a few philodoxes and their packmates. They move from sept to sept, trying to heal old wounds and get intransigent elder werewolves to talk to each other.”

    Visually this is presented as if it’s on a page. Even though it’s more of a sidebar. You’d think it would reserve these kinds of backgrounds for in-character text that you could use as a handout during the game… but no. Instead, sometimes you have sidebars that just get these fancier backgrounds for really no reason. It’s on odd choice.

    Next it presents the setting for the chronicle: Milton. It’s supposed to be a midwestern small town in the US. And the assumed standard for the PCs is “human-born and working class”. There’s a few example origin-stories listed “You tried to get a job but didn’t.”, “You got a job as security guard but then got fired.”, “You try to get by stealing stuff from big corporations and supermarkets.”. Somehow you’re already connected to Milton.

    Then you get a description of the Sept you’re gonna be dealing with in these three adventures. It’s called the “Sept of the Far Watcher” And imagine my surprise when it starts like this: “It’s an axiom among the Garou that there are not enough werewolves to fight all their enemies.” Remember the Storyteller’s Kit chronicle where I talked about how it felt like the real issue is a lack of numbers? Well, here it gets spelled out and it definitely is a part of this setting. It’s mentioned at one point: “In recent years, the name has become a joke among the sept’s members: It covers so much territory, you only see your neighboring packs from afar. In this sense, the sept is more a formality than a community, a tacit agreement of cooperation between packs that don’t meet all that often.” And really, you only get to interact with one Garou during these three adventures until the third adventure when a rival Pack shows up. The way it’s framed it also feels like the idea is that in the present day there are fewer Garou on the planet than, say, fifty years ago. More and more problems arise in the world while at the same time the number of Garou is dwindling year by year.


    Chapter Two: The Break Room


    You got three adventures in the book. Theoretically you could take the first two and turn them into stand-alone adventures. The third one definitely requires setup, though, that is delivered by going through the first two adventures. Furthermore, the book helpfully mentions from time-to-time opportunities where you can foreshadow later developments and then in the second/third adventure makes mention of when you can do callbacks to what has come before. So I think the best way to engage with these three adventures is to play them in the order they’re presented in the book.

    Now a word about the art: On one hand, the book has some great visual aids in presenting various locations in the book. The multiple floors of the Val-MOR warehouse or a particular office are depicted in detail. Those are great visual aids. On the other hand, the way it’s presented in the book is absolutely chaotic. For example, the section for getting into the warehouse goes from pg 24 to pg 30. The corresponding image for the warehouse is on pg 31. But get this: The section on entering the dentist’s office goes from pg 30 to pg 34. The corresponding image for that section is on pg 24. It gets even weirder: There’s an actual map of Milton, the city, in the book where all the major locations are marked that you are going to visit in the course of all three adventures. That image can be found on pg 68… Why?! Additionally, this chaos would make it way too much of a hassle to use that during a session when you have to go through the whole book in the hopes of hitting upon the image somewhere. Making use of those visual aids therefore demands some prep where you copy-paste the images from the pdf, so that they’re easier to go through rather than trying to find them in the book by chance.

    One thing the book also does is that during certain scenes that revolve around interacting with a character, you get these questionnaires with example-questions and what answers said NPC would give. I think it’s a bit of a mixed bag in how helpful it is. The first issue is that the questions are usually very dry and direct. It’s unlikely a player will ask a question that way and then an ST could just read the answer listed in the book verbatim. So, for example, take this bit from the page about interacting with Melissa:

    How long have you been working at the fulfillment center?
    Almost a year. It sucks because I got my work-from home job so I could have a more flexible schedule. Sammy’s got a lot of stuff going on. It’s nice to be able to pick him up from school, take him to Little League, and other af ter-school stuff. But it all costs money, so I started working warehouse shifts to be able to afford all that. Other wise, I’m afraid he’s going to melt into the couch and play video games all day.

    Any variations on the questions could mean that this answer has to be phrased differently or that bits of flavor from this answer could be useful in answering a different question. Making use of that on the fly during a session could be a bit of a challenge. Then there’s also the problem that occasionally there’s overlap between answers where I feel like the ST would unintentionally end up repeating themselves by sticking too close to the answers that are listed in the book which would make any dialogue feel less natural.

    Now let’s get to the first adventure. The structure is one of those pearl-beads. There are usually two different routes offered that both lead to the same event. And that process repeats. So, structurally it’s pretty straightforward and all three adventures are very reliant on having a particular NPC guide you through the adventure. There’s always at least one NPC who will give directions to the players as to what they should do and what their goals are supposed to be. Here in the first chapter it’s Mother Louise who is the one Garou you will interact with. Until the rival Pack shows up in the third chapter, there are no other Garou around.

    The first adventure is about Val-MOR corporation which is a mix of Amazon and Walmart. They have a fulfilment-center a la Amazon in Milton but they also have a big-store supermarket a la Walmart in the town. But the warehouse is the focus here. I’m not sure why the supermarket-component was even relevant but okay…

    The corporation is tied to Pentex but it isn’t that Val-MOR is producing Wyrm-tainted stuff or anything. It’s just your usual greedy mega-corporation on that front. Where the Wyrm gets involved is that Val-MOR’s profits have been dropping in the last few years and so Pentex has sent a “Risk Management Specialist” to Milton who’s of course a loyal follower of the Wyrm. And he has this special project that’s supposed to “enhance” workers who struggle – but instead it’s slowly turning them into insectoid zombie-husks/Fomori.

    I mean, as far as plans go, it’s a really stupid one, I gotta say… Sure, these zombie-husks are great monsters to throw at werewolves but now you can only put them to work during the night-shift and make sure that no normal person sees these mutated Fomori. And these Fomori are of course far less capable than an actual human at doing normal work. Not sure how that’s supposed to enhance the performance of the warehouse.

    If I were to run this, I would present that Pentex-guy as a bit of a buffoon who slowly realizes that the spirits he employs to create the Fomori aren’t exactly very interested in helping some corporation increase their profits but instead are more interested in infecting more people and spreading corruption.

    What the chapter is really good at, though, is to consider a lot of options when it comes to how you could get into the warehouse and what sort of scenes you can turn that into. There’s a lot of consideration in offering multiple paths. Of course, never say never, especially when it comes to what players decide to do but the chapter offers a good foundation in running this adventure, I think.

    Chapter Three: Shadows On The Prison Wall

    At this point, I think it’s clear that W5’s decision to strip away Garou’s natural ability to travel to the Umbra and make it an optional Rite instead was a dumb decision. The Umbra is an integral part of WtA and that is still the case in W5. And since this chapter focuses heavily on the Umbra as you can occasionally expect from a WtA-adventure, I guess, you will always get passages like this at the start: “If none of the Garou know the Rite of Shadow Passage, Mother Louise offers to teach it to a member of the pack, or (if they lack the experience points to learn a Rite) to invoke it on their behalf. Being able to travel in and out of the Umbra of their own volition is a huge benefit in this story, so if a player wants to learn this rite immediately and pay it off with experience points at the end of the story, let them.” You know what? At that point, just give the Rite to all the PCs and don’t make them buy it with XP. There’s no reason to have the game make STs and players jump through hoops like this because of a bad game-design-decision. Just give it to everyone for free and now nobody needs to figure out what to do with PC-Packs that can’t go into the Umbra because no PC has that particular Rite. It’s unnecessary busywork. But I guess, it will needlessly continue to eat up wordcount in future books of the W5 line.

    This chapter is about the group encountering a particular spirit of stories in the Umbra who calls himself the Wizened Raconteur. The introduction is pretty cool because this spirit explains to the Garou that the Umbra is suffering and that there are four different challenges the PCs have to complete to improve things. He’s very vague in what he wants the Garou to do… BUT in exchange for stories, he will give the PCs more hints. The only issue I could see here is that if you have a starting-group who really only have interacted with that first adventure, the PCs don’t have a lot of stories to share really.

    Structurally it’s also interesting because the four challenges correspond to that axis-map from pg 212 in the W5 corebook. So you get this: “‘A Hint of Malice’ is an individual active antagonist scene; ‘Polyphemus Security Solutions’ is a systemic active antagonist scene; ‘Reaching for the Sky’ is a passive individual scene; and finally ‘The Suffering’ is a passive systemic scene.” These can be tackled in any order.

    The first three all have an interesting gimmick. Hint of Malice has you battle against a spirit that gets stronger the more dots in Rage your character has. I think I would have to see that in action how well this actually works. But it’s an interesting idea. Polyphemus Security Solutions has you go up against a security-company that is tied to Pentex. What’s interesting here is that it really builds on the idea that there are approaches/solutions to problems based on the three Renown-categories. To earn Glory, you just go in and beat them all up or kill them to drive the security-firm out of town. To earn Wisdom, you sneak in, hack their computers, you sabotage their equipment and you use social manipulation to set the people working for the firm against each other. To earn Honor, you rally the local business owners and local activists to protest the firm who then will voluntarily leave. Now what you might already notice in what I describe here is the book talking about actions taken by players in these VERY broad outlines. We’ll get back to that in a bit… The third adventure is about freeing a spirit that’s at the top of the Milton clock tower in the Umbra. And what’s interesting here is that depending on how you free the spirits, its nature changes. So, in what the players choose to do, they’re shaping the form of the spirit.

    And then there’s the fourth adventure “The Suffering”… I mean, they kinda wrote themselves into that corner by wanting to adhere to that axis-model from the W5 corebook. The example from the corebook for “passive systemic” is climate change. Try packing that into a little adventure… And this book’s solution is to simply don’t. Conceptually you can kinda see why it’s like that but just pragmatically in what it expects STs to do it’s just half-assed. What this section proposes is that there’s this list of systemic issues in Milton: unhoused people, racism, far right extremists, bad politicans… And your PC group is supposed to fix two of them… somehow. Here’s an example: “Aiding the unhoused: Milton has a small community of unhoused people, centered on a tent city on the outskirts of town. Local politicians consider unhoused people a blight on the town and give the police wide leeway to displace and arrest them, as well as all but eliminating resources to help them. Garou can strike up relationships with the unhoused, support local initiatives to provide housing and assistance, or just gift them a few warm meals. This is an especially relevant approach for Bone Gnawers, who often live in similar hardscrabble circumstances.” Damn, provide them housing…?! Gift them a few warm meals…?! I’m sure, nobody has ever thought of that before! This is gonna change everything!

    The big problem here is that what’s outlined there isn’t even a story-seed. And just making the PCs spend hours in a soup-kitchen with nothing extraordinary happening doesn’t add anything of value to the game. You first have to turn this into a story. For example, there’s a Fomor-landlord who has come in and has been buying up buildings and then has thrown out tenants for spurious reasons which has led to the growth of the unhoused population. So, the Garou take out the Fomor, work with human allies who come in, get the building back and get the unhoused people off the street. Quick and easy. But what this fourth adventure does here is so barebones and half-assed that it creates more work for STs in trying to make this work rather than making their job easier.

    Then the chapter ends with the Wizened Raconteur turning on the PCs and attacking them. Him becoming an enemy suddenly isn’t as interesting as the book seems to think it is in my opinion. It’s a weird spirit that you do work for. Since you’ve been doing good stuff in the course of these four short adventures, I guess most people would assume the spirit is a good guy as well. I think the book does enough in helping STs foreshadow it, especially when players offer stories to him in exchange for hints/help. Because as the book notes, this spirit especially loves stories that involve a lot of pain, suffering and despair. But it is a weird spirit at the end of the day. I guess this is another thing where the book is really putting a heavy burden on the ST to make this work.

    There’s an interesting gimmick to this fight as well as the spirit is shaping the Umbra around them, creating Bane spirits that attack the PCs and turning the environment against them by constantly narrating what is going on/will happen. But the players can interrupt his narration with narration of their own that the spirit has to accept. So you can do stuff like narrate that his allies turn against him or use his Umbra-shaping abilities against him.

    Chapter Four: A Pound of Flesh

    This is essentially the finale and if you follow the advice in the book, the ST has been consistently dropping hints that until very recently there has been a Garou Pack called the Rakshaks in Milton who just suddenly disappeared. But now they’re back.

    It’s… weird. Like in the first adventure where I thought the plan of the Pentex-guy didn’t seem especially well-thought-out, I have similar feelings about what’s going on with the Rakshaks.

    Let me just quote from the book what the big idea is: “The decay of Milton was theirs to handle, and Horned Serpent’s support hinged on the Rakshaks managing it just as the spirit required. Milton was meant to be a controlled learning ground. The Rakshaks were to watch from a distance and determine what would happen if Val-MOR got their way, study how they operate, and find weaknesses they could exploit to fight corporate evil elsewhere. A petri dish, if you will.”

    And the chapter starts with the PCs finding the nearly dead body of Mother Louise because she learned of what Rakshaks were doing and she intentionally recruited the PC-Pack to thwart this plan(without informing the PCs, though, about what was really going on, though… for whatever reason). It’s unclear to me from how the text describes it whether this plan came from the Horned Serpent or from the Ghost Council Garou Ridhaan. The Horned Serpent expects Ridhaan to acquire more knowledge about the Wyrm and the Horned Serpent even is angry with Mother Louise interrupting Ridhaan’s plan. But also, the plan makes little sense. Val-MOR is a normal greedy corporation. If that Pentex guy hadn’t shown up, nothing overtly supernatural would have occurred there. And in trying to dismantle Pentex’s efforts or confronting a spirits, you learn plenty about them. How is watching from a distance the better way? How many weakpoints will even get exposed if your enemy can just do whatever they want?

    Anyway, eventually the PCs track down the Rakshaks who are in the process of summoning the Horned Serpent. And you learn immediately that Ridhaan is afraid of losing the Horned Serpent’s favor because of how effective the PCs have been in making Milton a better place. The Horned Serpent is pissed off as well when it appears. It says: ““I will have an explanation for the affronts against my domain. Both this night and the previous. I shall hear three speak from each side. Rakshaks, you shall speak first.”

    You know, I always wondered about that writeup of the Ghost Council in the W5 corebook. If you took it literally, it made me wonder why the Ghost Council weren’t considered another fallen Tribe like the Cult of Fenris. I think what this adventure is echoing is this part from the W5 corebook’s section on the Ghost Council (pg 67 – 68): “ ‘The ends justify the means’ usually comes across as a dangerous political doctrine, but for the Ghost Council, finding an answer or opportunity excuses how it’s accomplished. [ … ] As might be expected, other Garou often view the werewolves of the Ghost Council as having suspect motives. A fair criticism is that Ghost Council concern themselves with solving the “how” of mysteries for the sake of power, with too little regard for the reasons such power manifests in their means of discovery. The pack has the rival’s klaive — that’s what’s important. Results now, costs later.” Whoever wrote this part of the book took this description from the W5 corebook to heart. But that’s why I always liked the version from JF Sambrano more. Why would anybody put up with these guys? A whole faction that is dedicated to “the ends justify the means”?! Why would anybody trust these guys…

    So, anyway, back to the adventure… The Horned Serpent wants to hear explanations for what has happened. The book mentions three arguments that PCs can roll for that rely on Garou-traditions. The first one, interestingly, is to invoke the Litany: “Mother Louise broke one tenet of the Litany — Respect the Territory of Another — but the Rakshaks broke the very first tenet by failing to Combat the Wyrm Wherever It Dwells and Whenever It Thrives.” This just highlights, though, why the whole plan is weird. Also, if the Horned Serpent was okay with the plan, then it should be okay with Garou ignoring the “Combat the Wyrm etc.”-tenet of the Litany. This shouldn’t be a persuasive argument to the Horned Serpent. The second argument is that Mother Louise should’ve been judged by the Sept rather than being attacked by the Rakshaks. And the third argument is that the PC-Pack didn’t know anything about what was going on. I guess, that one counts. Also, in my mind that brought up another issue in my mind. If the Rakshaks were so invested in watching from the distance in order to acquire knowledge, why didn’t they contact the PC-Pack as soon as they started to interfere with the situation in Milton? Why did they wait this long?

    And even after all that, the Horned Serpent decides that a contest between the two Packs will decide who it sides with. What an asshole! The Horned Serpent just thinks both the Rakshaks and the PC-Pack lack Wisdom, so neither has a right to the territory. The contest is decided by which Pack recruits a Kin in Milton.

    That particular Kin is Gabriella, an artist, who is at the opening of a gallery. Well, shenanigans ensue. Turns out the art of the gallery is used by a particularly nasty Bane Spirit to strengthen itself and find a way to invade the real world. Fighting breaks out and it’s just in this moment that Gabriella undergoes First Change. She’s in Crinos and fights the Rakshaks and the PC-Pack. Depending on how this develops, you’ve got a variety of different endings: Whether Gabriella survives, which Pack she joins (there’s also the option where she asks the Horned Serpent for a third option and it takes her into the Umbra never to be seen again) and whether Ridhaan will lose the support of the Horned Serpent. Also, if the players do especially well, the Horned Serpent heals Mother Louise.

    There’s a weird bit in this chapter as well where one of the Rakshak-Garou, Saif, who’s a Silver Fang secretly meets with the PCs to bribe them. He tells them that he has connections and he can set them up with a different bit of territory to watch. All they have to do is just walk away and let Ridhaan repair his relationship with the Horned Serpent. I don’t know… this just feels a bit off to me. The tone of this scene is just so mundane and modern. It’s the Age of Apocalypse, the Garou Nation has fallen and this guy is running around trying to bribe a Pack of Garou by offering them a territory to defend somewhere far away that he somehow can give them. This is also the only time where the chagrin-mechanic comes up. That’s the mechanic where the ST judges a character for doing something that hurts their Renown and then their Renown gets reduced until they did something to redeem themselves. What’s new here is the idea that if you suffer chagrin that would take your Renown theoretically into the negatives, the PC gets the Adversary Flaw instead because now (somehow) a whole bunch of Garou know that your character has fucked up.

    Conclusion

    There are parts of this Chronicle Book, I really liked. It had some interesting ideas for spirits to battle and how to interact with player-choices. But I think the biggest problem I have with the book is that it isn’t a good starter-chronicle. Both in the sense of being played by people with no experience with WtA and having a ST who’s just a beginner. Especially the latter. I think there are a lot more challenges that a ST gets burdened with in this book that at first glance might not be apparent. The foreshadowing and the callbacks, for example. Absolutely necessary. In the course of these three adventures you end up assembling a whole cast of side-characters. Like the horse-spirit the PCs meet early on in the first adventure. If a player is attached to that character, the book offers little guidance on what they’re up to in a general sense. It’s easy to imagine that players will come up with way more instances of getting these NPCs involved than the few instances that occasionally get mentioned. And as mentioned, occasionally the descriptions in the book get very broad. Playing out what is described will take far more time than the way the book talks about it. Even though the whole point of a book like this one is to help STs with that minutiae.

    From a story-standpoint, I think the first adventure is okay, I quite like most of the second adventure (except for the fourth little mission you do) but I don’t like the third one. It just feels so harebrained. That Pack with this whole “watching from the distance”-thing only getting involved after everything has already been turned around, Mother Louis not really warning the PC-Pack about what was actually going on (that she was using the PC Pack as pawns for her personal rivalry), the Rakshaks suddenly attacking Mother Louis and then immediately getting a heavyweight like the Horned Serpent involved. It all just feels like a long series of bad ideas. And the worst part: It has nothing to do with the PCs. It all just happens to them. They weren’t there when Mother Louis was attacked, they can’t prevent the Horned Serpent from getting involved and everything else happened before the PCs even arrived in Milton. It’s a bit of a letdown.

    There are bright spots in here but the book certainly doesn’t succeed in being a good introduction-chronicle for beginners. There are a lot of hidden expectations in the descriptions of this book where the ST is expected to carry the weight of making it all work. This is certainly not something you can ask of STs who are beginners!

                                                      The way it seems to me is that what belonging to a Tribe in W5 means is that you have an individual Garou. And because of that individual's qualities, goals and/or methods, they're able to enter a pact with a Patron-Spirit. When the Spirit agrees, they technically become a member of that Tribe. But in W5 there aren't any inherent shared values and perspectives between members of a Tribe. They're all just Garou who were individually able to make a pact with the same Patron Spirit.

                                                      What doesn't fit, though, is the Horned Serpent's involvement and how it's portrayed in this book. To quote from the book: "For Horned Serpent’s support, the Rakshaks must feed their Patron Spirit knowledge. They’re expected to squeeze every bit of knowledge out of a situation so they may best apply it to future conflicts. As such, the pack’s bond with Horned Serpent demands that they let the decay in Milton progress until they’ve learned everything they can from it, then defeat it themselves." That's a relationship between Garou and a Patron Spirit that's much more forceful than how I would portray it in my games. Here in this book, the PCs are supposed to take it as a victory that they thwarted a non-fallen tribal Patron-Spirit's plans. I guess, you really have to take it as this just being A Horned Serpent Spirit, not THE Horned Serpent Spirit attached to the Ghost Council Tribe.

                                                      After playing Book of Hungry Names, I also thought what would be fitting for the W5 setting is if the majority of werewolves identify themselves as Garou who have made pacts with various Spirits for Gifts but due to a lack of opportunity and/or knowledge haven't made a pact with any tribal Patron-Spirit. Most of these Garou act alone with the support of human allies. And whole Packs of Garou are a rarity and even rarer are Packs of Garou who have made pacts with Patron-Spirits. That way the setting also feels more like the Garou-society is a fallen one.
