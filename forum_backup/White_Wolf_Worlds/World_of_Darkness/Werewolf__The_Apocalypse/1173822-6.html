    THE CHEMICAL INDUSTRY

    So, curiously enough, there are a pair of not-really-canon companies mentioned in Warriors of the Apocalypse, the collection of character stats for the Rage CCG cards: Vista-Abraxas Chemical Corporation and Cyadine Industrial Chemicals & Food Processing. The names are kind of ridiculous, likely just made up on the fly by whomever wrote the Fomori characters.

    Anyway, any discussion of the chemical industry revolves around two companies, du Pont and Dow.

    Du Pont was founded in Deleware in 1802 as a gunpowder manufacturing business, moving into making dynamite and nitroglycerine before being broken up into three companies by the Sherman Anti-Trust Act in 1912. The main company continued working with explosives while also branching into materials science. In the 20th century, they and their scientists created neoprene, nylon and teflon all before WW2, and continued with mylar, lycra, kevlar and other synthetic wonder materials in the Cold War. They've dabbled/diversified into the automobile and petroleum industries as well, although not for very long. At the start of the 21st century, they acquired the Pioneer Hi-Bred company, which makes GMO seeds for agricultural crops, and are now working on various strains of corn and soy beans, among other things. In terms of the things we think of when we think PENTEX, they're often cited as being leaders in carbon footprint reduction (for what it's worth), but are also cited for major air pollution and for OSHA safety violations (including the release of toxic chemicals, sometimes with fatalities to employees). They were also co-developers of the CFC refrigerants and aerosols that were blamed for the damage to the ozone layer back in the 80s and 90s. They've also pled guilty to price fixing charges.

    Most books about du Pont date back to the 70s and focus on the family as much as the company. I've not read any of them because they're hard to find. However, Gerald Colby's Du Pont Dynasty: Beyond the Nylon Curtain is available in ebook form. Others include Leonard Mosley's Blood Relations: The Rise & Fall of the du Ponts of Delaware, Mark Duke's The du Ponts: Portrait of a Dynasty, Alfred Chandler and Stephen Salisbury's Pierre S. Du Pont and the Making of the Modern Corporation, John Gates's The Du Pont Family, and Max Dorian's The Du Ponts: From Gunpowder to Nylon. And in the realm of expensive text books, there is Pap A. Ndiaya's Nylon and Bombs: DuPont and the March of Modern America.

    Of all of du Pont's famous products, only nylon really has any books about it, usually regarding it's impact on the world of fashion. Susannah Handley's Nylon: The Story of a Fashion Revolution and Ronnie Price's Man Made Magic - When Science Meets Fashion. Tangentially, there's also a graphic novel about the 1974 Flixborough nylon factory disaster, Nylon Years by Ramin Abhari.

    Finally, James Ferraro's Blindsided is the true story of a court case against du Pont over birth defects allegedly caused by one of their fungicides.

    Dow Chemical Company was founded in Michigan in 1897 to take advantage of local bromide deposits to manufacture bleach. They quickly diversified into making chlorine, magnesium, agricultural chemicals and dyes, before really hitting their stride with plastics in the 1930s. During the Vietnam conflict, they became infamous as the makers of both napalm and Agent Orange. Much more recently, there were the problems with their breast implants back in the 1990s. In the 21st century, they mostly dealt in plastics, styrofoam, insecticides, paint, basic chemicals and GMO crop seeds. Jack Doyle's Trespass Against Us: Dow Chemical & The Toxic Century is a fairly extensive overview of the company's problems over the 20th century.

    For those interested in Agent Orange and it's history, see Fred Wilcox's Waiting for an Army to Die, Edwin Martini's Agent Orange: History, Politics and the Politics of Uncertainty, and Peter Sills's Toxic War: The Story of Agent Orange. Robert Allan's The Dioxin War is a look at the history and effects of one of AO's active ingredients. Regarding napalm, see Robert Neer's Napalm: An American Biography. (For a rather graphic demonstration of the devastation of napalm and white phosphorus weapons in combat, see the 2002 film We Were Soldiers.) For those running chronicles in the Amazon, PENTEX likely has tried using both against the Garou and Fera in the rain forests.

    Back in 2001, Dow purchased the Union Carbide company, who were responsible for the gas leak disaster in Bhopal, India. It is one of the world's worst industrial disasters, killing several thousand people. Books on the subject include Dominique Lapierre and Javier Moro's Five Past Midnight in Bhopal, Themistocles D'Silva's The Black Box of Bhopal, and The Bhopal Reader anthology. There's also a Hindi language film, Bhopal Express (1999) which I've never seen.

    And, last year, the two companies merged into one giant chemical behemoth, DowDuPont.

    More generally, there's the area of pesticides and other chemicals of the sort. Fredrick Davis's Banned: A History of Pesticides and the Science of Toxicology is a good overview of the subject.

    I've not read Johnathan Harr's A Civil Action, the true story of a law suit over chemical contamination of drinking water in Massachusetts, but the 1998 film adaption with John Travolta isn't bad.

        THE FITNESS INDUSTRY


        Physical fitness has become a big business over the past few decades, and it's a market that's always been ripe for exploitation by something like PENTEX. While the ancient Greek idea of the gymnasium never entirely went away, it wasn't until the 19th century that the idea of physical fitness as an ideal rather than a practical thing really came back in force. The idea was known as Muscular Christianity, and is one of those sort of curious bits of culture from that era. This was part of what gave birth to the YMCA/YWCA. Bodybuilding became a big thing in the late Victorian Era (and can make an interesting/odd part of a Victorian Vampire or Werewolf: The Wild West chronicle) and had another resurgence in the 1950s and 60s. The bodybuilding culture continued to be a big thing in California, especially LA's Muscle Beach area. The 1970s and 80s also saw a boom in aerobics and other activities not focused on building mass, as well as franchise gyms such as Gold's Gym, Planet Fitness, 24 Hour Fitness and many other brands that have come and gone over the decades.

        Eric Chaline's The Temple of Perfection: A History of the Gym is a good overview of the subject, as does Daniel Kunitz's Lift: Fitness Culture from Naked Greeks and Acrobats to Jazzercise and Ninja Warriors. There's also Adrian Tan and Doug Brignole's Million Dollar Muscle: A Historical and Sociological Perspective of the Fitness Industry, but it's a college textbook and so ridiculously overpriced. Pumping Iron (1977) is an interesting documentary about bodybuilding in LA at the time, and introduced the world to a young Arnold Schwarzenegger.

        One aspect of fitness is the issue of body image. While the issue of body image among females is talked about quite a bit, less common is discussion about the issue with males. Harrison Pope, Katharine Philips and Roberto Olivardia's The Adonis Complex gives a good coverage of it. Philips has also written a number of other books on body image disorders, for those with an interest in the subject.


        A big part of the bodybuilding and fitness world has been anabolic steroids and other chemical enhancers. Shaun Assael's Steroid Nation is ten years old at this point, but does give a good in depth look at the problem in the sports world, including high schools. Also the documentary Bigger Stronger Faster (2008) and a kind of strange low budget work called Anabolic Life (2018). Weirdly, there are a lot - and I mean a lot - of books out there telling people how to use steroids and other enhancements, including ones specifically for female athletes. I find this a little unsettling. (For those wondering what steroid abuse tends to do to the female body, Thomas Harris's Hannibal goes into rather graphic detail about the subject in regards to one minor character.)

        The other bit part of the modern fitness industry is over the counter supplements, vitamins, weight loss aids, and other items. GNC/General Nutrition Centers, The Vitamin Shoppe, and other retail outlets specialize in these things. Brian Clements's Supplements Exposed goes over some of the problems of the industry.

        The old book Freak Legion gave us Dr. Viridian's Workout Program and it's resulting Throwbacks breed. This was sort of a parody of the old Charles Atlas ads from the back of magazines, comics and the like in the 40s and 50s. When Grant Morrison was writing Doom Patrol back in the 80s, he introduced the character of Flex Mentalo, who was literally an Atlas ad come to life and possessing the secret powers of "muscle mystery". It was deeply weird, and more of a Mage oriented thing, but still cool, and would make for a really weird spirit entity for a pack to cross paths with. More savage is Justin Jordan and Tradd Moore's graphic novel The Strange Talent of Luther Strode, in which a young man starts doing an Atlas style body building course only to find himself turning into a brutal super warrior who can kill people with his bare hands.

        There's also the realm of fitness modeling, which I can't really find any books on at the moment, but does make me wonder about the possibility of a Fomor breed that's a cross between an Enticer and Throwback.

        Freak Legion mentions the workout program as likely having a short shelf life as the resulting Throwbacks are so unsubtle that people will make the connection between the two very quickly. I like to believe that PENTEX abandoned said program years ago in favor of a new subsidiary, Viridian Fitness, a chain of gyms and supplement stores that encourage body image problems, locker room steroid sales, potentially dangerous supplements and all the worst aspects of gym culture in both men and women.

        (Given that I've long enjoyed naming and developing subcultures of Ratkin after any popular use of "rat" in a description - swamp rat, tunnel rat, sea rat, etc. - I'm now wondering about the feasibility of a bunch of workout and steroid obsessed Ratkin gym rats.)

        Next, hotel/hospitality and funerals, in some order.

            And after a long, busy and annoying two weeks ...

            The Funerary Industry

            Or as they like to call themselves, "the Death Care Industry". Said industry breaks down into three main branches: funeral homes, cemeteries, and the manufacturing end of things that make caskets, headstones and all the other stuff associated with modern mourning. Overall, it's a multi-billion dollar a year industry (nearly $15B in 2016) in the United States, and the four biggest companies own roughly 20% of the funeral homes in the US (the rest are all small family owned businesses). Since the 1970s, the federal government, in the form of the Federal Trade Commission, has kept an eye on the industry to try to keep them from taking advantage of customers (as most people have little experience with death planning and are under temporal and emotional strains when purchasing services). The Funeral Rule is the FTC's guidelines for all of this.

            One of the biggest "death care" companies in North America is Service Corporation International, based out of Houston. The operate about 1500 funeral homes in the US, Canada and Puerto Rico. They've had their fair share of scandals, including alleged price gouging, overselling cemetery plots (resulting in stacking bodies on top of each other, mislabeling grave sites, and digging up the older bodies and tossing them into the near by woods), and improper storage of bodies awaiting embalming. Other places have had worse scandals. One of the most infamous is the Tri-State Crematory in Georgia, which, instead of cremating bodies, just dumped a few hundred of them in the woods or stuffed them in sheds around the property. (I live less than an hour from the place, and this insanity was all over the local news for months; every time you'd turn around, it just got more and more weird.) The place ended up being inspiration for episodes of Law & Order: Criminal Intent and CSI: Miami, as well as the major inspiration for the sections written by Geoff Grabowski in the nWoD/CoD books Ghost Stories and Mysterious Places. (Both are pretty good and I've found can easily be adapted into Werewolf.) There's also a film, Sahkanaga (2011), that uses the incident as the back drop for it's story. Another infamous scandal was the Lamb Funeral Home in LA, a good retelling of which can be found here. Basically, the guy was harvesting organs, doing mass cremations, and a bunch of other shifty activities.

            Bookwise, the first go to about the funerary industry is Jessica Mitford's The American Way of Death Revisited, a 2000 updating of the 1963 original that helped spark the FTC's initial interest in the industry. Mark Harris's Grave Matters is a look at the modern "green burial" movement, much of which is probably of use for considerations of Garou funeral customs. There's also a number of books written by industry veterans about some of the weirder or more philosophical areas of the business. Tom Jokinen's Curtains is a good example, as is Caitlin Doughty's Smoke Gets in Your Eyes. (Doughty's also written a book about funeral customs around the globe, From Here to Eternity, that's really pretty fascinating.)

            I mention illicit organ harvesting with the Lamb case, and it's a major international black market, one PENTEX certainly exploits to buy and sell. Annie Cheney's Body Brokers and Scott Carney's The Red Market go into a lot of the morbid details on the subject. (Red Market especially gets into a lot of creepy illegal trades including ones that could easily lead a pack into conflict with vampires and other beings.) Also the 2014 documentary film Human Harvest, about the activities of the Chinese government, which may be of interest to those running Beast Court chronicles.

            Fictionwise, in the realm of funeral homes, there's a lot to go with, and some of it all over the place. I have never seen the tv series Six Feet Under, but it has a lot of fans. Death Becomes Her (1992) is a lot of fun, not least of which for Bruce Willis's plastic surgeon turned reconstructive mortician who admits to using spray paint to make up corpses. More on the horror side, one of the high points of the original Final Destination (2000) film was Tony Todd as a creepy mortician. Even better is Clarence Williams III in Tales From The Hood (1995). And Don Calfa in Return of the Living Dead (1985) is also great. And, as I've mentioned before, I'm a big fan of the old Friday the 13th The Series tv show, and it had an episode (#64/S3E12, "Epitaph for a Lonely Soul") with a mentally unbalanced mortician who came into possession of an aspirator which can literally suck the life out of a person and then bring a dead body to life. But the all time best inspiration for evil funeral homes is the classic Phantasm (1979) and it's antagonist, The Tall Man.

            I must give a shout out to glamourweaver and his Montaigne Services Group subsidiary. Voodoo style necromancy is a good hook for a PENTEX funeral company. Another are the ghouls from various parts of the Lovecraft Mythos. Brian Keene's novel Ghoul is a good read about such a creature in a local cemetery. Also, Dr. Herbert West of Re-Animator (1985) fame.

                        Hotels and Hospitality Industry

                        The hospitality industry is kind of a broad term, but for the purposes of this post, I'm mainly focusing on the realm of hotels, both luxury ones and major chains, and motels, as well as theme park resorts and cruise ships.

                        What we tend to think of as the luxury hotel business started in the 19th century, and one of the major creators of the industry we Cesar Ritz of Switzerland. He helped establish a number of major hotels in Europe, including Savoy Hotel in London. Luke Barr's Ritz and Escoffier: The Hotelier, The Chef and The Rise of the Leisure Class details the creation of the Savoy and its social impact, but is too recent for me to have been able to read yet. There's also Tilar J. Mazerro's The Hotel on Place Vendome about the history of the Ritz Paris, especially during WW2. Another big name were the Astors, with their Waldorf Astoria in New York still being a major city landmark. Justin Kaplan's When the Astors Owned New York is a good look at the family's long history and their roll in Gilded Age luxury hotels. Also there is Meryl Gordon's Mrs. Astor Regrets, about the fate of philanthropist Brooke Astor and the legal scandals about her mistreatment by her son in her twilight years. It's the sort of back drop that can work well for Victorian and WW2 games (maybe more so for Vampire and Mage, but for Werewolf as well). The really cool thing about a lot of these places, from a fictional standpoint, especially in the WoD, is that because of their age, they make a great place for hauntings and other weirdness. Stephen King's short story 1408 (as well as the really good 2007 film adaption) is a good example. Even more so is his classic The Shining (which is the subject of two adaptions, Stanley Kubrick's 1980 version and the more faithful to the book 1997 miniseries). And then there is American Horror Story: Hotel, for a look at the LA side of things. (Especially fitting is the idea of hotel as death trap, which comes from HH Holmes's Chicago hotel, the subject of Erik Larson's Devil in the White City.)

                        As far as modern hotel chains go, among the biggest in North America are Hilton Worldwide and Marriott International. Hilton was started by Conrad Hilton and his family is still involved with the company. Randy Taraborelli's The Hiltons is an overview of the family's history, up to and including celebutante Paris Hilton. Conrad Hilton's own autobiography is titled Be My Guest. Most books about Marriott are about founder JW Marrott, including his own autobiography Without Reservations. There's nothing all that shady about the industry, just that they build a lot of buildings all over the place, contributing to suburban sprawl, which is a major negative for Garou, especially when it starts to encroach on their Caerns and Protectorates.

                        In fact, the classic motel industry is dying off in a lot of the United States entirely because of hotel chains like Hilton and Marriott building small low budget traveler hotels next to freeways and interstates. If anything, this sense of age and isolation just adds to their horror potential. Robert Bloch's Psycho is a fun read, and the 1960 Hitchcock film adaption is a classic. Ian Fleming's The Spy Who Loved Me also offers up some potential plot ideas (it's one of the only Bond stories to be told entirely from the female lead's point of view; the film of the same name has nothing to do with the book). There's also the more recent No Vacancy (2007) and the science fiction miniseries The Lost Room.

                        Las Vegas Hotels are almost a world of their own. They've evolved into full fledged resorts. Modern Marvels has an episode devoted to them, but it's from over a decade ago and so is a little dated. Oceans 13 (2007) is an entertaining look at a lot of the behind the scenes goings on at such a place. Oceans 11 (2001) shows some interesting bits as well. Both film's villains also work well as inspiration for PENTEX executives. More historically, Mario Puzo's The Godfather looks at the city and it's mob influences in the 1960s and 70s.

                        As far as theme park resorts, the two most famous are probably the Disney empire and Universal Studios, both tied to major TV/Film companies (ie OMNI). There's also Busch Gardens/SeaWorld, with it's ties to Anheuser-Busch (ie King). Most material about Disney and Universal tend to be put out by their own press companies. But there is some interesting stuff out there. The YouTube channel Defunctland has a number of videos about closed down and abandoned parts of these theme parks and the history behind them. Bright Sun Films also looks at abandoned places, including studio theme park attractions such as Nickelodeon Studios and Port Disney. (They also look at things like abandoned shopping malls, buildings and other interesting places that would make for cool WoD settings.) Offhand Disney and Yesterworld Entertainment cover similar topics. But from a Werewolf perspective, one of the biggest issues is environmental damage and sprawl, both of which the theme parks heavily contribute to in Orlando and it's surrounding areas. Carl Hiaasan's Team Rodent: How Disney Devours the World is a good read on the subject, even if it is from about 20 years ago. (Hiaasen is a long time columnist for the Miami Herald and a fairly prolific writer of somewhat absurd crime thrillers set in South Florida, all of which I highly recommend for anyone wanting to run a Werewolf game in the state; his collected newspaper columns are also well worth a read.)

                        Finally, with cruise lines, one of the biggest is Carnival Corporation, owners of Carnival Cruise Line, Carnival Australia, Princess Cruises, and others. The cruise industry has gotten a fair amount of bad press over the past decade or so, including the incredibly low wages it allegedly pays it's staff, mechanical problems, and the infamous Poop Cruise. Still, it could've been worse. at least the ship didn't get turned upside down by a tidal wave (The Poseidon Adventure (1972)/Poseidon (2006)), eaten by sea monsters (Deep Rising (1998)), or murdered by a grim reaper (Ghost Ship (2002)).

                                MAGADON, INCORPERATED (Big Pharma)

                                This comic pretty much says it all.

                                One of PENTEX's biggest companies (perhaps even bigger now, as Subsidiaries indicated that PENTEX may have had future plans for them to buy out August Health; I've actually done this in my games, with the new company being Magadon Medical & Pharmaceutical). They are one of the six companies covered in the classic Subsidiaries: A Guide to PENTEX, which is well worth a read, as it covers their history, various divisions and products, as well as having rules for various chemical weapons and how they effect Garou and Fera.

                                The pharmaceutical industry is very large and makes a lot of money. The types of medications are many, and their development is often a long and complicated process. Laurence T. Friedhoff's New Drugs: An Insiders Guide to the FDA's Approval Process is a good and fairly easy to understand overview of the process (at least in the United States), as it's written for people not immersed in the industry, especially potential investors and patients/consumers.

                                From a historical standpoint, medicine and it's various treatments have, at times, been very weird. Two books, Nathan Belofsky's Strange Medicine: A Shocking History of Real Medical Practices Through the Ages and Lydia Kang and Nate Pedersen's Quackery: A Brief History of the Worst Ways to Cure Everything, are an interesting and at times amusing look at some of the stranger parts of medical history. I recommend them for historical games (especially if, say, a pack needs to rescue one of their Kin from the "modern" medical establishment).

                                While pharmaceutical companies have been around for a long time, new ones are coming along fairly regularly, some of them trying new processes and ideas that the established ones haven't focused one. One of those, back in the late 80s, was Vertex Pharmaceuticals, one of the first to focus on computer aided drug design and bimolecular targeting. Their creation and growth were documented in two books by Berry Werth, The Billion Dollar Molecule: One Company's Quest for the Perfect Drug and The Antidote: Inside the World of New Pharma. Both books give an interesting inside look at the industry back in the 80s and again in this decade, and, in addition to possible ideas for Magadon, can also offer inspiration for possible PC founded companies (by Glasswalkers, or other groups such as the Virtual Adepts).

                                Regarding "Big Pharma's" dirty laundry, one of the oldest looks at the subject is Corporate Crime in the Pharmaceutical Industry by John Braithwaite. Written in 1984, the book was ridiculously hard to find, allegedly because pharmaceutical companies bought up as many copies as they could to keep them out of public hands. (I've no idea if that is true or not, but makes an interesting plot hook for almost any PENTEX company.) A newer edition is easy to find, but on the pricey side. Braithwaite co-wrote a followup in 2014, Pharmaceuticals, Corporate Crime and Public Health, which is also fairly expensive to get a hold of, as both seem more aimed at industry professionals than the casual reader. (Even the Kindle versions are $50+.) Easier to find books include Jacky Law's Big Pharma: Exposing the Global Healthcare Agenda, Ben Goldacre's Bad Pharma: How Drug Companies Mislead Doctors and Harm Patients, Dr. Marcia Angell's The Truth About Drug Companies, Jerome Kassirer's On the Take: How Medicine's Complicity with Big Business Can Endanger Your Health, Dr. John Abramson's Overdosed America, Ray Moynahan and Alan Cassel's Selling Sickness: How the World's Biggest Pharmaceutical Companies are Turning us All into Patients, Shanon Brownlee's Overtreated: Why Too Much Medicine is Making us Sicker and Poorer, David Healy's Pharmageddon, Greg Critser's Generation Rx, Peter Gotzsche's Deadly Medicine and Organized Crime, Elizabeth Rosenthal's An American Sickness, and Glen Olsen's Confessions of an Rx Drug Pusher. (Yes, it's a lot; Big Pharma has been a popular target over the past decade or so, not altogether undeservedly in some instances, but one or two of the above can probably give you plenty of Magadon plot hooks to base an entire chronicle around.) Fictionwise, movies like The Fugitive (1993) and The Constant Gardener (2005) deal with Pharma company conspiracies. The Constant Gardener is adapted from a book by John le Carre, and is based on a real event that happened in Nigeria. Also, while not subject to any books or films that I'm aware of, the issue of price fixing/gauging and other problems with the EpiPen is also ripe for Magadon.

                                One of the biggest aspects of the pharmaceutical industry over the past few decades, especially at the time Werewolf was first being made back in the 1990s, is the market for antidepressants and other "mood stabilizers" (including anti-psychotics and other drugs). One of the GenX classics on the subject was Elizabeth Wurtzel's Prozac Nation: Young and Depressed in America. If you're at all curious about some of the mindset behind the original creation of Werewolf and the World of Darkness, it's worth a read. (There's a 2005 movie adaption which I've never seen.) One ongoing issue with antidepressants, especially among teens, is a possible increase in suicide rates among those taking them. Alison Bass's Side Effects is a good look at the subject and the lawsuit against the makers of the drug Paxil. Robert Whitaker's Anatomy of an Epidemic looks at some of the possible long term effects of psychiatric drugs. He's also co-written, with Lisa Cosgrove, Psychiatry Under the Influence: Institutional Corruption, Social Injury and Prescriptions for Reform. Mood stabilizers and other psychiatric drugs given to teens and children is an especially ripe area for Werewolf, given that the anger management issues and other behavioral problems (including the "hallucinations" of seeing spirits) can easily result in future homid Garou (and Fera) being forced on to such drugs which may interfere with the First Change and result in Lost Cubs and other problems. If PENTEX has actually figured out the possible connection between such behavior and future werewolves, Magadon may be making drugs specifically designed to repress the First Change (or worse, make the subject much more noticeable by Black Spiral Dancers in search of potential recruits).

                                The history of Magadon outlined in Subsidiaries mentions founder Gen. Bryant Vandergrift, USA(Ret.), as having been involved with the clean up and study of Japanese biological warfare programs at the end of WW2, and having supposedly created the company to make vaccines against such weapons to sell to the government. (Odds are it also carried out off the books research into such weapons and diseases for certain people in the military as well.) A few books on the subject include Danial Barenblatt's A Plague Upon Humanity: The Hidden History of Japan's Biowarfare Program, Sheldon Harris's Factories of Death, and Yan-jun Yang's Unit 731: Laboratory of the Devil. (Fictionwise, the first third or so of Stephen King's The Stand is a pretty effective look at a biological doomsday scenario.)

                                Another bit touched on in Subsidiaries is the tragedy of Thalidomide. Originally prescribed in West Germany to help pregnant women with nausea and insomnia, it ended up causing incredibly horrific birth defects, at least 60% of which were fatal. Trent Stephens and Rock Brynner's Dark Remedy is a good overview of the drug, it's history, the people impacted by it, and it's recent (very cautious) use as potential cancer and leprosy treatment in certain circumstances. For Magadon's purposes, the study of children with birth defects led to the company's Handicapped Psychic Latency Program, a part of Project: Odyssey that tries to harness and weaponize severely disabled adolescents and teens who have developed psychic abilities to compensate for their physical problems. This isn't the sort of thing you see in fiction that much, but films like Minority Report (2002) and Total Recall (1990) can offer some ideas.

                                Another of Magadon's long running projects is age-redardation "miracle drugs". One of their original successes apparently involved the use of highly deluded vampire blood, but with the destruction of Enzo Giovanni, the steady supply of raw materials dried up. They've likely found a number of new avenues to explore in the area, including stem cells, jelly fish and even shapeshifters (especially sharks). One of the more horrible possibilities is that Magadon secretly buys "parts" from Autumn run abortion clinics as raw material for some sort of anti-aging serum for high level PENTEX executives. Other ideas for this sort of thing can be found in films such as Cronos (1993), Death Becomes Her (1992), Cocoon (1985), and Self/less (2015), as well as books like Bruce Stirling's Holy Fire and Robert Sheckley's Immortality Inc.

                                Added 7-7: Something I forgot from the realm of fiction is Robin Cook's Acceptable Risk, one of his better efforts involving the development of a new psychotropic drug with ties to the Salem Witch trials and its horrific human side effects.

I'm likely to split comic books into their own thing apart from Vesuvius. I'm also planning something about the auto industry, just because. And the world of textiles/clothing/fashion.

                                    CONCEPTUAL SERVICES LTD (Fertility Clinics)

                                    Sort of a brief addendum to Magadon. Conceptual Services is one of those small things from Freak Legion, a one page thing for a fertility services that produces a breed called Bane Children. Bane Children, beside the personality description under CSL (they're all sociopaths in the Future Serial Killers of America Club), don't really have any stats, being all unique. But anyway, it's an interesting idea in a number of ways. For one, the entire concept of artificial insemination and modern fertility drugs can easily be the topic of in-character debates (especially between, say, Black Furies, Children of Gaia and Glasswalkers) on the issue of natural/Gaian methods vs Weaver ones. Such a thing can easily be extended into motivation for a slightly sympathetic antagonist in the form of an unstable Garou (a Glasswalker obsessed with the idea of trying to artificially impregnate as many Kinfolk as possible with Garou seed in order to try to mass produce new Garou, or a Black Fury advocating the use of such technology as a sign that it's time to purge the planet of males.)

                                    The idea of evil children is pretty popular in horror fiction, both in book and movie form. Possibly the most iconic is William March's The Bad Seed, which has been adapted into play and movie forms (the 1956 original and a 1985 made for tv remake). The Good Son (1993) treads much of the same ground with a male character. The idea of cloning comes up a lot with this sort of thing. Ira Levin's The Boys From Brazil is a classic of the genre, involving survivors of the Third Reich creating a bunch of Hitler clones. It was made into a film in 1978. Levin is also the author of Rosemary's Baby (made into a film in 1968), about a pregnant woman finding out her child is supposed to be the offspring of Satan. [His other major work was The Stepford Wives, which I highly recommend for anyone looking for a creepy Weaver oriented plot, especially in suburbs or small commuter towns; the film adaptions are hit or miss, with the most recent one totally missing the entire horror angle.] And speaking of satan-spawn, The Omen (1976/2006) is another genre classic. The sequels to the original are OK. (I'm slightly biased about the third one, as I'm a big fan of Sam Neil, and I have to give the makers serious props for actually knowing that the second coming of Christ is not as an infant.) I'll also mention Pet Sematary (1989), because the little kid in it is just so creepy. (Stephen King's novel is one of those things I highly recommend for Werewolf in general, dealing as it does with the concept of places that are just spiritually corrupt and will destroy anyone who gets involved with them.) Koji Suzuki's Ring is also a pretty good read. It is the basis for all of the various Japanese, American and other adaptions and spin offs, with The Ring (2002) being one of the easier ones for beginners. (I happen to like the fact that the American version is deliberately obtuse about just what it is that created Samara, so it could be mad science, aliens, demons, Fair Folk, or whatever else you can imagine.) Elsewhere, while The Fly II (1989) is no where near as good as David Cronenberg's 1986 film it is a sequel to, I admit that it's a guilty pleasure of mine, especially with the idea of some PENTEX lab-produced bane-child mutating into something monstrous as it reaches near adulthood. Splice (2009) is another good one, with the ending being especially suitable for Magadon related shenanigans. Finally, John Wyndham's The Midwich Cuckoos is an interesting read, and is the basis for the film Village of the Damned (1960/1995).

                                    Addendum: The non-fiction work Baby Markets: Money and the New Politics of Creating Families is a little on the technical side, but looks at the issue of modern artificial conception, adoption, and other related issues from the perspective of legal, biological, sociological and ethical scholars. Also, Mirah Riben's The Stork Market: America's Multi-Billion Dollar Unregulated Adoption Industry.

                                    Also, the various iterations of Law & Order have dealt with fertility clinics, adaption agencies and related issues in various episodes.

                                                    A brief thought on Mall Walkers ...

                                                    So, for those that don't remember them, Mall Walkers were a type of bane/possessed or ridden human who basically walked around malls all day in sort of a zombie like trance. Kind of a commentary on teenage mall rats and American consumerism (see also the original 1978 version of Dawn of the Dead). This was, of course, back when shopping malls and the teen social and consumer culture that revolved around them were still a ubiquitous part of North America's life and landscape. You can get a good sense of this for historical purposes from films like Fast Times at Ridgemont High (1982), Mallrats (1995), and Chopping Mall (1986), as well as major scenes from Commando (1985), Terminator 2 (1991) and Bill & Ted's Excellent Adventure (1989). EDIT: Also, the new Stranger Things Season 3 teaser.

                                                    As for the Mall Walker banes, I would speculate that they've evolved with the times, and now focus around smart phones. Sunburst/Tellus's products are probably designed to attracted them if they don't come with one already installed. So now instead of possessed teenagers just aimlessly wandering shopping malls, we have people of all ages aimlessly wandering around constantly staring at their phones. Not sure what you'd call such a thing. Phone Dummies, maybe. Or Phone Slaves.
