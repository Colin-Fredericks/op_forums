                    Heavy s..t.
                    The reality is that the use of the word "Wendigo" is quite complex, and the objection is fairly specific to circumstances. One of the running issues is that roleplaying games have borrowed from literature and mythology in a fairly decontextualised manner since the first edition of Dungeons & Dragons in the 1970s. The way in which this occurs is now so ingrained within gaming, and has spread out from tabletop games to other areas, that most people never really think about it in much detail. However, the gaming use of literature and myth tends to rob monsters of their meaning (and even the term "monster" is quite significant; it has an origin in the word "monstrum" and is supposed to denote that the appearance of an unnatural creature is a portent, a sign of divine displeasure or the fact that something has gone wrong in the world), so they end up being window dressing for a mechanical challenge, a set of stats which define an encounter and ultimately how much loot, in gold and experience, one gains within the game.

                    Quite a bit of this comes from an American, middle-class view of the world, in which culture is something which exists for no reason other than consumption, and myths are something which it is fair game to adapt in any suitable or convenient way, because no-one really believes in them, right? The problem is that one may not actually believe in the real existence of the wendigo (or basiliks, or rakshasas or whatever) but still have a deep-seated attachment to this as part of one's culture. Thus, seeing a term used lightly, or inappropriately, or whatever can end up being offensive. Thus, for a Native American to see the word "wendigo" being applied to the name of a Werewolf tribe is less a respectful use of Native American culture, than a white American deciding "cool name, bro!" Could "wendigo" be used reasonably and respectfully within a game? I think so, but, given that there are Native Americans within the conversation, maybe it's a good idea to ask them?

                    (It's worth noting that I find some of the use of European folklore in roleplaying games offensive for exactly the reasons outlined above. An example that very readily springs to mind is the black dog, which features in the Onyx path published Dark Ages Companion from 2017. The black dog is an interesting bit of folklore; in medieval, and into early modern times, it existed in British folklore as a stand-in for the Devil. Post the Reformation, the black dog starts to shift into a more generic sign of menace. The most common legend goes that seeing a spectral black dog means that a close relative will die within the next year. It is a monster in the original sense of the word, an unnatural portent, signifier of an unstoppable event which the observer is powerless to prevent. The Dark Ages Companion turns it into a monster in the Dungeons & Dragons sense. It has no cultural meaning, and is something that will go away if you hit it enough times with a sword.)

                                The Get-thing is weird because there KM is of the opinion that not having them be a white supremacist fantasy would rob them of their identity and yet with the Native American Tribes his take was that tying them to localized cultures is too much of a hassle and therefore should be removed. Especially once you're at the point where you're actively scrutinizing the writing for signs of Native American representation in order to remove them, you should wonder where you've taken a wrong turn in the development. This should've been a moment to cause some serious self-reflection.

                                Also, this discrepancy is even still in this version. After all, with this version the names of the Native American Tribes has been changed, the cultural ties to human culture have been removed (including with the Fianna after some backlash)... BUT what is one of the central new concepts of W5 called...? Hauglosk... a Norwegian word. And who's most representative of Hauglosk...? The Get Of Fenris. So the white supremacist Norse Nazi identity of Get was protected and preserved while with everything else it was "Let's go full steam ahead with erasure!".

                                Another issue with Hauglosk I have there is the idea of Hauglosk being this phenomenon that turned the Get into these Nazis as it emphasized all the worst qualities of the Garou Nation. Now James Sambrano's post is focused on thereby pushing people who still want to try playing Get of Fenris (out of nostalgia for their past characters) into associating themselves with what is now essentially a bunch of Nazis. A different problem I see here is Hauglosk. In W5 every PC can suffer from Hauglosk. So if you have an entire faction that tells you that being affected by Hauglosk might lead to things like thinking Nazis are cool, then you're indirectly telling players that one valid way to portray Hauglosk with their characters is to act like a Nazi/white supremacist. Like, the idea is this "extreme urgency" of Hauglosk can be expressed by engaging in extremely reactionary political stances like believing in white supremacy. And in my opinion that's a huge issue.
