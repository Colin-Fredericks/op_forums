    Why the Apocalypse in the title matters and how W5 fails to address it

    03-15-2024, 11:40 AM
    Even if it is by this point a joke, all WoD games have a title what creature it lets you play and then one of the core elements of it.

    Yet, it is odd that a werewolf game puts the end of the world as one of its most central concepts. After all, werewolves aren't really associated with the apocalypse in myths. The closest is Fenris-Ulf in Norse myths, but Fenris was a monstrous wolf, not a werewolf. Shouldn't Changing or Fury be a better descriptor?

    Well, WtA's werewolves draw from older material than the movies and the focus is not on being a werewolf. The focus is on the state of the world, the way nature is being destroyed and such. In the game, being a werewolf is more akin to a spiritual guardian than a cursed being.

    The Apocalypse in the title not only refers to the literal end of existence but to the little apocalypses happening every year. Species dying, people losing touch with their ancestral cultures, etc. WtA is about looking at the state of the world and feeling the horror of just how hard it is to fix it if not impossible. Never mind the sadly now very real horror of greed over care and ennui towards your fellow humans and nature.

    This genuine approach and call to action has, of course, created an opposite reaction that calls WtA's tone childish. More recently, as we actually start seeing the effects of climate change, the reactions have also become ones of denial, apathy and fear of doing the wrong thing.

    It is the latter that W5 shows the clearest in its depiction of the apocalypse.

    The apocalypse in W5 is invisible to normal people and other supernaturals. At most, it is the fall of the garou nation as the climate change happening in the real world. Despite this, W5 is very clear about discouraging its PCs from taking action further than locally.

    In effect, W5's apocalypse and what it wants the players to do about it is toothless. The game spends page space detailing what not to do, but very little on what to do and what the apocalypse looks like. Because it is afraid to take a stand, instead focusing on passive-aggressive remarks here and there.

    W5, despite its blurb stating it is about striking back at pollution, isn't willing to have its PCs be eco-terrorists (though some do slip through) and actively calls direct action the wrong method.

    It isn't just what W5 tells the PCs shouldn't do, it is also how much the PCs don't have to do. It's the end of the world as we know it and you can still go to McDonald's in peace. The world is lost and you still have to go to work. If we weren't told the apocalypse was raging, we'd assume it was still in the future. The game is, intentionally or unintentionally, saying that there is no need to do anything. Even if the world is ending, it won't inconvenience you.

    To put things plainly, W5's apocalypse is the way it is so there won't be paid sick days for the employees. It is an end that preserves and protects the status quo.

    Of course, an apocalypse that changes nothing is not really an apocalypse. Indeed, W5 wears the apocalypse label more out of legacy than any real intention of addressing it. It wants to focus on werewolf packs and caern tending, not something as serious as the fate of the world.

    In fact, I'd say W5 doesn't want to be about the garou at all but instead about werewolves. It wants to be Werewolf the Fury.

                          The environmentalist angle isn't really that difficult of a question to answer:

                          The initial premise of the WoD was to play as the monsters, but make them sympathetic in some way and the primary PCs be "dark grey" to the "pitch black" morality of their main antagonists. The games were unabashedly political and used political and activist causes as central themes to the playable supernaturals as the thing that made them sympathetic despite their monstrosity. While things changed over time, the WoD began as a 90s counter-culture release value where your PCs had the power to viscerally engage with systems that were failing society. Connecting werewolves to the wilderness and thus making them creatures that want to protect the environment from human encroachment is a simple leap.

                                  I think W5 is more concerned with lambasting Garou as a concept than anything else. The game's pessimistic take has to be considered in the context of the game also pushing hard for Garou-PCs to do nothing but confirm these pessimistic notions. Rage and violence are still at the heart of the game. The tools the game gives to players push into that direction and only that direction. Deemphasizing the Umbra, taking away Gnosis, making Garou more reliant on Rage mechanically and no outline for solutions that wouldn't involve destruction only serve to highlight that.

                                  So, I think as far as the environmentalist angle is concerned, the game has more to say about the idea of rage-fueled shapeshifting monsters trying to do environmental activism than environmental activism itself. The game ignores that angle as the game is instead about what the rage-fueled monsters do and how that's bad. W5 is a game that asks the players "When will you Rage?" and then when the players answer that question is telling them they've done something wrong.

                                                  Originally posted by Ana Mizuki View Post

                                                  The way the apocalypse is approached is still really interesting, because all of it is geared to be as little of an inconvenience as possible. It is like the game is detailing it out of a contract and would like to ignore it as much as possible.

                                                  Which, given the times we live in, really says a lot.
                                                  I don't think W5 has a good handle on the moral implications of its setting either because of that. "The ends justify the means" is normally considered a bad thing for good reasons. There are all sorts of problems with Utilitarianism and consequentialist moral equations in a normal scenario. After all, it's hard to look at any decision and have a complete knowledge of what the consequences are and so it's hard to weigh what exactly qualifies as the "most good" under the circumstances. That's the real world, though, and when you're describing a fictional setting that can become much more clear-cut. This is the case here. Before the Apocalypse was close but an avoidable and somewhat distant thing and so actions to prevent the Apocalypse could be questioned and judged accordingly as going too far. Meanwhile, W5, though, has raised the stakes and now it's the Age of Apocalypse and Garou know for a fact that the Apocalypse is nigh. Well, that actually makes the moral calculus far more complicated. Imagine any trolley-problem where one option is knowing for a fact that this path will lead to the end of everything in existence. A lot of things suddenly become justifiable in that context.

                                                  And that, of course, brings up Hauglosk and W5's notion of Garou being the wrong tool for fixing the problems of the world. If this is truly the Age of Apocalypse and that is the end is truly nigh, I would say this would lead to a resurgence of relevance for Garou instead. In the end-times the Garou are a great tool to brutally and radically turn things around. Lots of norms can be tossed aside as the fate of all existence hangs in the balance. Same as the game doesn't fully grapple with what it means for the Garou-Nation to have fallen, it also doesn't deal with the full implications of an Age of Apocalypse as the setting.
