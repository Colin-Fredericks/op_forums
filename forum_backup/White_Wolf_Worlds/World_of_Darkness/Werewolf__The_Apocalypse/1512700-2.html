                    I’m sympathetic to Younger Brother and I think that their inclusion is important, with the comparison to the Black Furies being significant.

                    For one thing, albeit it can be easy to forget, Werewolf was originally written as a pro-environmentalist polemic and in that context it would be a mistake to not include prominent indigenous representation, given how many environmental struggles across the globe have indigenous groups as central movers and shakers.

                    Furthermore, while Werewolf in theory is a global work, it’s always been focused on the United States in particular and if you’re telling stories about a struggle against the most destructive elements of society and being haunted by the horrors of the past, whose effects linger to this day, again you can’t really not talk about the native experience.

                    I think core conflict inherent to Werewolf storytelling is the problem of violence. On one level - anger (and violence) can be experienced as emancipatory. This shows both in irl conflicts where justified anger serves as a powerful motivation (slave revolts, Black Union soldiers in the U.S. Civil War, etc.) but that righteous anger is a highly popular power-fantasy and one which Werewolf has never shied from acknowledging as a key aspect of its appeal (I recall advice in a storytellers guide somewhere that if you and your group all have experience working at McDonalds or Burger King than you should consider running a session or chronicle focused on tearing apart O’Tooleys). There are also very real cases where we (barring the absolute pacifists among us) can imagine violence being necessary to achieve a righteous end. So on one level we have violence and anger both as potentially righteous and useful as well as prime storytelling fodder.

                    On the other hand, a core issue of the problem of anger and violence, is that they have limits. They’re both incredibly powerful but they’re not a one-size-fits-all solution to every problem, they can be internally corrosive, and they can be taken to excess and exasperate existing problems or create new ones. Werewolf dwells on this by pointing out the damage that can be caused, both internal and external, but also pointing out how violence and retribution alone is not enough. Even if one could muster the strength for one great knock-out blow against the structure of one’s opposition, there would still be the question of what is to be done in the aftermath. Furthermore in most actual instances of resistance there is no one great knock-out blow or final confrontation, there is a protracted series of confrontations where one must simultaneously strike one’s enemies and build up one’s own community, relationships and forces into something distinct from the old social order.

                    I don’t think it’s fair to expect either players, writers, or storytellers to have a perfect answer to the problem of violence that fits every situation. It’s something that humans have manifestly struggled with for centuries and probably for as long as we’ve been recognizably sentient/sapient.

                    I think that Younger Brother plays an important role with regards to the above as the Black Furies in that it both speaks real issues (the settler state in the former case, the patriarchy in the latter case) where righteous anger can support justified resistance as well as being fodder for an outlet of energy in a werewolf game. Both tribes can have flawed elements which acknowledge the real dangers of excess and the flaws of the Nation, but blowing up these flaws so that they smother the core of what made them appealing for play in the first place would be a mistake imo.

                    I’m reminded of something that James Sombrano talked about on a podcast, I believe with the Mage the Ascension podcast but it may have been with the Werewolf the Apocalypse podcast. He talked about growing up native and his complex feelings for Older Brother and Younger Brother, and how he felt that both reflected something he felt strongly about and important themes/strains of thought in indigenous movements: the question of assimilationism on the one hand (Older Brother) and the value of community with other oppressed nations and adaption but also the matter of resistance (Younger Brother) and the refusal to allow traditions and customs and sacred relationships to be profaned or destroyed or lost as well as the power of righteous anger and the importance of connection with that anger. He told a story about a LARP he participated in with a large indigenous player membership and how Werewolf was used as a vehicle to explore these ideas and themes, all happening in the irl backdrop of the recent Standing Rock protests and the continued suppression of the settler-state.

                    I can’t describe it as well as him or those who were involved, but hearing those stories is one of the reasons why I’m resistant to painting Younger Brother in particular with too cynical or dark a brush or with writing off the importance of the stories and themes which Younger Brother can bring to the game which other setting elements cannot.

                            In that vein, if I had to guess:

                            •The Fianna are pretty clearly influenced by the perception of The Troubles in Ireland and Britain from a Yankee perspective, with the odd voyeurism for the Troubles in particular being a sort of cultural snapshot of a period of time within which Werewolf was written. In retrospect it’s kind of fascinating how many sympathetic characters in Yankee media from the 1970s into the 2000s are former (or active) Irish Republican Army and related groups, I don’t think it really fizzled until the upswing of the War on Terror and having a good decade or so since the last major flare up. (I always recall the main female lead on ‘Burn Notice’ being former IRA - and naturally an explosives expert).

                            •The Silent Striders I’m not so sure about. I feel that they’re definitely influenced by the cultural fascination with the Romani and other ‘Traveler’ peoples, with the Egypt connection being a natural fascination for a group of goths and wiccans who were already predisposed to be fascinated by the legend of Egyptian mystery and magic (and the traditional connection made between the Romani and Egypt by European chroniclers - for instance the idea of Tarot being drawn from Hermetic concepts drawn from Hermes Thrice-Great in ancient Egypt as well as being associated with the Romani). In retrospect however, in a post-Iraq War world, the comparison of Silent Striders with middle eastern refugees feels natural - albeit a matter to be handled delicately, if one doesn’t opt to ignore it entirely.
