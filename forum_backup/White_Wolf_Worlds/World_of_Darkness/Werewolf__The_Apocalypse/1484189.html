                      I believe there will be a different answer depending on the circumstances of the combat, the reputations of the Garou involved, tribal culture, and the caern's totem and purpose at the sept delivering the judgment (whether that be Renown loss or even a Punishment Rite).

                      The exact same action by the same people might even be judged differently depending on the sept involved.

                      Was it a high ranking Garou known for his combat acumen and many past acts of heroism? He may be completely unscathed. Perhaps criticized by some, but enough people will say there is no way he ran from cowardice given his past exploits, and he made the right decision in order to fight again. But what if it was a low ranking Bone Gnawer without much Glory? He was just cowardly scum.

                      You are going to have tribes that are more prone to forgive a Garou who abandons a hopeless battle. Children of Gaia, Stargazers, Uktena, and Bone Gnawers likely among them. And you will have tribes that have a higher expectation that the Garou should fight even against overwhelming odds either because they are from a martial culture, or expectations of honor might compel them to fight. The Get of Fenris would be the most obvious example, but other tribes are in the same area.

                      A sept whose purpose is Healing with a plant as caern totem is more likely to excuse someone retreating than a sept whose caern purpose is Rage with Boar as its totem.

                      The Garou have a martial culture, but it is a mistake to think martial cultures prize fighting to the extent that they like it even when they lose. Instead, martial cultures like to WIN. Which sometimes means to avoid battle, retreat before superior odds, and hit the enemy where it is weakest, not strongest.

                      Judgments like this are going to be made in context. If a Garou retreated from an encounter, but then lead his pack or sept to victory destroying the enemy, the Garou will likely approve of the original decision to retreat. If that retreat just resulted in more defeats and losses, the Garou are likely to see the retreat as proof of initial weakness or cowardice of the Garou. A Garou was on patrol far from the bawn, stumbled into an ambush, and fight his way out and made it back home to warn the sept of a new enemy? That Garou did great. The Garou left a clearly unwinnable battle and by doing so abandoned the caern to the Wyrm? Cowardice like that should result in the Great Hunt or even Gaia's Vengeful Teeth.

                      Now, for the most part, a "tactical retreat" from an "unwinnable battle" should not provoke cries of cowardice. That may change depending on what was lost as a result of it. Consequences matter. As does the matter of upholding the reputation and honor of the Garou in general, the tribe, and the sept. Was the retreat done in such a manner that these are intact? Or was it done in a manner that brings shame? And more importantly, what one person sees as "tactical retreat" from an "unwinnable battle" might look different to other Garou. Questions like "Why didn't you do this when the battle first began?" or "Once you realized X, why did you do Y?" and so on. The person who ran, even justifiably, will probably be forced to answer a lot of painful questions.

                      In terms of running the game, the Garou can't be so foolish as to always fight unwinnable battles or they'd be defeated by their enemies. But they are also a culture that glorifies heroes who go down fighting even against unwinnable odds (especially if the Garou causes more damage to the enemy's forces than they inflicted on the Garou). There should be some kind of tension. Whether that comes from young hotheads eager for battle against the wisdom from their elders who have seen far more battles; or by younger Garou with more modern mindsets versus elder Garou with more traditional outlooks, or some other conflict, that tension should be present to some degree.

                              Herr Meister, from your name I take it that you and your friends may be german players?

                              There is a problem with the semantics and semiotics of the word 'Honor' and in my experiences there is a special kind of powder keg when you analyze the in-game and out-of-game usage of this word.

                              Some of the worst Werewolf experiences I've ever endured happened in a german speaking LARP, where there was this group who homebrewed everything (including their concept of honor) for decades, while their guests stuck to what you would expect from the books.
                              After this lead to very cringy 'honor duels' which even lead to character deaths, we did a lot of analyzing the reasons and it boils down to what people expect when they hear the word 'Honor'.

                              Funnily, with 'Glory' and 'Wisdom', the out-of-game usage of those words come pretty close to what you would expect in-game. 'Behave in a way that ends up in some kind of victory over your enemy' and 'be smart about it' tend not to bring problems into gaming groups.

                              But since you write that this even divides your friends, I guess you have the same problem we had:
                              'Honor' in the everyday usage in (German) out-of-game language tends to sum up some kind of a personal 'nobility' concept of 'things that noone is allowed to do to you'. It is a very human concept of having some kind of special right to be offended and leash out when not treated right, as if honor was some kind of energy that a person carries, which can be taken away by outside acts or their own acts. This leads to the idea that your personal honor is something you have to fight to defend, leading to people being pissed of at each other and fighting more often than not. (Imagine Prussian noblemen seeking an excuse for a duel all the time). This is also where I see your friends who have that Idea that fighting to the death might be something noble.

                              But when you take the Werewolf books and take a look into the lists of deeds which are examples of how characters get honor renown, you will see that it mainly boils down to something different:
                              For a society to function which is formed by a warrior race where absolutely every member is subject to frenzy, it is imperative to have rules which minimize the triggers for frenzies all around. When you take a look in that honor list with this in mind, you'll see that everything honorable boils down into being as predictable as you can be. So that your fellow Garou can count on you keeping your promises, following your duties and that guests from other Septs can count on some behaviours being safe bets for deescalation and not everything being subject of infuriating negotiations all the time when all participants are rage filled killers with short tempers.

                              These are not completely opposite concepts, but the central idea of the human honor version is 'My honor is worth dying for' while the central idea of the Garou version is 'Our honor keeps us alive so we can kill the right things for the right reasons'.

                              So coming back to your original question: The honor of staying in a fight you can't win extremely depends on context. Your tribe might have traditions in this matter (Gets and Gnawers would be obvious extremes here) or you could be bound by promises or oaths you took (being the warder of some place or the denmother of some cubs or any other promise where someone else counts on you to take this stand) where your honor depends on the fact that others could count on you doing what was expected.
                              Losing a whole pack in a fight just because it was a fight is not what is expected most of the time, so it is just unwise and bears no inherent honor out of itself, from the Garou usage of the word 'Honor'.

                              Tl;dr: Coming back alive from a fight against a stronger opponent may be a reason to lose Glory renown or to gain Wisdom renown, depending on how you tell the story; Mixing in Honor into this is a human concept born of the weird idea that dying is your 'duty'.

                                              I find the best definition of "Honor" to be the ideal public image of a member of the group. People who best embody that public image are tend to be seen as honorable. People who don't embody that public image gets less Honor, and while people who act in a contrary way get negative Honor. It is what Garou expect from other Garou (other than the things that fall under Glory and Wisdom). In particular, it values the traits a Garou wants another Garou to have in a survival scenario since they are a people at war.

                                              You want to appear to have self-control, to tell the truth, have a mastery of useful skills, to be able and willing to protect others of the group, and so on.

                                              Bringing your boys home alive is an important element of that. People value their lives. (At the same time, people are also willing to risk their lives, and under certain circumstances perform self-sacrifice if it means the survival of the group.)

                                              It is ALSO important for a Garou to display a 'nobility' concept of 'things that noone is allowed to do to you'. Why? Because not defending one's honor/reputation invites others to do the same and attempt more exploitation. If one doesn't defend their honor, the foe thinks, will they not defend their property that is far away from them? And if they don't defend that, maybe they won't defend their homes... and so on. Not defending one's reputation creates a perception of WEAKNESS. And weakness invites aggression. Even if you are really strong, you don't want to appear weak. You would rather have a foe deterred by thinking you are strong and best left alone, then have them attack and hopefully survive the assault. Furthermore, he makes his associates also look weak as a group.

                                              A Garou society constantly dealing with Garou fighting or killing each other over slights of honor is a highly dysfunctional one. But the issue isn't that it is wrong to defend one's own honor. It is that the Garou in that sept haven't been raised properly to treat each other with sufficient respect, and handling any disputes honorably though the approved Challenges and Dominance.. What that respect requires may be different upon rank, but to the Garou it should be obvious what respectful behavior is towards peers, inferiors, and superiors.

                                                  I think even with the Get you wouldn't suffer loss of Renown for running from a fight that you obviously can't win. Committing suicide by fighting an unwinnable fight would get them to sing songs of your bravery, but ultimately Gaia would be weaker for losing her warriors pointlessly. Even the Get of Fenris understand that while death is better than dishonor, there's also no dishonor in escaping from from absolute, certain death especially if there's no strong reason not to escape.

                                                  A Garou in such a Tribe who ran from a fight that he couldn't otherwise win would probably face other issues, like being challenged to "prove" his capabilities and such, and he might face bullying and such, or be given harsher assignments to prove his capabilities or otherwise end up dealing with various forms of official and unofficial hazing. But I don't think it would mean renown loss. And I think the Tribe would make it clear that escape was better than suicide and that these "punishments" aren't about punishing the werewolf for running from impossible odds but instead are intended to "toughen him up" and make him stronger so he won't have to retreat the next time.

                                                  And then you get into some tribes like the Bonegnawers who will absolutely use hit and run tactics, false retreats and real retreats to cause their enemies to overextend. They live by the motto "he who fights and runs away, lives to fight another day." If anything were a werewolf chose to stand and die against with impossible odds with absolutely nothing to gainfrom sticking around and for no reason other than stubborn refusal to be viewed as a "coward" that guy's funeral would probably involve the Gnawers standing around calling that guy a damn moron.

                                                  Of course these retreats would generally be planned. A werewolf who fox frenzies and runs away and gets his packmates killed would absolutely lose honor. Ultimately there's a big difference between retreating as a tactic when faced with impossible odds and retreating out of fear and cowardice. The latter would come with loss of honor and possibly various forms of punishment rites and maybe even expulsion and becoming a Ronin.

                                                      Yeah, there's a difference between a tactical retreat and running away like a coward. The easiest way to tell the difference is the emotional state of the one doing the retreating: Is he screaming (or otherwise reacting) in fear at the prospect of his impending death if he can't get away? Or is he grimly determined to survive in order to pay the enemy back for the defeat he's forced to endure now? Of course, the thing is proving to your compatriots that you were doing the latter to prevent (or alleviate) being accused of the former.

                                                      As for staying to fight an unbeatable enemy, it's only heroic if the sacrifice accomplishes something worthwhile. I tried (but failed) to find a particular strip of The Order of the Stick webcomic where the Hero Roy Greenhilt addresses exactly this by saying (roughly) that Heroic Sacrifices are only heroic when they solve the problem at hand. Otherwise, they're both pointless and worthless.

                                                      And the kicker here is that each tribe will have its own prejudices on how to answer these questions (as has been pointed out above several times).
