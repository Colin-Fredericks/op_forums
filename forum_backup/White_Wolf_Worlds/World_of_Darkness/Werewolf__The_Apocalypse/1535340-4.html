            Hello, long-time lurker. I've finished 2.5 runs of this CYOA (.5 because my progress got reset a few chapters from the end on one of them, and I didn't feel like doing the same things all over again) and I do have enough context for what it's like that a long review about it might be useful for people. A lot of Steam reviews on it, from what I've seen, don't go too much in-depth besides "It was engaging", "I like the author/Night Road so I like this too" while all the thumbs-downs are just noting the constant progress resetting. Which is annoying, but not very helpful for people wanting to know the narrative downsides or who might be fans of WtA and want to know how it holds up or if it makes W5 more interesting.

            If people have specific questions about the tone, the setting, or the like, I can try to answer those in a bigger review, but for my short summary:
            -If you don't know much about WtA and just like CYOAs, you'll probably like it as a generic you're-a-spiritual-werewolf story where you can choose to be more human or rip and tear. If you only know W5, you'll probably like it as it expands on some missing worldbuilding, but might dislike it due to the fact that said worldbuilding is mostly done by pulling directly from old editions as little interesting nods; some of them feel genuine, some just feel like pulling the nostalgia strings. If you are a fan of old editions and want to see a close representation of it as a DIY chronicle, you will probably not enjoy it. As noted before, there are quite a few nods to older concepts in favor of making this more of a continuation than a reboot; stuff like "We used to call wolf-born Lupus" and "This spirit used to be the tribal patron of X" is sprinkled in a lot of places, and you'll miss a significant amount of these references in only one playthrough. However, the way the world works is like some cobbled-together in-between of W5 and older editions; some things in W5 like Garou not knowing anything seem retconned. It's very human-centric like W5, where you are forced to get some kind of job to make ends meet, forced to be human-born, utilize phone chats as your main mode of communication, etc. The Umbra is also inaccessible like W5, but spirits are still around to talk to (and strangely, follow you around directly every time you use a Gift). It's not at all how spirits work in WtA, and I assume it's closer to WtF, but I don't have much but other people's explanations to go off of on that front. The way that tribes are more like a group or club that you sign up to because you share some mentalities with them is also quite the departure. There's more, but short summary.

            Overall, I am about neutral leaning slightly negative on the game, for a lot of reasons that I can only get into with a longer post. Some things like being a good CYOA with well-thought-out-mechanics is a positive, as are some neat spiritual moments and a few generally likeable characters after you get past the pack of jerks at the beginning. But the negatives are...mostly due to how jumbled the lore feels, and how utterly eye-rolly some important plotlines are handled, like the horribly stereotypical Cult and assorted other social-commentary things. The writing quality when it comes to those parts noticeably dips, in ways mentioned by that now-closed reddit post about them showing up to set fire to a concert with a bunch of liberal/hippie teens (where antifascist organizations somehow come out of the woodwork to help on the spot). I know that WtA wasn't always the most subtle of gamelines, but some parts here felt very distracting.

            As for some of the previous topics of discussion in this thread, I had the same "What?" reaction when the Cockroach thing came up, but yes, later on in the book, Cockroach is still around to bargain with, so that was likely a case of hearsay/exaggeration or some kind of fragment of him. But that brings up one of the problems with the CYOA where a lot of these sprinkled references are done offhand, or by some potentially unreliable-narrator character, or just seem to be there for nostalgia, that you're not sure which of them are meant to be taken as new setting information or not. Regarding Auspices, they somewhat matter, in that some of the Gifts that you have access to are dependent on which Auspice or Tribe you are, and the amount of Rage you have when talking to people makes you more likely to fail social interactions. There are also some slight fluff mentions when you're the same Auspice or Tribe as one of the NPCs, but they're minor. I'm not sure how far the demo goes, but since you get your chance at 'special powers' only several chapters in, and choice of tribe even later, you may not have gotten to that point.

                I have a bit of IRL stuff getting in the way of completing my second playthrough but some comments about what has been mentioned in the thread:
                • I really don't like the worldbuilding in terms of what people say. You get a lot of "Some people say...", "I've heard people say..." or "I've heard a rumour...". Exactly the kind of thing that Ana Mizuki posted. There's a story-reason why some of the characters you're interacting with in the game have flawed memories but the presented setting doesn't justify the lack of information about the larger world at all. When person A talks to person B about events happening at person B's location that are relevant to person B's community, we're not talking about rumors. Talking to direct wittnesses and first-hand-sources is better than a rumor. Something that's quite easy to do in present day of course. And since the game takes place in present day...
                • And it isn't like the game presents a type of Garou-society that is super-concerned with being secretive. You have a chat-room with your new Pack-buddies within the game. And another Glass Walker Garou hacks it which is treated like a mere nuisance. The tone of rumors and unreliable information doesn't fit. The change in Patron-Spirits from Cockroach to Spider is a good example. It happened. If you want that bit of continuity that connects it with W5, this is a thing that happened. So even if it's phrased like a rumor - it should be reflected in the setting. And Cockroach can be contacted in the game which makes me just wonder... so where did that original story come from? Like the one mentioned story about the Cockroach spirit is way too specific to just be a made-up story. It's phrased like a rumor at the beginning but it isn't written like one after that. And nobody would even benefit from it being a made-up story. The lack of information in Garou-society feels very artificial in the game. And it doesn't feel like numbers or competence are the issue. There are a lot of Garou and they are very aware of each other. But somehow now that the Garou Nation has fallen, there are all these barriers, I guess, that keep them from working together.
                • One thing I keep thinking about in my second playthrough is that for the presented setting in this game, "Garou" is a meaningless term. There are a lot of werewolves who identify themselves as Garou in this game but if you were to ask them individually what it means to be a Garou you'd get a lot of very different answers. I think the outlined setting in this game would've benefited from a setting that had moved on to distinctions like Camarilla, Anarch and Sabbat or Uratha and the Pure. I wonder if the Shattered Nations supplement will run into the same problem. Those Garou who are just doing their own thing, the ones who still run around in Packs, the Cult of Fenris... they all call themselves Garou but they all have very different ideas of what that means. I think the setting needs a lot more nuance in defining the idea of Garou.

                    The amount of focus on the job, shelter and money really baffled me.                             EDIT: I have to add this, but I don't think the writer for the book is bad. This is an issue where they weren't told how much Rage is a burden, even in W5. Unlike any other supernaturals, the garou have to constantly be aware that they might lose it in the worst moment. Rage is also FLUID, it ebbs and flows.

                            Like most written fiction for WtA, mechanics that make the game function are ignored or softened so the story can be told how the author wants it to be told. In this case, trying to think with Rage in mind makes you choose things the game frowns upon.
                            The Rage-system they have in the game is that there are various moments where character-interactions or events raise your Rage (it's hard to figure out when exactly it happens because you always have to go into the Status-screen to check whether it happened or not). So, the way it works is that if you get into such a scene and your Rage-track is already full, then you get a Frenzy-scene instead. Your character acts very violently and usually it's treated as you failing to succeed at something (persuading somebody, entering a building etc.) and it comes with negative consequences after that. I had to kinda dedudce that this is what happened to my character on my first playthrough. Getting to zero Rage on the other hand triggers a Losing the Wolf scene. This hasn't happened to me yet, so don't know how this is handled but I imagine it's similar.

                            There are some very game-y aspects to the game and I'm a bit torn as to whether it was a good thing to lean on them to this degree in this text-adventure-style game. I feel like I would've preferred a more story-focused approach that is focused on narrative choices rather than game-y success/failure-mechanics as well as these pseudo-equipment/inventory stuff like the place you stay at and your money.

                            One system I thought was particularly interesting was the way you interacted with spirits where instead of using your usual stats (Attribute + Skills), you got various options but what would matter is if those statements aligned with your actual beliefs. That's where these thematic tracks I mentioned in the first post (like "The Litany has failed - Rebuild the Litany") come into play. It's a system where what the spirits care about isn't what you want to do and how well you do it but rather what your character believes. An interesting idea for interacting with spirits. Of course, it's a bit constrained by the game-y-ness of using the limited tracks outlined in the game but this is something that I think could be expanded and used at the table as an alternative for spirit-interactions from time to time.

                            I also liked the way the Stormcat Spirit interacts with your character. Rather than communicating with your character you start to have random, intrusive thoughts. It's a good concept that utilizes the game's first-person-perspective and shows the weirdness of the spirit. I wished the game had leaned harder on that and explored that concept more but usually it turns into more of a dialogue at some point.

                            There's also a very loose money-system (with you having a job to earn money for stuff like a Laptop which, for example, you need to raise your mental stats) and one for your lodgings (that you can decorate later on as well). Like I said, I feel a bit torn about making things this game-y.

                            Exactly, there isn't even any safe house for new garou to stay in or people to contact to keep the Veil.

                            Then again, it at least Is loyal to how the core describes modern garou. They really are just doing their own thing at their own leisure.
                            Once you settle in Map, the city in this game, you get into trouble with the police but another NPC bails you out which is one of those Garou who's doing her own thing. And she's helping you out simply because you're a Garou. That's what I meant with Garou being very aware of each other. You don't have these formalized structures like a Sept but Garou as an identity still matters. It's a bit weird. It all gets caught up in the setting-setup, I think. The whole W5 premise relies on the idea that the Garou of the past have fucked up and that's why the Garou Nation has fallen and that's why it's the Age of Apocalypse. But the problem is that you can't go too big with the how and why of these fuckups because otherwise, it would beg the question of what would even be the point of all of this then? After all, if there's something fundamentally wrong with Garou then reclaiming/rebuilding caerns, for example, would be a waste of time as you're just gonna repeat the same mistakes of the past. So instead you get this murky description of just saying "This bad thing happened because the Garou of the past were bad." and it's just taken as axiomatic. Something was singularly wrong with those Garou of the past but now it's all different... somehow. Like, with the Impergium... logically the obvious narrative formula would be "We used to do x, we realized x is bad and so now we don't do x anymore.". And what thing is there that can be applied using that formula to something like the fall of the Garou Nation in the W5 setting? There's nothing. The Garou Elders are just bad. That's all. Like in this game when you learn about how the caern fell, the whole story with the Three Families and the one spirit (which had secretly fallen to the Wyrm) that tricked/betrayed them. It can't just be that a bad thing happened and it was a tragedy, no, it's always accompanied by the idea that the Garou in charge were also bad, so that's why it happened. But there's nothing to be learned from it. It's just "Those Garou in the past are bad. I'm sure we aren't. End of story.".

                            And of course, as mentioned before, what the setting suggests is what you should have is than rather this murky stuff with the fallen Garou Nation and the Cult of Fenris, is something more similar to that first attempt at W5 from By Night Studios. The one where you get new factions. "The Garou Nation has fallen and here are the factions that have risen up in its ruins." or something like that. Because it's not like there was this one day where things went "poof!" and the Garou-Nation's gone and suddenly nobody knows how to talk to each other anymore. That's why the lack of a formal structure feels so artificial in this game's setting.
