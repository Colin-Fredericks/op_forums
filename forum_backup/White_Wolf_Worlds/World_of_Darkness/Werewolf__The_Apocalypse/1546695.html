      I see two main ways of dealing with this:

      A short part of a bigger story where the PCs decide to deal with an invasive species. Fortunately the Garou are really good at killing things, and normal animals don't stand much of a chance (not just in direct combat, but with supernatural powers that can help summon animals/etc.). The biggest problem is making sure humans don't get too curious as to why a dug in invasive species is suddenly dying off.

      A bigger story of Garou politics as there's a lot to debate on how to approach invasive species. Some Garou are going to want to protect the local ecosystem, some Garou are going to accept changes as part of Gaia's plan (invasive species have been spreading long before humans came into being, humans just make it harder for this to occur without major disruptions) and be against killing innocent animals trying to live their lives, and some Garou are going to advocate for the Wyld and want to use invasive species to attack the Weaver's oppressive order. The PCs start on at least "killing some to protect the Caern," side of things and the game unfolds from there.

                          Originally posted by Samuelalpha86 View Post
                          How does killing an invasive species cause harm to the native species? From what I understand keeping them around causes more harm.
                          Killing the invasive species can can all sorts of problems that negatively impact the native ecosystem.

                          I'm going to go for a plant example because it's easier:

                          The tree of heaven, aka Ailanthus altissima pretty much puts a lot of the invasive animals to shame with how much is messes up new ecosystems. It can reproduce both sexually and asexually, there's nothing that kills it that doesn't kill everything else around it, it poisons other plants around it, and nothing in the US or Europe that will eat it eats it fast enough to matter.

                          The entire tree is poisonous to other plants: leaves to roots. Every time you cut one down, you're spreading deadly leaves around the nearby plants. The roots leech extra poison into the ground when the tree is under stress. Taking it down to the stump without other efforts doesn't kill it, you have to cut it down, drill into the stump and pour bleach into the holes, and repeat that multiple seasons to kill a single plant... and then all the poison roots are still there, and can clone new trees.

                          So, if you just run around kill the trees? You're left with lifeless soil that can't grow anything, you've poisoned most of the nearby plants that would try to recolonize the soil so the area of devastation gets even bigger, topsoil erosion ramps up, and so on. So, yeah, you need to be a lot more careful about what you do: you have to be prepared to do soil remediation, artificial root systems to keep the soil from eroding, and so on.

                          It's important to remember that once an ecosystem in relative equilibrium is destabilized, nature does not resort to the previous state on its own. Disruptions impact different organisms at different rates. So when you remove the disruption, things will recover at different rates. And that leads to rapid booms and busts in the populations of native species that can be unrecoverable. You have to take a holistic approach to these things.

                                                      I think as a chronicle element, it's fine. It works with the themes of the game. I think the real question is how do you make this gameable? Whether the Garou PCs succeed or fail, what are the complications that occur, what are the major story beats (plots), who are the antagonists, what important decisions do the PCs have to make, and so on.

                                                      These would be the points I think the chronicle would need to cover.

                                                      1) By definition, extirpating an invasive species from an area is hard. There's a reason humans haven't been able to do so. Killing individual members of that species, even lots of them, is easy. Eliminating them entirely is very hard. Even if the Garou are able to kill them more easily than humans and kill even more of them, it doesn't necessarily follow Garou will be more successful overall. So put a lot of thought as to what constitutes success and how it can be achieved.

                                                      2) The main part of the game is for the Garou to fight supernatural evil, not mundane threats. An overemphasis on an issue like this would lead the Garou to neglect more pressing (and fun for the players) issues and opponents. So there needs to be a balance between plots like this and other plots and antagonists.

                                                      3) Resolution on this issue (just like most kinds of real world environmental issues) should involve the mobilization and involvement of Kinfolk. It doesn't matter that Garou are big combat monsters and can kill feral pigs very good. They can't kill all the feral pigs. You want to involve Kinfolk who can go on hunts with mortal hunters, be elected to local office, staff positions in relevant government agencies, become activists who rally the local community, and take other actions that Garou - by themselves - are poor at. So I'd really emphasize the PCs making good use of their own Kinfolk backgrounds and Kinfolk NPCs in the greater setting. That will involve making use of their character sheet as well as internal politicking within the sept. That should be a critical element.

                                                      4) Think of the spiritual impacts of this in the Umbra. What are the spirits of these invasive species doing in the local Penumbra? What spirit broods are they a part of? Which of the other spirits don't like them, do like them, or are indifferent? And remember that simply because a species is invasive in this particular area, it should not automatically override the normal spirit "politics" in the area based on the spirit broods. Feral pigs aren't boars. But most likely the Boar totem, and thus packs of boar, are still fond of them and might get angry that his children are being killed en masse. Nutria, being a rodent, could very well be a member of Rat's brood, and Rat mostly cares about survival, tolerates his children being very fecund, etc. So Rat might become upset as well as packs of Rat and the entire local Bone Gnawer tribe. They simply don't care if Nutria is an invasive species. They'll support them. What if the spirit of the invasive species is actually now a caern totem of a local sept (even if small)? What about other spirits, equally invasive and not native, but which may not be as destructive like certain species. Mustangs and feral horses are certainly not native to the Americas and even have controlled hunts on their population, but many people don't view them as being invasive. Lots of non-native birds have also fit into the local eco-system. So how do spirits like these and other introduced species view any Garou who have dedicated themselves to wiping out non-native species?

                                                      While I think there may be a temptation to ignore complications like this, I think this will actually be the heart of dealing with this issue in the chronicle. The PCs will have to decide how to deal with Garou opponents who have their own reasons to oppose them. How do they deal with that? With violence? With compromise? With making political deals with non-involved Garou to outmaneuver them? What if such opponents are from different septs? And for the spirits, you are going to deal with issues of chiminage, proper use of rites like the Rite of Contrition and Prayer for the Prey, spirit notoriety and affinity, complications with spirit broods, and really making spirits an important part of the setting and resolution of this conflict.

                                                      5) Think of further complications. Adding in some possible fomori versions is one option. But so is introducing gorgons or even kami of the species. That would be a more unusual, and thus more interesting, options for the PCs to deal with. The Wyld isn't Gaia and has its own goals and preferences. Personally, I would try to limit the involvement of the Wyrm on an issue like this because approaching it from a different angle allows you to deal with other setting elements that are often neglected, and will lead to more interesting scenes and resolutions.

                                                      6) If you do make the Wyrm involved in some way, be creative and make it ancillary to the issue. Don't create something so the PCs can kill it and have an easy win. Keep it limited to an interesting sidequest that is just another complication. Maybe a Wyrm faction is helping to hunt them by introducing tainted poison traps that helped kill off many of the wolves in the 19th century. Maybe they are capturing certain species for grotesque experiments to create destructive fomori, or as guardians of important lairs. A remote Setite temple and its vampire priest might use these fomori guardians, but otherwise be uninterested in the invasive species. Or Gangrel vampires are also hunting them as an easy way to access blood they need to survive.

                                                      You don't want complications that simply piss all over the PCs and make them feel they have no impact. Buy you want complications that make sense and provide a sense of drama and accomplishment as the PCs navigate the issue.

                                                          Originally posted by Samuelalpha86 View Post
                                                          Anyways what you mean is that you have to fix the damage caused by the invasive species after you kill it?
                                                          No, I mean that this is a complex undertaking. It's not just "kill this, then fix that." You have to have a plan that incorporates the whole ecosystem in question if you want to return things to how they where. If you just want to kill the invasive species and let nature take its course, a lot of native species are probably going to die out as a whole new ecosystem emerges instead. If you want to restore an ecosystem you're going to have to cull some native species to keep them from overpopulating with the invasive species gone and their natural population controls not recovered yet, and you're going to have to work to get slower reproducing species back in place without compromising genetic stability. Etc.

                                                          "Life finds a way" doesn't mean nature does what you want it to do. If you want to guide an ecosystem in a specific direction it takes management over time.
