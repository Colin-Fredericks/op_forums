    Should Garou characters have Influence as a Background?

    06-17-2025, 11:33 AM
    As the title says.

    I was just reading the Backgrounds of other splats, to see if some of them could be used in W20, and it got me thinking? Should the Garou have Influence as a Background?

    V20 treats this just as political influence, but M20 expands the Background to other areas, too. Media, criminal underworld, and so on.

    Now, I'm aware most Garou are not close enough to human society to exert influence over mortals, but thare are some important exceptions: Glass Walkers, CoG, Silver Fangs, and Shadow Lords.

    So, what do you think? Should Garou from those tribes have the Influence Background? Yes? No? Why?

    Or perhaps should it be restricted to kinfolk PCs and NPCs?

    Just curious to know what everyone here thinks.

      I'm not the biggest fan of the Influence Background in the cWoD so... that colors my perceptions of this.

      I think, ultimately, a huge amount of this comes down to how easy you want to make Garou-human interactions. Some people like the game where the Garou can hold down jobs, be part of human society, and so on; even with effort. In that style of WtA, Influence makes more sense. Some people like the Garou - even Tribes that put more energy into it - struggling with being direct parts of human society. So Influence makes less sense. There isn't much "struggling with your Rage" if you can plop a few freebies into Influence.

          The Influence background in tabletop was always nebulous. It was a a general placeholder for any kind of mortal influence where the vampire who had it could get things done. It was meant to be purposefully vague so it could be tailored to any individual PC or NPC need. When LARP brought in a large number of players in one game, it quickly became apparent that the vague generic Influence background was leading to conflict and arguments, so the LARP rules quickly broke it down into various categories and this has been replicated in a lot of online games (and occasionally tabletop). That clarified things in games that had large numbers of people. The specific breakdown of Influence can be useful, but also unfairly restricting. In small tabletop games, the gaming group should use whatever method works best for them.

          There are two kinds of influence. One is "direct" influence which reflects the actual power and influence someone has publicly. You can control a corporation because you own it or are the CEO. You are also head of the local industry lobbying group so you have some sway with your fellow business leaders. You donate to politicians and host fundraisers so you have some clout with local politicians and friendships with similar people. Humans will almost always have Influence of this nature. Whatever it is they have, it is because if what they are doing themselves and sway with the people they know.

          "Influence" in this case (as a background) is probably best left to only the most powerful and influential human NPCs. Most people who have a more restricted/reasonable "power" is probably best left undefined as a mechanic (a clerk at the DMV can do certain things, but it's best to not provide them a background to mechanically explain it). And characters who know such someone is best defined with the Allies background.

          The other kind of influence is indirect. It is a hidden or secret influence. This is usually where vampires come into play because they have so many supernatural powers at their disposal, particularly Dominate, Presence, and the blood bond. This influence can be deep and wide because your pawns (who have direct influence) can be many and varied, and only activated once you need them. This is usually the case with the Influence background - the character has lots of secret pawns and can act as the string puller.

          Garou typically have a problem with influence because of the Curse (which is often ignored in practice by many STs and players). It is hard to form bonds with humans if they are always afraid of being murdered by you. However, I can also see some limited areas where such a reputation is not a detriment and may even be useful (low levels in Influence within a criminal organization for example). There are some Gifts that can help, but most Gifts give advantage in tactical situations, not long term secret pawns. However, in most cases such "influence" is probably best simulated with Allies and Contacts, not Influence.

          In general, I believe the Kinfolk background is meant to be the closest thing to "Influence" for most Garou. Kinfolk can provide a ridiculous number of individual kinfolk helping you, and most players will blanch at providing character names and brief description for the 50 kinfolk you can get at Kinfolk 5. Some combination of Kinfolk, Allies, and Contacts (the last two can be justified as being kinfolk as well) will almost always provide most of what a Garou needs to do in a game session.

          Also remember that in a lot of cases, no single Garou will "control" a single entity aligned with the tribe or sept. Very often another (or more!) Garou in that same tribe or sept will have their own Kinfolk, Allies, and Contacts in that same organization or social sphere. There can be lots of people drawing water from the same well. In a small town of kinfolk, the sept effectively controls all the local politics, commerce, churches, and so on. That will provide complete domination of the local area in a way that NO vampire with high Influence could ever do. The mechanical backgrounds are not the setting. They are merely tools to provide ways for individual characters to utilize the setting.

          That is what I would use in the vast majority of cases of Garou even in cases where the local Shadow Lord controls one of the major local organized crime factions, a Glass Walker who owns a powerful local business, or a Children of Gaia who operates a network of environmental protest groups. I don't think you actually need Influence to reflect the kind of favors a Garou can extract. Nor do I think the selection of Background depends on what tribe is used. There's no problem for any tribe to have one member that controls local politics because one of their Kinfolk is mayor of the town, or they have kinfolk on the tribal reservation council, or anything else. I've never liked the tribal restrictions found in the early editions of the game which said this tribe can't have this or that background. I thought it was mostly arbitrary. You can justify a lot with any single character concept even if it mostly out of place for the tribe as a whole.

          Does this mean the Influence background never makes sense? Sometimes it can, and if the ST thinks it is best to utilize that background go ahead. However, in most cases I think utilizing some combination of Kinfolk, Allies, and Contacts probably works best for most Garou.

                  Thanks, everyone, for the responses.

                  I agree that many GMs / Storytellers just shrug off the Curse, and that's unfortunate. Even in my campaigns, where I completely disregard avoid the most grimdark and nihilist aspects of the game, the Curse is present, and I make it very clear to my players that a rage 5+ ahroun will have a very hard time interacting with mere mortals (at lest while he is in the lower ranks), and most Ragabash and Philodox are better suited to act as the pack's "face" while interacting with humans.

                  There is also a terrible side effect of a GM / ST ignoring the Curse: if PCs can freely interact with the human society, the players tend to disregard kinfolk as unimportant, which is a shame. In my own experience running WW games, kinfolk are not only extremely important to Garou society, as a whole, but essential to PCs. Most high Rage, low Rank Garou just need their human kin to interact humans on a daily basis (I also have a house rule that, while in the presence of their kinfolk, a Garou's curse is not as strong).

                  Anyway, that's why I asked about Influence - a few ago I was thinking about the Curse and how it affects PCs / NPCs interactions, and that might be why Influence is not usually available to werewolves as a Background. It makes sense.

                  On the other hand, I really liked how Laws of the Wild treated Influence, and although I've never LARP'ed, I think I might adapt that to kinfolk NPCs (even if it will serve just to give me, as a ST, some gauge of how much clout said kinfolk might have).

                  Once again, thanks a lot for your responses. Really appreciated.

                                                      Not exactly. As I understand, although both paternalism and over-protectiveness involve interfering with someone's autonomy, they are quite different in both motivation and scope.

                                                      Paternalism is a broader concept, and involves interference based on a belief that this is done for the person's best interest.

                                                      Over-protectiveness, on the other hand, is more specific, often driven by fear or anxiety, and primarily focused on preventing harm (or potential harm) to another person.

                                                          OK.... so first off, it feels kinda weird to quote my post and then cut out the part that actually addresses what you're saying: that whatever the differences you want to make between paternalism and over-protectiveness on a semantic level, you're not addressing the context of the setting.

                                                          Beyond semantics, I think there's a more fundamental epistemological issue with this. Labeling someone as either (or any similar assessment) is essentially indirect knowledge. You can't directly observe "protectiveness" or quantify it to measure when it exceeds norms. These are assessments inherently based on judgements of context and behavior. No matter how well you can define these concepts to be as clear as possible as which applies... it's which applies in a specific context.

                                                          Is a Garou telling a kin that they need to stay in the Sept Bawn because it's too dangerous right now for them to be away from protectors paternalistic? Over-protective? Just being an asshole because they can and they don't really care? Completely reasonable? You can't say objectively without mind-reading powers, and you can't make a well reasonable determination subjectively without context.

                                                          Even if we go inside our own minds. You can't do something you think is inappropriate. Your internal narrative to translate motivations into actions has to include some rationale for why something you might normally consider unacceptable is at least in the moment of that context. You can accept other people might not agree. You can self reflect and later conclude you were mistaken in your thinking. But if you truly thought something was wrong to do you wouldn't do it. So even internally, we don't make objective judgements of our actions as we do them. We think we're doing something within the bounds of our self-perceptions, and maybe we're wrong and maybe we're right.

                                                          We can take these even into our fictional characters were we can have them do things for bad reasons consciously. Just because an author intends for a character to be "paternalistic" doesn't mean that's how things will end up. As the story goes on, intentions and plot might conflict and intention gives way to a different mode of behavior. The audience might just not see the actions the way the author intended. TTRPGs in particular can render such intentions trivial. If I want to make a particularly paternalistic Garou as a character flaw, but the ST only puts us in situations where the kin need protection despite their protests... that character flaw never really comes up.

                                                          Putting a lot of weight into what's over-protectiveness and what's paternalism just doesn't hold up to the nature of the conversation and our ability to ever fully assess which term is most correct.
