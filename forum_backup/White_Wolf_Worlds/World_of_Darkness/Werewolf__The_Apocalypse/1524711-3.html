            PHASE THREE PART TWO

            Garou History and Social Structure

            In the previous edition of W:tA, much of Garou history was recorded in the Silver Record, a repository of knowledge preserved in the special language of the spirits and guarded by a Keeper. Apparently, the Record doesn't exist in W5, as much of Garou history is only oral and prone to change over time in its retelling. This feels off, as there does appear to be some form of written language in the few glyphs seen in the book (as well as some more online, if one could call some of those glyphs). Too bad there isn't a Tribe dedicated to using technology as a means of serving Gaia. Oh wait, there is. It is hard to believe that there isn't more of a written record of events, of at least some of the more current ones after the invention of the printing press. Many of the new glyphs don't even convey the same sense of language as the older one did. As such, history in W5 is mostly just vague senses of what may have happened. This could be that the designers are planning some big reveals later on or that they want players to make their own history. Or it could be laziness. Most likely, The designers and management couldn't agree on what should be brought forward, so only the most important events necessary are included in W5.

            The one critical event mentioned was the Impergium, the culling of the human herd. There's no agreement about when it happened, why, how long it lasted, or what caused it to actually end. Basically, it feels that the was only even included as to give a reason for the Delirium mechanic. This also let to the founding of the Concord and the Garou Nation. There is no mention of the Wars of Rage from previous editions, although there is a mirror to the Croatan sacrifice and an obtuse mention of Garou in the Philippines. The possibility of other Tribes disappearing beyond the known sixteen is intriguing though, as it offers a broader look on the Garou experience. What is strange is that event fairly recent events don't have specifics known. Two Tribes have 'recently' left the Concord, but no timeline is given. It is suggested that both left relatively recently, but whether that is less than ten years or greater than thirty is unclear.

            A big deal has been made on how W5 has gotten rid of Kinfolk, and that shows in how one becomes Garou. Basically, no one knows anything. The oddest part, though, is that many modern Garou discover that they have a Garou ancestor. In fact, some lineages seem to have a good chance of having at least one Garou per generation or so. After until centuries or more, at least some research would've been done, at least some elite families seem to know something. Just another attempt at keeping some of the older material while trying to be new as well. Auspice is now determined when one undergoes their First Change. Tribe is usually found out through the help of a guardian who found the new Garou, and the new Garou frequently choses their guardian's Tribe. [I will be going over Auspice and Tribe more fully in the next part.]

            The main component of Garou society is still the pack. Some suggestions for packs are given, mostly very proactive types. However, the original 'balanced' pack of one of each Auspice is downplayed. Based on how the relationship map examples I've seen only feature four characters, I guess developers don't think troupes of more than three or four with a ST are common anymore.

            The main code of Garou society is still the Litany, sort of. Instead of the grand listing of the many tenets and a discussion over each individual tenet, W5 places the Litany in a sidebar in the lower right corner of a left hand page, with only a general paragraph on how the current generation of Garou don't believe in the Litany so much as it is to focused on how older, more powerful Garou retain their power.

            Finally, the structure of Garou society is explained. Packs are either formed by elders for current needs, or just when a Buch of werewolves just happen to be together at the same time. Packs from septs around caerns. Unfortunately, there aren't enough caerns to go around, so this leads to competition for them. Sept positions don't seem to exist any more, as there aren't enough caerns to support such a structure that could tame the competition. This combative nature is also why moots are rarer as well. No rules are given for them either.

            Basically, everything about the past of the Garou is left vague, and much about the current state of the Garou Nation is left in doubt. Even previous edition core books gave more information than this at the start.

                            The history of Garou in W5 isn't just a testament to the WOD-team's stringent aversion to lore/worldbuilding but also illustrates a poor understanding of history in general. Who knows who did what when it came to the creation of these first two chapters in the W5 Corebook but what they show is a piss-poor attempt at outlining a group's history. The very first of Chapter Two's section "The Shadow Of The Past" says it all: "A history of the Garou is both impossible and reductive." First of all, imagine saying this about any group's history that still has living members in this world. And what a choice of words! The infinite absoluteness of calling something "impossible" and at the same time invoking the limited confinement of it all being "reductive". If anything, we got the developers' mission-statement here: Garou-history of previous editions gets cut down a size with reduction being the primary tool and making sure it's impossible to understand Garou-history the primary goal.

                            Additionally, oral storytelling as a mode of transferring information through the ages matters little here as real-world-history has shown multiple times. It's pretty ahistoric how the second chapter treats this concept in regards to what Garou could know about their past. The text goes for a more superficial understanding of oral history as it reduces it to a game of telephone that has gotten things to a point where nobody knows anymore what was originally said. And it's portrayed with the implication that this means said history has just been lost. Oral history doesn't work like that, though. It's even more insulting that investing in Native American representation on the writing-staff more strongly probably could've led to a more informed perspective on this. But you wouldn't have even needed that. With a Western perspective, you still could point to things like Arthuriana, Robin Hood or the Illiad where oral storytelling shifts and changes depending on when and by whom the story gets told but at the core of these stories are still fundamental story-beats and truths that matter to the story and get carried throughout the ages. Or take some of these Viking-sagas from the middle-ages like the Heimskringla. It's a written work but without oral history that author wouldn't have been able to write it. Or a more modern example like Grimm’s Fairy Tales. Without oral storytelling/history you wouldn’t even have that.

                            The most basic form of describing a history is to just rattle off people, places and events. Just a chain of "who did what and when". Cause and effect. And it all sounds pretty straightforward and obvious because it's a retrospective. Hindsight is 20/20, as they say. A leads to B, B leads to C, and so on. And the actual study of history is in explaining how and why A led to B and B led to C and so on. W5 sucks at doing precisely that. And the justification is this: "The finer details, locations, and players in these grand dramas may shift with the times, and with the storytellers spinning the tale for those listening to them. But the Garou consider a few common events to be undeniable truths in their collective story." Following that, it really does only focus on a few “undeniable truths” while even expressing uncertainty about that. And the writers couldn’t even muster the imagination for the CliffsNotes-version, instead we got the CliffsNotes of the CliffsNotes. Talk about being reductive...

                            Take for example what the first paragraph on the Cult of Fenris casually drops on the reader: “At a great meeting of the tribes, the Cult of Fenris called for one final, glorious charge into the Wyrm’s maw to avert the Apocalypse, but the assembled Garou of other tribes called out their rally as suicide, whereupon most of the Cult of Fenris camp decided that all Garou had fallen, and only the Cult’s pure vision could serve Gaia.” Representatives from all the Tribes gathering to discuss what Garou should do would have been quite the event in previous editions. You’d think the Garou-Nation gathering to discuss the onset of the Age of Apocalypse would be an important event (not least because that’s when the Fenrir left). But that’s another issue with W5 in general. The text gets all fussy about Fenrir abandoning caerns and being too fanatical in fighting the Wyrm. Sure… so what do the other Garou do? This supposed gathering of all the Tribes is a good example. The Fenrir want to go all-out and attack the Wyrm directly. Okay, insane idea but… what are the other Garou proposing? What is the alternative to that vision? And the adventure “Deepest of Wounds” has the same issue. The Fenrir go off to fight the Wyrm after their caern just dries up – and that makes them the bad guys. Meanwhile, the Garou who stayed behind are inherently good just because they’re protecting a caern. Of course, Garou lost the war against the Wyrm when “good” Garou only care about it if it’s within walkable distance from a caern.

                            The other thing is to have history communicated through storytelling. Legends, myths and epics that may or may not be fictional but illuminate lived experiences of the past and societal norms and traditions. On that front W5 fails as well. First, it's hard to tell a story with lines like "The Garou Nation, as it was known, existed for a brief period of relative success, but even there, its actual duration is unknown and varies by who’s reciting the history. Was it decades? Centuries? Millennia?". This is just expressing a lack of knowledge. There's no story and no history here. This is the book just giving up and saying "Who knows.". It's insane just how much word-count gets wasted in the second Chapter on being extremely non-specific at the best times and being completely non-descriptive at other times.

                            Instead, I suspect that the lack of thought put into the history is that it was written backwards. The Age of Apocalypse, the fall of the Garou-Nation, Gaia dying: Those were the only ideas. And the game wants you to have the same reductive perspective. Don't wonder about the how, don't wonder about the why and don't wonder about the when…

                            It's now and it's bad.

                            That's all you're supposed to think about. "Capitalocene era"... "That's edgy, right?" was probably the thought when that was put into the book - but what has that got to do with Garou? That's one of the worst aspects here: It's the lack of imagination. In the same way the Garou of the game supposedly look at the world via their animistic worldview, the Garou you're expected to play are supposed to be a new lens to view our world through. “Capitalocene” is a human term for a human perspective. The fictional Garou are a way to use fiction/fantasy to explore different horizons and perspectives. So you have to be imaginative in how you portray an idea like “capitalocene”. Instead W5’s approach to history sees "reduction" as its main-tool and renders the understanding of the past "impossible" as its main-goal. This isn't just an aversion to lore/worldbuilding, this is an outright bad approach to a fictional setting.

                            And this all then leads back again to the overly pessimistic perspective of W5. If you go to the section “The Real Problem With The Garou”, you get that heavy dose of pessimism about how Garou suck and just aren’t the right tool to save the world. And Garou-history is just another part of that pessimistic vision. A past full of “Who knows”, a worldbuilding focused on “It is now and it is bad.” and the pessimistic attitude towards what you’re supposed to play as in “Garou suck.”. This is how you toss the premise of WtA in the trash. And really any premise for a setting would end up in the trash when its treatment is so careless and incurious.

                            I will write tomorrow more about my thoughts about the depiction of Garou-society in W5.

                                            PHASE THREE PART THREE

                                            Auspices and Tribes

                                            Much has already been mentioned about the changes to these two traits, but I still want to highlight some important bits about both.

                                            First, Auspices. W5 has kept all five Auspices and kept their roles in Garou society about the same. However, the mechanics of them has changed. In previous editions, Auspice determine a character's starting Rage. In W5, Rage has the same potential in all Garou, meaning that the main mechanic tied to Auspice was removed. Instead of having some werewolves better suited to interact with regular humans at the cost of being more combat-oriented, and vice-versa with others, there was some real considerations for choosing an Auspice. In W5, not only is that consideration gone, there was nothing to replace it with. The only real mechanical side that has stayed the same is the Gift list. At the very least, howling at the moon during the phase of your Auspice should offer some extra benefit, but no. Sure, Auspices can provide social uses, but without a mechanical benefit, there is no real difference in playing one over another.

                                            The Tribes have had many changes made, with the primary one being that Tribes are no longer tied to any one human culture/genetic line. In some instances, this makes a great deal of sense, even though much of the genetic side had been already phased out by Revised. However, without at least some human cultural attributes, Tribes kind of blur into one another. One could say that the caern where a Tribe came into being shaped much of the traditions of it, even after spreading far from the source, to explain why so much human culture is found within a Tribe, without tying them to a specific human group. The two just developed together, in sort of an expanded form of how local human culture shapes individual septs.

                                            Some of the Tribes had their names changed, which one could say had to be done. I'm perfectly fine by this, as I never thought that the names were their "official" one's in the Speech anyway, just close approximations in human languages. The ones that were changed were more phonetic, and the true meaning behind their names were never given. I admit, I would have chosen different names for the Uktena and Wendigo besides Ghost Council and Galestalkers (my first guesses being Trackers of the Unseen and Wind Racers, mostly to keep the alphabetical order the same), but they are just similar enough to the originals' scope as to be acceptable.

                                            The Fianna are a different matter. Their new name is Hart Wardens. For anyone who doesn't know, hart is another name for the European red deer. So, cultural ties are bad, but geographical ones aren't? Also, the Tribe associated with Stag has a name with a type of deer in it. Warden also used to be a title for a sept position, which no longer seems to be a thing either. I would have gone for the near-pun with Heart or Hearth Wardens instead, focussing on emotional well-being or family relationships respectively. Finally, the Tribe's main function involving the acquisition of territory is awfully close to that of British Imperialism and Colonialism, which is a little on-the-nose for the Tribe originally based in the British Isles.

                                            Two other Tribes had major changes. The Red Talons now accept Homids and the Black Furies accept all males. Admittedly, the Talons still have the highest percentage of Lupus of the Tribes, but the change to the Furies is odd. Not the acceptance, but in keeping the name, as fur color isn't inherited, so 'black' is not really adequate. [As an aside, I don't remember seeing anything about how fur color relates to hair color. Most of the art shows the regular grey to light brown seen in normal wolves, but I don't know if there is any other possibilities beyond those for Gaian Garou.] 'Furies' has feminine connotations, so it isn't that adequate either. At least 'red' can refer to bloody talons, so that still counts, but what does 'black' now refer to?

                                            The Furies are also one of the Tribes to have its Patron, formally Totem, changed. Instead of Pegasus, which has strong ties to Greek myth, they now have Gorgon, which has just as strong ties to Greek myth and is less familiar, aside as the term for Wyld-possessed beings. If the designers didn't want to use Pegasus, they could have just used Winged-Horse. They kept Gryphon for the Talons after all. (For those who don't remember their Greek myths, the immortal Gorgon sisters of Medusa did have wings as well as the serpentine features.) That's what they did changing Uktena with Horned Serpent. The final change was with the Glass Walkers in swapping Cockroach with Spider. That might be too close to Weaver-aligned spirits, but I can give it a pass.

                                            Gone are the starting WP and Background restrictions that defined previous editions. Instead, Patrons now offer a Boon and a Ban, similar to what Pack Totems did. However, such uses are at about the same level of power as those, even though a Tribe Patron should offer a greater degree of power, just by dint of being a more powerful entity. Boons are just the addition of a die to performing certain specific type of roll. Useful, but limited. Bans are about as bad. Instead of the varied nature of the optional Tribe Weaknesses from the previous editions, the Bans are about the same. If a Garou fails to perform a certain action when needed, or does perform a forbidden act, then they don't recover full WP at the start of the next session. While the actions are varied, having the same outcome is kind of lazy. Yes, WP is very hard to recover in W5 and other WOD5 games, but having the same outcome for each Tribe doesn't allow the differences among them to come through. There is just less there to make their differences stand out.

                                                    I think a good illustration of how much thought was put into the Tribes is the Ghost Council. This will become a bigger topic in the controversy-phase of the review but it's just such an illustrative symbol that something just went wrong here.

                                                    Old WtA had its issues and so any new edition would've to tackle those. One way was to remove any mention of ethical or cultural ties between the Tribes and humanity. And already on that count, there's plenty of evidence that this process hasn't been very thorough. But the focus of this effort were the Native American Tribes and so the Older Brother Tribe and the Younger Brother Tribe were renamed to Ghost Council and Galestalkers. We've learned that from James Sambrano speaking out and this will become a topic later but what matters here is how we ended up with the version of Ghost Council that is found in the released version of W5.

                                                    There was a previous aborted attempt at a W5 Corebook by Hunters Entertainment which James Sambrano worked on and that's where the idea of the new name of Ghost Council was introduced. Fast forward to the next version where James Sambrano wasn't around anymore but Ghost Council was still the new name. But while James' writing was used in the released book it's clear nobody talked to him because of this section:

                                                    To the Ghost Council — or “Ghosts,” individually — the destination or the path might be the objective; what matters most is that the seeker understands why they have undertaken a particular journey and can therefore learn about the Garou’s world. The journey itself is of great import to Ghost Council werewolves, as it represents an opportunity for discovery along the way… as well as a chance to tease out the secrets of the journey’s environment.​
                                                    You look at those two pages and the reader gets no explanation for why the name is Ghost Council. And you can see that in the cited section because - why would these Tribe-members call each other "Ghosts"...? Also, that should've been the clue that more is going on here. What is one of the the main-supernatural types of things Garou interact with? Spirits. And this Tribe calls itself the Ghost-Council. Instead what the writer took away from the name is this: "Whether tricking an unsuspecting spirit into letting them pass unmolested or learning where in his mistress’ apartment the executive hid incriminating documents, werewolves of the Ghost Council look for the boundaries others draw around themselves, the flaws they build into their own armor.​" (Emphasis mine) What the new version focuses on instead is to understand "Ghost" as some euphemism for this Tribe being some sort of rogue-ish knowledge-seekers that skulk around and are fans of trickery.

                                                    More so this new version adopted this weird idea of "using taboos against your enemy". The short blurb on pg 40 summarizes this idea: "Garou of the Ghost Council favor unconventional approaches and challenge taboos to use rivals’ weaknesses against them in service to Gaia." This is just such a convoluted idea! So you do something that's taboo for you but doing that also exposes a rival's weakness, in other words, this weakness wouldn't exist without breaking the taboo. But what does that even mean? And for that, we go back to the Tribe-section which has this illuminating bit:

                                                    This philosophy has earned the Ghost Council a reputation as subversives among more traditional Garou — and Ghosts don’t seem to care. “The ends justify the means” usually comes across as a dangerous political doctrine, but for the Ghost Council, finding an answer or opportunity excuses how it’s accomplished. If something comes of maintaining a relationship with a confidant​ from a fallen tribe or forging pacts with forbidden spirits, the Ghost is satisfied — and validated, in their minds.​
                                                    Besides making this Tribe another Tribe that should be far closer to being a candidate for going rogue than the Fenrir, giving a playable Tribe an "ends justify the means"-attitude is a strange choice, to say the least. By that logic, a Ghost Council PC allying with BSD and Wyrm spirits shouldn't be considered out-of-place if it serves them. And that makes this version simply villains. The moment you make this convoluted idea of "breaking taboos to expose new enemy-weaknesses" more legible, it immediately becomes clear what it's outlining could be summarized with another idiom as well: "The road to hell is paved with good intentions.".

                                                    Setting that aside, read the section again and wonder: Where did the Tribe come from? What does the Tribe think about the Age of Apocalypse? The fall of the Garou-Nation? Or Gaia's Howl...? Honestly, how would you even play a Ghost Council Garou using the W5 Corebook...? Besides coming up with a PC-concept what is the actual Tribe like? In all those questions the STs and players are just left alone. And they're left alone because there isn't even a hint of support for guiding the process of making this Tribe work. The main-thing that was done here was to strip away lore/worldbuilding.

                                                    And Auspices have been mostly written out of the game. Litany doesn't matter (so Ragabash and Philodox can't serve their purpose), entering the Umbra is hard and Spirits generally don't like Garou (so Theurges can't serve their purpose), nobody knows anything about Garou-history (so Galliards can't serve their purpose) and looking to bring the fight to Gaia's enemies sounds like something Fenrir would do and Fenrir are bad of course (so Ahroun can't serve their purpose). I guess in the way this version of W5 is conceptualized there really isn't a place for the Auspices of previous editions in this version of W5...
