                While I was talking to a friend about W5 and looking through the W5 corebook, I noticed something. So Jane Goodhowl first appears in that scene where Marcus Oakheart attacks the players but she interrupts it with her Talisman called "The Dogwhistle of Control" (again, bad name... really every name the writers chose in this book is bad, that's kind of an achievement) and it says this: "Blowing in this whistle calms any targeted Garou for a turn, no matter how consumed by Rage or Hauglosk, forcing them to consider their actions and listen to reason. [ … ] The talisman has been granted by the spirits to Jana Goodhowl specifically. It won’t cooperate with anyone else."

                Here's where it gets interesting: Technically that Talisman already existed in W5. It's called the "Harmony Flute" (pg 188)


                The version presented in the Red Tundra is not only much stronger but makes less sense. That version does 1 Superficial WP-damage and makes a Garou lose 1 dot of Rage and presumably, the description implies some sort of mental command where the targeted Garou has to calmly consider their actions and listen to the owner of the "dogwhistle". But in comparison, the "Dogwhistle of Control" feels more like a butchered version of the Harmony Flute. At least with the Harmony Flute the outline of what it does and how it works is pretty clear.

                Also, W5 already doesn't have a lot of Talismans to offer to players thus far, so to choose to create an alternate version of an already existing Talisman is baffling. And I suspect the reason the writer created this stronger version is entirely about using it as a crutch for the scene to unfold like it's described in the book. But this is another case of bad writing. This person wrote themselves into a corner by having a frenzied, Hauglosk-suffering Oakheart attack the PCs in Crinos-Form and then the only way the writer could think of getting out of that scene was by taking the "Harmony Flute" and based on that create that powerful "Dogwhistle" (which can shut down and change the trajectory of the scene in one turn).

                                                One joke you often hear about the previous editions of WtA is that it's just "Furry Captain Planet" and there's some truth to it although it's hyperbolic to describe it like that. And if anything, I think it's a far more accurate description for what W5 storylines are like. I think there are two reasons for this: First, it's the W5 design-goal to deemphasize the Umbra and the other are its take on Bane-Spirits.

                                                W5 has done a lot to discourage players from going into the Umbra. There's the Willpower-damage for being there (which translates into aggravated health-damage if a character runs out of WP), there's the depiction of Spirits being hostile or at least unfriendly to Garou and then the whole Umbra is gatekept behind having to buy a specific Rite first. And so a lot of conflicts aim to be solvable on the physical plane with the Umbra being more like optional content.

                                                More importantly is the second part which is how W5 treats Bane-Spirits. On pg 244 in the W5 corebook you have this section about "Storytelling with Banes" where it says:
                                                Narratively and thematically, Banes always represent something — they amplify, facilitate, aggravate, and sour what is already there. Which is to say that Banes aren’t responsible for human evil. Tragically, many of a Bane’s victims haven’t even done anything overt to earn their fate. As such, Banes should be symptoms and exacerbating factors in mortal problems rather than their initiators. A serial killer may attract a Bane interested in perpetuating violence and preying upon the bereaved, but a person won’t become a serial killer because a Bane 'made them do it.'
                                                On the surface, it might sound like a good idea to not blame human evil on supernatural factors but what's missing there is the animistic perspective/worldview you find in WtA. Spirits and the physical realm influence each other. The way it's formulated in W5, though, the idea is that as long as you have good humans, the Umbra is irrelevant. Say you have this Bane of endless murder but there isn't a single person with a murderous impulse around. By W5's logic, the Bane-Spirit would be irrelevant. Hell, there could be a hundred Bane-Spirits lurking in the Umbra but without any human who already is inclined to be a serial killer, they would be powerless.

                                                What this leads to is that rather than having this spiritual game where both the Umbra and the physical realm are important in dealing with the Wyrm, in W5 it all starts and ends with human evil. Whenever Fomori turn up in these W5 adventures, it's because they are either bad people or people in a bad situation (like the workers in the warehouse in Scent of Decay). You deal with the former by killing them and you deal with the latter by improving those people's situation. But dealing with the actual Bane-Spirits is entirely optional (I don't know how often I've now already read the phrase "And upon the host's death the Bane-Spirit flees into the Umbra"). And rather than dealing with Wyrm-worshippers, Pentex-subsidiaries trying to spread Wyrm-taint - you deal with bad people doing bad stuff. Oh and by the way, they're Fomori. Instead of the spiritualism, it's all far more mundane. You can also see that in this adventure where when it talks about the rich people who are going to join the hunt, it mentions that none of them are possessed by Bane-Spirits and then adds "At least, not yet.". Fomori is less about the Wyrm-Spirit in W5 and more code for "This is a bad person.".

                                                And that's why "Furry Captain Planet" is far more fitting in this case. Because if you were to get an adventure in the Amazonas Rainforest, you wouldn't get any of the epic battles with Bane-Spirits and Pentex from previous editions, it really would be just about a greedy logging-company doing mundane bad stuff and the managers as well as the security staff all just happen to be Fomori.

                                                The only other type of storyline is having to deal with other Garou who (because they're too angry and/or sad) are doing the wrong thing.

                                                    Like the the lead author outright said in they post about the game that the project is ment to appeal to the fans of W20 while streamline info for new players.
                                                    W20 fell short of delivering a fully serviceable RPG to it’s Storytellers, however well-intentioned. It had all of the main bones of the setting and stats but no guidance on how to turn it into a game for one’s players. Taking it a step further, some of the writing in this new edition only managed to alienate modern audiences.

                                                    The use of in-character narration to express setting information in prior editions seems an attempt to convey the horror and pathos of the world that would be difficult to get across in stats alone. The information contained in that first-person text is among the most important parts of the setting, but it often fails to convey the true horror of the world of Garou. In many ways the World of Darkness was intended by those who created it to be a place of genuine terror and horror, and not merely “savagery” for its own sake. Horror is a very complex basal guttural emotion that sits in the ganglia, ready to tug the emergency brakes on your body in the presence of what it believes to be a tangible threat. There are many complex higher emotions, but when it comes to the lizard brain, it takes considerable effort to trick it into getting spooked. Invoking a sense of horror in a horror chronicle is a complex enough endeavor that, by and large, these efforts fell short of delivering that experience.


                                                    Werewolf: the Essentials is to serve as a masterclass in using those old tools to introduce new players not just to Werewolf and the World of Darkness on the whole. It gives these important storytelling tools to new and future storytellers in any game, that they might continue genuinely terrifying their players for many more years to come. The passages found in this series can add narrative value to not just Werewolf, or even Vampire and other World of Darkness tables, but also horror writing on the whole. Furthermore, this project aims to streamline the availability of that information and provide guidelines for Storytellers wishing to conduct research using the labyrinthian older editions.
                                                    Like there's a reason that WtE focuses on the age of heroes that comes after the apocalypse. It's fine to be sad about the death of something you love but it comes the time to move on to something better else you wallow in something you can't get back.
