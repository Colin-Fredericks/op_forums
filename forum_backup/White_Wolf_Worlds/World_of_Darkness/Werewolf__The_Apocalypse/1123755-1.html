    Originally posted by CeltSPZ View Post
    This reminds me of when my friend wanted to run an epic level Forgotten Realms campaign. I chose a Paladin and with enough stuff and boosts and what not got a 32 charisma. The first thing that happened was I had players scream "IF HE STARTS ROLLING TO MAKE ME DO THINGS I QUIT" (...i just wanted to smite well....)
    This is always the problem with any kind of social interaction mechanic. Players hate if they feel Player Agency (free will) and the ability to make decisions for themselves is taken away from them. People can tolerate scenes where supernatural mind control (Charm Person, Dominate) impacts a particular scene. They may not like it, but know it is just for that one scene and afterwards they can react as they'd like to. (I also know that some players might be OK with some form of long term mind control that impacts them, but it is usually because they think it makes a good story to RP during, and the PC always needs to buy-in or approve beforehand - they don't like it by ST fiat).

    Usually social mechanics are things PC do to NPCs, and are not things the GM does to the PC or the PCs do to each other precisely because it kills fun. In this regard, Werewolf is no better, but no worse than any other system. It is a problem that RPGs have in general.

    As such, most good GMs seem to fall back on a set of principles on when to employ these mechanics on PCs.

    1) To establish first impressions.

    2) To adjudicate immediate reactions that don't negate long term player agency - players are usually fine with being temporarily distracted by flimflam, losing staredowns, being successfully lied to, acknowledging the NPC gave a great speech, song, etc., or even being told they are attracted to someone in some fashion.

    What they don't like is having their control of their own character taken away from them. It doesn't matter WHY (that NPC has Pure Breed, I made my PC's own social skill a dump stat, that NPC has a Merit, that NPC has some other bullshit power that I don't).

    It has been many, many years since I've seen a GM tell a PC what their character believes or must do because of some social mechanic because this is something GMs learn very quickly that PCs hate and will cause players to leave.

    Instead what I see is that PC and GM roleplay out the scene, and only for very specific and immediate reactions are mechanics used when there is some specific conflict that must be resolved, or there is uncertainty on what would happen so it must be left to chance. Regardless of the specific outcome of the encounter, the PC is always free to react or believe whatever they'd like.

    It is perfectly alright for a PC to be told that this person is very charming, commanding, or any other fluff you'd like. But the player will always decide whether they like this person or will follow them.

    Some scenes are harder to adjudicate than the other, but I usually see experienced GMs keep the game fun for the PCs, and inexperienced GMs go through the exact same learning curve regardless of the game they are playing.

                            Here a simple house rule to PB (probably needs refinement, I came up with this in a minute):

                            If a character with PB takes an action that would earn them negative temporary Renown during a scene, they take a social dice penalty equal to their PB bonus plus the total negative Renown for that action for the remainder of the scene, and PB rating plus half the Renown penalty (round down) until they can make amends for their improper actions. If they rack up multiple negative Renown knocks (as in different actions, if one action qualifies for multiple penalties on the chart, it's one penalty), only use the worst. If subject to a Punishment Rite, the penalty expires when that Rite's duration expires.

                            Boom, we don't have to jump through ST fiat hoops. A PB 5 Garou murders another Garou under the false pretense of claiming they're "of the Wyrm" suffer a -26 social dice penalty for the rest of the scene, and -15 dice penalty for a very long time. Your Ancestors and Tribal spirits have turned on you for turning on your heritage.

                                        If you think that Pure Breed is too powerful in your games, then you should stop prioritizing the social interactions between Garou. If the only social interactions that occur in your games are between Garou, then Pure Breed is going to be overpowered because it effective gives three Attribute dice (+1 Charisma, +1 Manipulation, and +1 Appearance) for the cost of one Background point. However, there is an entire world out there outside of the Garou Nation, and Pure Breed does not help out social interactions with animals, humans, spirits, or other supernatural creatures. If you make it so that Garou-Garou social interactions are 10% or less of the social interactions in your games, you will find that Pure Breed matters a whole lot less (it is one of the reasons why I like the Ahadi and the Beast Courts, the Garou are just one group of many, and Pure Breed just becomes an odd form of status within the Garou ranks).

                                        I do think that one possible solution though could be to make Pure Breed matter only for social interactions with Garou and Kinfolk of your Tribe. That way, the Tribes without Pure Breed are not excessively disadvantaged, and every character does not feel obligated to take some level of Pure Breed. After all, why should a Bone Gnawer or Wendigo really care if a Silver Fang resembles an Ancestor from 1,000 years ago?

                                                            As a rule of thumb, I try to not roll social dice too much and let the players RP both their stats and the situations... rolling instead of having the players argue with npcs is just not fun. But I will impose a roll if they're not correctly roleplaying their stats or position. So, if they bow when my elder Silver Fang npc enters the caern I won't roll, but if they try to speak with insolence I'll ask a contested roll.
                                                            Rolls are there to help interpretation, not to replace it.
