    Review of W5

    10-29-2023, 04:38 PM
    Late last month, I finally got my copy of W5 at my LGS. It was twenty-five dollars off with my pre-order discount and store credit, otherwise I might not have bought it. I am sort of a completist. I was hoping it wasn't as horrible (or great, depending upon your choice of social media) as I've been seeing. Now that I feel that I have gone over it enough, I will be reviewing it. To be as fair as possible, I will be reviewing it in four phases. Phase one will be a basic review of the book, divorced completely from any outside influences. I feel this will be the fairest way to be as neutral as possible. The second phase will be comparing it with the other WOD5 books, basically just V5 as my LGS hasn't been able to get H5 yet (not to mention any of the other books released since last winter). Ever since Renegade broke with some major distributors, many store haven't been able to get some of the new books. Phase three will be a comparison with the previous editions of Werewolf. Much of this will be based on W20, as most of my older books were destroyed in a flood some twenty years ago, and I wasn't able to replace most of them. (I'm slowly working getting the PDFs now that I've finished getting the WOD20 books.). The final phase will be based on the various "issues" raised since W5 was announced a year ago. Even though I want to be neutral and fair, such controversies need to be taken into account, just on their own and not with the main reviews of the book. As an example, I would give V5 B+, B, C+, and C respectively. So, without further ado, here is part one of my Phase one review.

    PHASE ONE REVIEW: PART ONE

    General Art Design/Layout
    The first thing one notices about W5 is the color of the pages. They aren't the standard white; they are more of a sepia tone with traces of grey. The bottom of the pages are also grey, as if they were mountains or a torn edge. Almost as if it were handmade. While this is a unique choice, the artwork suffers for it. Much of the art appears muddled on this tone, giving it a muddy look. The few traces of bright colors are almost garish next to these darker mixes.

    For many of the illustrations, the anatomy appears just a little off, making both humans and wolves an unworldly nature. While this can work with some of the more horrific forms, the 'regular' people just appear slightly off for it. Some of the pictures feature stock posing, almost unnaturally so. It doesn't help that many of the proportions and perspectives are also off. While this helps convey the otherworldly nature of the setting, using for almost every picture is unsettling. A little variety would have been nice. Art is subjective, so maybe there are those who like this style.

    The layout is non-standard as well. Multiple fonts are used on each page, giving a busy look. Chapter title are in a font similar to multiple stencils and slightly off-center, leading to a do-it-yourself vibe. A similar broken font style is used for major section headings, in a dried-blood looking dark red. This same color is used for smaller sidebars, with white lettering. (A sickly looking yellow-ochre is used for larger sidebars.) Subheading are in totally different font in dark green. In dim-lighting, both colors look similar, making it hard to differentiate. Those with difficulties with discerning colors might have problems as well.

    In all, the choices in color make the book feel closer to a text book than anything else. The uses of multiple colors and fonts weaken the layout, even without making it harder to read. Not the best way to introduce people to a new game.

    THWarted

      PHASE ONE REVIEW: PART TWO

      Introduction
      After a somewhat muddier repeat of the from cover, there is the credit listing, followed by the Table of Contents. Some pages have two or three listing for them. That seems like it is more than necessary. I prefer sweeps of page numbers rather than going down to such minutiae. Also, the first typo is on the second page. Just a missed capital, but still strange. After three full-page artworks and a brief in-character reference to the world, the actual introduction begins. Apparently, this Fifth Edition has absolutely no continuity to previous editions. Technically, that would make it a new "First Edition," but I guess that someone wanted to maintain something of the past. The first few sections try to tell you what the game is about and what these "Garou" werewolves are. They are nothing similar to werewolves in folklore or most movies, but there is nothing to explain any differences. What these Garou do do is fight in the name of their spirituality-driven duty to defend the earth. Following this is a section of storytelling games. Not sure if there needs to be such a lengthy discussion up front, but it could help newer players. The final section of the Intro is on mature themes. It might have been set off somehow, considering how important this part is, but it wasn't. After all of this is a one-page lexicon. Most of the unfamiliar and important terms are found here. However, there are quite a bit of terms that aren't here. Most of these are defined in the main text near where they are first used, but not all of them are. While their definitions can usually be sussed out, there still could be some confusion.

      Chapter One: A World of Darkness
      This is the setting chapter for the book. Basically, the world is dying, if not all but dead, and it is up to the Garou to to whatever they can to ...do something? The text is kind of vague on what exactly they should do, beyond they need to do it. This includes the spirit world that reflects physical reality, bound by an imbalance of three primal forces of creation, order, and destruction, called Wyld, Weaver, and Wyrm respectively. It is the Wyrm that appears to be the main problem, although it could just be a reflection of what is truly wrong. Whatever, the actual causal relationship might not be important.

      Next, there is a discussion of how the game world works and looks. It boils down to Gothic and Punk, or at least some of the elements of these two styles. This includes the fact that the characters are not the good guys, just the stars of the story. That is going to be hard for some players to take in.

      Following this is a large section on just how bad the world is. Very depressing and very disheartening. Even the parts that shine a little are just barely hopeful. Why the designers chose such a dark mood with so little light is unknown. It does make a very dark game.

      The chapter ends with a section on some of the enemies of the Garou, divided mostly along the spiritual lines Garou follow. This might be a lot for newer players to take in so early in the book, even before exploration of Garou proper can take place. Personally, I would have placed the 'heroes' of the story before the 'villains,' but I guess the designers know what they are doing.

              PHASE ONE REVIEW: PART THREE

              Chapter Two: Being Garou
              This is where one would think a big hunk of information would be found, but it isn't quite that way. Much of what is found here is contradicted just a later on. While such open-ended freedom can be appreciated, this chapter almost borders on laziness. I hope it is just the designers trying to be vague as the game world develops, and they can figure something solid out. As is, one can't be sure just what to expect. The chapter starts with a section on history, kind of. While the broad swaths are well known to the Garou, the details change from the telling. It is implied that this is due to the oral tradition of the Garou, except they do appear to have a writing system and their own language, so something had to be written over time, especially in the last few centuries. Apparently, the Garou have been around longer than humanity. Somehow. Anyway, during those many centuries/millennia, The Garou went from hunters of humans to hiding from them. Over time, the various Garou organized into tribes and created a set a rules that all Garou should live by. What is strange is that nothing is given even an inexact range of when things happened. This includes events that may have happened fairly recently according to later chapters, so a more exact timeframe should be known. It is almost as if large chunks of the past were deliberately forgotten.

              This section ends with an in-game letter comparing the Garou with indigenous tribes in North America. I'm not sure what the comparison is. Is it about how young Garou aren't being taught by their elders or how lost Garou aren't able to learn anything? Both versions are supported by the text, as well as others. This letter seems so out of place as to be confusing, even if was meant to be very important.

              The next section covers Auspice and Tribe, the two main ways of classifying Garou. Auspice is basically one's moon sign and determines one's duties as a Garou. Tribe is based upon which Parton spirit and their teachings one follows. (One of the tribes are out of alphabetical order in the listing here.) Information of each is in the next chapter.

              In the next section, the how's of becoming Garou are discussed. Although determined at birth, no one knows who will become a werewolf until it happens. Although it does run in some families, and many new Garou find out they had a distant ancestor. After who know how long, someone should have figured out something. After being accepted as Garou, one joins a Tribe and enters into a pack, frequently with other new Garou. While a pack of five, with one of each Auspice, was once considered the best type, no one is forcing anyone to follow that advice, and just come up with whatever one wants to play. Then why mention that this was once the best way?

              The next section is on culture. Garou law is based on The Litany, a code that is semi-flexible, but most newer Garou question its authority in modern times. The individual tenets are not dealt with clearly, and they are barely even featured in a small sidebar at the bottom of a page. Again, why bring something up without actually going deeper into why it's important or not. This is followed by an understanding of how Garou divide into larger groups and how they meet up. Basically, it can lead to more trouble than good, so players are subtly pushed to try and avoid such larger interactions.

              The chapter ends with a discussion of the emotional state of being Garou. Apparently, Garou are prone to bipolar swings of despair over the fate of the world and intense action of trying to do anything to change things. Both are to be avoided, with the action part being considered worse for some reason. Actual systems are to be found in a later chapter, but they were important enough to merit an early mention, outside of the main range of concepts in the rest of the chapter.

              Chapter Three: Auspices and Tribes
              This chapter should be the one where the nitty-gritty behind the main character types should be found, but it just barely scratches the surface of what each character class could be.

              The first part deals with the Auspices, moon-signs of the Garou upon which they first changed. Each of the five Auspices get three brief paragraphs that boil down to roleplaying hints. Aside from a list of exclusive Gifts, Garou powers, there's really no mechanical advantage of choosing one Auspice over another. A player could always break the mold and not take the roleplaying hints into consideration. A real missed opportunity here for something.

              Each of the eleven Tribes get two pages to explain themselves. However, half that space is given over examples of who would belong to each Tribe and illustrations of them. Some Tribes are given more history than others, while most just get an unclear explanation of their mindset. The main mechanics are the Tribe's Patron Spirit and the Favor and Ban it sets on their charges. Each Favor is basically the same, "Get a bonus in performing X action," without any variation among the Tribes. Bans are pretty much the same. "Suffer a penalty if Y action isn't performed on a regular basis." While such acts are different among the Tribes, a little more variation would have been liked. It does make it easier to remember them, since they are all mostly alike.

                                          PHASE ONE REVIEW, PART FOUR

                                          Chapter Four: Characters
                                          This is where the actual character creation happens. At the start is a small sidebar summarizing the process. I would have wanted something larger with all of the option together, as one has to go back to the previous chapter for some of the necessary information. Apparently, one can create a wolf-born character as well as the standard human one, but there are no particular rules to do so. The example suggests that wolves would be good at working with animals. I'm sure no one thought that a predator would know how to calm a prey animal such as a horse just because they once were a wolf. There should have been a more directed process for wolf characters.

                                          The first phase involves Attributes, the major traits used in most actions. These traits also help determine starting Health and Willpower. Theoretically, one could start out as a very weak and/or weak-willed character, which could be very detrimental. Next comes Skills, secondary traits used to focus on particular actions. Not everyone has every skills this is a way to individualize. Some skills can have specialities, but they should only occur in certain situations. I'm not sure if specialties should be so limiting, or work exactly that way.

                                          Next come Advantages. These traits are highly individualized. They are divided into Merits and Backgrounds, with Flaws that can cause problems for players . It is not clear if Flaws net players extra points to be used on other traits or not. Otherwise, why take a trait that is so debilitating. Also, it feels like that there aren't enough of any to these traits, like there might be something missing to add more variation.

                                          The next section covers Renown. It feels like Renown should factor into a sort of power level hierarchy, but that is not the case. It just helps determine the level of Gifts one can purchase and the number of dice used to roll for them. Really, this trait feels underused and underwhelming. There's a brief section about Gifts and Rites following this, but they are covered in a later chapter. Following this is a section on Touchstones, the people that a werewolf uses to stay in touch with their human, or wolf, side.

                                          The rest of the chapter is about finishing touches to round out a character, an explanation on awarding and using experience points to evolve a character, and notes on using a 'relationship map' to see how various player characters interact. This last part feels weak, as such things could change by the end of the first session, if not sooner. Maybe it is important to some groups, but this might not be as important to more experienced player.

                                          Chapter Five: Rules
                                          This is where the actual action of playing takes place. Hidden in the second paragraph of the chapter is a notice of free downloadable material for extended rules. However, unlike other web addresses found later on, this one is not set appear, and it could easily be overlooked. Anything that could benefit a player should be more readily apparent. The first section details how to time is handled in the game and how to use dice to determine results. Illustrations of a fight between two Garou are interspersed among the text, with the possibility that one is out of order. Special rules are presented for dealing with damage and a few basic maneuvers that could be found in combat. Straightforward material.

                                          Next is a section on rules specific to Garou. First is Rage, the animalistic 'fuel' a Garou uses for their supernatural powers. The more Rage one has, the more they can do with it, but the harder it will be to interact with humans. With less Rage, working with humans is easier, but you can't do as much on the supernatural side. There are even special 'Rage dice' a player uses to help show these negative effects. The dice used for the game are ten-siders without actual numbers on them, based on the description, but regular ten-sided dice in two separate colors could be used. [The dice especially meant to be used for this game are in an orange-pink for regular use and lime-green for Rage, although the actual photographs might not show the colors accurately.] Rage is used for regenerating damage, transforming into one of a Garou's five forms (human, near-human, wolf-man, big wolf, and wolf), using Gifts, and a few other things. The text suggests that wolves could sometimes be mistaken for big dogs in some places, but that is unlikely.

                                          The chapter ends with a discussion on some mental states. Frenzy happens when a Garou gives in to their Rage and can't control themselves. Harrano and Hauglosk are the depressive and active states mentioned previously. Renown is shown as a way to influence social interaction, while its opposite of Chagrin is a state of being outcast. Finally, Delirium is a state that most humans get when facing Garou in an obvious supernatural situation. Overall, none of these mental conditions are overly difficult, but just lacking in some minor quality to make them feel important.

                                          Chapter Six: Gifts, Rites, and Gear
                                          Finally, we get to the 'cool powers' chapter.
                                          First come Gifts, the main powers of the Garou. Gifts are divided into three classes: Native (which would be better called natal or ingrained), Auspice, and Tribe. Each type of Gift is further divided into three levels, unlike every other trait which is on a one to five/ten scale. Each variety of Gift uses a different scale to measure each level, based on one's total Renown. Each level goes up by three Renown, starting from a different base number (Native at one, four, and seven; Auspice at two, five, and eight; and Tribe at three, six, and nine). Theoretically, two more levels of Gifts might be available, as total Renown could go up to fifteen. Why they aren't mentioned is unknown.

                                          Native Gifts are available to every Garou, but Auspice and Tribe Gifts are usually only available to those of the particular group. Multiple mistakes in what Renown levels are used can be found, but one should follow the system mentioned above and just ignore any difference. The Gifts feature a wide range of abilities, but they do have limits. Supposedly, Gifts can only be used in supernatural forms, but there are so many exceptions that this rule really isn't so important. Some Gifts say that similar Gifts can't be used at the same time, but not all such Gifts seem to be classified as such, even it they could be.

                                          Rites are what they say, special rituals used for a special effect. Rites don't have ranks, so this mean a starting character could theoretically know something ridiculously overpowered. Rites are supposed to work better in groups, with just one character having to know the Rite. This feels off, as some Rites are rather singular or personal in nature.

                                          Next come Talismans, objects either permanently or temporarily possessed by a spirit that causes a supernatural effect. Only eight are mentioned (nine if you included a legendary one seen in a sidebar), but it feels like there could have been many more. There just doesn't seem to be a wide variety. Technically, Talismans are unranked, but some have an optional rating if used for the Talisman Background. Again, a good reason to have more variety.

                                          Caerns, the spiritual areas of the world that the Garou cherish and protect, are next, but they probably should have been listed after Rites, since Rites frequently work better at such locations. Various systems and traits of Caerns are included, but they are basically unranked beyond basic qualities.

                                          Lastly, various types of regular weapons and armor are listed. Types really aren't used, as simple generic descriptions are enough in most cases. In fact, this section almost feels like an afterthought, as it reduces weapons to a bare minimum of information. Somewhat disappointing.
