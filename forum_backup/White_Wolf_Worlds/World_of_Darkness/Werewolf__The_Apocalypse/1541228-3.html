    The forgetting is only the first part that is so baffling. Because the follow-up is also the implication that after the knowledge of the history has been forgotten throughout generations, the response to that was these newer Garou supposedly thinking "What happened back then...? I dunno.". And then they just moved on! Even some of the most ancient human cultures already cared about memorializing their past in some fashion (like having a tradition of oral storytelling). Even if it isn't 100% the truth, you'd think they would come up with some sort of story they could look at as a documentation of their shared history. But what W5 is suggesting is that after the amnesia, there was simply no attempt to create any sort of history for themselves. "The Garou Nation? Dunno what that was... was it a decade? A century? A millenia? Who knows.". That sort of ambivalence would only make sense if these newer Garou stopped caring altogether about being a Garou and didn't identify like that anymore.

    And you see that in the Legendary Rites. They are all these recent events (although of course according to Shattered Nation, the 80s and 90s are already "ancient history"). There's no creation-myth of Garou interacting with Gaia and Luna for the first time, no Rite about the Impergium and the Concord or any myths about how these Tribes with their respective Patron Spirits came to be... It's all just shrugged off in favor of more recent stuff. You'd think the most prevalent Legendary Rites would be these events that are fundamental to the Garou's sense of history and identity rather than having a Rite about one Garou's adventures from a couple of years ago.

                    There's this exchange that one young Pack has with this Ancestor Spirit called Old Trucker (who's a Native American Garou who lived in the 50s):

                    Old Trucker: Not a false descriptor. These primal words were kept incomplete among the walking wolves of all lands, but in particular right here in our nation. By the spirits and men of this land. This Kanien’kehaka nation. Anyway, in this corner of Turtle Island, as you say, the GEHRAU (whispered growl tone) living among the people of OH (Flint) kept the primal tongue for centuries while your overseas ancestors lost pretty central parts of it, as you lost your souls. The howls of the hunt were gone,
                    replaced by words scavenged from the humans. Here, they were conserved in oral tradition. We abided by the Impergium on this side of the ocean, and so we
                    honored the wolf, rather than slaughtering it, like medieval man was wont to do, in search of arable land.

                    Fast-Paw: So, then us Europeans came and we learned the words back!

                    Abbie: No. Remember Colonialism 101? We killed them all before they taught us. First Nation’s elders call us Wyrm-comers for a reason. So yes, the spirits know more words than us. Now shut up, stupid ahroun!​
                    This section doesn't really distinguish between talking about human history and Garou history. Also, this makes it quite confusing what the Garou do and don't know about their past. Also for this edition that supposedly wanted things to be more universal and go away from tying Garou to human ethnicity and regions, this section is pretty much in favor of making distinctions along those lines. In this section, it really starts to matter again what these characters' heritage and ancestry are.

                    And it does come off like Old Trucker's implying here that the Impergium was a good thing and other Garou like those in Europe were fools for abandoning it.

But now these kids like Abbie know what happened back then was bad. And it's still their story because now it's about them making amends for what happened in the past.                                                 There certainly wasn't a universal avoidance of killing wolves; the Navajo used wolf skins in certain ceremonies. It shouldn't be assumed in turn that all groups did at all times just because it's not true as a universal statement, though. It's not uncommon for there to be animals it's taboo to kill. What is true, as far as I know, is that there weren't organized campaigns of slaughter like there were in much of Europe during the period of wolf extirpation there. The usual rejoinder is to simply assert that Native American tribes couldn't have extirpated wolves as Europeans did, and assert that they would've if they could've; I think that's largely bullshit and Europe's relationship with wolves was unusually dysfunctional.
