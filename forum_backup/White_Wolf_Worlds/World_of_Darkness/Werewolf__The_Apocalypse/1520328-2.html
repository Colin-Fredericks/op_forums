    Originally posted by lbeaumanior View Post
    I actually think that making it a one dot Merit, whose only effect is doubling the number of language that the Language merit grants would be a simpler solution.
    Simpler isn't always better though. Sure, more languages for your buck is definitely simpler, but it has knock on effects on how to adjudicate what counts as a language, or how to make someone that's good with all the variations of a language. The whole point of Merits (and why they're generally treated as semi-optional in the WoD books), is to add granularity to the game.

    If you just reduce Natural Linguist to extra language, then it's at best a point trap: if you intend on getting more than one Language Merit, you should buy Natural Linguist first. At that point you might as well not even have it as a separate Merit and just roll it into a function of the Language Merit itself where after being bi- or trilingual, future Language Merits start getting you more languages so people don't don't waste a bunch of points if they missed that Natural Linguist exists.

    Meanwhile, there's nothing for denoting that your character is someone that's good at spotting accents, dialects, code-switching, regional slang and colloquialisms, archaic/academic/dead language uses, and so on. Something that I think social media has somewhat hidden in pop culture, is just how much written word vs spoken word can be radically different when it comes to mutual understanding. As well, and this can be pretty important for something like the WoD where you have beings that were around centuries ago, languages change, drastically, over time. An Ancestor spirit that naturally speaks English,but English from a thousand years ago, might as well mean needing to find someone with Spirit Speech to translate instead of trying to communicate with them via modern English.

    So, something that Natural Linguist makes much, much, simpler is reducing the number of instances of the Language Merit you "need" to include the sheer variety within linguistics. However much it's a massive abstraction still, forcing someone to buy modern English, Middle English, and Old English to speak all of English isn't really "fun." Unless your game involves a lot of time travel or something, it just feels penalizing to do that, but it would feel very weird to have someone that only knows modern English to be able freely communicate with older versions of the language without magic because... they are different languages. To me, this is the much more important function because it's what someone play an linguist style characters wants to be able to do: they want to be able to navigate all these subtle changes that build up over time; not rack up a giant list of highly specific languages.

        To be fair, language related abilities have always been something that RPGs struggled with doing the split between game convenience and realism. The ST system isn't alone with that, and they tried quite a few things throughout editions and game lines. The Natural Linguist Merit suffers a bit from trying to cover too many things at once; being able to easily pick up new languages and being proficient in using rhetoric efficiently are two different skill-sets and should be two different Merits imo.

        The 'fair' option would be to have separate Abilities for each language, a learning Merit that reduces XP cost, and a proficiency Merit that gives bonus dice. That's an incredible XP sink if you charge full cost, and there are people who grow up with multiple languages natively. Plus, as Heavy Arms mentioned, the application for dead languages is limited. Languages as Merit seems a good compromise, but then you're less flexible with what you can do with Merits mechanically. I personally still like to cover general proficiency with the Linguistics Ability and treat known languages as something that goes as note on the sheet, independently from dotted stats. There's also the option to cover dead languages with the Academics ability, for languages like Latin, Ancient Greek, Sanskrit, and Middle [European language].
