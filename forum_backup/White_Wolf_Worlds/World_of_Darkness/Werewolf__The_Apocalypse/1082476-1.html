                We actually do get to see the BSD perspective in a few books, and the frightening thing about it is they aren't necessarily completely wrong. If the Wyrm wasn't totally fucked, they might even be on the right side.                 As for labelling Pentex bad guys, well, that one is pretty unambiguous. Pentex are bad guys, and they would be whether or not they served the Wyrm. Their entire market model is 'maximize human suffering', and not for a 'noble' purpose like 'human suffering purifies the soul' or 'suffering breaks the chains placed on us by the demiurge', but purely for 'it will make us, on the individual level, wealthier/happier/immortal/whatever-we-want, even if it makes the greater collective live in wretched misery'. The greedy, malevolent corporation that literally plans to enslave the totality of existence for its own benefit is evil in a way even the Dancers aren't. Mistaking 'the world is doomed, we need to end it/stop trying to keep it from disintegrating' - whether to be reborn anew or simply to end suffering - for nihilism is a big mistake and undermines the entire Spiral mindset and theology. Nor is the rejection of the Litany a nihilistic act, but rather one designed to shatter taboos that hold back the believer.

                                                So how can they be the fastest growing tribe? That's simple. They breed prolifically. They breed with whoever they like, because why wouldn't they? No part of their theology forbids it, no part of their struggle makes it unwise, and the experience is both pleasurable and satisfying. Some rape, others seduce, some just get drunk and see what happens. All is permissible, so why shouldn't they?

                                                But instead of answers, all I can find are what look like randomly selected passages from something H. R. Giger wrote when he was going through puberty. That's just bad storytelling. I can't find the verisimilitude in it. One could make an analogy to ISIL or something, but even ISIL has more coherent ideals and motivations than the Black Spiral Dancers. ISIL is also much smaller than many Muslim sects, whereas the Black Spiral Dancers are somehow the largest Garou tribe, and I don't think it's overly optimistic to say ISIL doesn't have much staying power. The Black Spiral Dancers have been around longer than Islam, IIRC. Groups like that have more going for them than orgies on speed.

                                                What I think makes more sense is to suppose that what we see of the Black Spiral Dancers is how the Garou Nation sees them, and how the Garou Nation sees them is similar to what people in countries with sharia law must envision when they hear about things like "rape culture on American college campuses". The Garou Nation has such a reactionary and dogmatic view of sexuality that they can't picture a tribe that doesn't accept the First Litany as anything but a nonstop rape orgy. If one doesn't understand the social context, one pictures ritualistic gang rape, but the reality is usually a lot more mundane.
                                                The majority is that way, or an attempt to 'out of universe' downplay the impact of the Chronicle of the Black Labyrinth. For a more understandable BSD tribe, read it with gnosticism on the mind.

                                                Plus, the Garou are an essentially premodern society, so they're likely to tend more toward the old fashioned "property crime" definition of rape: it's not so much about whether the victim consented as whether the victim's parents or spouse consented. I'm using gender neutral terms here, because gender privilege doesn't seem to be as big a deal for the Garou. It's more about whether you can Change, followed by whether you have Gnosis.
                                                That depends on tribe, largely. It's an attitude you might find among some Fangs or Get or Fianna. It's not one you're likely to find among the Gnawers, Furies, etc.

                                                And finally, accusations of cannibalism are about the oldest trick in the book on cultural chauvinism.
                                                Of course, they actually are cannibals by their own account. But so are a bunch of the Gaian tribes historically. For the Spirals, the eating of human flesh - as forbidden by the litany - is another of those taboos to break to achieve liberation from the old mindset.

                                                And the Dancers are much more interesting. Pentex though is a 'simplistic' villain because corporate greed is a 'simplistic' evil. Remember, the two are different entities.

                                                What does "the Weaver tried to bind the Wyrm in its pattern web and the Wyrm went insane" actually mean? Does that even evoke a clear image for you? I don't see how it really explains anything. For starters, "insane" just isn't a very descriptive term at all. The entities involved feel more like abstract concepts or perhaps political factions than literal entities. It just doesn't strike me as interesting in itself, but it would make sense if it were a cover/abstraction for something far more complex.
                                                It evokes a very clear image and I'm not sure why it doesn't for you. The entities are abstract concepts-turned-spirits, but that's far from uncommon in religion and the occult, but the underlying concept is simple. The principle of Ordering ('the Weaver'), finding the 'chaotic' entropy infesting its works, sought to prevent the destruction of its works. Unfortunately, without the constant cycle of birth-death-rebirth, all things fall apart, and that's exactly what happens next. 'The Wyrm', as that concept-spirit of entropic destruction and breakdown into raw elements, as the negrido, fragments and becomes unable to perform its task correctly.

                                                Everything else is a physical or spirit-physical (that is, 'physical' in the Umbra, not purely spiritual/intellectual) reflection of the higher spiritual dimensions where this plays out. Is there a literal spider weaving a dragon into its web? Yes, but that is only one reflection of the struggle between the two concept-deities. It's also the level that the lowly creatures born to the Lowest emanations can interact with, being incapable of interacting with the higher levels.

                                                -- many Christians I've met in real life

                                                What you say definitely seems accurate when it comes to spirits from jagglings on up to anything you can take as a totem. But if you were born in the World of Darkness and didn't have access to the books, how would you verify that Gaia or the Triat literally existed? I feel like I would eventually have to resort to the same sorts of arguments that IRL theologians use, whether I was a werewolf or not. There's the old appeal to gnosis, except that the term and concept of "gnosis" was lifted directly from an IRL religion. You may have divine revelation, but claiming that you have divine revelation still doesn't set you apart from the religious.

                                                You can ask lesser spirits about their superiors, but then you run into what I call the "you can't have your god and meet him, too" problem. When the beings you call "gods" or "spirits" exist literally, and you can see, touch, and talk to them, and they have finite needs, resources, and motivations that can be threatened, not just in old stories, but before your eyes, your belief in them does cease to be religious. However, I would say that such a belief loses in gravitas what it gains in credibility. You're not talking about religion anymore, you're talking about interdimensional politics. The spirits you can actually meet in Werewolf are approximately as ephemeral and fallible as anyone else, so it's hard to say, as a werewolf, that their belief in Gaia and the Triat is any less faith-based than anyone else's.
                                                I dispute that meeting your gods makes them any less divine. It just means they don't fit quite into the Western conception of what a god is.
