                    There's also some serious confirmation bias that always happens when this comes up. Horror fans see "bite transferred curse" as iconic in ways more general audiences frequently don't. Urban fantasy fans (esp. the romance heavy side of it) are used to much bigger moves away from The Wolfman and An American Werewolf in London. And it's really simple why this happens: "I'm a normal human except on full moons I transform into an uncontrollable beast" doesn't work in most genres. The "alpha wolf" trope is far more iconic in pop culture werewolves because it's actually useful in multiple genres as a basis for werewolf social groups that operate differently from human society.

                    And, of course, most pop culture doesn't have a need to draw hard lines between werewolf and vampire. Teen Wolf was a pretty popular 2010s show that stuck to the "bitten" take, but that aspect fades into the background pretty fast to look far more like something most WoD fans would consider VtM territory (ex: where the wolf that bit you sits on the "alpha" hierarchy puts expectations on new werewolves regarding local politics; if you're bitten by an Alpha like the main character was, you're expected to become one yourself and people are going to treat you that way).

                                    While I'm prepared for people to post all sorts of shit for bringing this up it's part of the problem:

                                    In broad pop culture how many people can name Jacob as a famous fictional werewolf character vs. specific characters from any of those (even if Twilight fans will probably "well actually..." to no end because Jacob is a shape-shifter and those are different from werewolves in the Twilight setting even if Jacob is from a line of shape-shifters that can only turn into wolves).                                     Voluntary shape-shifting.
                                    Enhanced healing, physical prowess, and senses without needing to shift; leading to much longer potential life-span if they don't die in feuds with other supernaturals.
                                    Alpha wolf trope present (you didn't even bother listing how much that comes up in your own examples).
                                    Packs develop telepathic communication.

                                    So is that immensely popular franchise's depiction of it's main wolf character something that sounds like any of your examples, or sounds like someone putting their WtA PC into another project?

                                    Because even if you think Twilight is hot garbage, it was very, very popular, and a lot of people are going to see Jacob as a "normal" style werewolf because it was just that big as a franchise. People that have probably never heard of things like Project Metal Beast or Cursed (those are some pretty niche picks).

                                    If you want to talk about iconic, you can't just rattle off a list of stories, because you're making cultural influence and impact part of the evaluation. It doesn't matter if there's ten "bitten" stories for every other type of werewolf story, if the other type of werewolf story has a billion dollar franchise attached to it and most of the bitten stories are cult classics or niche films only horror genre fans care about.

                                    It also matters a lot how much "transmission" even matters to a given story. Like, a gigantic plot point of the Underworld franchise is that there's a combination of exposure to the different strains of the virus and genetics of those descending from the mortal son of the Corvinus family. Biting isn't even important because it's just a blood borne virus that can be transmitted in multiple ways (Lucian was the start of a whole second type of werewolf as the virus mutated from his mother to him during pregnancy, so he was never bitten to become one). So the bite is there, but it's really not what matters to the story and that the bite is simply one of many transmission vectors is very plot relevant. This is completely different from the Being Human series where the whole any normal human can become a werewolf by being bitten is a more central part of the drama for the werewolf character trying to navigate their human life, their flat/house mates, and the wider supernatural world were werewolves aren't really organized because they're more incidental compared to groups like vampires.

                                    And then there's points like should D&D, TES, Fable, or Van Hellsing even count because they're not stories about werewolves directly, they're broad fantasy stories that happen to have werewolves in them (and some of them are playable to an extent for the games). You can run D&D and never deal with a werewolf. TES and Fable (if they even come up in a given game) engaging with werewolves is largely optional and getting infected is made fairly simple to deal with if you don't want to play one; it's basically just a disease that any magical disease cure can get rid of (if on a timer). Etc.

                                    So... yeah, a list doesn't mean much, because it's not actually making a point. You didn't pull a mic drop, you showed off how little you care to engage in your own point's logic.
