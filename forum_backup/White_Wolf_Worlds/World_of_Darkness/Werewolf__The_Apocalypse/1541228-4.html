            Originally posted by Ragged Robin View Post
            hmmm bear with me because I'm a brit so my experience is minimal but I've been told a lot of white writers put first nation groups on a pedestal as somehow more in tune with nature as opposed to the eco-social ramifications of high population density and feudal/early era economics. The writing that the first nations didnt kill wolves universally as some sort of enlightened harmony mindset does smack of this to a degree.
            The people who told you that exaggerated things---a lot. Yes, it's true that people exaggerated the degree to which Native American cultures didn't damage the environment. But if you do even the slightest reading into the relevant literature it's very clear that there was an ideological component to environmental destruction, and that wasn't universally shared. This is true even of groups that had lifestyles where conflict with wolves would be an issue. The Navajo were sheep herders for much of their history, but based on what records survive it appears there was not substantial conflict with wolves until deer were nearly extirpated on the Navajo reservation and the wolves started eating livestock en masse. In Wolves: Behavior, Ecology, and Conservation, Steven Fritts and their coauthors note that despite indigenous foragers competing with wolves for game, there still wasn't much conflict with the species. And they're not idealizing Native Americans; they note that there was killing of pups. It just seems to have been sporadic and localized rather than a large-scale campaign.

            In contrast, by the point at which the Navajo started persecuting wolves (mid-1900s), the total extermination of wolves had been an official government fixation in the USA for many years and wolves had been exterminated in several European countries for centuries. Since the Middle Ages it had been seen as evil in much of Europe. Even in natural history books, such as The Natural History of Quadrupeds, you'd get quotes like this:
            Wolves are such ferocious and useless creatures that all other animals detest them, yea they even hate each other, and therefore scarcely ever live together, each one in its own separate hole. [...] Perhaps of all other animals, wolves are the most hateful while living and the most useless when dead. [...] The continual agitations of this restless animal renders him os furious, that he frequently ends his life in madness.
            And while unfortunately I can't find my copy of The Nature of Tomorrow: A History of the Environmental Future right now, in it Rawson talks a lot about utopian visions of the future after the early modern period, exterminating all other forms of life was seen as a positive good.

            It's kind of hard to read the scholarly literature on this subject and not come to the conclusion that for much of its history, most of Europe was simply unusually bad. It's not that Native Americans were perfectly in touch with nature, it's that much of Europe had a sociopathic disregard for other forms of life and a level of wolf-hatred that is best referred to as "psychotic."
