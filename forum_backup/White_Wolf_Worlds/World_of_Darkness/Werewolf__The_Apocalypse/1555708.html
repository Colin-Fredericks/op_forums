    Designing a "Chronicle" to introduce new players into Werewolf: the Apocalypse

    11-21-2025, 01:46 AM
    Before I begin, Disclaimer:


    ̶D̶o̶n̶'̶t̶ ̶t̶h̶i̶n̶k̶ ̶"̶C̶h̶r̶o̶n̶i̶c̶l̶e̶"̶ ̶i̶n̶ ̶t̶h̶e̶ ̶s̶e̶n̶s̶e̶ ̶t̶h̶a̶t̶ ̶y̶o̶u̶ ̶k̶n̶o̶w̶ ̶i̶t̶.̶ Instead, think more like a series of stories (player-focused in this case), where the players are all playing Homid-breed werewolves who have just had their first change, are together as a pack. They're not Garou YET. They don't have a tribe YET. They don't know what the Umbra is YET.

    BUT! They do however have each other

    That's the sale's pitch.

    Why would I (or anyone) want to make a chronicle like this? Simple:

    To reflect the players, who are all newbies, getting into the game for the very first time, and have LITERALLY NO IDEA what they're getting into, and for Storytellers who want to see peoples minds be completely blown and melted.
    "Oh, it's just a game where I run around as a murder-furry! How complex or deep could it possibly be?"
    - A player who has yet to see the wondrous beauty of The Umbra.

    Goal: For the Storyteller to teach the intricacies and mechanics of the setting to the players, and have the players learn stuff like "The Umbra", "The Triat", and "The Garou Nation" through the eyes of their characters.

    Obviously, the players would still know how to play on a MECHANICAL level (Attribute + Ability), and learn more as they play, but overall, they, like their characters, don't know a lot.

    Basically, kinda like Hunter: the Reckoning, but with the framework of Werewolf: the Apocalypse, and roleplaying those "First days of being a werewolf" of those characters.

    The Climax of the chronicle would be encountering the Garou Nation - or maybe the PCs learning their first spirit Gift. I dunno.

    Rule 1: MAXIMUM PLAYER BAMBOOZLE IN A WAY THAT'S FUN AND REWARDING.
    That's what I'm going for.

    This chronicle is meant to be designed for Storytellers who want their players going "Wait a minute, WE CAN DO THAT?!" Over and over again as they embrace the game. It'll be great.

    I had a two-page character sheet that was hand-crafted for this, but I lost it when my external hard drive crashed, so that sucks, and I'd need to rebuild it completely from scratch, and I don't want to do that! I have been using a homebrewed version of the Ronin rules, but it doesn't feel exactly right when your players are not just uninitiated cubs, but also people who haven't played a WoD game outside of maybe Vampire: the Masquerade and maybe the Bloodlines game.

    Anyways, if anyone wants to pitch in with their thoughts, two cents, or (in my opinion, extremely important because I can never seem to find them) the books that storytellers should take another browse through if they want to do this, then I'd highly appreciate it! Thanks in advance!

              Originally posted by DaFrontlineSpecter View Post
              Don't think "Chronicle" in the sense that you know it. Instead, think more like a series of stories (player-focused in this case)
              That's the definition of a Chronicle, it doesn't have to be about regular starting characters by the books.

              Also, that's one of the first ideas most new STs have for a game.

              It isn't a bad idea, but pulling it out is harder than it seems at first, so we need something more specific and elaborated than this sales pitch.

              What's the story? Having a sandbox for the PCs to explore while they learn the rules is nice and dandy, but without an actual story to follow it'll bog down quickly, because they don't even know how or why to try things. The biggest challenge is to design a plot that will work as tutorial and still be fun to play.

              Giving the right amount of info beforehand is also important, because players have their own expectations and may not match your enthusiasm when they learn the game isn't about what they thought it would be. They may also find it frustrating to be on the dark about everything they can do or they're facing.

              Your final challenge is to deal with actual player knowledge and understanding. Some may have a harder time getting what's going on than you expect, and that can mess with the game. But also, many will get it easier than we expect. How complex or deep could it possibly be? We live in a world post-WoD and post-Underworld, the bad movie that nonetheless was a success and made WoD-style Werewolves the Hollywood standard for the next two decades at least. Be ready for a lot of players getting things way before you expect.

              Finally, you have to deal with both players that pretend to not know the game, but actually know it, and players that do research about it between sessions and may end up knowing even more than you do. Not easy to deal with that unless you already know the group very well.

              So, as interesting as the pitch is, and it really is, I wish to see a more detailed plan than just "that would be cool". It's hard, been there, done that, and seen many others do it too.

                  While I tend to be much more adamant about not doing this in other WoD games (Mage the most), there is an axiom I hold to regarding this idea:

                  You can't learn without doing. The problem with starting with characters significantly below the WoD games starting point is that this frequently means the players never directly learn things in-play because they don't have the ability to do those things yet. Which leads to what monteparnas was saying regarding some players just reading to figure it out, and others just feeling lost and listless.

                  WtA also has a lot of tools for doing the sort of "as everyone should already know" exposition dumps in how Garou culture was designed. A Sept's monthly Moot traditionally includes:

                  - Recitation of the Litany with Ragabash hecklers to add color and nuance
                  - More experienced Galliards telling stories from ancient tales that they feel could be relevant to today, to boasting the Sept's latest accomplishments, to mourning the loss of warriors; and it all has to be stuff the freshest Cubs could get
                  - Watching the more experienced Philodoxes and Theurges running the event, settling social and spiritual matters, and ensuring all the ceremonial stuff is handled
                  - General announcements of news everyone needs to hear about
                  - Formal challenges to see how those are handled (intros to the mechanics for them as well, including combat)
                  - The party after business is over to get some informal social stuff in
                  - The thing ends with a big ritual hunt too (so more chances for some basic combat)

                  A lot of STs IME kinda speed through Moots because... a lot of that is fairly repetitive and gets boring fast for experienced WtA players outside of some juicy plot events that might be the real focus. But they make excellent starting points for new WtA players because it's all about Garou culture, it's designed to get Cubs and fresh Cliaths into the flow of ritual life, and info dumping is literally something that supposed to happen. There's almost no core mechanics to the game that can't come up in a Moot. There's also a lot of options if you want to adjust things. For example if you plan on the PCs operating out of a small low level Caern where the Moots they've been in so far are pretty small things because there's so few Garou around, the game can start with a visit to a major Caern that their Sept needs to keep on good terms with. If you want to start with a big Caern so you can have a big cast of NPCs, you can have visitors coming in to add spice, plot hooks, and reasons for things to get explained in more detail that "normal." Etc.

                      It's important to consider that the books recognize and state this difficulty directly.

                      Your "standard" starting character is expected to have been a full splat member for some time when the game starts, and also expected to have been directly educated about their condition by a senior member or more, precisely so the character would have had the time to pick on everything they need for that starting sheet to work.

                      Characters that don't get this education exist, but they're explicitly shown at considerable disadvantage, and one of those is precisely to be ignorant on a lot of basic stuff, including stuff they can do, sometimes for their whole life (which, granted, tend to be short).

                      The simplest option for a game in this style is to have an actual guide teaching the PCs who have some reason to not spill all the beans up front.

                              Well, when it comes to new STs Moots can be a lot because it's a lot to learn if you don't already have some familiarity with the game. I don't think WtA is necessarily the best first game to run if you've never done it before. Even with in the WoD games, there are games where the base premise is much closer to pop-culture expectations (VtM is the poster child of the franchise for a reason), and games where their aren't nearly as many small exceptional moving parts in the mechanics to keep in mind.

                              That said, I'm highly advocating for a "introducing a bunch of new players to WtA" not start with "a bunch of freshly changed Garou that have no clue" that climaxes with their first Moot. As you seem to agree, that means a lot of learning but outside of the context where doing is a primary consideration. I'm advocating starting with closer to base-line starting PCs and a Moot because it's a lot of doing in a highly controlled event in-universe to create a framework for the players to feel comfortable in while they learn about stuff.

                              Of course, I've also run a lot of HtR, and I think people vastly overstate the idea that the Imbued are all clueless and don't know anything. Beyond the numerous low level information gathering powers in the game, Hunter-net is there with lots of information they can easily have access too. What HtR does differently from the rest of the WoD - and frankly most games broadly - is that the Imbued are dealing with a much more modern problem. They have too much information, and they don't know how to sort the information by quality. Trying to find out information is like trying to learn about stuff from Reddit: a thread can easily devolve into dozens of tangents as people argue their pet theories instead of making sure a new hunter has the basic details they need. The survival guide is both a supplement and an in-game text (though not one made like the Book of Nod or Hunter: Apocrypha) specifically because some of the Hnet users wanted to create something to help new Imbued out so until they get more comfortable learning how to navigate Hnet's weird culture. HtR isn't about playing people with no information, it's about playing people with no context for way too much information.

                              The sticking point for Hunter - IME - is the Conviction economy, not the amount of information the PCs have access to. That's something I work to address early and quickly because it completely dominates the base mechanics of the game.

                                  Actually simple isn't the important part. The important part is to have stuff happening and clear goals to achieve at each step.

                                  Even a simple plot can be extremely obtuse in such a situation because it depends on doing something the players have no clue they have to try. Think about crossing into the Umbra: what do they have to do to accomplish that? How would they even know they have to accomplish that?

                                  Putting a mirror in the scene with a vision of the Umbra may work just fine. But if it don't, you may end up with PCs that not only won't try to even touch it, they may avoid or try to break the mirror, they may see the Umbra as a threat and the mirror as a barrier, and nothing else. And this will happen a lot down the line. So they need a clear reason to try to reach that strange place, and a clear goal to achieve there instead of just wander aimlessly.

                                  Your plot may actually be as complex as you want, as long as its pieces have clear paths to follow, so the game won't drag when they're lost, and some action happening, to keep them engaged and curious.

                                  Originally posted by DaFrontlineSpecter View Post
                                  3 - Design a plot that will work as tutorial and still be fun to play (High priority, as it is highest challenge).​
                                  The challenge here is the Tutorial part.

                                  What I mean by tutorial is how the plot will actively teach the players about the things they can do and how they do them.

                                  Basically that's what an instructor is usually meant to do. Eliminating the instructor or limiting their role means you have to make the plot itself do the work. That's a tutorial.

                                  And this is not a "so we just have to make a tutorial" thing. Actually doing it is hard. You have to jot down everything the players have to learn, and for each and every bit you must have a plot point where the players will both learn that it exist in some form and how to try it, and every single time you run the risk of your players not getting it and roaming around without a purpose.

                                  Everything else comes after that. If you don't have the tutorial structure, then you don't have a functional plot for a game you'll start by not telling your players what they can do.

                                  This is not just about foreshadowing, that's the easy part.

                                  What's hard is to devise an adequate pitch to invite the players.

                                  Making a game where the players don't actually know what kind of game they're going to play is fraught with traps, because you may find out too late that some of the things you love about the game are actually no-nos for a given player. Will they enjoy playing spiritual warriors? Eco-fighters? Having Auspices? Ripping their clothes to shreds? Being Rage-fueled killer machines that can loose their temperament at any time? Will they like the Garou society when all is said and done? After all, a bad ending may spoil the entire experience.

                                  You may also find out that they really want things you didn't consider, like playing through the drama of a curse or the idea of tying oneself up in full moon days. There are many werewolf stories out there that aren't just murder-furries, even if you didn't knew of them, your players may now and have expectations you won't deliver. And they may be important to them. That's all before we even touch on how they may take the foes they'll face, or the reasons for facing them.

                                  So that's the conundrum of the info, the most important one, how you explain things before the very game begins, so you avoid as much as possible the hard situation of dealing with player frustration, not pontual frustration, but overall frustration with the game as a whole.

                                      I have given this a lot of thought, but now I think I know where to begin:

                                      Let's Open the Chronicle with a Moot. The key here would be in letting the older players lead the new players, and everyone having fun with teaching/learning the mechanics.

                                      Veteran players can have fun teaching new players the ropes (which then builds party cohesion between the players, and pack cohesion with the characters, simultaneously and organically).
                                      This also allows the older players to be in control over the pacing so it feels more like organic character interaction and player-led mentorship rather than "We have players who never played werewolf, so this is our mandatory tutorial."

                                      Plus, this level of connection between characters and players being in the same boat as each other, feels unique because the older players' characters are the ones who can get a lot of fun out of helping the new players get comfortable in the skin of their characters.

                                      The player choices are about getting familiarized with game mechanics in a fun, in-universe way (again, getting the "wait, WE CAN DO THAT?!" reaction out of new players as they learn things).


                                      Having a Story (roughly 1-3 game sessions) dedicated to this, instead of a whole Chronicle is probably the smartest option here, and I was biting off way more than I could chew. NOT A WHOLE CHRONICLE, but definitely a good start to a chronicle, where a primary mood could be about learning(?)


                                      (Random side-note: In order to think outside the box, there needs to be a box. Player choices during character creation are a huge part of that box's construction, and I am learning as I brainstorm this.​)
