                                    Backtracking to the subject of "If Gaia is inevitably going to die, what's the point of all this?", because it hits a thing that's bugged me on the tone of W5, but it's different from everyone else's, from what I've seen so far.

                                    Because Gaia was always fucked. The Garou were never going to win.

                                    People have always had a wide array of reactions to the game's embrace of the pre-millenial* apocalyptic fervor, but the end-of-the-world is one of those things that's baked into the games, the backbone of both the gothic and punk stylings. Hope had it's place, the possibliltiy of change had it's place, but World leaned more heavily into the punk reaction of retribution rather than a more gothic turn towards redemption, in that it's deafult understanding about the Apocalypse(and it's contemporaires) is that there's no real stopping it. Whether you liked that or not, it was there.

                                    So that being the case, why is it notably sour to hear the fact this way? And I know the answer, and that just makes it all the more bizarre how amateur the way it's handled here is.

                                    I mentioned in a rant about a fangame once that fatalism and nihilism are some of those themes you have to handle carefully for it to be rewarding, and the main way I came up with to do so was that the system (both in terms of setting and mechanics) had to strongly imply that everything would come to naught, but never directly say that that's the goal of the game outright. My suggestion at the time was that it used it's small scale failures that it was interested in be used to build up to a larger scale success, and then leave the success of those projects to speak for themselves (relying on the idea that no matter the ambition, the realization of such was never going to be enough to effect meaningfully trangressive change), and I've since made the argument that the way Beast: the Primordial handles that is also pretty clever, inviting you make something of yourself, your community, and your environment while allowing for the human flaws at work as well as the determined rejection of Heroes to strongly subtextually imply they'll never actually succeed, not in meaningful enough ways, all without never actually saying they can't or won't-in fact, it's got a very strong surface of Earn Your Happy Ending, even down to having explicit end goals that signify an arrival to success.

                                    In Apocalypse, the general way that it balances out that apocalyptic nihilism and fatalism with the validation and celebration of player action is by making the Garou good at battles and bad at wars. As a player character, you'll probably had lot of major successes alongside your myriad failures, lot of little campaigns and actions where you get to feel like you're making a difference, but when you step back and look at it from a top down perspective (often provided by the books on running examination, both just in general and through the metaplot) you can't help but shake the feeling those victories don't amount to anything for the grand problem. In fact, part of the reason the Apocalypse is inevitable is because so many of their powers-that-be over this have a top down power and influence on things even the whole Garou Nation can't match (though with how large both are, you could mistake otherwise), and that the damage done by those powers is already so much more than anything on the individual level can do about. I haven't read enough of Apocalypse to comment on how it balances this across the line, but in the initial presentations from the cores and what supplements I have, it does a really good job letting players focus on what they think they can do and the changes they can see, while letting storyteller's keep perspective on what it's not doing on the grand scale.

                                    The way W5 is going, though, with the Garou Nation beaten down and focused purely on the street level, basically doesn't allow the gameline the room to continue the sleight of hand. It makes it pretty naked that player involvement isn't going to win the war. There's no hypothetical of one set of allies taking something and building into something larger, until all these things done build up to a binding and cleansing of the Wyrm and a harmonious existence with nature is formed, because nothing's big enough anymore to create that illusion. It asks the question "What are you going to do in the war against the Wyrm, knowing that anything you try is ultimately never going to be enough?" rather than "What are you going to do in the war against the Wyrm?" and just relying on the Wyrm as the manifold manifestion of various forms of entropy (both built up over time and inevitable from the start) to be it's own statement, that you'll get the more you think about it.**

                                    And the fact that they made an error that is most readily comparable to a fangame's eager mistake is just kind of....disheartening.

                                    *because this means something related but different from how it's used nowadays, the turn of the millenium had an apocalyptic flavor all of it's own, and it's that sort of horror I'm talking about as opposed to the generation that got it assigned as a name alongside with the "world continued with a whimper and a sigh" ennui and new rights revolution furor they picked up.
                                    **And, because this is one of the strengths of letting the situation speak for itself, it also allows play groups to decide to reject that thesis, and decide just how much they want to, allowing for heroics in monstrosity with a thundering victory for a better world to Ragnarokian rebirths of a new world.

                                            • Gaia's dead?-okay, sell it on a setting to bring Gaia back to life, lots of media deals with revival and the costs of resurrection that can be tons of fun. You don't even need to make it possible, technically, just plant the idea that you can and let players run into the brick wall their own way.
                                            • The Setting's Mid-Apocalypse?-okay, allow the overriding force of the Wyrm to let the setting get weirder and more unexpected-including ways that make it feasible to for bringing Gaia back, or creating a new world. Metrofinal, while predicated on more powerful actors, is a good example of how to pull off a story mid-apocalypse.
                                            • The Garou's Nation's been torn apart? Cool, but what can be torn apart can be sewn together-the new tension is in how factions think that should be done, and how to take advantage of the new setting conceit and it's quirks.
                                            All of those can be cool and fun quirks and conceits, and even keep in line with Apocalypse as a gameline (albeit, with limits). There is something particularly interesting in the question of what the sundered factions of a nation of predator-warriors do with the pursuit of global rebirth in the wake of an oncoming final death (hell, I'd love to see another gameline directly go into the idea). The current problem is that, instead of using these conceits as a foundation that will be built upon and expanded into interesting, plabyable ideas, they're simply presented as the playable ideas despites being conclusions on their own.

                                            I'm not saying these ideas are good, mind-but as anyone who works in creative fields can tell you, a bad idea executed well sells more than a great idea badly executed. Where this gets odd, again, is that I'm not sure if anyone is asking how they're going to make these things playable and fun. I'm sure they thought they could be those things, but I get the feeling no one did the homework to validate it, y'know?
