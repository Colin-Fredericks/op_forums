    W5 And Garou Oral Tradition

    07-02-2025, 05:11 AM
    I thought it couldn’t get worse for W5 but I just have to talk about what a new preview for an upcoming W5 supplement says. It’s about a side-bar called “Oral culture”.

    One aspect of the Werewolf the Apocalypse 5th Edition corebook is its insistence that the Garou barely know anything about their history. This was partially the result of that edition’s new approach of “re-imagination, not continuation” and a at the time core-belief that as far as Fifth edition was concerned, not only was the old lore from previous editions bad but the very notion of heavily investing in lore ought to be avoided. The Tribes had been redesigned to be neutral concepts with no ties to any human culture or any particular geographical region, for example. Meanwhile, core-concepts like the Garou Nation had been “destroyed” and things like the Litany were described as a badly designed idea that Garou Elders abused while young Garou stopped believing in it.

    Another way the corebook tried to emphasize its distancing from any sort of lore was to explain how little the present-day Garou actually knew. Garou still had the oral traditions from previous editions but in this version, it simply was an ineffective way to maintain knowledge of the Garou’s past. This is shown in how the Garou’s knowledge of the (now fallen) Garou Nation expresses itself (corebook pg. 37):

    The Garou Nation, as it was known, existed for a brief period of relative success, but even there, its actual duration is unknown and varies by who’s reciting the history. Was it decades? Centuries? Millennia? Because the historical events involved in it date to various times across myriad locations, to say with any certainty is impossible, and even spirits speak of it in terms unsuited to the physical world.
    Another distinction the corebook introduced was that while the Tribes weren’t tied to any specific human culture or region of the world, the way Garou interacted with Spirits was varied (from the sidebar “Spirit Veracity” on pg. 232):

    When it comes to individual spirits, especially powerful ones such as Patron Spirits or the like, how a Garou interprets interactions with those spirits may occur in the context of the culture they recognize most.
    [ … ]
    So, for example, a Ghost Council Garou with a Native American background may well perceive Horned Serpent in a different way than fellow Ghost Council Garou living amid Scandinavian, Iranian, or North African human cultures.
    Two things then became the pillars for how Garou understood themselves in this new edition: One, they barely remembered anything of their history and culture. Two, there were big regional differences in how Garou acted.

    Fast forward to the Shattered Nation supplement and things immediately start to get complicated because according to that book, Jonah Albrecht, the last king of the Garou Nation is alive. But this supplement was still doubling down on the notion that the Garou barely knew anything about their past and had a hard time retaining any knowledge of the past (Shatterd Nation pg. 40):

    The Garou are creatures of the present, their traditions built on oral storytelling. This means that factual accuracy is often not considered particularly important as long as the broad outlines fit what the crowd at the moot wants to hear. The stories of Garou from the '80s and '90s are already ancient history, having happened before most Garou today were even born.
    According to this, Garou can’t even retain knowledge from just the previous generation. So simply remembering in 2025 what the 80s and 90s were like is a big challenge for Garou apparently.

    And the insistence on the relevance of regional differences also gets mentioned (pg. 7):

    Modern communications technology means that Garou can talk to each other easier than ever before, and so have realized that their immutable traditions and culture are often actually entirely local. Other Garou in other places do things differently.
    With this lack of historical knowledge and all these supposed regional differences, W5 makes it very hard to believe there is anything that amounts to a common denominator between the werewolves in this setting.

    And this brings us to a preview for the upcoming Umbra-supplement “Moonlit Path”. But this is less about the Umbra but the sidebar in the Introduction called “Oral Culture”:



    I feel like this is really the coup-de-grace for W5. “To speak of an oral tradition is a misnomer.” – “Hey, I forget, did we say Garou have an oral tradition? Nah, they don’t.” Well, I guess, we now know why 90% of the Galliard-characters in W5 are all musicians. The idea of Galliards as historians, lorekeepers and storytellers has been completely tossed aside, it seems. Additionally, let's set aside the fact that there have been plenty of text passages even in W5 where it explicitly says that Garou have an oral tradition.

    Of course, there’s the sentence following that which talks of “multiple traditions separated by geography”. That’s why I mentioned the second pillar before of these regional differences, like how an Iranian Ghost Council Garou sees something different compared to a Norwegian one or how it’s mentioned that thanks to modern communication (weird idea but okay…) Garou realized that there are huge differences in how they practice their traditions and culture. So these mentioned “multiple traditions” play into that. But… But! I can’t stress this enough: There hasn’t been a single example of W5 delving into this thus far! The adventures are always exclusively located in Europe or North America and Shattered Nation certainly has a diverse cast. That character is Lebanese, this one is Chinese, this one’s from Nigeria etc. They do that and then never go beyond that. All these supposed big differences in local traditions or these aforementioned “multiple traditions separated by geography” have never been addressed. And at this point, it just feels like a lazy excuse that these books keep talking about that. Especially because now W5 is claiming Garou don’t even have a common oral tradition. What's the point of even saying "No, they don't have a common oral tradition. They have all these other different traditions based on geography... which this book won't talk about."? Great, thanks W5, that sure cleared things up!

    On the bright side, I guess, you can no longer say that W5 thinks oral traditions are bad and ineffective in maintaining traditions, culture and history. Because in W5 Garou don’t care about that anymore apparently. That one long sentence sticks out there which starts by saying Garou aren’t interested in studying and researching Garou traditions and history but then it ends with the lament of the knowledge that gets lost when they die in battle. Well, since they weren’t keen on learning anything, to begin with, I doubt much got lost…

          I think the issue is that they started out with the premise of "The Garou don't know anything." and then worked backwards from that and tried to work it into the WtA-setting in a way that makes sense. But since then there has been so much effort expended on explaining why Garou don't know anything that it's hard to see what's even left of the Garou as an identity. It's also a bit ironic that one of the moves in that regard was to decouple the Tribes from any connection to any human ethnicity or region. But since there's been so much about how the Garou know little to nothing about their own history and culture, you're left with these "multiple traditions separated by geography" - which is exactly what they had removed from the Tribes at the beginning. If anything, now it would make more sense to have the Silent Striders be associated with Egypt, the Black Furies with Greece, the Fianna with Ireland etc. So, in a way, this just leads right back to where this all started.

          Or you might as well follow this trajectory of bashing the Garou further and get this revelation next as an escalation of "Garou don't know anything." : "In fact, what most Garou don't know is that the majority of werewolves in the world don't call themselves Garou. These werewolves whenever they come into contact with the Garou are perplexed by their lack of knowledge and understanding. Further, the Garou's beliefs in Gaia, the Apocalypse and their all-consuming war against the Wyrm strike these werewolves as somewhat simplistic, unbalanced and needlessly uncompromising." I mean, why not go the whole nine yards and just write the Garou off as completely irrelevant?

                                              I get where you're coming from on this, but I'd push back a bit and point this criticism into a different sociological lens.

                                              W5 is the most GenX edition of WtA. By which I mean it's not a product of the 90s made by GenXers, it's very much GenXers trying to make a book for the 2020s.

                                              Maybe it's just me feeling a bit old (or more precisely middle age) lately, but as I have had to come to terms with the fact that I'll never be part of "youth" culture ever again - I can just be happy for my younger friends and family and at least know enough to follow along with whatever they're up to - I find myself in tension with things we all intellectually recognize as part of aging. It's completely normal for us to lose touch with the younger generations. It's also very normal to not want this to happen.

                                              Something I've increasingly seen amongst my age-cohort is the creeping cynicism we used to deride our parents' for having. However much we know the younger generation is getting dealt their hands by us, esp. as we're in a period where that's a pretty shit hand to be dealt, there's this deep set temptation to blame them for it. I feel like I do a decent job of not falling into it (esp. with some of the dumb ass shit I see people I grew up with saying), it's not an "I overcame it, now I'm at least acceptable to hang out with young people successfully." It's a constant pressure to deflect blame down. Gen Z and Gen Alpha didn't invent smartphones, decide when the appropriate age to give them to kids was, or decide to clog the Internet with algorithms that are actively sabotaging their brains so that they consume less critically.

                                              And yet, what does this latest snippet from W5 say? The current generation is too preoccupied with human cultural concerns to care about traditions. The young people don't know because their priorities are wrong - even while acknowledging they didn't make the world the way it is - is the tone the passage sets. It's something I hear all the damned time by people that always have an excuse of how they aren't part of the problem.
