                          On the theme of werewolves and what they are like ...
                          • Wolf (1994), mainly for its exploration of shifts in personality and perceiving the world through heightened senses, something that I've always felt should be considered with the Garou and how they interact with the world. (Bill Willingham's Fables comic book series touched on this at least once, with the point that Bigby Wolf admits the entire reason he'd a heavy smoker is to dull his sense of smell to keep him from being distracted by Snow White all the time.)
                          • Wolfen (1981), as it was such a major influence on the development of the Garou and the game in general, dealing as it does with werewolf-like creatures with ties to Native American mysticism in modern day New York City defending their territory from property developers and facing potential extinction. I'm still trying to track down a copy of the Whitley Strieber novel it's based on, as it's supposed to be pretty good in its own right, differs a bit from the film version, and plays up the extinction angle a bit more.
                          • Ginger Snaps (2000) and it's 2004 sequel, as they offer an interesting look at the potential upheavals of the First Change (including the idea of homid Garou being not-quite-normal even before they change), as well as the potential fate of Lost Cubs who are never found by the Garou.
                          • Wer (2013), mainly because the things going on in the back ground involving land disputes and possible corporate conspiracies fit freakishly well with the Werewolf setting.
                          • Dog Soldiers (2002), both for it's portrayal of pack tactics and of werewolves as being murderously territorial. There's also the added bonuses of both a person who hates being part of a clan of werewolves and a nice look at how utterly isolated parts of the Highlands can be. (Really, most of Neil Marshall's films make for decent Werewolf viewing.)
                          • An American Werewolf in London (1981) because it really is just a good film that everyone who has even a passing interest in werewolves should see, and I think the haunting visions the main character is cursed with play well with Werewolf's themes of dark animism.
                          • Pat Murphy's Nadya: The Werewolf Chronicles, about a race of European werewolves migrating to the Americas in the early 19th century. Especially good for those looking for Werewolf: The Wild West inspirations.
                          • Bruce Elliot's "Wolves Don't Cry", a short story about the reaction of a wolf turning into a human. A good example of a lupus Garou undergoing the first change.
                          Also worth checking out if you want some good reads are The Thing in the Woods by Harper Williams (aka Margery Williams, author of The Velveteen Rabbit), The White Wolf of the Hartz Mountains by Frederick Marryat, "The Hairy Ones Shall Dance" by Manly Wade Wellman, "Wolfshead" by Robert E. Howard, "Running Wolf" by Algernon Blackwood, "The Eyes of the Panther" by Ambrose Bierce, "The Mark of the Beast" by Rudyard Kipling, "The Werewolves" by H. Beaugrand, and Darker Than You Think by Jack Williamson, as well as The Company of Wolves (1984), Ladyhawk (1985), Teen Wolf (1985), The Howling (1981), The Wolfman (2010), and Late Phases (2014).

                          On the theme of savage horror ...
                          • The works of Robert E. Howard, as the overall mindset of his stories is fairly spot on for the Garou and their existence. Most of his protagonists are men (and women) who are physically and mentally more adept than average, largely owing to harsh upbringings, lives of constant danger, and a certain ruthless will to overcome obstacles. There's also a frequent theme that civilization is inherently corrupt on some level, and that it will inevitably be torn down by barbarian savagery and calamity. Of special note are his Bran Mak Morn stories, especially "Worms of the Earth", which had a major influence on the creation of the Black Spiral Dancers/White Howlers.
                          • The Hills Have Eyes (1977/2006). Not only is this the sort of thing that could easily be a pack or sept defending the territory of their caern, it's also interesting for the lengths the victims have to go to in order to try to survive.
                          • Death Sentence (2007), again, a seemingly mundane and peaceful life torn apart by brutal violence.
                          • ​Assault on Precinct 13 (1976), the John Carpenter classic about an urban gang laying siege to an understaffed police station. This film is very brutal, and has always served as one of my cornerstones of what the cities of the World of Darkness can be like. (Ignore the remake, as it completely misses the point of the original.) His Escape from New York (1981) is also really good.
                          • The Warriors (1979), another cult classic involving a rather colorful look at late 70s New York City street gangs and one gang's quest to cross the city with every other gang out to kill them. The novel by Sol Yurick which inspired it is also interesting, but for different reasons (it's set in 1965, so it an interesting look at the city in that decade).
                          • The Crazies (1973/2010), about a town turned into a mob of psychotic murderers and the handful of still sane people trying to stay alive.
                          • Splinter (2008), one of the better horror films I've seen in the past ten years.
                          • Primal (2010), which also gets into the ideas of dark animism.
                          • David Morrel's The Totem, while not exactly "savage horror", I do like it's mix of violence behavior brought on by disease, allusions to werewolves, and weird cults living in the hills. Also, First Blood, which is radically different than the movie series it spawned.
                          • Midnight Meat Train (2008), as well as the Clive Barker story it's based on. I don't want to spoil it if you've never seen/read it, but the first time I read it, my reaction was, "Yes, this would totally happen in Werewolf."
                          If you enjoy the splatter punk genre, there's stuff like Richard Laymon's The Woods Are Dark, Jack Ketchum's Off Season, and David Schow's Seeing Red, among others.

                          On the theme of dark animism ...
                          • The works of H. P. Lovecraft, which deal with a universe full of paranatural forces which are at best totally indifferent to humanity, and at worst look upon us as the equivalent of test subjects or livestock. His stuff is clearly a huge influence on the Wyrm and other aspects of the game cosmology. (I, personally, consider "The Colour Out of Space" to be one of the most atmospheric and creepy things I've ever read.)
                          • Alan Moore's run on DC's Swamp Thing, especially the early parts focusing on environmental horror, spiritual forces and their use as social commentary.
                          • Evil Dead (2013), as a good example of what happens when normal people draw the attention of unfriendly spirits. (The original version and it's sequel are pretty good too, but not quite as dark as the remake.)
                          • The Exorcist (1971) and The Exorcist III (1990) - ignore the second one.
                          Also a lot of J-Horror films, as Shinto has such a large influence on the culture. Ring, Ju-On, Dark Water, and Tetsuo: The Iron Man are all interesting.
                          .

                          On the theme of Native Americans/First Nations ...

                          (Since it's such an obvious part of the game and an influence to the original creators)
                          • Fergus Bordewich's Killing the White Man's Indian, written about twenty years ago at the hight of the Dances With Wolves inspired revival of the "noble savage" romanticism, looks at what is/was actually going on with Native peoples and how they were trying to take greater control of their fate, including what they were allowed to do with their land.
                          • The works of Jim Northrup, an Anishinaabe newspaper columnist, writer and performer. Walking the Rez Road, Rez Road Follies, and Anishinaabe Syndicated give a really good view of the upper midwest (Minnesota and Wisconsin) region.
                          • The works of Tony Hillerman, who's long running series of mystery novels set in the Navajo Nation frequently delve into aspects of traditional Navajo culture and beliefs, as well as those of surrounding nations.
                          • The works of William Sanders, a Cherokee science fiction writer, especially his anthology Are We Having Fun Yet? and his non-fiction Conquest: Hernando de Soto and the Indians 1539-1543 (a look at de Soto's expedition/invasion of the Southeastern US from a Native perspective).

                              In addition to what everyone else has mentioned, especially No One of Concequence's list, I'd recommend...

                              Wolf's Rain (2003-2004)
                              -An anime about the last of the wolves, who will supposedly lead everyone into paradise, but disguise themselves as humans to live in a dying world...need more be said?

                              An American Werewolf in Paris (1997)
                              -It's a comedic version of London's, but it holds fond childhood memories for me. It also has a conflict between werewolves who do their best to live with their curse and a gang of French skinheads that hate tourists.

                              The Ritual (2018)
                              -A good example of an out of the way Wyrm cult (or BSD kinfolk) and a powerful fomori or manifested bane.

                              The Void (2017)
                              -Despite it having more of a Nephandi/Mage feel, it could also serve as a good example of a Wyrm cult and how the process of becoming a fomori would feel like.

                              Resident Evil franchise
                              -To me Umbrella is a better example of Pentex, outside of the massive fuck ups where zombie plagues get loose (unless you're taking that angle with the Apocalypse actually happening in your chronicle)

                              Strange Days (1995)
                              - I mentioned this film in Mage as well, but it's great for the WoD overall. It's funny in hindsight, since it takes place in the last few days of 1999, but has technology way beyond our own. However, I liked how it presented a shittier 'present', showing off police corruption and how it entangles at many levels of society, and a city ready to explode at any moment due to this situation (very post 92 LA riots) . And it accomplishes this without getting into the ridiculousness these themes sometimes go down in popular cinema. Overall, IMO, a good general view of a 1st world city in the WoD in the similar vein of 'Assault on Precinct 13'.

                              Okja (2017)
                              - This film depicts how a Pentex subsidiary could try and paint themselves as a reformed corporate entity, while still doing horrible things. Then there's an interesting question that could be presented in a Werewolf game of presenting lab grown species being worthy of protection and respect as their natural counterparts.

                              Once Were Warriors (1994) and Rhymes for Young Ghouls (2014)
                              - These are some of the best examples of modern day depictions of indigenous people trying to survive in the modern world. There is no noble savagery or buckskin here. It doesn't sugar coat the racism, poverty, and the societal destruction that we've experienced. For me personally, they're hard to watch since I feel like I'm watching my childhood. So not only does it fit the indigenous consciousness that WtA tries to depict, but could serve as inspiration for the Garou's own internal disintegration in the modern world.

                                  Lessee;

                                  Films/TV:
                                  - Romasanta: an lupus Dancer wreaks havoc in the Spanish countryside.
                                  - Dog Soldiers: Speaking of BSDs, here is a group of regular british soldiers facing a family of BSDs.
                                  - Criminal Minds: How a mobile pack deals with various Wyrm tainted foes and mundane horrors.
                                  - Bad Moon: This movie is about a dog dealing with a werewolf in his home, the perspective in interesting even if the movie isn't otherwise.
                                  - Season of the Witch/ Black witch: A movie about what happens when actual medieval people end up with an actual supernatural creature.

                                  Literature:
                                  Watership Down: This book (or the film, but more the book) is a really good depiction of the natural cycle, the role of the prey animals and the myths they tell. Absolutely great for anyone wanting to get deeper into the spirit-world in a forested setting.
                                  Plague Dogs: By the same author as Watership Down, look into how dogs see things and animal testing without making it solely evil.
                                  Jungle Books: Though Kipling was no doubt a bit racist, this book is still worth a read for writing the animals and their thinking really well and having awesome characters like Bagheera and Kaa.
                                  Those Who Walk Away from Omelas: A short story about a seemingly perfect city and justifications.

                                  Videogames:
                                  The Suffering: A game about a prison island facing its own violent past in a literal way, of the corruption of the prison system and racial biases. This is an absolute gem of a game.
                                  Silent Hill 1 and 3: While Silent Hill itself is a great series, 1 and 3 have the focus on the cult of the town. BSD kinfolk material for sure. Not to mention, the town is a great portrayal of a purgatory-like realm.


                                  Anime/Manga/Cartoons/comics:
                                  - Stay Still Stay Silent (webcomic)- spiritualism and scifi in a post-apocalyptic world.
                                  - Best of Bad Decisions (webcomic) - A comic focusing on the life of two coywolves who want to be leaders. Great to get an idea how a lupus might think.

                                  Youtube Channels/Blogs;
                                  -Serial Killer files (youtube program): A look into various serial killers and unsolved murders. I recommend the Albert Fish episode for a great example of a Black spiral Dancer.
                                  - WolvesWolves(blog): A blog dedicated to showcasing wolves, wolf news and knowledge of them.
                                  - Fantasy Biology: Written by a vet, a look into how fantastic creatures might work in actual biology.
                                  - Pedigree Dogs exposed: What horrors humanity can do to a canine and where does it lead to. In addittion, the denial of facing the truth about such horrors and sticking to ones guns despite evidence suggesting otherwise. Also, great for Silver Fang mentalities on purebreeding.

                                          I will also toss in Neal Stephenson's novel Zodiac (aka Zodiac: An Eco-Thriller), written in 1988 about chemical pollution and environmental activism in Boston and it's surrounding areas. Most of Stephenson's work is more Mage-related, but this one is straight up Werewolf. Also, the majority of Carl Hiaasen's books, set in southern Florida, deal heavily with the environmental impact of development on the Everglades and other regional habitats. (Double Whammy is probably a good one to start with.) Robin Cook's Fever is also a decent read, dealing with water pollution and cancer in Massachusetts and New Hampshire. I've never read Edward Abbey's The Monkey Wrench Gang, but that's apparently where "monkey-wrenching" as a term for environmental sabotage/terrorism comes from.

                                              Two anime that fit Werewolf REALLY well;
                                              -Mushishi: Taking place in an alternative universe Japan in its middle ages, mushishis really about dealing with strange creatures called mushi. What this anime does really well is portray the animism of shinto and the moral ambiguity of the mushi themselves. They aren't evil, they are just creatures that do strange things. This anime is a must for anyone playing a theurge.

                                              -Mononoke: NOT Princess Mononoke, just Mononoke. This is a horror anime about the various phantoms that seek revenge for their deaths, with a mysterious medicine seller as the 'hero' of the story. Like Mushishi, this show differs from a lot of westerm portrayals of ghosts in that what is wronged doesn't have to be a person. What makes this a good Werewolf inspiration, is the eerie nature of the art and the feeling once shit starts hitting the fan in earnest. The world is a strange place and the creatures living in it aren't always evil in wanting revenge.


                                              A shameless plug, but I think it will be useful;
                                              -Kalevala: If you want to play a Get of Fenris, but don't want to go in the Norse Mythology route with them, this is a good myth book to read. Kalevala is the Finnish national epic, but its heroes aren't saints and the world they live in isn't black and white. There is a lot of fighting, arguing, wife stealing and cheating death. Plus a funny story about stealing stuff ruining it for everyone. I think this will be useful for anyone wanting to play a Fenrir.
