    Originally posted by Manfr View Post
    I don't know much about the functioning of the old Nation, how cohesive it was at national and supranational level?
    From W5 core, I got the impression that the two main axes of sociopolitical action are local Caerns and Septs, and tribes as shared philosophies.
    While septs were their own thing with their own cultures, there was this overarching sense of a nation. As garou are born and die and aren't immortal, the culture was taught from generation to generation in a much longer line than Camarilla traditions.

    More importantly, if sept A was in trouble, sept B could and was encouraged to help. While there was competition over caerns, generally everyone was working towards a single goal.

    Then there were the spirits, who could serve as a first warning in case of of Wyrmtaint. Of course there were pockets of such and anyone could fall. But if sept X was heard breaking the Litany, sept G could send judges to see if the rumour was true.

    Unlike Camarilla, the Nation had an unified goal of fighting the Wyrm which went over every single other thing.

    When I say the Nation is gone, I mean there is no longer this large spiritual and global oversight over the Litany being followed. There is no longer this unified goal that allowed garou to move from caern to caern. There is no longer any reason for each tribe to TRY and work together. The spirits have peaced out. First Changes no longer get covered up by a vast network of garou and garou relatives. And so on.

            Originally posted by Ana Mizuki View Post
            I personally find it really interesting to explore the idea of a bunch of garou who decide to hunt humans as their prey and even share hunting grounds with some vampires.
            I can't imagine vampires and Garou would willingly share hunting grounds. Setting aside that both are territorial - especially against each other - our hypothetical cannibal Garou pack would be directly depleting the "herd" of the vampires. Vampires, at least, don't need to kill their prey. Whereas these cannibal werewolves must, by definition.

            Plus, while a vampire can feed on people while drawing minimal attention to herself, if people start straight up disappearing (or showing up skeletonized), that attracts much more attention. There would be investigations. Even if people assume it's just man-eating wolves, that can create hunting parties of angry humans looking to purge the man-eaters in their area. People may also get scared, and stay at home more or travel in groups for protection.

            All this makes it very inconvenient for vampires (and werewolves) just looking to survive in peace. It doesn't matter if it actually causes problems. Just the possibility that any of this could happen is enough for the vampire(s) in the area to get pissed. Hence, being territorial.

            This is true regardless of what vampire sect we're talking about. Even the Sabbat don't "shit where they eat" if they can help it. And when they do kill people, they tend to either do it elsewhere (like in Camarilla territory, to make headaches for them), or dispose of it properly. They can't trust anyone else to deal with bodies piling up. Moreover, they're even more sensitive about other parties "poaching" their herds, because they also like to kill them. Can't have someone else literally eating into their numbers.


            (One may argue, "What if the Sabbat gives the bodies they drain to the werewolves? Win win!". The Garou would probably respond, "WE DON'T NEED HANDOUTS! ESPECIALLY FROM SOME F***ING LEECHES! WE CAN HUNT FOR OURSELVES!" Their pride wouldn't tolerate this kind of arrangement, no matter how beneficial.)

                    The thing with the Litany like with many other lore/worldbuilding-elements is that it's still mostly the same - except now it's defunct/worthless and in its presentation poorly explained. You could go by what the text says, focus on how Garou elders exploit it, how young Garou don't care for it, how Rage makes any interaction between Garou violent and difficult, how there are also young Garou who don't know the Litany at all... Okay, you do all that as ST, go into the same direction as the book describes, you present the same arguments as in the book and then you show the Litany as listed in the book to the players. And then when the ST talks about how elders abuse the part that says "Submit to Those of Higher Station", a player then might just point to the same Litany and say "Well, what about the one right underneath 'Respect Those of Lower Station, for All Are of Gaia.​' ? The Litany doesn't seem that bad to me.". Of course, the text in the W5 corebook doesn't go into it because it's almost exclusively focused on the negative parts. And the text also gives short shrift to the interplay between the Litany and the Philodox- and Ragabash-Auspice. Not that this is an isolated problem, the book doesn't offer a lot of help with depicting Septs and Moots either.

                    It's also that issue again that it isn't really cutting down on worldbuilding/lore because even the reduced list of the Litany still has a lot of tenets. VtM has six traditions while even with the reduction the Litany has eleven. And the V5-core-book (as it should have been done here) goes through the traditions one by one and gives some short comments. You got the old Litany here and yet it just gets this overly negative dismissive commentary while poorly explaining all the parts that are still right there. And since it's a reboot, it's strange that it was done this way. Since it seems the direction was so averse to the idea of worldbuilding/lore, they could've simply rewritten the Litany and simplified it for the sake of accessibility. But they didn't.

                    Rather than re-imagination, the style of changes made in this edition are merely cutting out the context and depth of previous editions. And the ST is burdened to make sense of all these various setting-elements.

                        I suppose the unified Nation fell apart as Gaia started to send his mysterious Howl: Stargazers quit, the Cult of Fenris rose and probably a number of Septs and Caerns fell to civil war or millenaristic gloom.

                        That said, I don't think that the fall of the Nation would instantly mean total anarchy: spirits still respect Renown and ancient binds, even though many of them may be resentful of Garou failures. Tribal spirits, of course, are the more important: they actively recruit Garou in their ranks, I think it's implied, and their Ban and Favors provide a rough sort of moral code to recruits.

                        Septs and Caerns will maintain old ties and alliances, and in most instances, to facilitate relations, the Litany will probably just be the equivalent of Lex Mercatorum or other Common Law set of shared norms which have emerged from constant interaction.

                        Of course, differing political setups will probably result in different interpretations of the Litany, and there will be pockets of dissidence, apostasy or total rejection and autarky- but even when the Core says that the Litany was often abused, those who try to uphold it are not defined as "the villains" or the abusers, mostly as utopians. So, it's not like the Litany is totally discredited, I think it's mostly seen as something like "real Communism was never tried" or "Yes, capitalism gave us CIA death squads, but also development and wealth".

                        The Garou live in a post-apocalyptic society hidden within daily routine - which adds to the feeling of isolation and urgency - and the more I think of it, the more taking some cues fromTwilight 2000 seems interesting -

                        I know this is not going to be everyone's setting of choice, but I rather like it.

                                                            Originally posted by Ermenegildo View Post

                                                            In my experience, changing/challenging the metaplot is way more difficult than creating from nothing. Players can get attached to what they find in the books and could react badly when the storyteller changes something because "It's not the GAME anymore". Sometimes the along-the-line messages become "How dare you change something that I spend time learning" or even "How dare you change something that I studied making me equal to the novices".
                                                            The flipside of that is that due to poor worldbuilding you ask four people what their takeaway is from reading the book and you get four different answers. That's why in those situations the burden of worldbuilding is shifted either completely onto the Storyteller or it's something you have to address during Session Zero. Like the book says it's happening during the Age of Apocalypse... so when did it start? Then if someone's read the book closely, you're probably gonna have a discussion about the capitalocene era and when people think that started. Or the event of the Fenrir and the Stargazers leaving? Or the Garou-Nation falling? When did this happen, ten years ago, fifty, a thousand...? Who knows...

                                                            And W5 isn't like an Apocalypse World where beyond some tentpoles in worldbuilding (the post-apocalypse genre, the Maelstrom and what the Playbooks imply) you're mostly working with a blank canvas. W5 has a lot of distinct concepts but there are a lot of questions left unanswered as to how these concepts work. Also, unlike any good PbtA-game the game just either shrugs about giving information or just ignores it. There's no point in the book where it gives guidance on how to fill out the blank spaces or what sort of questions you might ask in Session Zero for the sake of completing the process of worldbuilding. When you play this game and in the fourth session somebody asks the ST about how long the Garou-Nation lasted, I certainly don't think giving an answer like "The Garou Nation, as it was known, existed for a brief period of relative success, but even there, its actual duration is unknown and varies by who’s reciting the history. Was it decades? Centuries? Millennia?" would be satisfactory. ​And you can't move it too far back into the past because that would imply the Cult of Fenris and the Stargazers left the Garou-Nation thousands of years ago. But if you put it too close to the present, you start to wonder why nobody knows a damn thing about it. And that's an example of how you're not working with a blank canvas here because "Fall of Garou-Nation", "Cult of Fenris/Stargazers leaving" and "What was the Garou-Nation like?" are all tied together while also being hamstrung by the book's careless "Who knows"-attitude towards worldbuilding.

                                                            W5 is in the tricky spot where it does too much worldbuilding to allow the sort of freewheeling collaborative worldbuilding you do in a lot of PbtA-games but it does too little to serve as a reliable guide to how its setting works.
