                                        For me, the reason Garou don’t feel entirely like “werewolves” isn’t due to a lack of mythological grounding, but rather due to the lack of drawbacks and personal horror inherent in being one (things that do tend to be present in other depictions of werewolves). To me, “werewolf” implies at least a certain degree of being “cursed”, where any benefits are always offset by an underlying risk of losing control, that you’re risking letting a beast out by using them.

                                        By contrast, Garou shapeshifting is basically effortless, they get all kinds of spirit powers, superhuman durability, and so forth. Things that are strictly beneficial, and don’t come tied to any real personal cost or drawback. It’s like if vampires could go out in sunlight, and only needed blood to fuel disciplines. By my reading, there’s only two inherent downsides of being Garou: the risk of frenzy and the Curse. While neither are bad concepts, they just aren’t present enough in the themes and psychology of the game to create a “werewolf” feel. In fact, the setting basically encourages characters to all but withdraw from human civilization, rendering these drawbacks somewhat moot.

                                        Now, of course, the argument against this is that there’s all kinds of drawbacks to being Garou. Namely, the variety of things that want to kill you, the societal conflicts you’re drawn into, and the wider apocalyptic dread that gives the game its name. But this is my point: none of these things evoke a inherently “werewolf” feeling, and none of them are inherent to the Garou nature itself, they’re consequences of the setting. In isolation, being Garou is almost strictly beneficial.

                                        The end result is that the game is centered much more around being “furry Captain Planet” than actually being, you know, a werewolf. You could replace the wolf shapeshifting with combat mechas and the things Garou do on a day to day basis wouldn’t really change. This analysis is a bit reductive, I admit, but it highlights the thematic shortcomings I, and I think others, have with the game’s depiction of werewolves. This doesn’t mean that WtA is necessarily going be a bad game, and that someone can’t enjoy it for what it is, just that it doesn’t feel nearly as much like a “werewolf” game as it seems it should. Forsaken is indeed improved in this respect (even if I personally I think it could have been better still).

                                        The longer I study science the more I am convinced that it is functionally indistinguishable from what our ancestors would refer to as sorcery. And I would know, being both scientist and sorcerer.
