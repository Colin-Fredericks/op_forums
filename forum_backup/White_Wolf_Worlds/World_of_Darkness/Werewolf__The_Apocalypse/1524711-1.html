            PHASE ONE REVIEW PART FIVE

            Chapter Seven: Storytelling
            I really don't have much to say about this chapter. While I might not have chosen some of the ways the designers chose to convey this information, it still is fairly decent. This chapter usually is one of the least interesting ones in rulebooks. Necessary, but mostly important to newer players. There might be something here for those more familiar, so it still needs at least some looking over.

            Chapter Eight: The Umbra and Spirts
            This is the secondary setting for the game, the spiritual side of the world where almost anything could happen and is almost nothing can be easily defined. This includes something called 'Gaia's Howl," a feeling Garou get when entering the Umbra. I wish I could explain it better, but the citation for more information just refers back to the previously read paragraph on the same page. This is a very bad case of pasting/editing.

            Basically, it is very hard to enter the Umbra, but easy to leave. The environment is inherently hostile, and it could kill you the longer you stay there. The relative locations and spirits could change at any time. Things may have been different in the past, but this is how it is now. Why include such an interesting possibility for play, and then make it so difficult to actually use it?Anything more than a quick foray will be tough. The sidebars calls this "mysterious," but it feels more like the designers don't want players to spend much time here, if any.

            The chapter ends (it's barely fourteen pages long) with rundown on how Garou categorize spirits in relative power terms. The spirits themselves 'know' their places in the hierarchy, but don't really use the same terms. The most powerful spirits rarely if ever come into contact with lesser beings such as Garou, but the weaker ones frequently do. A few ideas for dealing with spirits are mentioned, but not many firm examples are given.

            Chapter Nine: Allies and Antagonists
            This chapters covers many of the secondary characters players can face. The chapter's title is slightly wrong, though, as the vast majority presented here are likely enemies and not friends. In fact, hardly any of the examples given would flat out be friendly to most characters. I would have called this chapter "Antagonists and Possible Allies," but I guess it doesn't have the same ring to it. As the book says, Garou have lots of enemies.

            The first section covers spirits. Apparently, spirits love to possess people and things in the physical world for various reasons, most of them leading to conflict with werewolves. Why they can't just form their own bodies is unknown. Most are considered hostile to indifferent to Garou, making them enemies. The spirits are classified by primary patron: Wyrm, Weaver, Wyld, or Gaia. With the first one being most likely to be foes, and the last as indifferent to friendly.

            Next comes Fomori, people who have been possessed by Banes, corrupt Wyrm spirits for lengthy periods of time. Being possessed for so long gives them abilities far beyond 'average' spirits. They feel like they are to be seen as a primary type of enemy. They are given a wide range of powers, of various levels of commonality. They are often found working with/for a multi-national holding corporation known as Pentex. This enemy feels like the typical evil corporation cliché. While the overwhelming majority of its 'evilness' isn't supernatural, even its mundane practices can be seen as bad. I'm not sure I like how they are presented as the "man-behind-the-curtain" who really is the big-bad that takes multiple chapters to locate, yet that is how the storytelling chapter prefers to set such game sessions up.

            Following this is a section on Garou and other shapeshifters as enemies. Three 'fallen' tribes are presented, with just a bit more sense of history than the eleven playable ones. In fact, two of them could almost be playable if the rules and designers really wanted them to be. Then there are werewolves who are not Garou who got their abilities through other means. Very little information is given to them, but they seem to be more like the werewolves from folklore than the Garou. There are also 'spirit' Garou who got trapped in the Umbra and became a type of "ghost" starving for a way out. Finally, there are shapeshifters based on two other animals, ravens and spiders. There are hints that there are others possible as well. Not enough information is given in the two examples to figure out anything about what they are looking for.

            Next comes other supernatural beings. Vampires are the primary focus, as they appear in the first book in the game line. Magic-users, ghosts, and fairies round out this section, hinting at what games are in the pipeline. This is followed by a small section on werewolf hunters, who feel unimportant in the big scheme. The last part feature mundane humans who might come into conflict with Garou and a smattering of common animals. Both feel like afterthoughts, just there to round out all of the basic types of threats. Although, why horses are mentioned as a source of blood suggests that this listing may have, in part, been lifted from a book for vampires

                        PHASE ONE REVIEW PART SIX

                        Appendix
                        At the end of the book, there are three appendices, an index, a two-page character sheet, and a set of templates, specifically designed to be a well-rounded start for character creation. I'll take the designers word about how well they work. The character sheet is fairly average, with nothing that stands out. The appendices and index do have things that stand out.

                        Appendix One: Loresheets
                        These are the Backgrounds mentioned in the character creation process, specifically designed to allow players a deeper connection to the lore/backstory of the game. However, since there has been so little world-building, these Loresheets are quite bare. There are only five of them, none of which have any limitations to them. They also don't add much extra to the game at such an early point in the product cycle.

                        Appendix Two: No Matter How Small
                        This is the introductory scenario for new players. Such products are frequently separate, so having one with the main book can be a godsend for newer players. It is fairly brief and follows many of the guidelines set up in previous chapters. Maybe a little too straightforward, but I might not be the best judge of such things.

                        Appendix Three: Advise for Considerate Play
                        This is an unusual section, as such advice is frequently presented at part of the introduction or storytelling chapters. Someone must have thought it was important enough to warrant its own section, but not enough to present until the very end of the book. Much of this section deals with advice various Nordic roleplaying groups, with web addresses provided for those who want more information or a deeper understanding. Some of the ideas are unfamiliar to me, but they might be helpful to others. Of particular note are the trigger warnings. While the ones for sex and violence are fairly common, there is also a warning about fascism. This is unusual, as I'm not sure this would be a trigger warning for many people, although I could be wrong. Someone must be have had strong feelings about this to include a warning. Maybe this a generational or personal, I can't be sure.

                        Index
                        Normally, an index doesn't merit the need for review. However, some of the ways this index is set up need to be addressed. First, some of the entries have their page number directly beside them. This makes them slightly harder to read/find. Second, almost all of the entries direct to just one instance. This can lead to problems if you are looking for some details. For example, while looking over the section on damage, I remembered a sidebar mentioning that silver can cause problems for Garou, but I couldn't find any systems for that yet. I looked in the index and was directed to the original sidebar only, missing the entry for silver damage directly above it. I would have combined some entries as opposed to this book's system, but that is a personal preference.

                        The most unusual thing about this index is the capitalization. While important words in main entries are capitalized, this isn't so in sub-entries, where only the first letter is capitalized, with one exception. This includes names that are usually capitalized, like the ones for Gifts and Tribes. For example, "Children of gaia." Now, most spell-checkers would automatically correct that to a capital "G," like the one on my computer. For it to be overridden is unusual. Especially since the one exception is found under "Caern Traits," "Balm of Gaia." I have no idea how something could be capitalized in one instance and not the other. It should be impossible for this to happen, but it did. It, it just is so beyond what should be possible.

                        OVERALL GRADE: C
                        After reviewing this game, I have three conclusions. First, this book was rushed through to finish it upon some deadline. While all books would have some mistakes in them, the number and types of mistakes I have found leave a lack of confidence in this publisher for future work. It feels like things were being changed right until the very end. This is not the way to edit a book.

                        Second, this book feels like it was made for a target demographic--inexperienced player in their late teens/early twenties. Maybe one/two years playing TTRPGs, little to no heavy violence, no mature themes. The simple rules set, the introduction scenario, the 'fair-play' advice, all point to this type of player. While older, more experienced players, like myself, might be able to get something out of this game, I have doubts that it can retain players unless later books can add a larger experience.

                        Third, it feels that this game had a direct mindset guiding it. Whether a singular individual or a groupthink venture, it feels that certain things had to be placed in the book, possibly to cater to the second point above. This mindset may have changed at some point during development, leading to confusing issues and the mistakes in the first point. Some designers may have tried to direct the game away from this vision as a means of adding some other ways to play, but got overruled at one point or another. This just added to the confusion.

                        While there feels like there is a strong foundation to this game, I just find it is to focused on providing a certain level of gameplay. Anything that distracts from this style was removed whenever possible. Players can try to add some of this back on their own with house rules, I do think that players would leave if more material to allow different aspects of play are not considered. If this happens, maybe I could raise the grade to a C+ or B-. Until then, I just have to give this a C for just an average game. Nothing too horrible, but nothing great to stand out either.

                        Phase two will compare W5 to V5, where many points I only hinted about will be further fleshed out.

                                            I think one of the principal mistakes this game makes is that it takes its pessimistic outlook on Garou and does nothing with it. Garou suck. That's it. One of the themes is supposed to be "Theme: Old Dogs, New Tricks, Young Blood​" where you get the description that the old ways have failed but older Garou will still cling to them and so there's a struggle between young Garou trying to find new ways and those veterans.

                                            But what are the new ways?

                                            The outlook in W5 may be pessmistic but strangely, it's a game entirely stuck in retrospective. It's all about how a past ridden with mistakes and failures led to an apocalyptic present - and nothing beyond that. The book is very clear about that much at least. The war against the Wyrm? Lost. The Apocalypse? It's happening. You fight against the Apocalypse - and you can't stop it. Garou have powers - that aren't the solution to the world's problems.

                                            So, what do you do then? Good question. Pity the W5 corebook never gets to that part.

                                            Instead what you get is this: You still fight the Wyrm and its minions. You still fight against the Apocalypse. You still use Rage and Gifts to fight your enemies. You still accrue Renown and practice Rites in the same way your Garou-ancestors have done. So where are the new, supposedly better ways? They're nowhere to be found. Because what this game does instead is set up PCs for failure and hopes players will feel bad about it. To borrow Monster-Hearts-terminology, following the mechanics of the game is to embrace your "Darkest Self", in this case the Garou's "Darkest Self".

                                            Harano and Hauglosk are these two fail-states with the former being "depression" (don't like the book's explicit link to genuine mental health issues here but that's how the book portrays it) and the latter being some fascistic zealotry (also don't like the book's explicit links between Hauglosk and fascism). These tracks are linked to Chronicle Tenets and Touchstones but there are two systems that express those same ideas elsewhere in the game. With Harano you have the concept of "losing the wolf" which for whatever reason allows a character to just abandon Rage and their supernatural Forms for an indefinite amount of time. There's nothing more Harano-like than a genuine option to just give up and walk away from being a Garou. For Hauglosk, you have the whole Rage-Dice-system with Brutal Outcomes. With Rage being fuel for Gifts, Regeneration and shifting you need high Rage. Brutal Outcomes even become beneficial when you're doing something brutal and/or violent. Meanwhile, Brutal Outcomes turn your rolls into automatic failures when you don't do that. So what if you were to just... not do the latter? In fact, the system will reward players for sticking to high Rage and focusing on direct, brutal solutions to problems. You get rewarded for acting like a rampaging monster while low Rage limits your use of Gifts, Regeneration and shifting while also potentially triggering "losing the wolf" for your character.

                                            And where's the way out of this...? There isn't one, strangely. So in a way, the vague suggestions of young Garou trying to do better and overcoming the problems with the old ways is the part that really doesn't fit into this game. The old Garou were failures and the young Garou you're asked to play in W5 are set up for failure as well. The vision of doing better and being better never once materializes in this book.

                                            Meanwhile, there's another glaring issue with the book: worldbuilding. The W5 corebook finds plenty of words to castigate the past of the W5-setting, like with the Litany-section and blaming Garou for the apocalyptic state of the world. Other than that, you get a lot of "Who knows" that's less about setting up mysteries and more akin to a student in an oral exam trying to cover their ass for not knowing an answer. Not knowing something isn't a mystery, it's ignorance. You can't build a setting with ignorance. The current WOD5E's direction of disliking lore/worldbuilding, in general, is more a handicap to corebooks like this than anything else.

                                            Even game-play-wise the street-level-only scope is a handicap and nothing else. Your PC-Pack defeats an enemy, gets a caern... and then what? The horizon ends there. And same as with H5's direction of "You have to play a local hunter-cell! Hunter-orgs are evil!", this game is running into the same wall. What's particularly bothersome is that it's one thing to focus on a particular scope, it's another to be invested in actively sabotaging any effort to expand the scope. So in a way, W5's pessimistic take on Garou and the stringent regulation of games being street-level-only really ended up being a match made in heaven for this game. Both are efforts to restrict and restrain what you do with this game and rather than enhance gameplay it's more invested in blocking people from playing in a way that disagrees with the developers' vision.

                                            But hey, it's better than H5, so there's that at least...

                                                Thanks to all for following this thread.
                                                Time for Phase Two. This part of the review is mostly a comparison of W5 with V5. When the Fifth Edition books first came out, hopes were high that each of the game lines would be more able to interact with other. Many of the original WOD games introduced mechanics that were only useful for themselves, making crossover games difficult to manage, if not impossible on some levels. COD managed to to that fairly well; it was a major benefit to the games and one thing many players would like to see in 5th. I hinted about some of these possibilities in Phase One, but will follow through here.

                                                PHASE TWO PART ONE
                                                Comparison of W5 and V5

                                                Overall Views.
                                                As I look over my copy of V5 for this review for the first time in awhile, I'm surprised at how much I missed when I first got it. The first thing is that V5 is smaller than W5, by about an eighth of an inch shorter in width and height. [Note, my copy of V5 is an early Modiphius printing. I believe that the current Renegade edition might have different dimensions.] For some people, this slight difference could be a trigger. It might also be a subtle hint for people to buy newer printing just that their collections look/feel even.

                                                W5 is also just slightly thicker than V5, which is odd since V5 has 400 pages 330, or 332 is you count the endpaper. Part of this is due to the fact that the covers to W5 are also slightly thicker than those on V5. Also, V5 used a thinner grade of paper which worked better with the photographic artwork, while W5 used a thicker grade that gives it a do-it-yourself vibe. This makes the two books look like they have the same amount of material in them, but that obviously is not the case.

                                                As I just mentioned, the majority of the artwork inV5 is photographs. Some of the artwork is the prominent part of the page, while on others it is worked into the background instead. Some portions are set with two columns, others have three. Sections are set off with a mixture of fonts and sizes. Sidebars are usually set off with just a set of thick bars at the top of bottom of the section. Overall, the effect reflects the style of a high-end fashion magazine or a coffee table book. A sense of exquisiteness pervades the book, setting it off from previous editions.

                                                In contrast, V5 has illustrations only, much of them using a subdued palette, all but muddy in places. The pages themselves are yellowed, almost sepia-toned. Multiple fonts are used per page, dependent on the type of heading required. Multiple colors are also used. Sidebars are in a dark yellow or rusty red, with the former using bold and the latter using white lettering . Every page is in two columns, with some shifted due to artwork. Sections are set off in standard style without any variance. Overall, the effect reminds me of a grade-school textbook, one that has been in use for many years. While that could fit the do-it-yourself themes of W5, it also makes the book feel childish, as if it is more for kids than adults, which is it most assuredly not

                                                        PHASE TWO PART TWO

                                                        Introductions
                                                        V5 starts with thirty page in-universe collection of documents that hint at what the game will be about. Some of the points brought up have yet to be explored in any supplements so far. Further documents are found throughout the book. This gives a feeling of history, that things have happened and are still happening. These documents are backed by quotes and passages by in-universe characters, deepening this connection. There is a four-page lexicon that gives both current slang and archaic terms that suggests the struggles of ancient vampires and the more modern ones that are a strong part of the plot lines of the game. Much deeper into the book, just after the chapter on character creation, there is a list comparing how the vampires of the game differ with those found in folklore and fiction. The rest of the early sections of the book give not just a brief summation of the basic history of the Kindred of the game up to the current nights, but also a piece on how to run a game for those unfamiliar with the concepts.

                                                        W5 offers no introduction beyond a few paragraphs summing up the broad strokes of the game-style. While there are quotes and even some passages by in-game characters, they feel more like opinions rather than anything deeply integral to the game. There are also a few in-universe documents, but they almost feel out of place. The one-page lexicon doesn't even cover all the terms used in the book, some of which aren't even given a solid definition within the text. There is no one section comparing the Garou to more traditional representations of werewolves found elsewhere. The first two chapters of W5, which I must admit are easier to read than the broad sections of V5, give hints about the game world and the history of the Garou, yet something feels missing. For instance, V5 gave two pages to discussions the Traditions of the Kindred, basic laws that all vampires usually abide by, and how they are followed differently by different vampires of different ages and sects. W5 give two columns across two pages about the Garou's comparable set of laws, the Litany. However, most of the tenets aren't explained beyond the fact that many of the youngest generation don't think much of them.

                                                        Even with what information is given, W5 is open-ended to the point where players are all but given total leeway to do what they want concerning the history of the Garou and even current events.

                                                            W5 has way too many areas in its worldbuilding where you're presented with a blank slate or something pretty close to it.
