    Originally posted by Damian May View Post
    Which seems more realistic to me. If the Coggies, Furies etc. thought it a terrible crime why would they not keep its memory alive and if others thought it a great victory over their corrupted brethren why would they not sing of their triumph?
    That's what they did in the legacy editions. In W5, the War of Rage was a source of shame so they didn't discuss it. After a few generations the event was mostly forgotten. If younger generations knew what had happened, maybe they would sing songs about it, but they lack the knowledge to do so. That is something that happens - stories, sometimes even important ones get lost as the older generations that knew it didn't talk about it with the younger generations. Even with writing and modern information storage this is still something that you can see occurring in certain places.

    Look at the difference between Japan and Germany when it comes to general knowledge of WW2 crimes. In Germany there's a strong pressure to teach the younger generations what Germany did during WW2. They learn all about it in school, so most Germans are familiar at least with the general points of what crimes against humanity their country committed during WW2. Meanwhile in Japan, that's not the case at all. Children in school typically aren't taught the details of the brutality that Japan inflicted in Korea or in China or other places. The average citizen (especially younger people) has a vague understanding along the lines of, "Yeah, we did some bad stuff, but everyone was doing bad things, even the United States, we had nuclear weapons dropped on us, and then we apologized for everything we did, so why are people still angry about it? Nobody is mad at Germany. Korea, China and everyone else needs to get over it already."

    In school, most Japanese people don't learn about what Unit 731 did. And in those textbooks that describe the Korean Comfort Women, it usually identifies them as "mostly" prostitutes who were willingly selling sexual services to soldiers. The real knowledge of what Japan did is largely confined to specific college classes or historical books, and aren't things that most of the citizens are familiar with.

    Ultimately if events aren't actively discussed and taught, they'll fall out of common knowledge in just a couple of generations. WW2 was 80 years ago, the War of Rage was 8000+ years ago. It doesn't strike me as weird of strange that by the modern day, the vast majority of Garou would never even have heard about it or know about it.

    Note that I do think it plays even harder into the "everything sucks and nothing you can do matters" aspect of W5. In the Legacy edition, trying to make amends for the War of Rage and bring the shapeshifters back together was a legendary task. In W5 it's essentially impossible because most Garou don't even know it happened. So before they can apologize to the other Shifters and try to make up for the wrongs, first they have to realize it even happened, then decide that it was a bad thing, and then try to convince the rest of the Garou nation what happened and then convince them that they need to apologize.

    I suspect that even if they did find out it happened, and believed it happened, most Garou would say, "It happened 8000 years ago. Why am I supposed to apologize for that? Those other shapeshifters need to get it over it already."

    You heard a lot of white people in the US recently saying the same thing about the Tulsa Race Massacre when still-living victims who were directly injured went to court to seek amends. "It happened so long ago, nobody around today should be considered responsible."

    So to be clear, it's not a change I like, but it's one that I feel makes sense.

        Originally posted by AnubisXy View Post
        This one makes sense to me. The Garou weren't proud of what they did and so they just stopped telling the story of how they slaughtered all of the other shifters. Modern Garou have never heard any stories about a War of Rage and have no idea that it ever occurred let alone what role the werewolves played in it. Meanwhile with the other Shifters, the story of how the werewolves almost wiped them out gets handed down every generation and is probably the most important story that gets told. Ultimately within Garou society, the story of the War of Rage is likely known to a handful of Galliards who don't really spread it around (because they probably wouldn't be believed anyway) and maybe a few Children of Gaia (who have heard about it from other Shapeshifters).
        The issue for me is that what you're describing is you adding to what the book offers. It's a problem similar to the Stargazer-section. There it says: "That said, none know the Patron Spirit granting its boon to the Stargazers. In the past, Chimera bestowed favor upon the tribe, but that great spirit seems to have amicably parted ways with the Stargazers — or so it seems to those Garou who interpret such things." And there's the same problem: How does no one know who the Stargazers Patron-Spirit is? Has anybody asked them? "No one knows and the only thing the other Garou know is that it isn't Chimera anymore." You would only need one more short sentence saying something like "The Garou tried to talk to the Stargazers about it but they remain tight-lipped and act very secretive when asked questions."

        And it's the same with the War of Rage. Other Shifters are unfriendly, they say it's because of a conflict between them and the Garou in the distant past and the Garou are... what? It would make more sense if you either removed the hint of the War of Rage or added a short comment on the Garou-stance. Like you mentioned, maybe Garou have stopped sharing the tales about the War of Rage or it's now considered "secret knowledge" and only high-Renown Garou get to hear the stories.

        It's these half-formed ideas whose logic only makes sense if you start to bring in your own ideas. Like trying to fill a plothole but for worldbuilding. But this isn't how lore/worldbuilding should work. You want to set up mysteries because they raise interesting questions that can be answered through play. Or you set up elements in the setting that can be used as a plothook. But the process shouldn't start with the first step being "Okay, let me think about how I get this bit to make sense.".

            All this applies to humans, yes. They also know that there are some Fera that weren't so lucky, and so aren't inclined to trust the Garou on that basis as well; they might well just want to finish the job.

            So, yeah, if we were dealing with normal humans, without long memories, a secret culture, and not just actual but incredibly strong ancestral memories, you'd be correct. But that's not Werewolf: the Apocalypse, so we're back where we were.
