    Originally posted by Black Fox View Post
    I think as a chronicle element, it's fine. It works with the themes of the game. I think the real question is how do you make this gameable? Whether the Garou PCs succeed or fail, what are the complications that occur, what are the major story beats (plots), who are the antagonists, what important decisions do the PCs have to make, and so on.

    These would be the points I think the chronicle would need to cover.

    1) By definition, extirpating an invasive species from an area is hard. There's a reason humans haven't been able to do so. Killing individual members of that species, even lots of them, is easy. Eliminating them entirely is very hard. Even if the Garou are able to kill them more easily than humans and kill even more of them, it doesn't necessarily follow Garou will be more successful overall. So put a lot of thought as to what constitutes success and how it can be achieved.

    2) The main part of the game is for the Garou to fight supernatural evil, not mundane threats. An overemphasis on an issue like this would lead the Garou to neglect more pressing (and fun for the players) issues and opponents. So there needs to be a balance between plots like this and other plots and antagonists.

    3) Resolution on this issue (just like most kinds of real world environmental issues) should involve the mobilization and involvement of Kinfolk. It doesn't matter that Garou are big combat monsters and can kill feral pigs very good. They can't kill all the feral pigs. You want to involve Kinfolk who can go on hunts with mortal hunters, be elected to local office, staff positions in relevant government agencies, become activists who rally the local community, and take other actions that Garou - by themselves - are poor at. So I'd really emphasize the PCs making good use of their own Kinfolk backgrounds and Kinfolk NPCs in the greater setting. That will involve making use of their character sheet as well as internal politicking within the sept. That should be a critical element.

    4) Think of the spiritual impacts of this in the Umbra. What are the spirits of these invasive species doing in the local Penumbra? What spirit broods are they a part of? Which of the other spirits don't like them, do like them, or are indifferent? And remember that simply because a species is invasive in this particular area, it should not automatically override the normal spirit "politics" in the area based on the spirit broods. Feral pigs aren't boars. But most likely the Boar totem, and thus packs of boar, are still fond of them and might get angry that his children are being killed en masse. Nutria, being a rodent, could very well be a member of Rat's brood, and Rat mostly cares about survival, tolerates his children being very fecund, etc. So Rat might become upset as well as packs of Rat and the entire local Bone Gnawer tribe. They simply don't care if Nutria is an invasive species. They'll support them. What if the spirit of the invasive species is actually now a caern totem of a local sept (even if small)? What about other spirits, equally invasive and not native, but which may not be as destructive like certain species. Mustangs and feral horses are certainly not native to the Americas and even have controlled hunts on their population, but many people don't view them as being invasive. Lots of non-native birds have also fit into the local eco-system. So how do spirits like these and other introduced species view any Garou who have dedicated themselves to wiping out non-native species?

    While I think there may be a temptation to ignore complications like this, I think this will actually be the heart of dealing with this issue in the chronicle. The PCs will have to decide how to deal with Garou opponents who have their own reasons to oppose them. How do they deal with that? With violence? With compromise? With making political deals with non-involved Garou to outmaneuver them? What if such opponents are from different septs? And for the spirits, you are going to deal with issues of chiminage, proper use of rites like the Rite of Contrition and Prayer for the Prey, spirit notoriety and affinity, complications with spirit broods, and really making spirits an important part of the setting and resolution of this conflict.

    5) Think of further complications. Adding in some possible fomori versions is one option. But so is introducing gorgons or even kami of the species. That would be a more unusual, and thus more interesting, options for the PCs to deal with. The Wyld isn't Gaia and has its own goals and preferences. Personally, I would try to limit the involvement of the Wyrm on an issue like this because approaching it from a different angle allows you to deal with other setting elements that are often neglected, and will lead to more interesting scenes and resolutions.

    6) If you do make the Wyrm involved in some way, be creative and make it ancillary to the issue. Don't create something so the PCs can kill it and have an easy win. Keep it limited to an interesting sidequest that is just another complication. Maybe a Wyrm faction is helping to hunt them by introducing tainted poison traps that helped kill off many of the wolves in the 19th century. Maybe they are capturing certain species for grotesque experiments to create destructive fomori, or as guardians of important lairs. A remote Setite temple and its vampire priest might use these fomori guardians, but otherwise be uninterested in the invasive species. Or Gangrel vampires are also hunting them as an easy way to access blood they need to survive.

    You don't want complications that simply piss all over the PCs and make them feel they have no impact. Buy you want complications that make sense and provide a sense of drama and accomplishment as the PCs navigate the issue.
    Well, killing invasive species would be more of a sidequest for a story or two, searching for them is more or less the same as hunting in game, and killing a lot of them counts as a success. I have thought that in the case of killing Feral Cats bothering Cat spirits then something can be done as an apology if someone is associated with such a spirit. I have also thought of the possibility of a Hippo Fomor resulting from the pollution and death the species is causing in Columbia. Involving Kinfolk also sounds like a good idea.

    Also, Horses are fine. They won't be a focus.

        Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post

        No, I mean that this is a complex undertaking. It's not just "kill this, then fix that." You have to have a plan that incorporates the whole ecosystem in question if you want to return things to how they where. If you just want to kill the invasive species and let nature take its course, a lot of native species are probably going to die out as a whole new ecosystem emerges instead. If you want to restore an ecosystem you're going to have to cull some native species to keep them from overpopulating with the invasive species gone and their natural population controls not recovered yet, and you're going to have to work to get slower reproducing species back in place without compromising genetic stability. Etc.

        "Life finds a way" doesn't mean nature does what you want it to do. If you want to guide an ecosystem in a specific direction it takes management over time.
        How would a species multiply after having their existence threatened?

            Originally posted by Samuelalpha86 View Post

            How would a species multiply after having their existence threatened?
            We are talking about a food web with multiple linkages and knock-on effects here. Initial removal of an invasive species can have some native species take advantage of niches that are now unoccupied in places that the existing system didn't allow, and delay the restoration of the prior system or potentially make it impossible without further action, particularly if humans are around with their thumb on a scale that wasn't anticipated beforehand. I don't know how things work in AUS with regard to land ownership and these issues, but the US has a rather extensive problem with the intersection of private property laws and endangered species protections, to take one example.

                Sometimes the harm is a species multiplying too much.

                Let's remember that humans are also an incredibly effective invasive species. The entire US east of the Mississippi river has a major problem: deer. Not that deer are dying, but they're vastly overpopulated because we humans killed all off (at least locally) the major terrestrial apex predators that used to eat them. Coyotes are starting to return (and in theory could replace wolves as the apex predator for deer), but with humans in the way coyote populations are far too low and far to inexperienced with hunting deer to keep the deer herds in check. The fact that the deer are local is actually part of the problem. The area is where they thrive, they're perfectly designed to eat and breed and repeat. And they have no concept of a need to stop; their instinct is to reproduce as much as possible before predators kill too many of them. If humans stopped managing deer herds... they'd explode out of control, strip whole grass lands and forests bare, killing hundreds of local species that needed those plants for their own survival... and then the deer populations would crash because there's no food left for the giant population that would have been produced without humans hunting them to take the place of wolves and cougars hunting them.

                It's really hard to describe the projections of what would happen if humans just decided to stop hunting deer and didn't do anything else about it. "Dust bowl" would seem quaint to the kind of damage we're talking about.

                This is one of the things that consistently infuriates me with how the books write about the Red Talons. They're perfectly OK with thinking like humans when it comes to justifying hate and violence, but completely opposed to thinking like humans when it comes to using their long term planning abilities to actually restore ecosystems to pre-human conditions,

                                    Originally posted by Samuelalpha86 View Post

                                    The thing is that humans are self-aware and know to control the Deer population but other animals don't. This includes Invasive species who destroy everything with no self-awareness, they don't fill a niche, they cause an imbalance by outcompeting local species.
                                    Nature has no balance. Lions will eat all prey and reproduce like crazy, until there's too many lions for too little pray. Then they'll all starve to death. That's what is called "dynamic balance" that is Nature. It is absolutely brutal. No organisms remain within some arcane "sustainable" boundaries because they are so fluffy. They all "destroy" their ecosystem, until that comes back to bite them in the ass, and they all die by the droves. That's how mother Nature keeps its balance: DEATH.

                                    Humans are actually the only species that goes against that - to the point that humans decide not to reproduce uncontrollably. But that's only a modern thing. Before the last half of the 20th century, there were only 3 ways that human populations would be kept in control: plagues, famine and wars. Which is why wars were always inevitable, despite of what some hippies may think. With modern technology, we may make war obsolete, but until now it was a necessity.

                                    As for other organisms, death is the only thing that keeps anyone of them in check. That can be done by predators, that can be done with internal competition - a wolf's worse enemy will always be another wolf, a lion's worse threat will always be another lion, and a deer's worse threat will always be another deer, because organisms of the same species compete for the same resources, including reproductive rights. Or it can be done by massive famine, after a species depletes an area of all the resources.

                                    If the deers eat all grass, they'll starve to death and their population will greatly decrease. Then, over time, the surviving grass will spread again, this time very fast because there's almost no deers to eat it. Then, there will be plenty grass for very few deers, thus the surviving deers will be in overabundance, meaning they'll start breeding like rats, until such a time that their population exceeds the sustainable levels. Then the grass disappears once again, they all die and the cycle continues. That's how Mother Nature works.

                                            The problem isn't self-awareness or instinct, but rather context. Most animals are self-aware, and humans are incredibly skilled at convincing animals to act outside of their normal instincts. But our history with incorporating other animals into our social systems has also taught us the importance of context when communicating with other animals. A dog is not born with the instinct to sit on command from a human. They have to be taught. Teaching them requires providing them contexts they do grasp instinctively and building from there (also this would be impossible without some level of self-awareness on the dog's part).

                                            Deer understand on some level that there is a time to stop eating and move on to green pastures. In the wild, they don't naturally strip biomes bare before moving on, because as herd animals they know food scarcity is a problem larger than one individual and they need to migrate while everyone is full instead of eating all the food possible. The context problem they have is what happens when there isn't a new feeding ground to move to that's abundant enough. That's when the problems start because the math changes. If, within their perceptional context, all places are scarce on food, they start stripping what they can find bare because there's no sure bet somewhere else. That's when the grass might never recover, or the deer might never recover, and all the knock on effects from that spread damage even wider.

                                                When that happens, it's usually locally. Yes, the grass may disappear from one location, causing all the deers to go "extinct" on that location... For a while.

                                                But there will still be grass and deers in other locations, and in time, they return. Or not. Extinction is also a pivotal part of Mother Nature. We ourselves are only here after all because 99.9% of every species that has ever walked on this planet went extinct. Without the meteor to wipe out the colossal Jurassic chickens, we wouldn't be here.

                                                Many species have many survival mechanisms, and diverse ones for that matter. Mammals have well developed brains that allows them a good level of cognitive capabilities, but that's an exception. Most organisms don't have that, but they have several other mechanisms. Even bacteria have chemical strategies - for example, developing protective "carapaces" of protein when the environment changes, which essentially puts them into "hibernation". And several other schemes.

                                                But, oversimplifying, every organism consumes and reproduces as much as possible and until it is possible; there's no "mother Gaia consciousness" about "sustainability". Survival evolutionary tactics to endure in hard times are the result of the weak dying to the harsh conditions, leaving the fittest alive to propagate the victorious genes that were more prone to survive. When deers migrate, it is to postpone the inevitable. Grass too will evolve to better survive - including to survive the predation from deers. Heck, kill all predators in north America and give it some 100 thousand years and you can be sure that some branches of deers will turn carnivore. Or a South American Puma will eventually migrate north. The "Dynamic Balance" always sets itself in the end. It's just a matter of time. After each one the major extinction events, happens an explosion of biodiversity. "Invasive Species" is just a new adaptation. Soon, they'll get in this dynamic balance within their new environment, and will start to diverge from their original branch.

                                                Sure, deers will probably migrate before all grass is gone; they evolved brains for that exact purpose. UNLESS there's nowhere new to migrate to. Then yes, they could all be gone. For good. That's extinction. And that would just simply open up the way for new species of grass and new species of herbivores to set root on the great plains, even if they need to cross the ocean to do so, and even if that takes hundreds of thousands of years.

                                                I am adamantly against the naturalist idiocy of thinking that "mother nature" is perfect. It is not. It is worse than cruel or brutal or violent. Because, if it were any of that, it would at least be a will that guides it.

                                                No, it is worse, because it is completely indifferent. For the unconscious "mother Nature", if the entire planet Earth were swallowed by the Sun, it wouldn't make any difference. The universe wouldn't cry over the loss of all that biodiversity.

                                                That's why it's only we humans that truly matter. Because we are the only ones that care. The only ones that feel and that can bring meaning to things. Deers are pretty and fluffy little beasts, yes, but they wouldn't be making epic poems about the "twilight of the Gods" once they perceive that their extinction is imminent. They'll just simply suffer and then die from starvation up to their last member. Only we humans care. Which why we are special, despite the post modern tendency of self hate that has been propagated in the last decades.
