    Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
    When one takes the full known factual nature of the WtA setting, the deformities have to be a curse. I've done really detailed breakdowns of this before, but the key points:

    1) It is not inherent to shapeshifters. Some of the Fera simply can't reproduce with others of their kind, some have deformed kids, and some have kids that actually get bonuses. So any explanation of the "spirit incest overloads the child" doesn't work because it doesn't hold true across all of them.

    2) The highest level of spirits created the Fera, and can impart or change aspects like what metal harms them, or various additional weaknesses.

    If it's not inherent to all shifters, and we know Gaia and the Celestines can impart significant changes, then the deformities must come from such high level influences. The only real alternative, is that it is innate to all shifters, but instead the ones that don't end up in deformities were altered to protect them instead (which doesn't really change the fundamental message in the metanarrative sense).
    These are good points, and I think this is where the concept needs to be clarified, because it's kind of arbitary that it would be so different across different Fera to me.A few of the differences make sense, but some of them just don't, and I think having a solid answer would help. If it is a curse, that needs to be changed, though.

                I mean, to me, not everyone wants to avoid addressing marginalization within games.
                I agree. These are good points, and I think this is where the concept needs to be clarified. I'm a fan of taking this in the direction of 'unreliable narrator' at my tabletops, where the notion of it being a "curse/supernatural incest" is something perpetuated by the Western Concordat to justify their treatment of the warborn. I do, however, think the warborn, and charachs especially, are synonymous with queerness. Their treatment wasn't just limited to heterosexual garou, but is extended to same-sex garou as well, regardless of whether they are bearing warborn children or not. If one were to make a minor shift away from everyday ableism, it can stand as a compelling representation of the Garou experience representing queerness, on a fundamental level. I wrote an essay on this topic that kind of lays out more specifically what these parallels are and why I think simply deleting them in W5 wasn't an appropriate response. In short, it doesn't 'fix' the problem, but rather, it merely renders them invisible, which in many ways is even worse.

                An approach I'm taking to the warborn is to move away from giving them real-life disabilities and instead lend them aspects of horror that are indicative of an imbalance of Wyld influence, as though they are touched by The Flux. Things like a tree limb where a leg ought to be, a limb that cannot change it's wolf form, glowing fur patterns, significantly more eyes on one side of the head than the other causing blurred vision and headaches, and other features of supernatural asymmetry that inhibit their ability to operate openly in human society while still pushing narratives of social inequity and accommodation. Think less "supernatural incest," and more "pan's labyrinth" or "hellboy" features at the heart of this mistreatment. This would hold space for explorations of queer culture as well as disability at the table without having able-bodied players caricaturing real-life disabilities. As for those wanting to have characters with real-life disabilities still, these features have always been available in merits and flaws, and no longer have to be compulsory, nor the reason for this codified ill treatment.

                                On the ableism stuff:

                                Two key points to remember, are that much like Younger and Older Brother, there are a lot of people that gravitated towards the deformities as prominent representation - even if problematic representation - because it was something rather than nothing. Unlike being able to work in disabilities as quirks or Flaws, having a prominent character creation choice that resulted in a disability helps push the unfairness of disabilities into the forefront of the topics that game touches on. It's not "everyone gets a weakness, yours is just something a bit more real," like Malkavians. The Crinos-born were just screwed over. and it's not fair even in a game sense

                                Second, a lot of disabled gamers hate the idea that being disable makes you "stronger," aka things like Flaws giving you bonus points. This is something that actually come up in a panel Eddy did that they replayed on the Onyxpath Cast ages ago (keeping in mind that Eddy is hearing impaired). The whole idea of "overcoming the adversity of your disability and being a stronger person for it," is a really toxic abilist narrative of its own. Disabilities are a drag on your life... that's kinda the whole deal. Learning ways to cope with them is just spending time and energy on that instead of thing you'd want to do. Medical treatments are more time, recovery, and money that means missing out on things you'll never get back in the hopes of being able to enjoy stuff later.

                                        I think there's two fairly straightforward paths to improvement. The first is very lore heavy in order to keep the literal born Garou part of the equation, and thus doesn't really fit with W6 (I've discussed this in past threads, the basic idea I have is that the Crinosborn were originally even harder to birth, but weren't deformed and were in fact honored and special for it - kinda like how the Nagah and Kitsune work already - but that the Garou themselves are responsible for a ritual that tainted the Crino-born). The second gets rid of the whole birth angle completely, and thus requires far less setting to implement: steal from BNS WtA with the Bitten.. By taking out being born that way you lose some of the disability representation (though lots of people develop disabilities later in life), but the idea that some times, randomly, humans that survive werewolf bites become werewolves has the benefits of the deformities stemming from a process that isn't how things were "supposed" to be. It also sots the Garou better into existing pop-culture werewolf media. This creates an upper class of born werewolves and a lower class of made werewolves, where the born werewolves are still ultimately responsible for the tactics and behaviors of their members that leads to increased risks of it happening.
