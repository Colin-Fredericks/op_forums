                    Originally posted by Ragged Robin View Post

                    Its ultimately a product of online nerd culture trauma of late 10's far right ressuragance and the increasingly disconncted nature on online spaces. We're not really engaging with facism here so much as middle class nerds fears of facism hence why fascists are stupid or mentally ill.

                    a problem emphasised by the worrying lack of belief in a larger cohesive way of resolving the garou (our?) nations issues were fascist fenris win the arguement
                    because they're the only ones proposing anything at all.

                    I suspect a lot of these people would benifit from getting involved in local politics and actually engaging with people with alternative political stances. Rather than trauma dumping into commercial products.
                    That is almost certainly an aspect of this; the writing of W5 in general (and, also, that which has appeared in a fair number of World of Darkness in recent years) has all the hallmarks of being fairly insular, where the authors' frame of reference is gaming, online forums, and other related popular culture. This is quite striking in terms of the evolution of the gaming universe. Back in the 1980s, the people who wrote for Dungeons & Dragons had a very narrow frame of reference. Most people had their notions of a fantasy world informed primarily by Tolkein (albeit a narrow, Americanised version of what Tolkein represented) and a well-read person within this context is someone who might have extended themselves to Edgar Rice Burroughs and Michael Moorcock.

                    Anyway, at the beginning of the 1990s Vampire: the Masquerade appears, and is considered by many gamers to be a breath of fresh air. The authors have, of course, read Bram Stoker and Anne Rice, but also Upton Sinclair and Charles Dickens. They were creating a universe informed by the leading edge of current affairs, talking about environmentalism and racism, and other similar matters. These were games made by people who looked outwards for inspiration, beyond what we would now call "nerd culture", and came up with interesting, challenging ideas. Of course, over time, the World of Darkness stops being this challenging novelty, and has, itself, become part of nerd culture, with writing driven by insular viewpoints, and too much time spent online. The end point for this trajectory is something like W5, a game written by people who don't quite have the broader context to take it to where it needs to be, and whose viewpoints are dominated by concerns generated within the online world, but which don't quite mesh with reality.
