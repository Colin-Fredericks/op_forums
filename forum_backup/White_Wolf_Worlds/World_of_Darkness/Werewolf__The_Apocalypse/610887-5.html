    Originally posted by Su-tehp View Post

    Yeah, "A Straw Death" from Rites of Renown II. I just finished reading it.

    What a waste. Selma charges into three packs of Spirals by herself when she knows reinforcements are on the way but doesn't bother to wait for them and gets herself killed? That's not heroism, that's stupidity. I could never see the "honor" in charging off into an unwinnable battle. All Selma had to do was wait a few more minutes and the Silent Strider would have returned with reinforcements and then Selma could have avenged her pack without dying, and thereby lived to gather more glory in more battles later on.
    .
    The story has a lot of cultural baggage. I saw the whole point of the story as being a chance to illustrate Fenrir culture, what's often described as their "fatalism." It's a culture that, like their Viking ancestors, places a higher value on dying in battle than dying in your bed of old age. She debates the strategy of the situation with herself, but in the end, she chooses Fenrir valor over strategy and even family. It was a perfect way to illustrate the often-repeated cliché that a Fenrir "would gladly allow themselves to be devoured by a Wyrm demon just so they could rip out its tongue before they died." Most warrior cultures have some version of the idea that strategy and circumstance are sometimes subservient to the sole focus of a warrior: the destruction of your opponent.

    I'm not saying it was the wisest choice, it's just the most Fenrir thing to do. It reminded me of the end of "The 13th Warrior." Or maybe "300," a perfect example of how charging off into an unwinnable battle can have a greater purpose.

    And whether or not she died is never confirmed.

We can assume, though, that we're talking about its nearest layer from the Material perspective, what is almost well defined.

                                                    Well, she's actually right! Hey, people, the Umbra IS an alternate dimension. And that is precisely why it didn't recovered as fast in Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Because it isn't the material plane, it is an alternate reality. What we do know and the book describes isn't that the Umbra is the same world, but that it is linked to the Material. So those rules about how one world affects and reflects the other aren't rules about one world working, but about two worlds interacting.

                                                    And needing to interact, lets be clear about it. The Material and the Umbra are two, alternate, worlds. But they need each other. Like the gravity equilibrium in our solar system makes Earth dependent on every other planet, and to some degree respond to eventual changes on them, without being the same planet at all. In a sense, you could describe the relation between the Material and the Umbra as the relation between Earth and Moon and their gravity interaction.
