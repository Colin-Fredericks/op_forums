    ST Question: How do people deal with skills and stats?

    04-24-2016, 08:33 PM
    I've been running a Werewolf game for about two "seasons" and I'm very easy going when it comes to skills and stat ranks. I have multiple people with fours in multiple stats and two with fives. A lot of these were started at a high level during character creation with bonus points.

    I'm curious how others feel in regards to skill ranks. Do people let their players start with Melee 5, and let them rank up easily? Or are people much more harsh regarding stats and skills?

          At creation, I'm generally fairly lax. As far as I'm concerned, if it makes sense and fits the rules? Go for it.

          During play, it depends a lot on how long we're expecting the characters to be played consistently. The longer the campaign, the more strict I tend to get about advancement. This usually isn't really a problem. People understand that if we're only going to be playing for a few sessions, there's no reason to be picky. If we're going to be playing for two years, it helps the group to have stronger structure to keep the game sensible over a longer period of time.

                      I make it clear during character creation what the ranks actually mean. 4 is outstanding and 5 is world class. I also make it known that achieving those levels requires the character to devote a lot of time in game to accomplish. Their actions in game justify the expenditure of XP. I simply don't allow it just because they have the necessary XP.

                      During character creation I know I'll be seeing one 4 in Attributes. I also know I might see one or two Abilities at 4 as well. Some players have their heart set at having an attribute or ability at 5, and if so, I let them. But it is clear that is exceptional and is a noteworthy trait. I make it known their background really needs to incorporate that.

                      I also try my best to run games where ranks of 3 can get lots of things done. I try to minimize rolling dice all the time as that encourages people to increase their ranks just so they can accomplish mundane stuff or be halfway competent.

                      There is one PC with a strength of 5, but his dexterity is only 2 so it's not like he's unstoppable in combat. And most of his downtime (when people are doing other stuff with their characters) is basically spent just maintaining that level of physical strength. He is in constant training. The first thing is the role play/concept, and the mechanics simply support it.

                      The people I game with are very good so I trust them not to get ridiculous. I've found it's not so much what you allow players to do, as it is to have good players who want the same thing out of the game as you do.

                          Generally speaking, I allow just about anything as long as there's a narrative explanation to go with it. With that in mind, obviously people will justify literally anything if they're given free reign to fabricate whatever backstory they want (e.g., "I'm an Olympic power lifter and kendo champion who was also a Navy SEAL").

                          If the explanation is tonally inconsistent with the rest of the game, is simply unjustifiable within the canon (assuming you're using the canon), or the character build would logically have other skills and abilities that the person doesn't have the points to make (i.e., in my example above, a Navy SEAL would have such a wide variety of abilities at at least 3 that it's unlikely anyone is going to make a starting character that accurate reflects the abilities an actual SEAL would have), then I veto. If it passes narrative muster but was obviously min-maxed, I warn that I'm going to make their "mins" of overwhelming importance--I've rarely had a player just take their chances after that warning.

                                          I think it is a problem to attribute concrete equivalents of a level in a given skill to a system that is supposed to be abstract. Having a dot or two or five in a given skill is first and foremost a die in any dicepool with that skill. And, unless you use the instruction skill and it rules, there is hardly any guideline on how long it takes to raise any stat (save for gifts, there is a rule of thumb for those). Rites on the other hand might call for weeks of teaching/learning, but no experience is required what so ever. Same goes for building instructions for talens, if you don't handwave them entirely, or require skills for them like Craft (basketweaving) and Craft (arrowmaking) and so on.

                                          Also, while it seems common knowledge that you need to train to maintain peek physical power, there are no rules that any attribute or skill a character has might decrease if not used - save for backgrounds and age depending attribute adjustments and Battle Scars. And thing is, we don't really know how fast such things happen. Sure, medicine scientists run tests like how heavy can weights be an astronaut can lift before and after his space voyage and stuff like that - but unless one is into sports medicine one can't really translate such effects into a five dot spread.

                                          For our LARP I established this rule: All regular, open-for-all games are 'increase relevant', and marked as such in our character database. To raise a stat a number of such games must have passed since the last raise (character start is considered a raise for all stats). I doesn't matter if you participated in the game (we don't blame you if you have to work and can't play), but of course you need the XP to raise a stat, too.

                                          So, for attributes so many games need to have passed since the last raise, as the level of the attribute. So, raise from 1 to 2 after one game is possible, raise from 2 to 3 is possible after two additional games, and so on.
                                          Skills from 0 to 1 or 1 to 2 is possible after one game each, 2 to 3 and 3 to 4 requires two games each, and 4 to 5 requires three games.
                                          Totem is considered an attribute for the games required.
                                          Willpower, Rage and Gnosis require one game to have passed for each raise up to 6 and two for each raise beyond that.

                                          With this method we prohibit that a character starts game with one low stat and raises it within two games to a high stat, even if the character has gotten the appropriate XP.

                                          Usually I would, in a table top game, allow for a player to raise every stat they feel suitable. But I consider the background of the character when I handwave rolls. Meaning if a theurge who recently raised medicine to 5 tries a difficult operation I let him roll. If a character/NPC who's backstory is that he is a trauma surgeon tries the same, I might handwave it, even if the skill rating is only 4.

                                                  Werewolf had ageing rules too, in the Player's Guide 2nd Edition (and maybe reprinted in PGttG).

                                                  RPGs aren't real life simulators, though. One could devise more or less complicated systems to raise and lower stats, but it would involve a lot of bookkeeping and certainly distract from the actual story that is played.

                                                  I usually don't see the problem in keeping stats and real life equivalents separated. Trying to find exact representations is rarely satisfactory.

                                                  There are RPGs that allow for a set of traits to be set to a certain level and the ST and player can decide if a given narrow task is within the scope of the trait set or not. Like being rocket scientist 4 or ER doctor 2 or White Claw Ninja 5.

                                                  I usually prefer systems that don't have abstract skills at all. RuneQuest for example has traits in %-values. It is very easy to grasp how good someone is in a given task. If a task is more difficult than average, there is a penalty in % and likewise easy tasks have bonuses in % and it all in all is very intuitive and one can really easily transfer the likelihood of succeeding in a task of a given difficulty to a real life equivalent.
