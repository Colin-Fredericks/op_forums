    PHASE TWO PART THREE

    Clans vs. Tribes
    In V5, an entire section is given over to introducing the Clans, as well as non-Clan options. Six of the seven Clans have six pages dedicated to them, while the seventh only has four pages. (of the non-Clan options, one has six pages and the other four.) Two full pages are given over to artwork for each Clan. The start of each section has a large symbol for the Clan at the top of the page with a unique logo for each. Alternate designations for the Clan are on the opposite page with the artwork. The next part is a brief discussion about generalities about the Clan. This is followed by five character archetypes, each a few sentences long. The next part are paragraphs explaining how each of the Clan's three favored Disciplines are viewed within the Clan. Finally, there is part on the Clan Bane, a weakness that affects every member to a different degree. Each Bane offers different roleplaying opportunities even when some use the same mechanics to deal with them. Clan Compulsions could have been added to this section, and latter supplements would include this information when introducing the other Clans. (The two non-Clan options no not have a symbol nor a unique logo. The Discipline and Bane sections are replaced with a brief discussion on the special rules for these options instead.)

    In W5, Tribes are treated a little differently. Each of the eleven Tribes mentioned (three other NPC options are found in the Antagonists and Allies Chapter, but they offer slightly less information than for the main Tribes in some regards) have only two pages. On the top of the first page there are illustrations of the four character archetypes mentioned on the second page. Each archetype has a longish paragraph detailing them. This takes up about half of the second page. Below the illustrations is the name of the Tribe, given in the same font for each. The same font is the one used for section headings throughout the book. Beside the name is a small glyph that represents the Tribe. Under this is a discussion on the Tribe. The vague hints that plagued the history chapter continue here. After this is a section on the Tribe's patron spirit, the one that unifies the Tribe and gives the Tribe its special powers. Of note, this section also mentions the Tribes' primary Renown, the only place where it is found. This is actually a very important part of character creation, and it should have also been repeated elsewhere. The patron spirit also gives its Tribe a special Favor and Ban. The Favor is basically a bonus on certain dice roles involving particular actions. The Ban is a failure to regain full Willpower at the end of a session if a certain action was/was not performed. While there is a variety of actions that cause this penalty among the Tribes, having different results beyond not regaining Willpower would have shown the differences between the Tribes better. Finally, there are the archetypes mentioned previously. There are no non-Tribe options currently available.

    The Auspices are also found in this chapter. Each Auspice gets a column for them spread over three pages. They are presented in the chronological placement of moon phase, instead of alphabetically or other way, but there isn't a clear reason why this is done. The top part of the column features an illustration of a character of the Auspice combined with the Auspice's glyph. Below this is a quote from someone of that Auspice. Next, there is a discussion on how that Auspice is perceived in Garou culture and what roles that Auspice performs. Aside from a unique set of Gifts, there are no mechanics currently involved in playing one Auspice over another.

    Basically, while the Tribes and Auspices have about the same amount of information given over to them as do the Clans, V5 tried to make each Clan separate from each other, while W5 emphasizes what each Tribe and Auspice have in common with each other. Less variety and more unity, even when the previous chapter suggests otherwise.

    Character Creation
    Each book has basically the same considerations for character creation, but the two books have many differences.

    V5 starts with multiple pages on considering what a new character should be, including discussions with the other players in the group. There is also a brief summary of the process at the start of the chapter. W5 goes directly into character creation without this step, with just a small sidebar mentioning the steps and a note on how to create wolf-born characters (basically, not much of a difference).

    Next, V5 goes into how to set up the 'human life' portion of traits, those that would continue on after the Embrace. Namely, Attributes and Skills. V5 even offers some tips on choosing tips for certain professions, as well as alternatives on how to divide skill points. The actual explanation of these traits are to be found after the full discussion of creation. W5 goes straight to these traits, practically combining them with just a little break in describing how to calculate the Health and Willpower scores. As a side note, both books use the same example involving Muay Thai martial arts as not being an option for a Skill specialty for the Brawl Skill, as it too broad.

    V5 goes over the values of Convictions, Touchstones, and Ambitions before moving onto the vampire specific traits. The explanations for these Traits are given after the part on Skills. The only one of these W5 uses is Touchstones, but they don't cover it until the end of the creation process. Initially, V5 said that only living humans could be Touchstones, but this got expanded to other things in later supplements. W5 only allows humans, and/or wolves, as Touchstones at this time.

    The first part of the vampire traits to consider are Disciplines. While the rules for choosing them are here, the actual descriptions are in a later chapter. The W5 counterpart, Gifts, aren't mentioned until later in their process, but are also fully treated in a separate chapter. Next for V5 is Predator type, the primary means a vampire hunts for blood, but it is not the only means they use. By choosing a Predator type, a vampire gains extra traits, such as a Skill specialties Disciplines, Advantages, and/or Flaws. Types are mentioned int eh next section following the previous one. W5 offers no such means as a counterpart, although it could have been possible. For example, one could have tied Auspice to certain roles to achieve a similar effect, probably without Gifts as a benefit as they are granted on a slightly different system. Still, it would have given a greater use for Auspice beyond roleplaying or a Gift list.

    Next for both books are Advantages, which are divided into Merits and Backgrounds. The former are usually tied directly to the character and usually can't be altered during play. The latter can change from story to story and can be taken multiple times. Of note, V5 allows coteries to share Backgrounds but W5 does not allow packs to do so, even though packs are supposedly tied much closer together than coteries. This should be changed later on as it seems very natural to occur. Also, not all of the Backgrounds are the same. For example, vampires have multiple physical helper Backgrounds, while werewolves only have a spirit guide. V5 gives vampires the Haven Background, while W5 has the equivalents Caern and Safe House as Merits instead. Strangely enough, W5 has no equivalent to the V5 Background Status, which is unusual since it is later mentioned in the mechanics chapter as being a possible trait for some social rolls. For all the talk about how the current generation of Garou distrusts their Elders, not having any mechanic to determine this is odd.

    The rest of the V5 traits are more thoroughly covered in the following chapter on mechanics. However, W5 has one last special mechanic for character creation, Renown. While Renown is used to determine what Gifts a Garou can purchase and as dice rolls for those Gifts, it feels like it should have something else tied to it equal to the Status state mentioned above. This mechanic just feels incomplete on some level.

    Both books end with discussions on experience, both in awarding of points to increase traits and creating veteran characters at the start. While both books have the same basic concepts covered, W5 just seems to give the basic tools needed to create a character, while V5 helps players more along the way in the process.

                I keep finding mistakes and issues the further in I go. Anyway, time for the next part of the Review.

                PHASE TWO: PART FOUR

                Mechanics
                While the two types of supernatural being are different on the basic level, one being alive and the other not, one would expect similar mechanic when applicable. This is not always the case. The main mechanic is the "bonus/penalty" dice system. In V5, these are Hunger dice and represent the craving for Blood, as well as the influence/interference of the Beast on the vampire's mind. The less blood/greater need a vampire has, the more dice they have to substitute for rolls, causing the higher chances for something to go not they way they were planning. This happens until the hunger is taken care. This can also lead to Compulsions, subtle influence from the Beast upon a vampire's actions, whenever things go wrong a roll. If a vampire is fully sated, they have no Hunger dice and act however they want.

                The W5 version is Rage dice. While having more dice substituted for some in a pool leads to similar problems, they are not quite the same. For one, there are no long term conditions such as Compulsions is one's Rage is too high. For another, having more Rage is considered a positive for Garou, as it can lead to better outcomes in some situations. On the downside, not having any Rage at all mean that the character is just a regular human, for all intents and purposes, until they can regain Rage. No supernatural abilities at all, the opposite for V5. Also, the range is different for the two systems. In W5, a '2' on a Rage die equals a Brutal Failure. In V5, a '2' on a Hunger die is just a failure. [The dice really should have numbers. I don't have the new sets, but I don't think the number are printed on them, making them useless for other purposes.]

                A main factor for vampires is Humanity, a measure of how one can remain close to their original state. Various benefits and penalties exist along the scale. W5's closest equivalent is Harano and Hauglosk (that is a very unwieldy word0. The former is measure of futility with the latter a measure of straightforwardness. For some reason, a Garou can suffer from both at the same time, which shouldn't really be possible. One should cancel the other out, but they don't. A Garou gains Harano whenever they suffer a major setback and Hauglosk whenever they violate a Chronicle tenet. There aren't any repercussions until either one reaches the maximum of five, although there are hints that suggest that such mechanics might exist, but were not included in the rules.

                A major mechanic for V5 is Blood Resonance and Dyscrasias. Emotions can 'flavor' blood and provide bonuses in experience or additional dice to Discipline rolls. At certain times, blood can provide even greater advantages. W5 has no equivalent, but it could. Various spots could provide 'spiritual resonance' to act similarly. Certain spirits flock to locations in the spirit world that are attractive to their type. Garou could also use such sites for certain bonuses, such as regaining Willpower or Rage, healing damage faster, not having to spend points on effects, or other uses. There could even be 'tainted' areas that could provide such bonuses, but pose a threat to the character, leading to their potential end. For instance, a Wyld tainted area could allow a Garou to shapeshifter without needing a Rage check, but could also lead to the Garou to start shifting uncontrollably over time.

                Disciplines are the main supernatural powers for vampires in V5. They are scaled from one to five, with multiple options per level, and a limit on one power per slot on the scale. In W5, the powers are called Gifts. They are divided into three types (Native, Auspice, and Tribe) and are roughly gauged into three tiers. One must possess a minimum amount of Renown to purchase a Gift. Also, each type of Gift has different minimums of Renown for each level of Gift. While Renown is on a 1-5 scale, Gifts are not, and there really isn't a good reason why, since it is theoretically possible to achieve five levels of Gifts. There is no limit to the number of Gifts at any level a Garou can learn. Maybe this system is more complicated because Gifts have no other limits on learning them.

                        PHASE TWO PART FIVE

                        Storytelling, Setting, and Antagonists

                        Mostly, there isn't too much difference in these chapters, aside from game line specific needs. In fact, W5 basically adapted (lifted) some material from V5 to use as their own. There is something I'll be mentioning at the end, with the final grade, though, that needs its own spot.

                        Appendices

                        V5 doesn't really have any appendices, as it just has sections that blend into one another. However, it does have a section on Loresheets after the main body of the book. V5 has fifteen of them, with two Clan specific and others that easily could be tied to a certain Clan. W5 only has five Loresheets, none of the with any exclusions. None of the five really provide much for the Background or go too deeply into game lore, retaining the vagueness that pervades W5.

                        W5 also provides an introductory scenario and information for conduction fair/safe play. V5 had a separate introductory scenario, which I didn't have, so I won't compare them. Also, V5 did include the fair play information, but it was added later only to the PDF version. I lost my PDF when my old computer died recently, and I didn't feel like buying the PDF just for that one section, so I am not reviewing it either.

                        If there is one thing that W5 does better than V5 is the index. V5 just lists everything without explanation, so you might not have an idea what something is just by looking at its entry. On the other hand, W5 uses subheadings to divide various categories. Therefore, one knows what some of the more obtuse entries are when just looking, or they have an idea of where they should be looking. Even with the odd capitalization, this is plus for looking things up.

                        Finally, both books have a two-page character sheet and some quick templates. V5's templates are on the inside front and back covers. They are for random victims. Why waste the space on something so basic is unusual, but it might be better than just graphics. W5 has six templates as either bases for character creation or random encounters. The developers' choices might not be the ones I would have chosen, but they might be workable.

                        Final grade: C-/D+

                        One would expect two game lines set in the same universe should be comparable and compatible, but V5 and W5 aren't quite there. While it is obvious that the two books share many systems and rules, they don't feel or look like they have much in common. V5 has a more sophisticated air about it. It features a deep sense of lore that can be used when needed. The systems are fairly easy and scalable to a group's needs.

                        W5 feels like it is targeted to a different, younger demographic. Much of the world-building is left to the group, with only the barest of hints of greater lore. The rules are mostly simplified, or even removed, to allow a more basic style of play. The one complicated system, that of Renown/Gifts, is way more complex than it needed to be, to the point where it is almost better not trying to improve those categories than actually using them. So much just feels missing and/or incomplete.

                        If this was all that was going on, my final grade would be C- on how W5 is connected to V5. However, there is one last thing to bring up that lowers my grade. In the chapter on the Umbra, the special spirit world setting in W5, there is an interesting sidebar. It describes that while other supernatural beings have their own ways of entering the spirit worlds, they are almost never entering the same Umbra as the Garou. In fact, the Umbra is pretty much for Garou only. This little fact not only encourages future game lines from dong their own thing, but it discourages any attempt at allowing crossovers from ever happening. Each game line would be its own thing, with their own rules totally unlike other game lines. This is not the way a shared universe should work. If this remains so throughout any future releases, then the disparity and disunity featured within just these two game lines will grow. Some interaction must exist, even if such crossovers are frowned upon. Otherwise, the game lines could collapse on themselves without a shared unified structure to build upon.

                                    No Lore as a philosophy works fine if you don't have to do much explaining from what a person would know from popular culture. Vampire doesn't need you to explain what a vampire is or how one functions. Even if you have only consumed Twilight, you'd still know the basics. An undead blood-drinking creature that avoids the sun.

                                    But that is not true with WtA. Even if we go solely by the folkloric werewolf facts, we still are left with MASSIVE gaps we need to explain to the players. The Wyrm has no role in werewolf folklore or media, there is no entropic force of destruction a werewolf has to fight. Same with the other members of the triat, they have no role in werewolf myths nor is there even anything close to a similar concept in them. In werewolf myths/media/folklore, the focus is not on protecting nature over humanity. Most focus on what being a werewolf is like and the closest to a united mission are the Benandanti who go to hell every night to fight devils for the Christian God.

                                    This is why you often see people not in the WoD community call WtA Furry Captain Planet. Because they wanted a werewolf game and instead they got a game about environmental destruction.

                                    This is why I find the "lore light" approach so dishonest, it is taking something that works for one game and then forcing another to use it without thinking of the game itself. If they wanted a lore light werewolf game, they could have always just followed the actual popular culture/folklore rather than gutted WtA.

                                            PHASE THREE PART ONE

                                            This is going to be the hardest part. This phase focuses one the differences between W5 and previous editions (I refuse to use the term "legacy," as I feel that it doesn't really do justice to older versions.) Of particular note, I'll mostly be working from memory here on the earlier editions. About twenty years ago, most of my WOD collection got destroyed. While I was able to replace some of the books, I wasn't able to restore all of them, as the original game lines were being replaced with what would become the COD. As to the WOD20 line, while I do have most of the W:tA material, they were on my old computer when it died recently. I haven't had the time to re-download them, as I've had a lot to do lately. As such, I will probably make a few mistakes about the older material, which I hope will be pointed out to me.

                                            Furthermore, much has already been said and posted about the differences among the editions, basically W5 and the older ones collectively. This phase will try to focus on points that I either feel have not been mentioned as frequently as they should have or on those points that have been mostly ignored. I will probably be going over much that is already old, but it is to set up a pattern (along with the previous two phases and the final upcoming one) for the ultimate comprehensive review. Please bear etapwith me as I continue this undertaking.

                                            Metaplot and Back Story

                                            The overall story for the original Werewolf: The Apocalypse games is that the world is in grave danger, on the verge of being destroyed, predominately by the influence of the Wyrm. While there are secondary problems involving the Weaver and other forces, the main antagonists are those in league with this spirit of destruction and corruption. In W5, the Apocalypse has already happened. No one knows when, why, or how, but it is currently happening. One would think that there would be at least some concrete answer, or at least an inkling, but there isn't one mentioned. The Garou were supposed to help forestall this from happening, but they failed. This is a major shift in how the game is played. There could be a fair amount of gameplay involved in surviving such an outcome, but it is suggested that it will be for naught. While small victories can be made, there is no suggestion that the Apocalypse can be reversed or even halted. This is a very nihilistic view, one that sets out the characters as 'losers' from the start. The previous generations failed, so we can't do anything but try and survive.

                                            Another change is that the Wyrm, as well as the other two members of the Triat the Weaver and Wyld, are no longer individual spirits, but just the names for the cosmological powers of creation, order, and destruction. While each has a number of spirits that follow them, they are no longer 'real' spirits themselves. Yet, according to W5s own rules, they could be. In fact, in some places, they are almost considered spirits. While previous editions hinged upon the Triat being powerful spirits that could be met with, at least on some level, the Triat is now just the combination of forces that define the cosmos. Basically, the Triat is now just the Garou terms for Dynamism, Stasis, and Entropy from Mage.

                                            This change is important, as the original editions tied theTriat's problems from when the Weaver grew a level of sentience great enough to capture the Wyrm, leading to the Worm's corruption. Without spiritual being, the Wyrm's corruption in W5 has no explanation. How can a force of nature become 'corrupted' enough to break the cycle of creation to renewal? For example, how can gravity be corrupted when it is just a force of nature? By removing this spiritual component, the entire underpinning of the game breaks down. One is just left with widespread destruction without reason, barely removed from real life woes. In fact, W5 even suggests to peg every little problem upon humans, leaving the spiritual side as the effect more than the cause. It is subtle distinction from previous editions, where some of the direct attacks on a cosmological scale are definitely outside of humanity's blame.
