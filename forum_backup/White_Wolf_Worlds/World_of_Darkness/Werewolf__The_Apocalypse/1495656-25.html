    Originally posted by Ana Mizuki View Post
    But in general, I get the feeling JA WANTS to have a world-saving game, but only in the confines of a small area.
    At some point his attempt to make an epic personal horror kid's cartoon (even you can save the planet via recycling!) Is going to run into a major tonal clash. Which isn't something you can't work around, but I'm not sure he really has a vision.

    Hell, I think that Justin's big concept would work much better if he dropped the werewolves. You're a bunch of jaded, vaguely spiritual people who want to save the world but lack the organisation and resources to do so, so you focus on stopping local issues and finding others willing to start more chapters. You could definitely spin a whole corebook out of that concept, although possibly more 150 A5 pages than 300+ A4 pages. Base character building around like 7-10 philosophies and use them to drive intra-party conflict. Sure it's a narrow single-concept game, but it is one you can build.

    Then you add werewolves into the mix,and I have no clue what they add. Before they fit with the exaggerated nature of the threats, now you're fighting against the things humans would be you're really good at killing people.

                                    Originally posted by Ragged Robin View Post

                                    I think that's more part of a general design-decision Justin Achilli has made with WOD5E. With H5, he talked about how he deliberately wanted to make it "rules-light" and we know he has some misgivings about V5 for not being "focused enough" or whatever. In the blog-article for the expanded mechanics he explained his approach to WOD5E:

                                    With this in mind, the fifth edition of the Storyteller System is designed to be flexible first and foremost, favoring narrative engagement over simulation realism. Once you learn how to make story rulings on individual topics, you’ll find that it becomes easier to improvise, to draw upon that experience, and to keep the story going and your players eagerly participating in the call-and-response that is roleplaying games.
                                    And part of that is also about reducing the page-size of the corebooks.

                                    If you have certain printings of Vampire: The Masquerade, you have a lot of this already, but we want to both make it available to everyone and to free up 20-ish pages of core book space for things that are specific to each of the games themselves.
                                    That only makes sense if Paradox is imposing a hard page-limit on these corebooks or is at least urging Justin Achilli to make these corebooks thinner. It's that or Justin Achilli genuinely believes this:

                                    But edge cases accumulate word count, and word count makes books bigger, and from what we’ve learned about our audience, bigger books don’t help accessibility.
                                    I mean, how big is the D&D5E Player's Guide? I don't think page-count alone is really the be-all end-all of RPG-accessibility... Like he calls them "edge-cases" here but there's stuff in the expanded-rules-document like a short one-paragraph-explanation for how to handle "climbing" or there's stuff like how to handle "sneaking" and "tracking" and how to handle "feats of strength" and modifiers for social actions. All of that isn't gonna be in W5. And without the expanded rules, the ST is just expected to improvise all of that, I guess.

Even if you want to somehow make this about "narrative engagement" where worrying about specific rules for rolling what when is less important... I think it's hard to get past the fact that you still have a char-sheet with nine Attributes, 27 Skills, Backgrounds, Merits, Flaws, special splat-specific powers and equipment on them. If the ST is just asked to wing it in a "narrative engagement" non-"simulationist" manner, why should any player have to pick points for their skills from among 27 options, for example?

                                                And the same with combat... I mean, prioritizing "narrative engagement" by making it simple and short but at the end of the day, it's built on a system with health-tracks and damage-types. But what the new system is going for is "Let's make a couple rolls, see who comes out on top and then let's just narrate what seems fitting." So why even bother with the detail of damage-types?

                                                Story-first games express "narrative engagement" by focusing on what's supposed to be the center of the story, With PbtA you have the specific flavors of moves each game has or with Blades In The Dark you have stuff like its loadout-system and the simple separation of downtime/mission. I don't think you get to claim a focus on "narrative engagement" by taking a simulationist system and then simply cutting out parts from it so using it is more of an improv-game.
