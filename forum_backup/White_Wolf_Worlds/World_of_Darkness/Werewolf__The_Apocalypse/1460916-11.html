                The way language exchange work in WtA is also just another one of those massive clusters of trying to fix bad ideas from 1e.

                Like, the Garou have apparently been using the word ronin for thousands of years before it developed in Japanese culture. So the Japanese clearly got it from them. Though, of course, this is nonsense because the authors just liked the word ronin and shoved it in to a lexicon that's mostly a mashup of Gaulish, Germanic, and Romance languages with some sprinkling from nearby languages like Greek (though that's normal for Romance languages anyway).

                Unfortunately they never retconed that, even while pivoting strongly towards the idea that Garou High Tongue has dialects and the common modern lexicon is largely a product of the Fianna and Silver Fangs, with High Tongue loan words from other dialects that - like ronin - capture a meaning more elegantly. But the books haven't really explored other High Tongue dialects in any real detail (including Native American despite the isolation of the Three Brothers and having multiple books where discussing such matters would have been a useful bit of world building), and their existence clearly backs up the idea that Garou culture ends up borrowing from human culture (as the source for these dialectical differences is based on human divisions). But we never got any real coherent way for human culture to incorporate Garou language elements. Kin can't actually learn High Tongue (at least not without being a Kami - which is a really strange word for the authors to use - or some other supernatural that's going to set them apart from humanity too). When Garou are conversing in High Tongue, and they translate any important messages to their kin, it's going to be into the local language. Irish kinfolk in the 1000s didn't know the word ronin, they knew whatever Irish phrase their Fianna relatives translated the High Tongue word that we translate as ronin now.

                                There's a fairly massive difference between, "I'm an Arcanum scholar that's studied werewolves extensively and I've begun to understand there's a global werewolf culture with subcultures that have distinct names and identities with in it," and, "As a normal human being [unknowingly living in a universe called the World of Darkness], and for some reason the idea of red talons feels really wrong to me, and when I talk to people they all seem to agree, but nobody knows why exactly."

                                Because the second one is what's actually being discussed. Where Garou language has penetrated into human culture, not whether or not the rare outsider humans that study the Garou can figure out stuff if they're lucky enough to not get caught by the Garou and killed.

                                The problem with ronin is that we know when it became a thing in Japanese culture: the 700s and the rise of a form of feudalism. Before it's most commonly used meaning now, it meant anyone that wasn't part of that system (so a serf farmer who's farm was destroyed and became a beggar would be a ronin for no longer serving as a serf to a local lord, not just the idea of masterless samurai). The books however, to explain the presence of ronin in High Tongue, did claim that the Garou invented it first, and implied that somehow the Japanese got it from them; not some incidental parallel process.

                                However, this reasoning as never widely applied because the authors didn't feel the need to explain the presence of English and English adjacent words in the standard High Tongue Lexicon, since the impression there is that they're only English with archaic twists and some borrow word from languages English borrows from heavily as a translation convention.

                                So the books created the idea that Garou words have filtered into human use, but never explains how or why or how many. It also maintains that the High Tongue is incoherent to humans, and that the words we use are translations of it, not the true words used in that language. This creates an incoherent setting element.

Note that the Garou and Fera view of the stars differs substantially from the human one, because they were visiting the Aetherial Realm for tens of thousands of years and never told any human shit about the planets that humans missed prior to the invention of powerful telescopes. Any bits that get out aren't significant cultural knowledge, they're stray bits that get the Bob Schnoblin treatment, especially after humans started fitting everything into their own religions, and even more so if or when said religions started to centralize heavily and clamp down on anything that went against a central authority.
