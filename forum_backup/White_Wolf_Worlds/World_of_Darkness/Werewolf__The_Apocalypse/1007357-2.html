                                                    For a neat interpretation of "Stepping Sideways" where you exist in a parallel world where you can still see what's happening on the other side?

                                                    Watch The Fight Between Goku, Vegeta and Metal Cooler in "Dragonball Z: Cooler's Revenge" Movie Special

                                                    One particular moment, where Cooler (the villain) uses Instant-Transmission (Ie. Teleport) and leaps into a parallel dimension, completely tinted in eerie shifting red colors, to deliver a surprise killing blow on a just-knocked-down Vegeta.

                                                    Now, from the Audience's Point of View, Cooler is standing over the corresponding spot in our dimension where the unconscious Vegeta is lying, but Vegeta is in FULL COLOR as opposed to red-tint because from Cooler's point of view, he is looking into OUR current dimension from the paralell realm.

                                                    Goku now rushes into the teleportation realm (becoming red tinted as well) to intercept the low-blow, and both combatants shift back in full color as the former pulls the latter back into the current dimension.

                                                    It's a brief 10 second moment, but an excellent reference to what "Stepping Sideways" would look like to one who has never played "Werewolf" but an avid Dragonball Z fan.

                                                        Given the prevalence of Native American/First Nation things in Werewolf, I find the following interesting and somewhat informative.

                                                        Jim Northrup's Res Road Follies, Anishinaabe Syndicated, and Rez Salute; collections of his newspaper columns and stories about Native American life and issues in the western Great Lakes area.

                                                        William Saunders's Are We Having Fun Yet?, a collection of the Cherokee writer's short stories, as well as his non-fiction work, Conquest: Hernando de Soto and the Indians, 1539 - 1543.

                                                        Fergus M. Bordewich's Killing the White Man's Indian, from 20 years ago, but a look at modern Native American issues and politics and how they really don't conform to the overly romanticized images and stereotypes held by most outsiders.

                                                        Tony Hillerman's Leaphorn & Chee novels, about two members of the Navajo Tribal Police, covering a period of about 30 years, often going into details about the cultures, beliefs and histories of the Navajo and their neighboring tribes.

                                                        S. C. Gwynne's Empire of the Summer Moon, a very readable history of the Comanche and their 19th century empire in the western United States.

                                                        (Saddly, both Hillerman and Northrup have passed away in the past few years.)
