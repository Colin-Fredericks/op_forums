            The Textile Industry

            So, one of the backbones of civilization is the creation of cloth, and the shift from traditional homespun to mechanical weaving was one of the first phases of the industrial revolution.

            Most textiles start as plants. Cotton and flax are the most common used for clothing fabric. Sven Beckert's Empire of Cotton is a good history of the global cotton trade and how it's industrialization helped drive a lot of the development of modern industry. Stephen Yafa's Cotton: The Biography of a Revolutionary Fiber also gives a good history of the subject and some of it's more curious stories. There is also Giorgio Riello's Cotton: The Fabric that Made the Modern World. Flax is the source for linen, and oddly doesn't have many books written about it or it's industry. Linda Heinrich's Linen from Flax Seed to Woven Cloth is a text book on the subject, and is sort of pricey for casual use.

            Animal based textiles are the other book one, being various types of wool and, of course, silk. Strangely, about the only book I can find on the actual history of silk and it's manufacturing is one by Rajat Datta and Mahesh Nanavaty entitled Global Silk Industry: A Complete Sourcebook. It seems a little skewed toward the technical reader rather than the casual one. Peggy Hart's Wool: Unraveling an American Story of Artisans and Innovation is a look at the history of wool in the US.

            Animal hides also have a place in clothing manufacturing, though they aren't technically textiles. Leather and tanning are also short on casual histories. However, from a PENTEX/Werewolf perspective, you can undoubtably find various inspirations for horror and tragedy from groups like PETA. But for those running a Rage Across New York chronicle, Barbara McMartin's Hides, Hemlock and Adirondack History is a look at the tanning industry in upstate New York.

            As far as the actual industrialization and manufacturing goes, Fiona McDonald's Textiles: A History is a good overview of the topic. Beverly Gordon's Textiles: The Whole Story is a college textbook, but covers the subject from the Stone Age to the 21st century, making it useful as a historical resource (especially for chronicles set in Ancient Rome or the Dark Ages). In the US, the textile mills were a big part of New England's history. Paul Rivard's The New Order of Things: How the Textile Industry Transformed New England, William Moran's The Belles of New England: The Women of New England and the Families Whose Wealth they Wove, and Steve Dunwell's Run of the Mill are all good treatments of the subject. After the American Civil War, the textile industry moved into the South, especially the Carolinas and Georgia. Brent D. Glass's The Textile Industry in North Carolina: A History is about 30 years old at this point, but does give a good account of the subject. The Images of America series has photo books on the West Georgia Textile Heritage Trail and Central Georgia Textile Mills. (Images of America has a lot of really fascinating photo books about various regions, towns and industries which offer great visual inspiration for historical chronicles or anything set in the Underworld.) There's also an Images of England series which includes books on their textile industry, such as the Burnley Cotton Mills. Jennifer Tann's Wool & Water: The Gloucestershire's Wollen Industry and it's Mills is another college text book, but does give a look at the woolen industry in one part of the United Kingdom. There's a British tv series, The Mill, about children working in 1830s British mills, which I've unfortunately never seen. There's a companion book, Children of the Mill, by David Hanson, which looks at the real life stories which helped inspire the series.

            Fictionwise, I've mentioned Stephen King's short stories "The Graveyard Shift" before. It involves a crew cleaning the basements of an old New England mill and the monsters they find down there. It was made into a rather poor film adaption. I think I also mentioned that a large number of these old, closed down New England textile mills are effectively toxic waste dumps and listed as EPA superfund clean up sites. This makes them the sort of places that could easily breed fomori or become hives for Black Spiral Dancers. Tangentially, one of the Giovanni vampire clan's families, the Milliners, are from New England, and their name could easily imply a long standing connection with mills in both New England and England. It could easily be where the family's initial fortune came from, as well possible ghost slaves/servents created from those killed in mill accidents or workhouse fires. It does offer a potential angle to lead into a crossover.

            Textiles are currently a big industry in Asia, especially China, Bangladesh, India and Pakistan. This makes such companies a potential antagonist/problem/social issue for any games involving the Beast Courts or the Garou and Fera of South Asia. Such places likely have much worse pollution and and worker safety issues than would be found at places in North America or Europe.

                The Clothing Industry

                Coming out of the textile world is that of clothing and apparel. This is a pretty big area. So I'll start with the idea of modern cheap apparel and it's impact on society and environment, as that's the most relevant to Werewolf. A number of books have been written on this subject in the past few years. Regarding the global clothing industry, there is Kelsey TImmerman's Where Am I Wearing: A Global Tour to the Countries, Factories and People The Make Our Clothes, and Pietra Rivoli's The Travels of a T-Shirt in the Global Economy.
                Elizabeth Cline's Overdressed: The Shockingly High Cost of Cheap Fashion, Lucy Siegle's To Die For: Is Fashion Wearing Out the World?, and Andrew Brooks's Clothing Poverty: The Hidden World of Fast Fashion & Second Hand Clothes, focus on the impact of modern cheep clothing.
                Dana Thomas's Deluxe: How Luxury Lost its Luster, Clare Press's Wardrobe Crisis: How We Went from Sunday Best to Fast Fashion, and Teri Agins's The End of Fashion: How Marketing Changed the Clothing Business Forever examine the impact of modern business on fashion and clothing.
                Greta Eagan's Wear No Evil: How to Change the World with Your Wardrobe is a guide to making your clothing choices have a little negative social and environmental impact as possible.
                Film wise, The True Cost (2015) and China Blue (2005) are documentaries looking at the clothing industry, especially in the 3rd world.

                Historically, I'll mention the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory Fire, one of the more infamous industrial disasters in US history. David von Drehle's Triangle: The Fire That Changed America is a good overview of the event. Margaret Peterson Haddix's Uprising is a pretty good fictional account of the factory and the disaster from the points of view of three different fictional women. If you're running a game set in New York City, the site likely has strong spiritual significance, both in the Umbra and the Underworld, and can serve as a potential historical backdrop. More recently is the 2013 building collapse in Savar, Bangladesh, which killed over a thousand people, many of them garment workers.

                Fashion design is another big part of the industry. Fashion houses are usually based around their head designer, people like Christian Dior, Tommy Hilfinger, or Vera Wang. They have traditionally specialized in haute couture, or custom made clothing for elite clients, rather than mass market apparel. However, over the past few decades, there's been more and more "ready to wear" apparel, lines of clothing designed by major designers intended for mass manufacture. The world of haute couture is more in the realm of Vampire or even Changeling than Werewolf, but one could easily image a PENTEX connected fashion house run by a vampire or some other immortal creature. Imogen Edwards-Jones's Fashion Babylon is a semi-fictional look at a London fashion house. (She's actually done a series of Babylon books about various industries, including beach resorts, hotels, weddings and others, all of which make for good PENTEX reading.) The film Ready To Wear/Pret-a-Porter (1994) is a fun satire of the industry and fashion shows. Gill Stark's The Fashion Show: History, Theory & Practice is a good overview of fashion shows and how things work behind the scenes. The soap opera The Bold & The Beautiful (1987-present) focuses on the Forrester Family and their fashion house in LA. Glamorama is a good and fairly savage satire of the New York fashion scene in the 1990s, written by Brett Easton Ellis (of American Psycho and Less Than Zero fame).

                Finally, and something very fitting for Werewolf and the World of Darkness, is Airica D. Kraehmer's Models Stop Traffic, a novel about the fashion industry as seen through the eyes of an aspiring model who falls victim to the human trafficking industry.

                Added: I forgot to mention William Gibson's Blue Ant trilogy (Pattern Recognition, Spook Country, and Zero History). While these are really much more of a Mage related thing (as the bulk of Gibson's work tends to be), part of the subplot running through them, especially the final book, involve a sort of underground fashion company, called Gabriel Hounds (another name for hell hounds), which makes high quality clothing as a "secret brand" (one that doesn't advertise or even label their works). It's the sort of thing that is very fitting for the World of Darkness, especially for the likes of werewolves, mages and changelings, who tend to shun major mass market brands for various metaphysical reasons. I could easily see a sept of Glasswalkers or others (Ananasi, Kitsune, kinfolk, etc.) doing such a thing.

                Also, regarding the world of modeling, I've been told that the film Broken Side of Time (2013) is pretty good, in a very arty sort of way, but have never had the chance to see it. (It's currently on Hulu, I think.) The Neon Demon (2016) is another I keep meaning to watch as well. There's also a 1995 documentary, Catwalk.

                    Addendum: Shoes

                    One of the most ubiquitous items of mass clothing in the past 50 years is the sneaker or athletic shoe. They're a big business, have become a major part of pop culture, and have the same issues of third world sweat shop labor as most other clothing types. So, I've always suspected that PENTEX would have a shoe company. Reebok, Adidas, Sketchers, Converse, Nike and others are all major brands and can provide possible ideas.

                    Although 20 years old at this point, Tom Vanderbilt's The Sneaker Book: Anatomy of an Industry and an Icon gives a good overview of the history, manufacturing, economics and cultural impact of sneakers up to the late 90s. More recent is Elizabeth Semmelhack's Out of the Box: The Rise of Sneaker Culture is the companion book from a recent traveling museum/art exhibition, and though a bit pricey, is a really good look at the subject from the curator of Toronto's Bata Shoe Museum. Rodrigo Corral, Alex French and Howie Kahn's Sneakers is also good. Sneakerheadz (2015) is a documentary on sneakers as a cultural obsession, including people getting murdered for their shoes. Kicks (2016) is another of those films that's gotten a lot of good reviews, but which I've sadly never had time to see.

                    Historically, Barbara Smit's Sneaker War, about the two brothers who founded Adidas and Puma and the bitter rivalry between the two, makes for interesting reading and can offer ideas for the founding of a PENTEX sneaker company.

                    On the Beast Courts front, Manami Okazaki and Geoffrey Johnson's Kicks Japan: Japanese Sneaker Culture is an interesting look at the subculture in Japan as well as about the numerous designers that live there.

                            KING BREWERIES AND DISTILLERS

                            (Finally.)

                            The alcoholic beverage industry is a really huge one, with it's various facets having their own long and interesting histories. For a brief overview, Modern Marvels has episodes devoted to Brewing (9:40 and 12:59), Wine (13:54), Distilleries (11:27 and 13:48), Whiskey (15:07), and even Saloons (8:23). These aren't really in depth, but do give a decent history and engineering of the subject, and some of the trends in the industry (such as they were a decade or so ago). Also, the documentary Beer Wars (2009).

                            In the world of beer, the single biggest brewer and manufacturer in the world is Anheuser-Busch InBev, created when InBev (it self a merger of Belgian's Interbrew and Brazil's AmBev) was able to launch a semi-hostile take over fo America's Anheuser-Busch. (They've also since acquired Britain's SABMiller.) If you're interested in this take over and might want to look at ways such events might play out among King and other PENTEX companies, Julie MacIntosh's Dethroning the King: The Hostile Takeover of Anheuser-Busch is a really good coverage of the event, as well as some of the backdrop of the 2008 financial crisis during which it took place.
                            Regarding Anheuser-Busch itself, the company was founded in St. Louis in the 1850s, and until 2009, was owned by the Busch family. William Knoedelseder's Bitter Brew: The Rise and Fall of Anheuser-Busch is a really interesting account of the family in all their glorious disfunction. Peter Hernon and Terry Ganey's Under the Influence is another good account of the dynasty. Both offer a lot of potential skeletons in the King family closet, as well as possible seeds for historical games from Werewolf: The Wild West to the modern day which would involve King and PENTEX.
                            From an environmental standpoint, Anheuser-Busch actually has a fairly decent track record for a company of their size. The semi-exception is the amount of CO2 given off by the brewing process. Not that they weren't/aren't without controversy. Their 2015 "#Up for Whatever" ad campaign was criticized for promoting rape. (King's version of this campaign was undoubtably completely intentional and even more blatant about it - "King: Anything Goes!" and "No One Says No to a King!") Then there was the short lived Spykes malt liquor brand, which came in tiny bottles with fruit and chocolate flavors, which was, as you can probably guess, criticized as being targeted towards children. They also own SeaWorld theme parks, and so are connected to all the criticism directed toward that business as well.
                            ABInBev has collected it's own share of scandals. This includes accusations of buying beer distributors just to prevent them from distributing competitors' products, partial ownership of beer rating sites that rate their own beers, attempting to buy the entire South African hops crop to prevent other brewers from getting any of it, and trying to silence whistleblowers. So, the usual PENTEX shenanigans.

                            The other major American brewer, and clearly the biggest influence on King (likely because of the conservative political views of late former CEO Joseph Coors), is Coors Brewing Company, based out of Golden, Colorado since the 1870s. They are now part of Molson Coors Brewing Company. Dan Baum's Citizen Coors is a good biography of the family, and by extension the company. Also of note is Phillip Jett's The Death of an Heir is about the kidnapping and murder of company CEO Adolph Coors III in 1960. Molson Coors is also the current owner of Miller Brewing Company, out of Milwaukee Wisconsin. Tim John's The Miller Beer Barons is a biography of the family and some of their more interesting members. (For those running a Milwaukee by Night chronicle, it can offer some interesting historical flavor.)

                            Mircobrews/craft beers are a big thing now and have been for the past few decades, and the big brewery companies have been eager to jump on that wagon where they can. Josh Noel's Barrel-Aged Stout and Selling Out: Goose Island, Anheuser-Busch and How Craft Beer Became Big Business is about how Chicago's Goose Island brew pub eventually sold out to AB and the impact this had on both the industry and beer culture. Elsewhere, Ken Grossman's Beyond the Pale is an autobiography of how he started Sierra Nevada Brewing Co.

                            On the fictional front, the film Strange Brew (1983) is a rather weird comedy (and honestly upper midwest/Canadian humor of that period is not really my thing), but the basic plot of Max Von Sydow as an evil brewmaster planning world conquest via mind control drugs in beer which render the customers docile until activated into a violent frenzy by special signals is the sort of thing you can get a lot of mileage out of in a Werewolf game. On a smaller scale, see the "Beer Bad" (4:5) of Buffy: The Vampire Slayer. On the more horrific side, I highly recommend Stephen King's 1973 short story "Grey Matter" (part of the Night Shift anthology), about tainted beer. It's very Lovecraftian, but not the part of Lovecraft most people think of. (The Shining is also good, as it deals heavily with the themes of alcoholism and its impact on families.)

                            The wine industry tends to be separate from the beer industry, but King has it's Dragon Valley Wines. Kevin Bagos's Tasting the Past: The Science of Flavor and the Search for the Origins of Wine is a really fascinating look at both the science behind wine and it's somewhat myth filled history. James Conoway's Napa: The Story of an American Eden is a history of California's Napa Valley and it's various wine empires. Falcon Crest (1981-1990) was a prime time soap opera about a feuding extended wine making dynasty and their corrupt, tyrannical matriarch. Bottle Shock (2008) is about the 1976 "Judgment of Paris", in which French judges picked Napa Valley wine over French wines in a blind taste test.

                            When dealing with wine, one can't overlook Dionysus, the Greek God of Wine (among other things) and his Roman counterpart Bacchus. The YouTube channel Overly Sarcastic Productions has a great video on the rather weird history of the god. Andrew Dalby's Bacchus: A Biography is also pretty good. (Dionysus will apparently be the final book of George O'Conner's incredibly excellent Olympians series, and therefore probably won't be out until 2020 unfortunately.) The idea of a dark, chaotic and even evil version of the god makes for an interesting idea for anything horror related that involves wine or wineries. Ernest G. Henham's The Feast of Bacchus, while set in turn of the century England, has a lot of interesting ideas that could be transplanted to California or elsewhere. Even better is Bentley Little's Dominion.

                            Finally, there's the world of hard liquor and spirits. Victorino Matus's Vodka, Charles Coulombe's Rum, Wayne Curtis's And a Bottle of Rum, Olivia Williams's Gin Glorious Gin, Richard Barnett's The Book of Gin, Reid Mitenbuiler's Bourbon Empire, Fred Minick's Whiskey Women, Ana Valenzuela-Zapata and Gary Nabhan's Tequila: A Natural and Cultural History, and Maria Sarita Gaytan's Tequila: Distilling the Spirit of Mexico are all good reads about various spirits.

                            Finally, Adam Rogers's Proof: The Science fo Booze, and Amy Stewart's The Drunken Botanist are both interesting and fun overviews of alcohol in general.

                                CONSOLIDEX WORLDWIDE

                                Randolph Duke: Now, some of our clients are speculating that the price of gold will rise in the future; And we have other clients who are speculating that the price of gold will fall. They place their orders with us, and we buy – or sell – their gold for them.
                                Mortimer Duke: Tell him the good part.
                                Randolph: The good part, William, is that, no matter whether our clients make money or lose money, Duke & Duke get the commissions.
                                Mortimer: Well, what do you think, Valentine?
                                Billy Ray Valentine: Sounds to me like you guys a couple of bookies.
                                Randolph: I told you he’d understand.
                                • Trading Places (1983)

                                A brokerage firm who deal in financial securities, which can include corporate stocks, bank notes, debts, commodities, and a number of other things. That’s the bare bones of what they are. The actual ins and outs of financial trading is almost mind-numbing with all of it’s variations, special rules and loopholes. (It resembles both the law and government bureaucracy in this way, and if one were extremely cynical, they might suspect that all three fields have been designed that way in order to make them impenetrable to outsiders and preserve the special status of those on the inside.) Hence, people hire brokers to help invest their money for them. In the case of Consolidex, that money, more often than not, gets invested in companies owned by PENTEX. That’s the way the company primarily serves the Wyrm. (There are a few others, but I’ll get into them a little later.)

                                For those wanting to get a better understanding of the basics of financial trading, the ADAMS 101 book series has a pair of books on Investing and the Stock Market that are a pretty good introduction. (They’ve also got ones on Economics, Day Trading, Accounting and many other subjects, so you can case that particular trail as far as your heart desires.) There’s also books like Joel Kurtzman’s How the Markets Really Work, Kirk Meyer’s How the Stock Market Operates, and Even Houpt & John Border’s Stock Market for Beginners Book. Related would be DK’s How Money Works and Oxford University Press’s Banking: A Very Short Introduction. (The Very Short Introduction series also has a huge number of books on various economic and other subjects.) And finally, John Kay’s Other People’s Money: The Real Business of Finance, which is a really good overview of the history of the financial sector, how it’s recovered from the 2008 crisis, and a lot of the problems it had and still has.

                                The financial market isn’t exactly a new thing (though I sort of suspect that Consolidex, at least in it’s current form, probably dates back to the 80s or 90s). If you’re interested in it’s history, Edward Chancellor’s Devil Take the Hindmost: A History of Financial Speculation is a good read, going back as far as the Roman Empire and looking at how the modern markets developed. I’m personally a big fan of Niall Ferguson’s work, which includes The Ascent of Money and The House of Rothschild (about what is probably the single wealthiest family dynasty in world history). Ron Chernow’s The House of Morgan is a good biography of JP Morgan and his dynasty’s place in the development of American banking and finance. Liaquat Ahamed’s Lords of Finance is about the bankers who helped start the Great Depression.

                                The 1980s were when the stock market became “a thing” and the obnoxiously greedy yuppie stock broker became one of the faces of the decade. Bryan Burrough & John Helyar’s Barbarians at the Gate is a classic work of non-fiction about Wall Street in the 1980s. There’s a pretty good 1993 movie adaption of it. Also, and especially fitting for a PENTEX brokerage firm, is Brett Easton Ellis’s American Psycho, as well as it’s 2000 movie version. Other People’s Money (1991) is about buying up companies just to liquidate their assets. Michael Lewis’s Liar’s Poker is a good non-fiction book. And while I’m not really a fan of Oliver Stone’s work, the movie Wall Street (1987) isn’t bad. One the other hand, Trading Places (1983) has long been one of my favorite films, not least of which for Don Ameche and Ralph Bellamy as the Dukes. They’ve long been one of my role models for wealthy manipulative puppet masters, partially because their motivations are so petty.
                                As we start to drift into the 1990s, the big one is Jordan Belfort’s autobiography, The Wolf of Wall Street and it’s 2013 movie adaption. There’s also 2000’s The Boiler Room, which I’ve never seen, but it’s subject of crooked brokerage companies and stock schemes is very appropriate. Overseas, there’s Nick Leeson’s autobiography Rogue Trader, about how he more or less single handedly destroyed Barrings Bank. There’s a 1999 movie adaption if it as well.
                                In the 21st century, there’s an almost overwhelming number of books about the financial sector and it’s recent history. Just to hit a few of the high points, Bethany McLean & Peter Elkind’s The Smartest Guys in the Room (and it’s 2005 film version), Roger Lowenstein’s When Genius Failed, Andrew Sorkin’s Too Big To Fail, Michael Lewis’s The Big Short (and it’s 2015 film version) and Flash Boys, Scott Patterson’s The Quants, Scott Wapner’s When Wolves Bite, the movie Margin Call (2011) and the documentary Inside Job (2010).

                                Regarding the more criminal aspects of finance and Wall Street, Bastian Obermayer & Frederik Obermaier’s The Panama Papers revealed the underground networks in which the super wealthy hide their money. Sheelah Kolhatkar’s Black Edge looks at insider trading. Dark Pools is about underground trading networks and automated trading bots (which in the WoD could easily be spirit-based “AIs”). A number of the material above also deals with various financial schemes, especially the practice of "pump & dump" (hyping a stock - usually a bad one - just to inflate the price so you can unload it for profit). Odds are that Consolidex involves itself in all of these things.

                                Finally, for a bit of cheese, I offer the werewolf film Wolves of Wall Street (2002).

                                A lot of this information is equally suitable for aspects of the Glass Walkers (especially the Corporate Wolves camp), and for Mage’s Syndicate (and parts of the Virtual Adepts), as well as Vampire games involving Wall Street or high finance.
                                Obviously, most of this focuses on the American financial markets, but other countries have their own, including massive ones in Europe and Asia. If anyone from around the globe can offer any tips or information on their nation’s markets, I’d be interested in seeing it.

                                Added: I forgot to mention the film Pi (1998), which is a very good film involving attempts to mathematically predict the stock market, Hebrew Gematria/numerology, and Wall Street conspiracies.

                                            BRADFORD INCORPORATED

                                            The aim of public education is not to spread enlightenment at all; it is simply to reduce as many individuals as possible to the same safe level, to breed and train a standardized citizenry, to put down dissent and originality.
                                            - HL Mencken

                                            So, I've talked a little previously about private schools, but Bradford (a minor company I admit I forgot about until I went back and found them in the revised Storytellers Handbook) is built to profit off of the public education system. Generally speaking, Bradford promises that they'll produce results in the form of better scores on the standardized tests. And they do. It's just that in the process, they exacerbate the already existing problems with public education - that it's more designed to produce cogs in a machine rather than people who can actually think for themselves. (There is a school of thought that this has actually always been the true purpose of modern public education in the West since it's creation in 19th century Prussia; to make better workers for the State while curtailing the liberal thought of the Enlightenment, and ensuring that the children of the masses never seriously compete with he children of the elite who go to private academies. If you believe this, then it serves PENTEX's needs perfectly well.)

                                            John Taylor Gatto's Dumbing Us Down is a collection of essays about the problems with the modern public school system. Gatto is a 30 year veteran of the New York City public school system and winner of New York state's Teacher of the Year award. He's written a number of books, including Weapons of Mass Instruction, A Different Kind of Teacher, and An Underground History of American Education.

                                            Samual Blumenfeld and Alex Newman's Crimes of the Educators is a look at the history of American public schooling as a vehicle for progressive technocratic social engineering. Paolo Lioni's The Leipzig Connection also looks at John Dewey, BF Skinner, and their influence by Bismarck's Prussian education system.

                                            While not primarily about the education system, James Loewen's Lies My Teacher Told Me is an interesting overview of certain aspects of American history, and I mention it because it includes a discussion about how textbooks tend to be written and selected by education boards, and why this process results in such bad text books, especially in history/social studies.

                                            In the realm of fiction, the film Disturbing Behavior (1998) is a riff on the classic Stepford Wives concept, in which teenagers are being reprogramed into ideal students. While not a great film, it's the sort of thing that works really well for PENTEX, especially in that it offers the idea of a horde of attackers - brainwashed children - that Garou may be unwilling to just slaughter the way they would standard fomori. The Faculty (1998) presents a similar idea, just with a totally different cause.

                                            There used to be a short lived genre trope/cliche of schools in which one or more of the faculty ended up being the mastermind behind a youth gang (usually selling drugs or some other enterprise). The Substitute (1996) is kind of cheesy, but remains a guilty pleasure of mine. It's a decent example of the concept, which employees from Bradford might well be engaged in.

                                            Added: I mentioned textbooks, so I figure it's worth going into that subject a little. There are basically five major producers of educational books and materials in the US. These are CEngage Learning, Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, McGraw-Hill Education, Pearson Education, and Scholastic. Besides textbooks, these companies make and market reference materials, educational software, standardized tests and other assessment materials (including career aptitude tests), children's fiction and nonfiction books (Scholastic publishes Harry Potter and The Hunger Games), workbooks, mobile apps, instructor materials, reading clubs, educational videos, and more. This makes for a lot of potential synergy with Vesuvius, Sunburst/Tellus, and OMNI.
                                            The most obvious avenue for shenanigans is the subtle (or sometimes not so subtle) skewing of material to fit PENTEX's narrative and shaping students into consumers of subsidiary products. Also, through use of tests and aptitude assessments, they can find potential future employees and/or fomori subjects. In addition, the use of Good House, Rainbow and other companies materials enables potential tainting of products (including OMNI and Sunburst's subliminal messaging). It might also be possible to subtly derail and ruin the lives of students via tests with wrong answer keys, deliberate failings and other forms of sabotage.

                                            As an aside, I have this mental image of a PENTEX version of the old Oregon Trail game where players were encouraged to over-hunt all the wildlife and to kill as many natives as possible. Perhaps with secret cheats that enable Donner Party style cannibalism or sacrificing one or more of the party to an ancient Wyrm demon to ensure a successful journey.

                                                    THE MUSIC INDUSTRY

                                                    Again, this is one of those things I've always been a little surprised wasn't in the original list of PENTEX Subsidiaries. It's a large and profitable industry, one with a lot of cultural impact, and a frequent subject of scandals, conspiracy theories and fictional portrayals. Currently, the "big three" are Sony Music Entertainment (Japan), Universal Music Group (France), and Warner Music Group (USA). One of the best and easy to read overviews of the industry I've read is Gareth Murphy's Cowboys and Indies, which starts with the very invention of "talking machines" in the Victorian Era and goes up to the present decade, covering advances in technology, the development of important labels, and the "record men" who discovered and developed major artists. Also interesting is Greg Kot's Ripped: How the Wired Generation Revolutionized Music, looking at how the internet completely changed the industry and how artists and labels were adapting to it. (It was written almost ten years ago, but still a good look at the subject.) And Bobby Borg (formerly of the band Warrant) has written The Musician's Handbook is a guide to the industry for new artists, helping them understand the ins and outs of how the business works.

                                                    There's a large number of documentaries about the industry and various musicians. Before the Music Dies (2006), about the increasing commercialization and superficiality of mainstream pop music, is one fo the more suitable when talking about PENTEX products.

                                                    I've generally always played the World of Darkness as one where the "Gothic-Punk" concept carries over into popular culture. By this I mean that in the world of entertainment, mainstream pop culture, including music, tends to be this big corporate thing that looms over and oppresses artistic creativity, while you have an ongoing counterculture of independents rebelling against that system. So you have a lot of boy bands and pop tarts augmented with autotunes. I think a good template for how a PENTEX record company would manufacture such talent is the system used by Asian J-Pop and K-Pop industries. Euny Hong's The Birth of Korean Cool is a really interesting look at pop culture in South Korea, and includes a chapter on the K-Pop industry, touching on some of the scandals and abuses (including charges of sexual abuse by managers against artists).

                                                    Regarding potential misdoings, one of the big conspiracies attached to the industry is the use of back masking to spread subliminal messages promoting Satanism, suicide, drug use and other anti-social or self-destructive behavior. One of the major instances of this was the lawsuit against the band Judas Priest, blaming them for the suicide/attempted suicide of two Nevada teenagers in 1985. The documentary Dream Deceivers (1992) is a good (and surprisingly even handed) look at the trial. A much more humorous take on this is found in the movie Josie and the Pussycats (2001), in which the record company is using subliminal messaging to get listeners to shop, purchase and consume more. (One of the villains is Alan Cumming, the guy who plays Loki.)[Or not. See below.] And if you're looking for full on crazy, there's Mark Dice's Illuminati in the Music Industry.

                                                    On a more supernatural level, there is the long running urban legend about musicians selling their soul to the devil - or rather to the Dark Man at the Crossroads archetype - to achieve skill, success and stardom. This is usual attributed to blue musician Robert Johnson, but the story about him actually originates with a different musician, Tommy Johnson (no relation). I am a long time fan of the film Crossroads (1986), which is inspired by the story (and includes some great guitar work by Ry Cooder and Steve Vai). [Several decades ago, I read an anthology, Deals with the Devil, edited by Mike Resnick and Martin Greenburg, which included a story in which a guy makes a deal asking that he be able to play guitar "just like Eric Clapton", only to find that he can only play Clapton's songs. I am sadly unable to remember the author or title.] Personally, I've long had this idea of the devil promising would be musicians fame and immortality, only to have it come after their tragic early death. Chuck Klosterman's Killing Yourself to Live, which having nothing to do with the devil, is a really good look at tragic mortality in the music business. I highly recommend it to anyone with an interest in the subject.

                                                    Finally, I'll mention two shows. The first is Empire (2015- present), which I confess to never having watched, but have been told by people I trust that it's good and has a lot of wonderful politics and backstabbing, among other things. The other is the 80s cartoon series Jem (1985-1988), which, when it was at its best (usually the episodes written by series creator Christy Marx), was a interesting look at pop culture and the record industry in the mid 80s. Also, Eric Raymond is the kind of person who belongs at PENTEX. (Also, I think I am one of nine people on the entire planet who actually liked the 2015 film, because I am clearly weird.)

                                                    More later.

                                                    Added: I'd be slightly remiss in not mentioning the large number of musician biopics, as several of them do occasionally touch on the business side of the industry. Walk the Line (2005), Ray (2004), The Doors (1991), De-Lovely (2004), Great Balls of Fire (1989), Coal Miner's Daughter (1980), I Saw the Light (2015) [​This one stars Tom Hiddleston as Hank Williams, Sr.], The Runaways (2010), Selena (1997), and Straight Outta Compton (2015). Also, Who Do You Love (2008), one of the few films about a record producer (Leonard Chess of Chess Records). Cadillac Records (2008) is also about Chess Records. And then there is the completely fictional That Thing You Do (1996).

                                                    Added Pt 2: On the subject of back masking on music, there is a somewhat obscure and odd gem of 80s shlock weirdness, Trick or Treat (1886), involving a dead heavy metal singer who returns from the grave thanks to having his final record album played backwards. Also, an episode of the 1980s anthology series Amazing Stories (1985-1987) had a Halloween themed episode, "Go to the Head of the Class" (Season two, episode 8), involving the playing of a record backwards to cast black magic. So, yeah, this was kind of a thing in the 80s. (I actually like to use it as a way for some Mages of the period to hide their grimoires.)

                                                    Nov 15: A book I forgot about at the time, David Hajdu's Love for Sale: Pop Music in America, an interesting history of popular music going back to the late 19th century. There's a lot of cultural stuff that might be useful for historical games, in addition to providing a broad overview of music history.
