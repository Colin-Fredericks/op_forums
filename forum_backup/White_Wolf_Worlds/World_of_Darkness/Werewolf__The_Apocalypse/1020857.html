          My critique on the article, and the whole issue, is actually complex, but let me try it.

          You are completely, undeniably right on the matter of wolf behavior, and how that should affect a Lupus Garou. At most, I would add that the very Litany would be a fairly alien concept to them, and certainly as far from natural and inborn as it is for Homid Garou. The only Garou that should have contact with the Litany from early development in a regular basis are Metis. I agree with everything else, and do thing that wolf-born werewolves should start from this mindset, maybe developing later in a personal manner due to personal experiences, as any sapient being.

          That said, the problem is setting. First, the problem isn't with the players, but with the authors. Players don't see Talons that way because they are ignorant on actual wolves, but because the setting uses Talons, and to a far lesser degree every Lupus, that way precisely. Both the general Talon behavior and the tribe's outlook compound to that, are informed that way in the books, and had implications in setting because of that. Everything of the Talons in the setting derives from such. Second, because of such, to change the tribe to better mirror actual wolves, though theoretically in the spirit of the setting, would be very awkward without some heavy retcons.

          And finally, and you see that I present this as the factor that can explain the actual Talon portrait, make it a logical possibility, not as justifying said portrait as somehow better. But we should always remember that Talons aren't actual wolves. As werewolves, they're fairly intelligent beings from early on, and more susceptible to culture and to more complex cultural constructs. That means that the Talons, as a Tribe, aren't the way they are because they are wolves, but because they are, in a sense, humans. As sentient "human" beings, they can form any cultural concept or bias, including things like heavy prejudice, murderous hate, zealous religious beliefs, byzantine social structures and counterproductive whining behaviors.

          So, its a shame, but the fact remains that the Talons are what the Talons are, and the players think they disruptive for a good reason, unfortunately.

          Personally, though, I disagree with the Talons being a needed counterpoint to the Glass Walkers, though it is a compelling point. They are just "the Lupus tribe" to me, and could be fairly easily replaced by a better and more thorough use of the Lupus breed in other tribes. The GW get very close to this, also, but their approach on the Weaver still remains as a little more deserving as a thing in the game.

                  Granted, players are unusually able to take stereotyped visions about things and make everything ever worse. I saw players that do such with every tribe: Skin-head Fenris, Hysterical Furies, Backstabber Lords, Mad-eye Fangs, and so on, and so forth. You name it, someone found a disruptive way to do it.

                  But the game isn't innocent on that behavior. It is the game, with description and tone, that lays the ground for this behavior and its likely intensity and frequency. Disruptive Fenris are more common than disruptive Fangs, disruptive Uktena aren't as common as disruptive Wendigo, a seriously stereotyped Glass Walker isn't as disruptive as a seriously stereotyped Shadow Lord. And from all this, the Talons are the worst. They are the most common and most disruptive in a general basis (of course, groups and individuals can experience variations), and yes, the game is to blame, simply because it doesn't help.

                  Lets see, surely the game discourages strongly that any Talon goes against its superiors. Directly. But the Talons are frequently depicted as resentful from the pro-human view of most superiors. The tribe lives in the wild as much to stay with the Wyld as it is to avoid the other tribes and sects, so they don't have to deal with superiors they don't agree with and don't want to listen to. It is pretty childish to me. They are eager to blame the Silver Fangs on everything they do because "they were following the Alphas, as good wolves", but also resent and avoid them. The game puts the structure and religion of the Garou Nation on them, as alien as it is for true wolves, and them makes them among the most zealous about it. Of course, except when they don't want to, since they are as hypocritical as everyone else.

                  So, if there is a mistake in this disruptive interpretation of the Talons, it is because almost all Talons are either isolationists or passive-aggressive, or both, and the others are considered weird by their peers. Yes, a Talon can be incredibly distinct from this and be both well integrated into other septs and capable of dealing with humans, it isn't any overt crime for them. But it is considered weird and frowned upon. Again, passive-aggressive to the core. Packs are family. A passive-aggressive a***ole, or a depressive member who's being bullied by its own tribe, aren't that better to make things work (but a little, indeed).

                  And to make things better, there's Griffin. If this totem was truly about the wild, and living as an animal, I would say nothing. It goes, however, further in xenophobia and hate.

                  Yes, Talons could be better defined to be the counterpoint of GW. They wouldn't, though. Even the GW could have a better work, though they are in a far better shape.

                                          I don't like the MET wiki thing, but I think some of the critiques are lacking as well.

                                          One thing is that the "wolf behavior thing," is an issue, but at the same time Garou operate more like wolves in zoos than they do like wild wolves. A Garou pack isn't a wild wolf back. It isn't parents and a few generations of sibilings with maybe some aunts and uncles. A Garou pack is a bunch of individuals that are brought together without the continuity of family that makes wild wolf packs so different from what people used to think about wolf behavior. Lupus in general have to deal with a major disconnect between the idea of a pack that they grew up in, and the idea of a pack that the Garou live in. The structure of Garou hierarchy at all levels operates differently from normal wolves, and there's going to be a struggle dealing with it (and trouble for homids too).

                                          I also think there's a bit of uncharitable reading in insisting that the MET wiki article is trying to say that Talons are constantly physically fighting for dominance. The article in question mentions staredowns as well (keeping in mind that there are only three standard challenge types in the game: combat, staredown, and gamecraft). Staredowns should be very common for younger lupus still trying to figure out their place in the strange and unique thing that is Garou society; even if they're expecting to lose most of them. As well, wolves do use physical punishments frequently to correct behavior. Lupus are going to be very used to short bursts of violence as higher "ranking" individuals putting others back in line. As Garou they're a lot tougher than a normal wolf, so the idea that they'd be a bit more ready to be rough and tumble than homids makes perfect sense (and they'd likely be very confused by homids that insist on a more human/thoughtful approach to corrective measures).

                                          Of course, I think we all know that I'm not a fan of the Talons in general. My experiences with them being banned/restricted in online games or LARPs is a bit more expansive than either article really addresses. Part of it is the books themselves, but a lot of it is just that Red Talons that work well in a large Sept with most Tribes represented end up eating most/all of the lupus population and deny it to other players, while frequently either being too important or too inconsequential to what's going on.

                                                      In my perspective, Red Talons are constantly fighting a battle of Weaver (human) versus Wyld (wolf) within themselves, and it colors their perspective. Neither human nor wolf have the same battle and, in the case of wolves, survival matters more than existential crises. A wolf can except a human as equal and, from time to time, will adopt humans into the pack (early researchers into wolf behavior were often surprised with how accepting wolves could be of humans when the wolves determined they were no threat to the cubs). Red Talons lack the pragmatic flexibility of wolves because they reject anything that they associate with humans, but humans and wolves share many features, so they inevitably reject some of the Wolf as well.
