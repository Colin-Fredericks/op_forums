                    Since we're slowly necroing this thread, I'll chime in. The problem with "Grimgroth" is the weird mix of consonants that aren't that natural in English. "Grimgoth" would sound more natural. "Grimroth" also works. Even "Grimgroff" would be slightly better, though I think people would instinctively want to pronounce it "Grimgoth" anyway.

                    I tend to use name generators a lot these days, but each one can only be used a few times before it starts getting repetitive. I like looking at names from mythology or searching for names with thematic resonance.

                    For example, for an STV title I'm working on, covering the Tremere antitribu, I wanted to reintroduce an epic Dark Ages character as the new leader of House Goratrix. I eventually settled on "Abdelygma", adapted from the Greek "bdelygma", a descriptor for the Whore of Babylon, meaning "abomination". I really wanted to use "Kostroma", a fertility goddess whose name means "bonfire" (especially poignant given the antitribu's near-demise), but it's East Slavic and the character I had in mind is Romanian. But Biblical stuff works because the Romanians were Christian at that time, and elder Tremere would know classical languages anyway.

                    Another Spellbinder character is called Sébastien Péché, taken from his modelling days. He was an HIV+ artist's muse, posing for mythic and religious-themed portraits in the style of Pierre et Gilles. St. Sebastian was a common subject of Pierre et Gilles, and is often seen as a patron saint of gay men; "Péché" means "Sin". So he's "Sebastian of Sin", a kind of patron saint of queer martyrdom. He's now a Knight Inquisitor, so I've stuck him in Montreal, where he's dreaming about Metathiax (the disease demon) and has committed to destroying it. The name and the story fed into each other nicely and helped me really flesh him out.

                    For the Telyavs, I'm looking into Lithuanian gods to give me some ideas. I'm having a lot of fun. Then I'll have to do Mexican names for the main chantry.

                            Absolutely. It's definitely vibes-based. Though I think that's partly a convenience for OOC reasons (WW writers having fewer readily available tools for research back then). If you take Malgorzata, for example, her name is actually just the Polish version of Margaret. And while "Mal-" could come from her mortal name (Malana) and "-gor-" from Goratrix, I couldn't find an Eastern European goddess of dark magic whose name includes "-zata". Perhaps they meant Marzanna or perhaps Midnight Zorya, which would have made her Malgorzanna or Malgorzorya/Malgorya instead. My best guess is they came up with the name first, though, and then made up an etymology afterwards.

                            So you do have a lot of latitude. But I reason that anyone who was Embraced as one of the early Tremere would also be a huge geek, so if you can sneak extra layers of meaning into their name, it's even better. Or maybe that's just me, since I get overly excited by cool etymologies! 😂

                            Someone with more technical skill could probably program a name generator that mixes 2–4 syllables together in a vaguely convincing way to come up with these shadow names—there's already something like this for Exalted. In the meantime, I've had some decent results with this generator: https://www.scabard.com/world-buildi...name-generator.

                            I use the following prompt: "Mononymous, House Tremere-style names for Vampire: The Masquerade, mixing two to four syllables from mythically and mystically important names and words, rooted in classical and ancient languages (Latin, Greek, Aramaic, Hebrew, Celtic, etc), with gravitas. Avoid cutesy or on-the-nose names."

                            That gives some names that sound suitably epic enough. You may want to swap syllables around between the suggested words, or play around with swapping vowels and consonants, but it's good for inspiration.

                                        It's not that it's a made-up name that's the problem, I think. The name has nine letters in it and a whopping seven of them are consonants. "Grim-" and "-groth" are closed syllables, constricting and "pinching" the vowel sounds and emphasising the consonants at the end of each. It has a very harsh mix of phonemes that require awkward positioning in the mouth to pronounce. "-Groth" is also not a standard word or syllable in English, but it sounds like a mix of syllables that are more common.

                                        So, with "Grim-", you start at the back of your throat for a "g", roll it forward for an "r", move it up for an "i", and end on an "m", moving the sound to the front of your mouth as you close your lips. That's fairly simple and fluid on its own, but the "m" feels like that's where the name should end. That's why names like "Maugrim" or "Anngrim" feel quite satisfying. You can put "Grim-" at the start, but the syllables that follow are usually simpler and rounder, so the overall effect is less disjointed ("Grimaldo", "Grimild", "Grimshaw" or the very rare "Grimbald").

                                        But then with "-groth", you get a "spiky" consonant again. You have to quickly pull the sound to the back of your throat again, roll it forward again, but then drop the noise in your mouth and draw it forward for the "o" and slide your tongue forward for the "th". The spikiness puts the "m" and the "gr" at odds. Your brain also instinctively wants to repeat the first syllable again, but then gets diverted. It's a sort of uncanny valley effect, worsened by the clunky consonants and clipped vowels.

                                        Mechanically, the result is a juddering start-stop that is particularly counterintuitive on first pronunciation. You have to practice it in your head a few times to get it right. Compared with "Tremere", "Goratrix", "Etrius" or "Malgorzata", which alternate consonants and syllables in a more natural-sounding way, it's a clunky mess. "Grimroth" is more balanced. But even "Grimgoth" is easier on the ear, as you at least lose the uncanny valley thing ("-goth" is easier and more familiar than "-groth").
