      There are exceedingly few things in Mage that are a hard "no" in terms of what they can do. Time travel is, however, risky, difficulty, and so on, in a fashion that leads the majority of mages to avoid it. Basically, the game wants to keep time travel from taking over the game once one PC can do it; unless you basically want to go full on "lost in time" as a game premise. Since most mages would rather not explode from Paradox or get stuck blindly jumping around time hoping they can find where they started before they're stuck in a collapsing time-branch... most mages consider time travel a fairly "weapon of last resort" to do with hung/conditional spell to rewind a scene or something of the sort.

          The simple answer is yes, but it's probably the most perilous working a mage can try to perform.

          Aside from most forms of time travel raking in potentially enormous amounts of Paradox, it's a practice which attracts the attention of other people and entities (even aside from Paradox spirits) who are capable of sensing or detecting such anomalies in the tapestry, and in many cases that attention can make even most sorts of Paradox backlash seem trivial by comparison. A mage would do very well to remember that they are not the only one capable of meddling with time and the course of events, and that some of those other parties are powers which no sane willworker would ever want to come face to face with.

          A long time ago, I would routinely sic Wrinkle on characters who made use of Time effects I found troublesome or inconvenient to plots and storylines. I fully recognize how immature and petulant that was, and I still cringle a bit over it. That was also very sloppy thinking on my part, as I simply hadn't thought the matter through. Like "game breaking" uses of Correspondence, there are much more logical and reasonable ways of explaining why mages rarely try to meddle with history. I simply learned to think ahead and consider that events important to extremely powerful parties are inevitably guarded or watched over by those parties, in the same way that places important to those of sufficient power are warded/guarded/monitored against snooping by seers, and that need not involve any capricious use of Storyteller fait. Does this mean it's not possible for a mage to get away with that sort of intrusion? No, but it does mean such a mage is taking an enormous risk in some cases, and needs to tread very carefully. In some instances, it's just not worth that risk.

          This is largely anthropic reasoning. In theory, could a mage look back in time and try to discover the truth about Caine? Yes. Why hasn't anyone ever done it, or revealed what they discovered if they did? Think about it a moment. There are things out there which are going to take notice if someone even peeks into certain events, let alone puts themselves into a position to alter them. Depending on what one attracts the notice of, the results could range from troublesome to the sort of thing one ought not even whisper of. While there are ways of reducing the risk of that, there are some "places" the wise do not tread.

          That said, How Do You DO That contains some interesting advice on working with Time, including different sorts of time travel (there's more than one) and the possible benefits and consequences. As a bit of personal advice gained from long experience, characters being able to work with Time itself was very good incentive for me to develop skill at running non-linear storylines for Mage. Getting away from attempting to guide linear plots ironically saved me a countless headaches, once I got the hang of it.

              I see it this way. It's what you plan to do. If you want to prevent the Kennedy or Lincoln assassination, paradox will fry you hard. Everyone and everything will be transformed. Paradox will erase your very existence.

              However, there are other possibilities. Any mistakes while pursuing these possibilities will fry you as badly but it isn't a necessary outcome. It is a profoundly likely outcome, but not necessary.

              Say you go back to ancient Greece and gather up a few dozen high quality literary works that deal with the Greek myths and those of several nearby peoples whose mythologies were mainly lost. You place these scrolls and codices in a large jar which you seal up in a place that hasn't been touched by archeologists. If you play your cards right, you could bring forth an explosion of new interest in the culture of classical antiquity. Something any Hermetic would love to do.

              It would be a profoundly dangerous mission but, as it wouldn't change anyone's experience of the past, it shouldn't invoke paradox.

              Any trip to the past would have to be insanely careful not to change anyone's experience of the past. That's anyone no matter how obscure or unimportant to others. It is a desperately difficult standard. Which is why time travel is avoided.

                                      Immutable - you can't alter past events and if you try Paradox burns you badly
                                      Butterfly - past events are readily alterable but tend to have huge changes on the 'now'
                                      Deterministic - history tends to fall into a pattern, you can murder baby hitler but someone just as bad ends up filling the same space
                                      Timelines - any change in the past puts you onto a new timeline
                                      Multiverse - many different timelines exist at once and time travel is more like switching between different dimensions
                                      Quantum Leap - changing past events can gradually improve the 'now'

                                              Originally posted by quelsec View Post

                                              Im a fan of Novicows self consistency principle. If youre going to the past that means you already did.
                                              That's not exactly how Novikov's self-consistency principle works. That's a result of the principle in broad application. The only requirement of self-consistency is that anything a time-traveler does can't cause a temporal paradox. The MCU Loki show actually demonstrates one of the proposed ways to do stuff in the past of the time-stream without violating self-consistency: go to a place and time where nothing you do matters because a natural disaster is going to so thoroughly destroy the area that no evidence remains. The past remains self-consistent, even if superficially altered; and whether you did or didn't already do something because of time-travel is unresolved until you actually do it.

                                              There are problems, such as the fact that inevitably multiple people are going to have the same idea and such spots would become unstable due to tragedy of the commons on a temporal resources scale.

                                              There are also benefits because the bigger the disaster the more you can get away with: you could build the most impressive supercomputer ever, put it in the path of a planet killer asteroid ala the one that killed off the dinosaurs, and in the present be able to perform immense computations virtually instantly from the perspective of people around you in the "now."
