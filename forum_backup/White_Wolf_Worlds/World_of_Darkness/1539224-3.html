                            The reason the Catholic Church disliked Freemasonry was that Freemasons wanted to have a subcommunity outside of State control and communicate freely. In the Early Modern period (1450-1790) the Catholic Church, like many European churches, was an arm of the State. Freemasonry defied the State and thus threatened Church authority. Further, conspiracy theorists proclaimed the Freemasons caused both the American and French revolutions in a bid to take over the world. (The Bavarian Illuminati were a minor freemason-like group that got a wildly overblown reputation as masterminds. They were more of a joke really.)

                            By the 1920s it was fashionable to blame Freemasonry for any and all resistance to the elites of Europe from the masses.

                                    Yeah of course there are huge differences between the extent of influence that irl Freemasons had and what has later been purported by occultists, but like my Sewer Gator principle, the ideas of the latter can be just as interesting for storytelling as the former.

                                    One can quibble on the extent to which Freemasonry had an impact on the revolutions of the 17th-19th century. I agree that any suggestions outside of fiction of a grand Masonic conspiracy orchestrating affairs is wildly overblown - the American, French, & Haitian Revolutions would most likely have occurred even if Masonry did not exist; though in WoD it can be fun to propose that organizations such as the Craftmasons did have a large influence, if only as a personification/embodiment of the larger social & ideological forces concurrent with such historic developments.

                                    However I do think one could argue that Freemasonry did provide a certain amount of cover and social technology to the men of the social circumstances who were most likely to be at the heart of these political ruptures when they occurred. These lodges would have provided a means of developing group-solidarity (akin to how a country club or chamber of commerce might do the same) as well as the requisite anonymity needed for discussion of heterodox ideas. The ubiquity of para-Masonic organizations in this period showcases imo the validity of the social tech, regardless of the purported ideology of any particular organization.

                                    That same ubiquity, imo, showcases one important element of Freemasonry that makes it valuable for storytelling in Mage. One didn’t need to share Adam Weishuap’s visions of a universal world-republic to find merit in his suggestion that Masonic lodges could be useful places for ambitious men to gather. The men who joined the first KKK didn’t need to know or care about Albert Pike’s elaborate quasi-theosophical para-Masonic occult schema. These organizations did have beliefs and ideologies that were the object of immense interest for certain members, some of whom would become revolutionary thinkers within their ideological sphere; even if others, perhaps even a majority, cared far more for the practical effects of such an organization’s values. I find this phenomena interesting in how it speaks to a larger aspect of Mage: the image of the Mage-as-ideological-juggernaut who becomes a gravitational center for a number of other individuals, who may or may not agree with or understand the Mage, but who, for their own reasons, help to remake the world in the Mage’s own image.
