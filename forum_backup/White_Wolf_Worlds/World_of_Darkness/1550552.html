    Mašmaššūtu - Mesopotamian Sorcery

    05-01-2025, 05:30 PM
    I have always been interested in development of magic in history. Sumer, Akkad, and Babylon provide the earliest historical records of magic rituals, and I thought it would be a fun exercise to wrap what I had learned into the World of Darkness context. I had the question about ancient magic and magic history inside the game come up several times in game sessions, and maybe my notes can be of use for Storytellers who come across similar questions.
    Much of the sorcerous praxis endured almost unchanged through the 5000 years from Sumer to Assyria, and parts of it even longer through the Classical Greek and Roman period and onward. Famously the basis of Mesopotamian Astrology and Astronomy is still used in the modern western versions.

    The World of Darkness version of Mesopotamia is not a nice place. Dead Magic gives us a glimpse of the Babylonian period, with evil cults using worship as a cover for their own ends. Demonic presence is rampant, and absurdly powerful vampires use the city states as a stage for their jyhad.
    Yet, there had to be a layer of functioning mortal society. My attempt is to take the historical record and see what fits mechanically for sorcerers largely independent from the wider World of Darkness, since I think integration is better left to the individual Storyteller.

    As a disclaimer, I’m not an Assyriologist. I will be using the Akkadian words for professions where applicable, but I only have a very superficial understanding of the language.
    On that note, I will be mostly using masculine nouns for ease of writing. Feminine nouns exist for almost all of the professions, indicating that while there may have been a bias within a profession towards one gender or another, there was little exclusivity.


    Cosmology and Society
    Before diving into the Sorcery proper, it is important to establish the framework it operates in. First, there is a clear duality of the world: pure and impure, divine and earthly. This concept extends into social hierarchy – not only as nobility and plebs, but also “the ones who enter the temple” (ērib bīti) and those who do not. Sorcerers existed in both classes, but most of the written corpus comes naturally from the upper stratum of society. We get some (obviously biased) glimpses of street sorcerers through those texts, mostly in the context of combating their unsavory sorcery. It is necessary to be aware of this divide, because certain praxes were reserved for either divine or earthly providence.

    At the same time, the divine and earthly was much, much closer when compared to most later religions. Each of the city states had a patron deity, unique to the city – the temple was the literal home of the god or goddess, their statue inside the temple conflated the concepts of both divine and earthly. When the Marduk statue was taken from the Esagila by outside powers, the god had literally left the city. Rituals could no longer be conducted, and the citizens had lost the protection of their patron. This works great for using powerful supernatural beings as the true power behind the patron deities of the cities and transforming the map into a board for power struggles.
    In addition to the city patron deity, individuals also had their personal patron deity to whom they mainly prayed. These were often tied to their profession, such as Adad for agriculture.

    The pantheon of Mesopotamia is large and complex. Its families and descendants, minor gods, demi-gods and so on produce Demons (gallû) and Spirits (rābiṣu) down the lineages that can be, for the most part, transferred to their respective World of Darkness equivalents as concepts. Many translations will describe rābiṣu as another type of demon, but they seem to mostly perform their function without moral considerations and were seen as agents of divine will. Both types have several families and sub-categories and provide ample opportunity to search canon material for matches.

    Wraiths (eṭemmu) were recognized as a part of reality. With proper burial rites, a deceased person would pass on to the underworld, but could return if it was dissatisfied with the conduct of their family and tell them as much. Ancestor worship was an integral part of Mesopotamian religion, and dealings with the dead were not seen as anything taboo or scary. As a consequence, much of what today falls under the Necromancy category was simply religious service.
    However, the rightful place for ghosts was the underworld, and without receiving a proper burial or due to being evil during their living days, the deceased could become a Spectre (utukku) – again, the word utukku is usually translated as ‘demon’, but their description is a good match for the World of Darkness Spectre or at least, malevolent Wraiths.


    Fundamental Concepts
    The strong divide between the divine and earthly creates the concept of liminality. For Mage players, this one is easy to grasp – liminal spaces exist between the umbral realms, and a sorcerer or priest can enter a liminal state of consciousness between the two worlds. In game terms, much relies on an awareness of the Gauntlet or Shroud. Purification rituals work on the premise of moving a person or object closer to the divine by pushing these boundaries.

    The most important concept for understanding Mesopotamian sorcerous praxis is that of the Omen. An Omen was understood not only as a portent, it was also as much cause as it was effect. For example, a devout citizen would petition their patron deity for a good Omen, which then in turn would cause good fortune. Similarly, bad fortune could be avoided if the corresponding bad Omen could be prevented from happening. Omina can be seen as the visible part of the liminal interaction between the divine and earthly, as mentioned above. An Omen could be anything that was considered ‘strange’, from birth defects to stellar configurations to shapes in smoke.
    As a consequence, Omina are probably the most common Focus Ability for the Fortune Path. Instead of affecting a target directly, it would manifest an Omen with the desired effect.

    Finally, it is worth mentioning that almost all sorceries except Divination employ something called apotropaic magic. The concept is basically scaring off entities responsible for an effect, and the effect with them. An example would be placing a plaque depicting Pazuzu (a demon) to scare off Lamaštu (another demon). Another would be invoking the names of deities to prevent a bad Omen.


    White Magic, Gray Magic, Black Magic
    The distinction between good and bad sorcery was very straightforward: If it was in concordance with the will of the gods, reading the signs, or preserving health and security, it was considered acceptable. It is important to point out that communing with the dead falls into this category as an accepted kind of Necromancy.
    Anything that amplified personal gain or prowess to get an ‘unfair’ advantage was in the gray area. For example, attracting more customers to ones food stall by using sorcery was seen as dubious – of course, that did not stop people from performing this type of spell, and even high ranked and prestigious sorcerers would accept commissions for them. These spells are however suspiciously absent from the corpus of texts in ‘official’ libraries.
    Kišpē, often translated as witchcraft, was anything that brought harm to another, caused bad Omina, or disrupted the natural order of things – such as summoning demons or ghosts. Of course, officially sanctioned sorceries of this kind were perfectly fine. Sending a bad Omen to a ruler of an enemy city was just warfare, even if the receiving side would not hesitate to call it out as witchcraft.


    Sorcery or Theurgy
    There is an argument to be made that many of the professions that follow should technically fall into the purview of Theurgy. However, there was little difference between the two since the concepts were so intertwined it makes them virtually indistinguishable. I do not see a problem with introducing and additional Faith element, but will not make use of it here.


    Sorcerous Professions
    A large number of specialist professions that employed sorcery in one form or another is mentioned in the recovered texts we have. Note, while the focus on these professions may be on certain Paths of sorcery, almost all had knowledge and proficiency outside their expertise. Recognizing and understanding Omina is a requisite for all of them, and the specialization often comes from what kind of Focus the sorcerer would employ.
    An earthly providence does not necessarily mean that a profession was of low status, just that their sorceries were more concerned with the earthly aspects of life. As an example, while the barû was concerned with earthly Omina, he would still be part of the temple. On the other hand, it is very likely that all these professions also existed in lower social strata, but without their official titles.

    The following list is a selection of the more prominent professions with textual evidence.

    Format:
    Name of the profession: Primary Path
    (male and female nouns for the practitioner / divine or earthly providence)
    Suggestions for secondary Paths


    āšipūtu: Summoning, Binding, Warding (alternative: Exorcism)
    (m: āšipu, f: āšiptu / divine)
    Secondary Paths: Quintessence Manipulation, Ephemera (alternative: Omina)

    Probably the most famous and well documented profession, the āšipu was the divine counterpart to the asû and performed healing rituals that targeted Disease-Spirits, Demons, and Ghosts who plagued a victim. Also an expert in counter-magic, repelling witchcraft was a big part of his repertoire. As quasi-head of the priesthood, this position enjoyed a great deal of prestige. Together with the kalû he would perform rituals of city-wide significance, or attend royalty directly.

    Praxis: The first step would always be to read the Omina and determine the exact cause of a malady, because an evil Demon would require a different approach than a disgruntled Ancestor Wraith or curse by a warlock. The more information could be gained about the specific problem, the more power the āšipu had over it.
    The exorcism rituals then employed invocations of holy names, the superiority of the sorcerer, symbolic destruction of the aggressor, or diverting the effect elsewhere. Remarkably, the ritual texts often read like a stern contract that covers every eventuality and refer to the proper order of things like a law text.
    Example texts: Maqlû, Šurpu, misc


    asûtu: Healing
    (m: asû, f: asītu? / earthly)
    Secondary Paths: Herbalism

    The asû was what we would call a physician today, whose expertise lay in mending injuries and treating disease with medicine. Of course, this was far from anything like modern medicine: ingredients for remedies were mostly chosen for their symbolic rather than officinal value, although this was not always mutually exclusive. While of earthly providence, healers still received a good deal of respect from the public (depending on proficiency; quacks always existed).

    Praxis: While splinting a broken bone is relatively straightforward, diseases could have multiple causes (see āšipūtu, above) and would require the asû to read the Omina to prepare the correct medicine. This would go together with incantations that petition the gods for health, or setting up charms to deter evil Spirits.
    Example texts: Sakikkū, Geller, M.: Ancient Babylonian Medicine


    bārûtu: Divination (Focus: Extispicy, alternative: Omina)
    (m: bārû, f: bārītu / earthly)
    Secondary Paths: Fortune

    The bārû was an expert of reading and interpreting earthly Omina, in particular through extispicy and even more specifically through hepatoscopy, the examination of animal livers. A highly involved procedure with a proper system, it needed to be studied in depth. As one of the oldest professions it endured for a long time, and the practice spread into the Mediterranean and north-east Africa.
    With providence over earthly Omina, the bārû was the worldly counterpart to the astrologer scribe.

    Praxis: The entrails are divided into ten categories, each dealing with a different aspect of entrail divination. Clay models existed to guide the bārû through the process, and extensive documentation described the Omina that could be identified.
    Example texts: CCP 3.4.1.H - Bārûtu 1 Isru, Jeyes, U.: Divination as a science in ancient Mesopotamia


    eššebûtu: Spirit Chasing
    (m: eššebû, f: eššebītu? / earthly)
    Secondary Paths: Shapeshifting, Ephemera

    There is very little known about the so-called “owlmen” and their role in society. They are an example of street sorcerers that seem to have been relatively common, but get little mention in surviving texts other than implications of their shady character. Some texts suggest they are a kind of ecstatic, but this and their name is all there is to go on.

    Praxis: Unknown. These types of ‘unsavory but not illegal’ sorcerers lend themselves very well to tie into the supernatural World of Darkness canon outside Mage. Maybe owlmen had ties to the Fera of the region?
    Example texts: none.


    kalûtu: Gauntlet Manipulation (alternative: Consecration)
    (m: kalû, f: kalītu? / divine)
    Secondary Paths: Quintessence Manipulation, Fascination

    The kalû was the second most important position in the priesthood. While predominantly male (although some female examples existed), they used a feminine dialect of Sumerian in their incantations and dressed like a woman, sometimes even adopting a female name. Translated as lamenting priest, their purpose was to petition the gods with songs and poetic prayers for favors or to absolve sins.
    Their liminal nature between pure and profane made them the intermediary between gods and men, and part of their duties involved purification, such as consecrating artifacts, cleansing sin off persons, or blessing the fields for harvest. In contrast to the āšipu, their purification worked by bringing an object or person closer to the divine.

    Praxis: The kalû had a large repertoire of different categories of prayer and hymns, and had to choose the right lamentation for the situation. The šuʾila prayers addressed the highest gods, the balaĝ was for city-wide rituals, eršaḫungû to sooth divine anger, and so on. The kalûtu corpus is quite extensive.
    There was also a physical side to the rituals, especially for purification. This involved ritual washing, visiting holy places, meditation and lengthy invocations.
    Example texts: Mîs-pî, Lenzi, A.: Reading Akkadian Prayers and Hymns


    kišpē: Fortune (Curses) (alternative: Omina)
    (m: kaššāpu, f: kaššāptu / earthly)
    Secondary Paths: Necromancy, Maelstroms

    Witches and warlocks were the stereotypical villains behind sorcerous maladies. They summoned demons, raised the dead, gave you the evil eye, and most of all were a cause of bad Omina. When an āšipu assessed a case, there were three main culprits to consider: Angry gods, angry ghosts, and witches. Practicing forbidden sorcery was a serious crime, and usually carried the death penalty.
    As is usually the case, the stereotype might not be completely accurate of what practitioners of ‘illegal’ magic did, but in absence of textual evidence there is no way of knowing. It may be a similar situation to the witch hunts of medieval Europe, or it may not – considering that even unsavory types of street sorcery were tolerated.

    Praxis: There are some mentions of black magic in the Exorcist’s Manual, but they are vague and seeped in stereotypes. It is much like the ‘how to identify a witch’ sections in the Hexenhammer, and don’t go much beyond ‘a witch is evil and does evil things’. It is perhaps worth mentioning that blood magic (not the vampiric type) was not unusual, mostly in the form of animal sacrifice, and practiced by ‘legal’ sorcerers as well.
    Example texts: none.


    mašmaššūtu: Enchantment
    (m: mašmaššu, f: mašmašštu / earthly)
    Secondary Paths: Any

    There is some debate what exactly differentiates the mašmaššu from the āšipu. The texts make the two professions look synonymous in regards to their expertise, tools, and spells, but they are never both used for the same person. For the purpose of this text, I have decided to use mašmaššu as a more generalized type of sorcerer that is distinct from the high temple office of the āšipu, and caters to the needs of the middle to upper class citizens. Sorcery was in high demand throughout all of society, and the easiest (and profitable) way was to specialize in charms, amulets, seals, and other knickknacks.

    Praxis: While the basis of the sorcery is the same as for the others, it is much more oriented towards utilitarian use. The tool of a craftsman would be inscribed with an invocation of their patron deity for fortitude, small seals were available at market stalls to gently get rid of an annoying ancestor ghost, charms provide protection from disease and evil Spirits, amulets protect from demons during childbirth and so on.
    Example texts: Online collection of the British Museum, Lamaštu amulet


    muraqqûtu: Alchemy (alternative: Perfumes)
    (m: muraqqû, f: muraqqitu / earthly)
    Secondary Paths: Fascination, Ephemera

    While not considered sorcerers, perfumers were important suppliers for aromatics used in all kind of rituals. Depending on the task, their perfumes might aid cleansing, apotropaic wards, or inducing trance. Naturally, their main business would be to supply those who can afford it with pleasing scents, but the role of aromatics should not be understated.
    Tappūtī-Bēlet-Ekallim is a pretty famous muraqqitû as the oldest surviving name of the profession, but it existed many years before and after her.

    Praxis: The concoction of perfumes was a very physical and elaborate task. Generally it was distinguished between oil-based (šamnu) and water-based/dry (riqqu), with the former usually more potent and expensive. It should be noted that the procedure involved techniques and apparatus that would become the hallmark of later alchemy, like glass-work and distillation.
    Example Texts: KAR 220, The Scents of Larsa


    mušēlû eṭemmi: Necromancy
    (m: mušēlû, f: mušēlītu / earthly)
    Secondary Paths: Summoning, Binding, Warding

    As previously mentioned, asking a family ghost for guidance, resolving a grudge held by them, or veneration and observance of proper burial rites were perfectly acceptable. Raising the dead was absolutely not. Necromancy thus was a somewhat gray area, especially since ghosts were seen as dangerously powerful and a bad Omen delivered by one could mean certain death – at least, in the view of the general public. There are as of yet very few translated records that deal with Necromancy directly, so information is scarce.

    Praxis: Little is known outside of funerary rites. The fragmented rituals we have mention brewing some kind of concoction which attracted the ghosts and wards as precautions against their powers.
    Example texts: Burial in ancient Mesopotamia, Necrormancy in ancient Mesopotamia


    mušlaḫḫūtu: Animal Psychic
    (m: mušlaḫḫu, f: mušlaḫḫtu? / earthly)
    Secondary Paths: Psychic Hypnosis

    Like with the eššebû, there is little to go on for the Mesopotamian version of the snake charmer. He appears as a source of unsavory sorcery, and was obviously associated with dangerous and venomous animals. Some researchers speculate he may have been a specialized healer for animal bites, or perhaps handled removal of dangerous animals.

    Praxis: Unknown, but their name suggests some kind of animal mesmerism.
    Example texts: none.


    šaʾilūtu: Oneiromancy
    (m: šaʾilu, f: šaʾiltu / divine)
    Secondary Paths: Fortune, Divination

    There were three types of dreams that fell into the purview of the šaʾiltu (most of them were women): a message, oracular, and ominous. The first one was a message from beyond, by a ghost, spirit, or god. Oracular dreams gave portents about the future. The ominous dreams, as the name implies, were Omina that needed to be decoded and sometimes dispelled. Like the barû, with whom she often worked in tandem, the šaʾiltu had an extensive corpus of dream-Omina that could be used to decipher individual symbols.

    Praxis: Outside of dream interpretation, the šaʾiltu would also directly attend a sleeping client. Sometimes this would involve visualization of the dreams with the smoke of incense, other times she would engage in shared dreaming. This was mostly the case when a bad dream Omen needed dispelling, and the oneiromancer could manipulate the dreams directly.
    Example texts: The Assyrian Dream Book, Gudea Cylinders


    ṭupšarrūtu: Divination (Focus: Astrology, alternative: Omina)
    (m: ṭupšarru, f: ṭupšarrtu? / divine)
    Secondary Paths: Fortune, Scrying

    Tupšarrūtu means ‘art of the scribe’, and in wider sense might be translated with ‘science’. While a far cry from modern science it denoted not only literacy but also ‘learnedness’, having studied in libraries under the tutelage of older scribes. All of the temple offices mentioned so far fall into the category of a ṭupšarru, as the deeper mysteries of sorceries required the study of records of the masters who came before. Unless you were a street sorcerer who no doubt had their oral traditions to rely on, a court or temple sorcerer was also a scribe.
    The wide range of the ‘sciences’ means that this entry is just one specialization of many, in this case theṭupšarrūt Enūma Anu illil (lit: scribe of the “When Anu and Enlil”) which directly referred to an important text on astronomy and astrology. The night sky was the most important and significant place to look for Omina, because it showed the Diviner events that moved the world – it was like looking at the actions of the gods directly.

    Praxis:This one is fairly familiar. An astrologer would observe the heavens, the planets, and the stars to look for Omina. The zodiac was used to partition the sky along three paths for the gods Anu, Enlil, and Ea. While the system did not have something as involved as planetary aspects, more attention was given to phenomena like eclipses or moon phases, and their conjunction with weather patterns.
    Example texts: Enuma Anu Enlil, MUL.APIN


    Some thoughts on integration into the World of Darkness
    Taking the historical record by itself and looking at it from the perspective of a player would be, frankly, pretty boring unless they are a history buff. Where is the powerful ancient sorcery? How do Sorcerers even begin to compete with Mages unchained by the threat of Paradox, or the world-changing powers of Vampire Blood Gods?
    The truth is that much of the ‘power’ from ancient Magick likely comes from its environment, and mainly from existing in a society that accepts magic as reality. Sorcery shares the benefit of not caring about Witnesses here, and I would argue that Path levels greater than five would have been more common as well – the level six powers of some Paths in Sorcerer Revised are perhaps a good escalation that would even out the field a little.
    But I would say this solution is, in terms of narrative expectations, rather weak. A player whose character unearths a cuneiform tablet with an ancient Sumerian spell and goes through the trouble of translating it would likely expect something more extravagant.
    To account for this, I made three new (or very old) Paths that I would consider more powerful in scope than many of the existing Paths, but still limited by the core mechanics.


    New Paths

    Consecration
    Consecration is the preparation of a person, object, or place for contact with the divine. The Path is a specialized version of Gauntlet Manipulation: It decreases the Gauntlet Rating for interaction with and attracts a specific type of entity while repelling and increasing the rating for opposing entities. For example, the consecration of a wheat field would lower the rating for Fertility Spirits, but raises it for Drought Banes and the like. Note that this does not cleanse any lingering taint the target might have. A curse of infertility upon the same wheat field would have to be dispelled first.
    The Aspects of the Path are optionally Number of Targets or Area. The strength of the increase and decrease in the Gauntlet Rating is equal to Path Level of the sorcerer. Consecrations can only be performed as a ritual.

    Exorcism
    An extension to Summoning, Binding, and Warding, this Path concerns itself with the banishment of supernatural beings. While its main strength comes from expulsion from the earthly realm, it also prevents the exorcised entity from interacting with the mortal realm again for some time. Usually, the banished entity returns to their native realm, but some sorcerers prefer to trap it into an object instead.
    The Aspects for this Path are the Metaphysical Weight of the entity and the Time Period (see Divination) for the duration of the banishment. Similar to its parent Path, it can only be conducted through ritual, and each type of entity (Spirit, Wraith, Demon) needs its own ritual.

    Omina
    The ability to not only recognize, but also cause or prevent an Omen combines principles of Fortune and Divination. Causing an Omen for a target will act like a Fortune spell with the Severity Aspect, but the Path can also be used to identify twists of fate (either caused by someone else, or the great wheel in the sky) using the Accuracy Aspect of Divination. In contrast to the specialized Paths, there is usually no information when exactly events will happen, and neither does it allow for specific queries. It can however be used to undo the effects of an Omen before they happen: a successful roll of the Path Level against the difficulty of (Severity of the Omen + 2) will prevent its effects. Sending and undoing an Omen requires a ritual, but reading an Omen can be done as a spell.

    Perfumes
    A specialized form of Alchemy, this praxis employs scents to attract Spirits instead of transporting an effect directly. The Perfumer prepares a special formula that tries to coax the Spirits into using one of their Charms on the affected Area; success depends on whether the Potency Aspect can meet the required strength for the desired Charm effect. As a guideline, Potency should match either half of the Essence cost or Difficulty for the Spirit to use the Charm (see M20 p. 489ff), whichever is higher.

      I thought maybe it is helpful to see how Omina were used with a concrete example. The story was found on a tablet in the house of Kiṣir-Nabû, a young āšipu in Aššur who lived around 700 BC.
      Unsure about the cause of an ailment, he provokes an Omen for a diagnosis:

      One day, Kiṣir-Nabû was approached by a man who suffered from impotence. The young āšipu considered the application of a potion and a phylactery suitable for curing the ailment. He was, however, reluctant to administer the therapeutics without having determined the ulterior cause of the man’s deficient potency. Therefore, he decided to undertake a diagnostic divination procedure before mixing the potion and filling the little leather pouch that the patient could wear around his neck [nb: likely filled with apotropaic ingredients or objects].

      Kiṣir-Nabû kneaded emmer dough with clay and used the malleable mixture to form two figurines, one of a man and one of a woman. He put the two figurines on top of each other as if having sexual intercourse and placed the arrangement next to the patient’s head. Having recited an unintelligible incantation [nb: some incantations contained abracadabra type glossolalia] seven times over the figurines, he took them out onto the street and placed them near a pig.

      The behaviour of the pig would then reveal to him the cause of the man’s impotence: If the pig approached the figurines, the suffering had been caused by the anger of Ištar, the goddess of libido and love. In this case the therapy would certainly include measures to sooth the goddess’s wrath.
      If the pig, however, kept away from the figurines, more sinister forces were at play. The fact that even this impure animal, which was notorious for eating the rubbish in the streets, did not touch the figurines indicated that the impotent man had fallen victim to kišpū.​​

                      I added Perfumers. Although perfume making was very much considered a craft and not a sorcery, I thought they make an interesting addition to the roster of professions and present an unusual spin on alchemists.

                      Originally posted by voidshaper View Post
                      muraqqûtu: Alchemy (alternative: Perfumes)
                      (m: muraqqû, f: muraqqitu / earthly)
                      Secondary Paths: Fascination, Ephemera

                      While not considered sorcerers, perfumers were important suppliers for aromatics used in all kind of rituals. Depending on the task, their perfumes might aid cleansing, apotropaic wards, or inducing trance. Naturally, their main business would be to supply those who can afford it with pleasing scents, but the role of aromatics should not be understated.
                      Tappūtī-Bēlet-Ekallim is a pretty famous muraqqitû as the oldest surviving name of the profession, but it existed many years before and after her.

                      Praxis: The concoction of perfumes was a very physical and elaborate task. Generally it was distinguished between oil-based (šamnu) and water-based/dry (riqqu), with the former usually more potent and expensive. It should be noted that the procedure involved techniques and apparatus that would become the hallmark of later alchemy, like glass-work and distillation.
                      Example Texts: KAR 220, The Scents of Larsa

                      New Path

                      Perfumes
                      A specialized form of Alchemy, this praxis employs scents to attract Spirits instead of transporting an effect directly. The Perfumer prepares a special formula that tries to coax the Spirits into using one of their Charms on the affected Area; success depends on whether the Potency Aspect can meet the required strength for the desired Charm effect. As a guideline, Potency should match either half of the Essence cost or Difficulty for the Spirit to use the Charm (see M20 p. 489ff), whichever is higher.

                          I know it's in common academic parlance still, but I'd be wary of translating gallû and rābiṣu as "demons" or "spirits". The concepts aren't really similar. the gallû in particular are far more of a "natural species" of the underworld than malicious in the modern concept of a demon.

                          Similarly I'd avoid calling Pazuzu and Lamaštu demons, they're more properly deities, even if malicious and/or destructive by the time of the records we have.

                          Overall "demon" has a particular connotation of a type of relationship with evil that didn't exist before Christianity, not even in Judaism. It has an origin in post-exilic Judaism (4th and 3rd century BCE), but only Christianity solidified the current notion.

                          Necromancy is harder to explain because we use today a rather distorted concept for it, but Mesopotamian necromancy is the real deal: it's not control over the dead, but communication with them. With extant ghosts this would be used to understand their messages, sometimes as part of an exorcism. With those in the underworld it was mostly divination, you summon the dead with propitiation just so you can ask about things they know of the afterlife of their time as living.

                          Command over animated corpses, necrotic curses or inflicting ghosts upon others is not necromancy in their language, or any language before the late medieval.

                          Faith is part and parcel of every magical tradition before the Renaissance. It's the anthropocentric principle of the time that first create the idea of a distinction between magic practiced as a secular craft from magic linked to religion. This is not particular to the region of Mesopotamia.

                          Apotropaic magic is anything done for protection, not necessarily scarying spirits, and is also a more universal concept. It is still a common practice today across religions, and a lot of Christian memorabilia is apotropaic in nature.

                          White, Grey and Black magic are also modern and not really fitting concepts. What we have is more precisely two ways of categorizing magic there and in many societies: by how a spell is regarded by society and by the legal status of the practitioner.

                          Kišpē was a particular type of harmful magic that worked as a contamination that could be removed, and was illegal in regular use, but it's important to observe that it was defined above all else by being unsanctioned by the Temple, which means not everything deemed kišpē was deemed harmful by the practitioner: it could be more and accusation levied against traditions and religions persecuted at the moment than certain harm.

                          Personally I'd avoid making Perfumists into a Sorcery Path, but would consider a side rule that is lacking anyway in WoD: sorcerous providers. The idea is to, instead of making the profession into a Sorcery Path, define in game terms how a proper ingredient is crafted.

                          Many magical traditions require specialized ingredients or tools that have to be prepared by particular professionals following specific craft techniques. A perfumist wouldn't practice any magic on their own, but magical rituals likely demanded perfumes that couldn't be made by anyone, and skill alone was not the problem.

                          Love this thread, by the way, excellent work. That's one of those cases were the criticism requires a lot of words not because it's extensive, but because it's in very technical details, which is itself a good sign.
