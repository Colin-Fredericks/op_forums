        *plunks two cents on the table*

        Spoilers, I'm going to do comparisons to Lost(the nwod game, not the show).

        Why?

        Well, here's the thing. Changeling, in either incarnation, is about the death of innocence* and what to do about it. More specifically, it's about when the sense of endless possibility and the actual attainment of joy mixed with a sense of simplicty is betrayed, and how hard it can be to find that feeling again, even in new and quieter forms. Both of these games are brutal in how they do this, with Lost perhaps being subtler while still beaning you in the head, but Dreaming is upfront about this...and more importantly, it's also fuller than Lost.

        It's not a dark game at it's core, though it can be-the fae and the banal have enough horror and grimness to eat at you. But Dreaming is more sinister than that. Dreaming is a bleak game. Lost is an autumn game where Dreaming is a winter game. Because, at the end of the day, it's not dread and fear that drives the game, but despair and fatalism. By hyper-aggrandizing the feeling of loss of innocence into a high fantasy story and then juxtaposing it with the grey reality, Dreaming's setting allows for harsh twists, turns, and heights.

        The big thing to it is that you're invited to explore the entire process, getting to explore a crazy magical land and know secrets others don't, then get traumatized by the fae and suffer as the banal finds ways to sink it's teeth into you, and it slowly shreds away at you until you're at a tipping point, and you have to decide-what am I going to do to keep my life special? How do you escape being a cog in the machine and truly be?

        Dreaming is accepting that the world hurts in a subtle, sinister way-that our dreams often will never be, and learning to accept some of it, defy other parts of it, and ultimately find a new way to keep a small fire alive.

        In fact, simply put, Dreaming is about any small thing that keeps you alive-that keeps your life worthwhile.

        It's a bleak, bleak game. But a quietly hopeful one too. One that needs silly bears with balloons somewhere in a hopelessly grey city, I feel.

        *Well, one of the things it's about in either incarnation.

                    Speaking as someone who is only tangentially interested in Changeling, but who enjoyed reading this conversation, allow me to voice a couple of points.

                    1. I see great potential in the "laboring futile against being crushed by the grey monotony of reality" angle, but its loss state is, to echo a previous poster, nothing but mere reality. And as we find wonder (and i hope all of us do) in real life, and we live relatively fulfilled and happy lives, I cant fully buy that as a loss state.

                    2. This is a terribly smug thing of me to say, but just from looking, I feel like I am too old for it. I'm now 29, and my life is more or less on track. Were I my 22 year old self, just at the cusp of true independent adulthood, Changeling would hit way closer to home.

                    3. I actually like autumn and winter, and I like the melancholy inherent in these metaphors. To speak in Wraith terms, I never got why "Oblivion as a windswept, empty, snowy mountain-top" is bad, and likewise I don't see bleak as synonymous to bad. I see the dramatic potential of something like "emotional desert versus self-destructive turbulence", but I get that fix from Vampire and Wraith aplenty.

                    4. All in all, if it is executed in a dark romanticist way, I dont see why it is needed when those stories can be told (perhaps better) in Vampire, Wraith and perhaps Werewolf. When it is executed in the "oh no, I'm growing up" way, I find it frankly to miss the mark and be almost insulting.

                        Yeah, as a 30-something, even one with a "when will I actually feel like an adult?" sense to my own identity (despite having a good and steady job and just having bought a house), I look at the "loss of childhood" angle as a bit limiting.

                        I could actually see that as a great angle to play a cross of C:tD Childlings with nWoD's Innocents, or even as a way to expressly focus C:tD for Childling perspectives.

                        But for Wilders, the perspective needs to shift: now, the struggle becomes less about "loss of childhood," though it still includes elements of "loss of innocence." It gains the "struggle for identity vs. conformity" angle, however. Wilders are teens and young adults, and "banality" to them is a dread combination of being outcast from the social group AND being lost TO said social group. Almost a stage fright vs. fear of being overlooked issue, combined with a "adult responsibilities are banal" fear coupled to a "adult freedoms are enticing...but cost innocence" concern.

                        For Wilders, then, Banality would be about comfort zones and finding the "safe" path, while Glamour is about individuality and taking risks. As even your Glamour starts to erode your Innocence, you need to find other ways to stoke your belief in yourself and your sense of wondrous connection to the Dreaming to stave off Banality. At the same time, clinging too hard to your Innocence becomes a rote rejection of the new, and it, too, starts to make your Glamour fade as Banality sets in: your "faith" becomes a rote recitation of things you are too afraid to grow up and face the truth of, rather than a true childhood sense of acceptance of magic.

                        This transition is hard, and fraught with danger, because failing to attempt it is as Banal-death-inducing as trying it and collapsing into a cynical blob. Thus, it appears to many Changelings that growing up is hazardous to their Chimerical health.

                        Grumps have navigated those waters, or have undergone Chrysalis VERY late. For them, the struggle is less about identity vs. collective; they are already established and know, at least well enough, who they are and how to value friends and groups versus their own well-being. Mostly. (It's obviously a struggle one's whole life, but we get better at it with age.) They've also faced the demon of "adult responsibilities," and found that they actually ENABLE the adult freedoms they once craved even while dreading the former.

                        For the Grump, the struggle is in reconciling the demands of daily responsibility with the personal yen for grandeur. The fae soul demands More than mundanity. Add to this the same "Masquerade" problems that face the other splats: in a world that doesn't believe in magic, you know it's real and are deeply enmeshed in it. But you also are an adult Changeling; you're practically an elder of their kind. You have adult responsibilities even in the Dreaming world, and those can become sources of monotony and complacency. Complacency is the Banal enemy of the Grump. Complacency and Despondency - a loss of hope for something better. The hazard for the Grump on the Glamour side is almost entirely the descent into madness as one separates from the mundane world.

                        Grumps, more than Wilders or Childlings, actively live a double life, and must balance between them lest they lose half of themselves: their magic to the Banality of everyday life, or their sanity and connection to the real world in the magic of the Dreaming.

                        Perhaps it IS less tragic if Sir Leigh the Nocker Knight finds himself so enmeshed in the banal joys of his business as a mechanic, the stress and joys of his recent wedding and now taking care of his pregnant wife, and the time spent with his mortal nieces and nephews, that he loses sight of his faerie nature. He's not miserable; he just...forgot...that the "LARP" he used to do with his best friend was more than that. But it still is a loss, and there may come a time when the Faerie world needs Sir Leigh again, so they reach out and try to rekindle his Glamour.

                        It certainly is horrific, to Sir Leigh's wife and young son, if their husband and father is acting strangely more and more often, and starts taking risks, vanishing for periods of time, and apparently is involved with something that is drawing vandals to his place of business. If his son complains of monsters scratching at his window, and Lee bravely "fights them off," that's him being a good father. But his wife may be concerned when Lee maintains a haunted look, like he expects those "monsters" are both real...and likely to come back. What's he involved in?

                        The revelation that there's nothing to them, that there isn't a gang of thugs or mafia types or anything, is not particularly reassuring to discover if Lee keeps acting paranoid, and even starts taking extended leaves of absence.

                        As Sir Leigh gets more involved in the Dreaming, his mundane life suffers, and he separates himself to spare them...until the Nocker Knight barely recognizes those silly mortals that Lee once called "family," and finds their needs trivial compared to the great battle he is helping his Freehold fight against the Redcap Menace. Even his fellows may be uncomfortable with his zeal, with his descent into the proclivities of his Nocker nature, as he is less willing to consider real-world impacts and cares only about the Dreaming.

                        Again, not AS tragic, to the Changelings; he is "mad" but it's a madness familiar to them. And at least he's happy. Until something Chimerical kills him, and the man left behind is a madness-ridden husk.



                        Childlings, then, play the game almost as presented, but with a bonus that they don't have to separate their worlds so much. The safety of being a child in the real world, and the acceptance of their "pretend" games, means they can treat both worlds as real, most of the time. Their struggle is, as presented, a loss of faith, belief, and innocence versus a need to protect themselves from the harsh realities of the world by understanding and exploiting them. (And the fact that both worlds can be used against the other is a bonus.)

                        Wilders play with the overall WoD tendency to love the teenage wasteland motif, where the struggle for identity vies with the struggle for inclusion. They are in a transition, having to separate their worlds more, and in danger of mistaking it for "childhood" vs. "adulthood," when in reality their whole paradigm is shifting.

                        Grumps play it more straight with the WoD theme of two worlds. They have to maintain a double life and keep up the Secret. For them, it really is a struggle of finding balance: banality is a defense against the madness of glamour-indulgence, but it can kill the magic within. Glamour is power, and joy, and life for the connection to the special world, and can make even the "mundane" world a better place, but garnering it courts insanity, and the beauties of the Dreaming risk losing one's human self entirely.

                                    Originally posted by Crowley View Post
                                    i got a copy of changeling around here. i been trying to read it but i just dont get it. the whole idea of roleplaying technically two characters with their own separated lives and such. trying to understand if they are aware of each other and whats the role of these beings in the world of darkness.
                                    The big misconception you have is that you think they're two different characters. They're not. They're one person, in one body. Those who can see the Dreaming see the Fae Mein, which is the Troll or Sidhe or Pooka. Those who can't see only the Mortal Seeming, which is the kid or the youth or the mischievous old man. Changelings often appear a little insane to those who can't see the Dreaming, because they are a kid or a youth or a mischievous old man interacting with things that are not there. The youth ducks under a plenty-high archway, because his Troll mein is just an inch shorter than it and his horns would have gotten caught. The kid is arguing with his stuffed animal, because in the Dreaming it's a chimerical talking cat (which the kid also blames for the broken milk jugs, even though his mom knows there's no way Mr. Whitepaws could have done it, no matter that little Billy left Mr. Whitepaws next to it, nose soaking in the souring milk). The mischievous old man insists he doesn't feed any stray animals, but people see that mangy squirrel and its posse that come and go from his place frequently.

                                    When interacting with each other, Changelings mostly act like they're the fae their Meins portray, because they see and interact in the Dreaming as much as the real world, and the overlap is almost always more convenient to work with the Dreaming on. They have to pretend to be "normal" around mundane humans, because the Mists conceal the Dreaming from those poor people, and even if they show something supernatural, those mortals will forget in time, and chalk it up to the Changeling being weird and play-acting.

                                    But Changelings aren't two beings; they're one person, in one body, with two different appearances and acting on two, overlapping worlds with different but distinct dangers. And they have to keep the two straight. Mercifully, at least, the Dreaming's entities can generally SEE the regular world, even if Chimerae can't usually interact with it. So Changelings only look crazy to mortal eyes, not to other fae and Chimerae.
