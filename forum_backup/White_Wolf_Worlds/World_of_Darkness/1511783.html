    Bloodhunt going down

    05-16-2023, 05:40 AM
    I don't know if anyone's seen this yet but there's an official announcement from Sharkmob about VtM: Bloodhunt. They're ceasing development and will keep the servers open while there's enough interest.

    It's quite disappointing because despite numerous flaws I've actually managed to enjoy playing the game a lot of the time. What I did find funny is that Sharkmob say they've learnt lessons from the process, so I'm wondering why they can't seem to apply this knowledge to the game?

    I'm curious about what people think about how this will impact the WoD brand? It doesn't look good to me.

      I don't think it will have too much of an impact. It was pretty clear that Bloodhunt wasn't able to sustain enough players to endure until enough was done to mount some sort of revival. With enough of the market seeing Bloodhunt as a game that was at best too late to the Battle Royale party to carve out a profitable niche, this was pretty baked into existing expectations. The Battle Royale genre is also just... not a great thing to have even tried to get into. The big names in the genre haven't really changed in the past five years. Lots and lots of high quality well reviewed and well liked ones have just failed to break into the market, while even if the big names are struggling to keep up their user-bases. It's a saturated market with a lot of genre fatigue.

      So, the big hit to the brand when Bloodhunt didn't become the next big Battle Royale that would manage to be the first new game to join the top tier ones happened already. The glimmer of hope that it could resurge was never that bright, and now it's gone, but that's what happened to a bunch of equally good games trying to offer something different from PUBG/Fortnite/Apex/Warzone.

                      The thing is... it's a Battle Royale. Roping in VtM fans is a side benefit. Like any f2p game, it's operating on a minnows/dolphins/whales model. You need a lot of minnows to fill servers with players, and make the free experience/low time invested fun for them, or the game fails because the dolphins and whales aren't going to play an empty game. You need to make sure the game play is tight and competitive so the dolphins play a lot, and even if they're not dropping a lot of direct cash on the game, they're making sure e-sports and streaming of the game is profitable. Then you need to price your cosmetics appropriately so the whales spend in a fashion that feels satisfying in the context of the multiplayer experience.

                      Making the game feel more "VtM" or adding more lore is minnow stuff, and frankly those of us that would really care about it aren't big enough to fill the pond with minnows. For a more traditional game that focuses on single-player or coop over a narrative, it's an entirely different calculus.

                              The trouble is that there's some fundamental flaws with that model that are overlooked and that contribute to failures like Bloodhunt.

                              Firstly, the Dolphins don't appear at the same rate as the minnows and whales. dolphins are the sorts of player who will play the game to death for one or two weeks until they feel that they have 'won' the game/completed the battle pass and then they move on to another game. It's very rare for one of these guys (and it's almost always guys) to stick around or pay any money unless the game is genuinely challenging or engaging in some way - and that's very rare considering all these sorts of games take a cookie cutter approach to design.

                              Minnows who are the least bit aware of this sort of cycle, or who try the game and get hammered in the first couple of weeks end up avoiding games like this because they don't want to be farmed over and over. Games like this don't cater to the minnows at all, they just treat them as content for another group of players and assume that the resource will never dry up. Again, if there's no engaging gameplay for the minnows then they won't stick around either.

                              Designing a game around a minnows/dolphins/whales approach is fundamentally flawed, as is evidenced by the number of battle royale games that fail.

                              and this is why I think Sharkmob haven't learned anything from this game, they used a cookie-cutter approach, bolted on some minor flavour, and expected to profit without any innovation. That sort of approach is doomed to failure and doomed to be repeated.

                                  The M/D/W model is nothing new. It's something casinos have been doing since basically as long as there's been organized betting rings. The problem with M/D/W models is that they're less resilient than most models due to the ability to produce more product vastly outpacing the ability to attract new customers. If companies overextend, it doesn't just mean taking a bit risk for their investments, but for the niche-industry in general.

                                  To go back to casinos, in the US we know that the amount of money spent in the US on gambling has basically plateaued (relative to things like inflation). Building a new casino in the US is a big risk, because there's basically no hidden cache of players to tap into (esp. with online gambling). You have to try to grab enough business from your competition to become profitable. This all happened here. There was a boom in casinos after laws around building them on Native American land got looser in the hopes that gamblers would want to go to closer casinos and the travel to hot spots like Vegas and Atlantic City were leaving markets untapped. Those costumers didn't actually exist, so a lot of those new casinos went down. That bust also took down a lot of older casinos as well, because the new ones brought on all sorts of things to attract new customers, that existing customers still wanted, and a lot of older ones couldn't adapt quickly to sudden changes in demand.

                                  You can say that's flawed, but esp. as customer desires shift, there's only so much the companies that remain successful can do about it.

                                  I think your assessment of dolphins, esp. in the Battle Royale space, is off. Dolphins by definite pay some money even if not whale money. Players that play a lot for a few weeks and move on without spending anything are still minnows. Dolphins also don't tend to be as fickle as that. They'll try a new game, play a battle pass/season/etc. and if they don't like it for whatever reason, they go back to the big names, not on to the next new title.

                                  I also already pointed out that one of the difficulties with the M/D/W model is you have to deal with the fact that minnows and dolphins have exceedingly different desires from the game. Satisfying both is hard, and failure to do so is pretty much why most of these games fail: they're either too casual for the dolphins which means no whales and no money, or too competitive and no miinnows so no whales and no money. Anyone that can come up with a better alternative for making money off of these things (and not just Battle Royales) is going to get rich..

                                  Whether or not Sharkmob learned anything will be something we need to see from their next endeavors. Saying they didn't learn anything from making a failed Battle Royale game because their game failed doesn't really make any sense. The learning experience was Bloodhunt failing. They can't learn from something they never got into.
