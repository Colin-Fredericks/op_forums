The rest doesnt matter, until such a time when in does, and when that matters I "summon" his traits out of thin air.

                        Yes, they have mechanical notes. Guards have will 2-3, physical pool 5-6, social pool NA (not relevant) and mental pool 3-5. That's all I need to know about my NPCs.

                        As for the BBBs, those pools will simply be larger (to be a challenge to the PCs) and they'll have some "signature traits". If my hot headed "spank 'em!" BBB will only ever solve his problems punching away, he'll have Potence-Celerity-Fortitude 3-5 (or more depending on the players). My Technocrat baddie will have his "Big-Blaster-Gun" and maybe his "cheat pool of Luck" (Entropy 2), but I dont need to listen his Time 1 Sphere or Life 1 or any other Spheres he'll only ever use on the lab. He has his tricks and the equip he bears with him, and that's enough. IF there's something that will be a trademark of his - for example his BBG (big blaster gun), I mark it on his notes, and THIS one he'll carry around in future battles (if this is a villain that has a future).

                        Also, his dice pools being fixed means Im not "cheatting" my players - his pool is pre-determined just like the players sheets, it's just that Im not specifying how much Animal Empathy or Ettiquete my Big Baddy has because that will never be relevant for his role in the story, so instead I just determine a "generic dice pool" which is how competent that NPC will be AT THE THINGS THAT'LL MATTER.

                            My advice is to set an amount of dice pools for each type of "challenge", in physical, social and mental interactions, compared to the PCs.

                            For example, if your players are neonates, any dice pool that is 3 above the average is a challenge to them - but please do take notice of their min-max, both between their own sheets and amoung the table (the typical combination of "bricks" and "blasters" and "mentalists" that optimize the players when combined). Sometimes this has to be reduced to only 2 above - for example, if you send a "physical" villain on their way, but they are a bunch of guys with basically only Auspex and there"s just a single player who is a "muscle", 2 dice above the physical average of the muscle of the table will already be a big challenge and a good enough BBB for them all.

                            Big Bad Bosses however dont just come out in the open - not even the physical types. They'll ALWAYS use underlings, and the final confrontation against one is the result of a long string of events that thwarts his plans.

                            I like to use BBBs NOT for a Final Confrontation - but rather AFTER THE END. After the players utterly ruined ALL the BBB plans, that's when he comes on the open - and now it's no longer part of his games, now it's personnal, the BBB wants REVENGE, he lost his resources and is now acting in person fueled by hate; the Big Evil has already lost, so now he'll hit the players where it hurts - a physical elder may come rushing in frenzy, but a mental or social one may turn on your dependants, allies, loved ones etc. This may or may not end up in a blaze of glory for him.
                            Think of "The scoure of the Shire" by Saruman after the war of the ring was already over; the Big Bad cannot win, but he'll make sure the players pay in kind.

                            The same goes for all the others, but outside of physical you can go a little deeper, except if you're considering breaking powers (such as Domination); usually confrontations against the BBB that arent physical wont involve death risk, so your BBB can come as high as 4 dots above the average social pools of your players (or 3 above the best "face" of the table), and that will usually mean he convinces third parties to turn on the players and such, and it's going to be a challenge to disarm his plans (as opposed to direct confrontation).

                            As for the mental pool, it depends on either that's for combat or not; 4 above is a challenge if not for combat, 3 if it is. This means the BBB gets a better security software than the players can, or is aways 2 steps ahead of them, or the player has to have some insane blind luck to be able to get a jump on him using Obfuscate (which for the Big Boss should ALWAYS be a bad idea).

                            As for Rivals, I define those as adversaries that are roughly at the same level as the players; usually, no single Rival is a challenge for the entire players' group, so they'll only ever make their move if they manage to isolate a single player (and I like to have Rivals that have a particular "affinity" to one of the players instead of all), but one thing that I like to do is the "anti-party", a GROUP of NPCs roughly the same "challenging rate" as the PCs, and I usually pair those with each single player as their "focus".

                            For Rivals, you either have them have the same average pools the players have, or 1 or at most 2 above. One trick I like to do here however is to use what I call "Reverse Rivals". It works like this: the Rival of a "Brick" player, instead of also being Physical, could be a social "Face" or a mental "Genius". Therefore, for example, if the player has prioritized Physical/Mental/Social, the Rival could instead have Social/Mental/Physical, and would challenge the player in an entirely different way; you can even min max this, giving him 1 or 2 dices ABOVE the average Physical Pool of the player (in his Social Pool) while giving him at the same time 1 or 2 dices BELLOW the player's Social in his Physical. That would make a pathetically weak Rival, but a master manipulator to confront the big strong player.

                            Bellow those are the Underlings, those should have 1 or 2 dice pools bellow the players. Now, it is entirely possible to have important Underlings of note however, those who are weaker than the players but trying to catch up with strong obstination. Such Underlings can still be a challenge, particularly for their annoying tendency to keep trying despite their failures (Hunters for example make a good example of challenging Underlings).

                            Bellow those are the Mooks, those are the faceless crowds that exist only as Cannonfodder against the players or minor inconveniences (the typical Guard). Those will always be bellow in everything, at least 2 or 3 dices bellow the players in everything (except in exceptional circunstances), and very limited in powers and resources.

                            BBBs can make use of Rivals, Underlings or Mooks mostly at will; Rivals will usually only use Underlings or Mooks, while Underlings will usually be the one "mini bosses" for groups of Mooks; however, Rivals and persistant Underlings can sometimes "summon" a BBB for assistance, if they are constantly taking a beat from the players.

                            Finally, the last category are the "Demi-Gods". Those are so above ALL the players that there's no challenge - therefore, no need for Stats AT ALL. The "game mechanic" here is simple: one of those shows up, you lose. Period. If they want something, you give to them; if not, they take, and there's nothing you can do about it.

                            Now, Demi-Gods dont just show out of the blue. Also, NEVER "summon" one to simply destroy the players - for example "I come here to replace the Prince, and I cant suffer his supporters to live, I need my next Pawn to be fully supported in this city; since you guys are supporters of the Prince, Im sorry but you gotta go". Dont do that. This kind of NPC serves better as a mystery - and he'll NEVER just outright burn the players alive with a funny eye. Even if the players ATTACK him, he will just effortessly subdue them, like an adult playing with children, and then make some condescending remarks like "just stay out of my way children... I have no time to waste on you... And in future, try to be smarter, or you wont last long in this world..."

                            As for the funny stuff - the supernatural mojo - anything goes, just make sure to have those fit the concepts of your NPCs and to balance those up to the "Challenge Level" they represent (in relation to the players). If your players are neonates with a single dot in 3 disciplines, so too would a Rival "Brick" Brujah only have a single dot in Potence and Celerity - and if he's NEVER going to challenge the players socially, there's no reason to bother with Presence. If the BBB is a Lasombra, he obviously will have high Tenebrosity, but other than that just assign ANY powers that would fit him, even outside the clan - so high levels of Potence, Fortitude and Celerity if he's physical, Dominate and Presence if social, Auspex and Obtenebration if mental etc. And in order to not need to check all powers constantly, just mark a few "signature moves", which is what he'll ALWAYS use - like Presence 5 for example (and ignore the rest if you never plan to use those).
                            You can also give them new, cool and unheard of powers too, no need to stick to tables; that's easier to justify for lvl 6+ disciplines or ones such as thaumaturgy or necromancy, but dont feel too bound by that thou.

                                    Originally posted by Asmodai View Post
                                    Even those don't need it. In my campaign notes major supporting characters and antagonists have way more story details and relationships then mechanical notes. They will usually have a trademark skill or trick that I might note which might denote power, but they don't need a shit. What their themes, desires and relationships is way more relevant than fixed numbers, and once you note those down the numbers will come to you naturally.
                                    At one point in time, I would have been horrified by this mentality, but it's one I try to adopt as much as possible now.

                                    The terrible thing for many STs to admit is that 90% of our preparation work is irrelevant to actually running the game. We provide more details in our game notes than is needed at the gaming table. Writing complete PC style character sheets for NPCs falls into that category.

                                    The reason most of us do that (and other superfluous things about the game) is because it's fun. (And because it's what see in sourcebooks for NPCs.) We like world building. We like creating characters and filling in dots on a character sheet. But at the end of the day, all we need to determine is "What size dice pool should I be using for this character for this type of action?" The Storyteller system actually makes this much easier than other game systems because its dice pool system makes a lot stats inherently generic.

                                    In terms of productive use of time, most STs only need to write up some very brief generic stats for major character types. Those will work at least 80% of the time. For that other 20%, you can go into more detail, at whatever level may be needed for what you want to do with them. STs shouldn't think that if they don't have completely statted NPCs that they are somehow "cheating". It's not.

                                    I'm actually a GM who massively overprepares when I run a game. The reason I do is I don't do "on the fly" very well. Some GMs are great at it, and their games never suffer. Others do it, but fail miserably because their "on the fly" decisions don't make sense and undermine the campaign. In order for me to do things "on the fly" well, I need to do a lot of prep work beforehand by working out the most likely things that will come up during game myself. But that generally is about major things about the setting. "PCs do stupid things, I need to figure out ahead of time what happens when they do the ones I've done myself or seen other players do at the table." "This thing is bound to come up, so I need to be ready how the NPCs will handle it." And so on. It's not about determining individual stats for individual characters.

                                    It's not a problem if STs have fun creating NPC stats, and want to do that. Or if more detailed mechanics actually help them. But what I discovered over the years is that very often a GM can get away with a lot less prep work than they might expect especially when it comes to grindy things like statting out individual characters.

                                                        Originally posted by Asmodai View Post
                                                        Don't make sheets for them. Assign them a pool for tasks they're good at (I.e. Physical 6, Mental 4, Social 3) and give them any power that seems suitable to them as a character. The fine nuances and mechanics are irrelevant. They are there to serve a narrative purpose, not to force you to have a million extra sheets to bean count.

                                                        I go one step even simpler, I rate (some) NPCs simply on Primary and Secondary Dice Pools, so it's as simple as 'Name 6/4'. Primary is for stuff they're good at, and Secondary is everything else. That just leaves things like choosing attacks/weapons and any special abilities.

                                                        Obviously for NPCs that the players are likely to interact with a lot I flesh them out a bit better but doing it this way saves a lot of time. Here's a snippet from my current campaign:

                                                        Mages
                                                        1. Augustus Koehne, Verbena Rune Mage (8/5)
                                                          Member of the Iron Circle
                                                          Arete 4
                                                          Spheres: Forces 4, Matter 3, Entropy 3, Prime 2
                                                        2. Oscar 'Leo' Schilling, Ghoul Sorcerer (8/5)
                                                          Member of the Thule Gesellschaft
                                                          Disciplines: Potence 3
                                                          Mythic Numina: Shapeshifting 3
                                                        As you can see for me the important points are their name, association, and powers - the dice pools are simply '(8/5)' are to give a rough idea of their relative power level and frankly most of the time I'd list just one number if I wouldn't forget what it was for!
