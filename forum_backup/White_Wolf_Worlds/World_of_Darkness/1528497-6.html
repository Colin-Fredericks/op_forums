        Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
        Because "success" isn't an objective thing. If you want to consider success "as many copies in as many hands as possible," the evidence is considerably different than if you consider success, "making a net profit," or, "making the best VtM game possible."
        ^This.

        I recently read Ben Riggs's "Slaying the Dragon", about the slow death of TSR back in the 80s and 90s. (Good book, highly recommend).

        One thing the book makes clear is that many of its products sold a fair number of units (at least at first), as far as the creatives working at TSR knew. What they didn't know - what TSR management refused to tell them - is that many of those same products were not, and likely could never be, profitable. Many of these had to do with unnecessary expenses, like leather-bound prestige books, or including VHS tapes or CDs in the box sets. And then the products price not reflecting these added expenses. So even if they sold gangbusters, they would never make their money back at the price being asked (and selling for what the products were actually worth would have meant selling fewer units).

        To be fair, as the book shows, there were many problems with the way TSR was run, that contributed to its slow decline into insolvency. (Including quirks seemingly unique to TSR, like the "loan" style arrangement it had with Random House).

        Another pertinent angle to TSR's decline, irrespective of sales, was how it supporting many different campaign settings simultaneously ended up cannibalizing its own fanbase. They would put out a new setting, usually in one or more box sets, in the hopes the various kinds of D&D worlds would attract new players. A "bait & hook" strategy, as Riggs calls it. That this would turn more people into D&D players. In reality, most people won't or can't juggle multiple campaigns, in different settings. If you invest lots of money into the Forgotten Realms, for example, you probably won't play in other settings. Either because you don't have the time for more than one campaign, or because even when you end one and start another, you're more likely to start a new one in the setting you know (and which has a bunch of other published material you haven't gotten a chance to explore).

        In effect, each new setting - almost a game unto itself - put new players into silos. It created Ravenloft or Forgotten Realms or Dark Sun or Birthright fans. Not D&D fans. Meaning no matter how successful those were, they weren't translating into a larger D&D fanbase, willing to buy lots of products. And because most product lines are never as profitable as their core product (none of the supplements for Ravenloft were as profitable as the Ravenloft boxed set, which in turn was less profitable than the AD&D core books), each new line was an increasing expense for diminishing returns. (It's why WotC these days usually only puts out one book for a given setting, unless it proves really popular or it's Forgotten Realms).

        A product can move units, and still not be profitable (or not profitable enough). Given TSR's unique situation, it was never enough. (Indeed, the same incentive structures it worked under were also killing it).

            Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post

            I actually felt Fall of London was an attempt to actually give some detail to a major event in the setting that had originally just been thrown in.
            The issue is if writers, designers, and leads are insistent on a metaplot with distinct canon events which fundamentally shape the game world, those metaplot events make extremely poor content for a game module. It mandates railroading players, and -- at best -- only offering aesthetic differences in outcome relevant only to the player characters in question. In which case (and it's always the case), just commission a companion novel. That's been a consistent factor in published game modules throughout WoD's history, and it's still consistent today.

            Few players are interested in a game in which their characters get ushered from one set piece to another, and ultimately get to watch from the sidelines as every problem is fixed (or broken beyond repair) at the end by one metaplot character or another. Tabletop roleplay thrives on player agency, and that's at unreconcilable odds with metaplot event modules which must by definition arrive at a predetermined conclusion with a predetermined resolution.

            That's where Warhammer/40K and Forgotten Realms goes right. Rulebooks are toolkits with information on a default world-state, and just enough context to explain the why's and how's of it. They leave metaplot the purview of fiction external to rulebooks, and there if players want immersion deeper than that which is necessary to play the game, but ultimately optional.

                            Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post

                            From my perceptive, you've literally described the worst Storyteller possible . Because the whole point of metaplot incorporation should be that players feel like they're actively a part of the world and able to influence the events happening around them...
                            It's not the ST, it's how the books were written from the beginning. If a workable ST has to break published content down to bare bones and rebuild to give players a sense of agency, it's not workable content from the perspective of providing a good chronicle/adventure path. Good fluff maybe, but not good game content.

                            At this point I've written literally dozens of threads about Transylvania Chronicles alone -- including when I ran it -- about the problems with it from a player agency standpoint, and how to make it a chronicle first and a World of Darkness theme park ride second, compared to how the module is published which puts the "theme park ride" aspect first. You've participated in a lot of those threads, so I know you're aware of them.

                            ...It also sounds like a spectacularly shitty ST that wouldn't be able to make the players feel like theyr'e able to influence events even if certain things happen beyond their control.

                            The Fall of London is also a weird example because the player characters can have probably more effect on how things play out than probably any other published module. You can result in the death of Mithras, the takeover of London by the Camarilla, the elimination of the SI, and many other possibilities...
                            And at the end, there has to be a "canon" resolution for metaplot to continue. Even if it's the rote standard White Wolf "shit happened, nobody knows exactly what, those involved disappeared" narrative punt which is the ultimate slap to the face of player agency as it essentially retcons out anything players might have done in the course of the chronicle.

                            At least put something akin to an "this is the canon ending, but proceeding otherwise pushes the chronicle into alternative continuity and it's on the storyteller to work out the narrative beats from then on" disclaimer somewhere. Much as how Transylvania Chronicles' book four has multiple sidebars on how to proceed from the last three stories in alternate continuity, as well as how to segue into a modern nights methuselah chronicle, or move straight into either Lair of the Hidden or Gehenna. But at that point, why even bother with the published module in the first place if it's to move into alternate continuity anyways?

                            ...It's a much more TSR/WOTC-esque module in the end because the player characters can basically do what those epic level campaigns for 5th Edition allow. You can slay Tiamat, fail, side with her or whatnot in THE RISE OF TIAMAT module just like you can do here. THE CURSE OF STRAHD is one of the all time most popular modules of all time (and based on the all time most popular RAVENLOFT) and has less ability to influence the outcome than the Fall of London...
                            This highlights the issue more than anything else.

                            In the former, regardless what player characters do Tiamat regains her full portfolio, and even if the PC's fail to banish her to Avernus someone else does. The latter isn't even a metaplot event, and in fact is something of an anti-metaplot event.

                            Characters from any setting can be drawn into Ravenloft, and their adventures in Ravenloft exist entirely separate from that setting. Strahd's titular curse is to exist in a quasi-time loop, repeating the same events over and again. The books go out of their way to highlight how the PC's romp is neither the first time it's happened nor will it be the last time; regardless what happens, Barovia will repopulate, Strahd will resurrect, and there is no way to actually break the cycle as Strahd simply cannot comprehend what he did to be confined in Ravenloft was wrong. If I've heard correctly, the latest re-release of the module even includes guidelines on how to run it a second time, under auspices the now high-level PC's return to put an early end to a subsequent cycle -- or were themselves corrupted and "punished" to try redeeming Strahd for all eternity.

                            I'd be interested in how people think metaplot should be handled in modules by contrast.
                            Well, I already threw my two cents in on this: keep metaplot and game content separate. Publish game content with a baseline stable world-state, and publish companion fiction for metaplot events that move the story forward and/or bridge editions. I'm unopposed to modules like Fall of London, but they need to be designed and written from the under the context of "this is what happens, these events are simply too big for one coterie to impact, but the story is in how PC's meet Final Death or by some miracle make it out the other side" opposed to "players get to flirt with affecting metaplot, but it's really just a facade of agency as things happen the way they're published to happen anyways."
