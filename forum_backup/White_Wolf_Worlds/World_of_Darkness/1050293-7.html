        If I run a game with an antagonist who espouses fascist ideals I'm secretly a fascist pushing a hidden agenda?
        Promotion isn't the same as "secretly pushing a hidden agenda."

        And, the answer to this question is more complicated. Antagonist vs. not antagonist is not the point. Those are narrative roles in the story. Antagonists can represent/promote positive traits (we tend to call these sympathetic and compelling villains because they have positive traits we can identify with as whatever their negative traits are that override those positive aspects).

        How you treat the espousal of fascist ideals is what matters to whether or not you're promoting fascism. Is the character an antagonist because of their fascism detracts from what you think are better ideals, or is the fascist part being treated as effectively good but the character is bad for some other reason?

        Characters within fiction are not hidden messages of what the writer truly feels.
        Generally because they're not hidden.

        Characters are not all representations of the authors. However characters are always representations of the author's feelings. You can't write a character that doesn't reflect your personal perspective, because every character you write is a creation of your personal perspective.

        That doesn't mean writing a fascist character makes you a fascist. It means how you write that character is going to reveal things about your personal perspective about fascism.

        Ever read a book with a serial killer character, do you think the author is a serial killer?
        It is exceedingly taxing on my ability to treat your posts charitably to actually think you're asking this in good faith.

        Even just sticking to the early material, the books are attempting to address a far deeper question than this: if someone's a serial killer, can they attempt to follow an ethical standard to channel their urges?

        Lindsay is arguing that if you have to kill (esp. considering the later books and the source of Dexter's urges), you should try to put that to a societal good by being a vigilante that goes after proven evil doers instead of risking your urges being let out on the innocent. But the setup of the character is important here. Dexter has to kill. He doesn't have a choice. His choice his who he's going to kill. You can argue different moral positions (such as just killing yourself), but it's a much more nuanced issue Lindsay explores than, "Dexter is a serial killer vigilante, so he's saying you should be one too!"

        Lindsay also had some reason for wanting to explore the central question of the Dexter series. I have no idea. But you don't just do that for no reason.
