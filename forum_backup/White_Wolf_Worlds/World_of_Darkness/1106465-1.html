    Originally posted by Saikou View Post

    Just as some general advice. Don't skip out on the mundane, think about how their adventures affect their sleeper lives, their friends and family, their work, their home life. Part of what makes the technocracy so powerful is how much influence they have on the stuff we take for granted. Does your player want to advance with their career? The technocracy can help with that. Are police taking a sudden interest in your life? The Technocratic Union can make the investigation &quot;go away&quot;. Want your name on the lips of every academic in Harvard? A bit of media attention to bring your Kickstarter campaign the money it deserves? Or maybe just a little extra cash to help you through the week? The Union provides. Helping the Union can give you a plethora of enticing benefits, as well as access to a whole slew of tech. Advanced security? Genius pills? The fastest internet speeds this side of the Digital Web? All of it can be yours.

    Granted, this style of material bargaining is much more the Syndicate's game, the NWO are no strangers to creating mutual agreements for a few diplomatic endeavours.

    After all, why brainwash when they already think you're the good guys?
    On this specifically, very yes. I want the campaign to be 1/3rd external supernatural, 2/3rds mundane/personal supernatural.

                            Well, Akashics are rather notorious for their strict discipline, adherence to the rules of Do, and their insistence on eastern style hands off teaching. This can be a very frustrating environment for a new Mage to struggle with. It's all of the rigour and expectation of the Hermetics with none of the support of one to one tuition.

                            This is especially the case if an initiate is interested in things their mentor disapproves of, such as tech of western Psychology . Their master would only tell them that they are failing, and their disregard of the old ways is the reason for their failure. Being told time and again that something you rely on makes you a disappointment can be enough reason to pull out the middle finger and walk away.

                            Perhaps they find the NWO method of psychology has much more in common with the Akashic principles of mental betterment than their masters lead on. Perhaps they even see that the Akashics methods can be explained with NWO psychological studies, and it's in fact the Tehcnocracy who is more open to these techniques and other traditional techniques being incorporated into normal practice while the Akashicks are being stuck up about how Do is the only real path to ascension.

                            The reality may be that the NWO only try to describe the Akashics techniques scientifically as a method to demystify it, and make it seem more "oh, I guess anyone can do it". Effectively making the masses trivialise the work and mental stamina that the Akashics practice into a series of self help book style Eastern Medicine stuff.

                            How aware of this your NWO lady is is up to you. Perhaps they are still stary eyed on how amazing the technocracy is, perhaps they actively want to bring the Akashics down a peg because she knows they aren't as special as they think hey are, and their arrogance is iritating her.

                            Alternatively, it could be more personal than this. Perhaps this agent was from Myanmar, and she saw first hand how brutal the Bhudist monks are in that region, bhudist monks backed by Akashic values. Perhaps a more fictional thing happened where she, her family, or other things she cares about where betrayed by these so called "enlightened few".

                            Or maybe it's a lot less insidious, maybe she tried some psychology, ended up liking it, and the technocracy is much more tolerant of religious views than Tradition propaganda would have you believe, at least as far as she knows.

                                    Session 2 complete. We didn't have the full 2 hours tonight as we had to finish everyone's character sheets off, but it worked really well - a lot of my prep wasn't necessary as the characters ended up in the right mood without much prompting.
                                    They awoke in the private hospital ward, recognising that it wasn't NHS, and Sarah became paranoid straight away when the first nurse she spoke to refused to talk to her about the incident and ran off to get someone more senior when she mentioned wildebeest.
                                    I decided that John had been buried under the entire tram-load of corpses and had a near death experience (that's what you get for choosing a euthanatos-orphan awakening) but the others were fine, although they were all strapped into these strange frames. Roe comes in after a brief conversation between the players, summons the friendly emergency workers who are all so pleased that the characters are OK, then dismisses them abruptly. She doesn't identify herself beyond being in "national security" and refers most of their concerns to "my superiors" or "the security blackout", then takes them each aside to an interview room to ask their version of events. They each give a different one - Laura flat-out denial that she remembers anything, sarah outrage, especially when she starts thinking she's a terror suspect, and john a morphine-addled sweating paranoia as he admits seeing some strange things - but the best bit is what the other two, left in the room, are saying to each other.
                                    Paranoia mounts, and they're left alone by Roe while she "reports to her superiors". That's when they start talking about how they should have coordinated their stories, shortly before discovering that there's cctv and they were probably being listened to the whole time.
                                    John scouts the loo in a fit of paranoia and discovers that the mirror is false. He tries to hang a towel on it subtly and fails.

                                    They discover the windows are barred.
                                    They try and fail to talk to the armed guards outside the door.
                                    They discover that their exo-suit bed-frames are extremely advanced and can be remote controlled.
                                    They decide by committee that they hate Agent Roe.
                                    They're ignored from late morning to night.
                                    They stew.

                                    Then, at 20 past midnight, they're woken by a "psst", and find a middle-aged afro-carribean cleaning lady in the room, mopping the floor.

                                    She asks, "would you like to get out?"

                                                        The only advice I would give is cast a wide net.

                                                        Personally, I try to avoid having specific requirements that the players can either succeed or fail at. Rather, I prefer to have the characters and setting exist, and see how the players approach it, and react from there.

                                                        This is probably not the best thing to do if you need the players to be in a certain place by the end of the second hour of real time, but it's still doable if the scale of possibility is influenced somewhat.

                                                        Rather than choose between Corr and Spirit escape routes, why not allow the players to decide? Have things open to them, let them weigh up the risks vs rewards, and allow the players to surprise you with their own hair-brained scheme. Pay careful attention to what and how they decide to do it, and always allow for some degree of success if it seems plausible enough. Usually it's much more fun to let the players get away with something, and then let their mistakes unfold as consequences later down the line, than to just hit them with a brick wall with the message "Wrong! Try again" etched on the side.

                                                        Some of the best games I've run involved my players frantically searching the book for ideas on how to exploit their spheres for whatever obstacle I placed in front of them. More often than not, the best and most effective solutions (or at least the most entertaining or spectacular) are ones that never crossed my mind.
