                Perhaps they are, but not in a fashion that can be solved by heavy handed aplications of Paradox. Solving that unbalance requires a more careful examination of how magick works. Among other things, you gain nothing by punishing quick cast even more.

                I've debated in VS threads long enough to know that with mages the difference in "power level" it's very relative (being a game that requires substantial ST fiat) and even with a lenient ST mages often requires a lot time and resources to compete with the other splats.

                Unprepared mages can't do a whole lot against a vampire. That's why they do so poorly in white rooms. Mages are perhaps among the weakests supernaturals out there as long as they depend on their skill to quick cast...It's well prepared mages the ones that can pull an almost unsurmountable leverage that crushes werewolves and elder vampires. And it's also these the ones that probably will have their Paradox reserve "well feed", increasing the disparity.

                Magick should be risky, by all means, and Paradox Dice are a good oportunity to hit that nail, but this risk shouldn't make mages weak nor squishier than they already are as consequence.

                Being sent to a Paradox Realm or erased from reality shouldn't be something that can happen because you forgot your bead and had to put a fight, but rather as a consequence of acts of great Hubris. Preferably as a consequence of lengthy rituals (that happen to be the gereatest power source mages have, the one that trully puts them above all others). And it has every right to be a story of it's own.

                    I'm not really a fan of making the Cauls something easier to do, as mentioned earlier in the thread. It doesn't really make sense to me as something that one's Avatar would steer one toward, given that the process is supposed to actually invert the Mage's Avatar. I feel like the Nephandi should be treated as something that fundamentally perverts both Mage and Avatar, in a way that can't just happen accidentally.

                    I'd like to see more of an exploration of the way power can corrupt outside of the Nephandi. I think there are seeds of that in the game already: the way that both of the more human factions, the Traditions and the Technocracy, are their own worst enemies. The way the Traditions have alienated the Disparates. The fall of the Janissaries and House Helekar. The power plays in Doissetep, and even maybe in the idea that Porthos knew Heylel wasn't guilty but letting everything happen because of his jealousy over Eloine. Heck, even in the way that Mistridge's fall happened as much because of internal Hermetic politics as the Craftmason's rebellion.

                    That'd be the corruption that I think the Avatar could cause: the idea that as a Mage becomes more powerful, they become more isolated, selfish and full of hubris that they lose track of higher ideals for their own personal desires and ambitions. The dark side of personal godhood.

                    Basically, I'd like to see more of an examination as to why characters like Mark Hallward Gillan went rogue from their Traditions and what that could mean for the player characters.
