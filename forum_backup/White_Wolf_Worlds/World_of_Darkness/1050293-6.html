                            If anyone wanted my perspective, everything in the entire world is political, and as the writer of a fictional world chooses literally every aspect of that world, our fictional worlds are political too.

                            If my main character has red hair, I chose to give her red hair, because I created literally everything in my world, from nothing. Maybe it's because I have red hair and I'm making my character a particular self-insert--side point, literally every character a writer creates is a self-insert to one degree or another. Maybe it's because there's some symbolism behind red hair in the world I've created. Maybe it's because I want her to stand out and I recognize that red hair is the rarest hair color. There is still a reason for every single thing I specify. I might not even recognize the reason. But as I am a human, not a robot, and humans are, by our very nature, political, every choice I make in my texts is a choice and is subject to your analysis.

                                Originally posted by Dusksage View Post
                                There's one question that, unless I misse it, hasn't been asked yet that I feel needs to be: Who are these Preludes for?

                                They were marketed to be introductions to the world of Masquerade and Ascension for new players who've never heard of the setting before, and if so then what themes were gone over in the Preludes? How exactly did they sell VtM and MtAs? Taking all of the design decisions in the best possible light even, or at least the most liberal benefit of the doubt, what exactly was told by these stories?
                                ...since no one seems to want to answer this, I will. Especially since I still consider myself a relative newcomer to the WoD setting.

                                Both stories, at their core, maintain the central story arc that a beginning player character would face in the VtM and MtAs settings. The VtM one being about needing to adjust and adapt to your new life as a nocturnal monster and all the awful things they would have to do because of it. The MtAs on how there's a war of belief and it concerns people who have the literal Will to make their beliefs reality, and how a beginning PC needs to decide which side they fall on said war. These would be the kind of things that would come up a lot in your typical VtM or MtAs game.

                                Strip everything away with the central story and I personally think it was successful on that end...

                                The issue is, of course, everything else. In this case, the framing of the narrative.

                                VtM it's the execution of the narrative...and is something already been discussed way too much here and that I won't get into. For MtAs it's framing the narrative around the refugee crisis in Europe and the Middle East.

                                The thing about bringing "politics" with these products is how...absolute they are. That they portray their "politics" as: "This is the way how things are, and should be. Anything else to the contrary of what I say is inherently WRONG. And there is nothing you can say or do that can prove otherwise". Which, in a piece of interactive fiction like these Preludes, is a big turn-off.

                                You are essentially forcing a viewpoint on the player(s). Whether they like or not. In the VtM Prelude, this is an issue with the game design in the last section of the game. In the MtAs Prelude, it's by declaring that only one perception of the refugee crisis is the correct choice. And any other choice is the wrong choice, and always will be.

                                Although what's confusing to me is that this is nuWW's first notable product release. And with the subtitle of "Prelude" it makes you think that there's going to be more after this...but, isn't the Werewolf video game nuWW's only major digital product on the horizon? If so, why isn't there a WtA Prelude?

                                And it's endlessly amusing to me that Mage was one of the first digital products by nuWW since for me, Mage is the least suitable property to be made into a video game.

                                                        Ok, I re-read the end of the book itself, because I wasn't sure that the hunters actually killed any gypsies in the book ( I remembered that there was a firefight in the Coppola-movie) and rightly so, because no, they didn't. The gypsies sported knives and pistols and aggressively defended D, they didn't shown any inclination to run away "from the rich men with guns". The hunters called for surrender repeatedly but didn't shoot anyone. Van Helsing didn't shoot anyone. There was no slaughter of innocent gypsies by the Triumphant White Men. When the hunters pushed through the gypsies to D, Mr. Morris got stabbed and that was all the blood spilled. So, I'm sorry in fact the scene you based your interpretation wasn't in the book. I'm not 100% sure, but I think the only ones killed in the whole book by the hunters were only the vampire brides, Lucy and D.

                                                        As for how much Dracula is about "immigrants steal our women", I have serious troubles with that, because the fact that Dracula was Eastern European didn't get the fraction of focus in comparison to the fact that he was a vampire. The Carpathian origin was more or less just an exotic place on the edge of the modern world where ancient horrors could still walk the night. In a sense that is a distinctively Imperial thought, but I won't call it downright racist. I don't say that the fear from immigrants didn't influence Stoker, because it certainly did to some extent, but it's not the focus of the book in my eyes and certainly not some nefarious underlying message.

                                                        As for Stoker's picturing of Carpathian ethnicities, as a local, I'd say he didn't do it in an overly negative way (honestly he didn't do it in much length, it was more showed as exotic curiosity for tourists) and get it mostly quite close to reality (and reality isn't nice every time, so a not entirely flattering portrayal of any group is okay to me).


                                                        For closing it, I want to address the "everything is political" notion. We won't agree on that, but I figure it's worth saying that in my eyes that's a quite lopsided view. Yes, every author is and was influenced from the surrounding socio-political environment, his/her upbringing, etc. but that's not the same as everything having a political message or statement as a goal. In fact, it says more about the consumer side to me if one sees political messages in everything and looks at everything through the lens of political worldview and forcing everything into that framework. It's somewhat like how Marx simplified history into the struggle of classes.
