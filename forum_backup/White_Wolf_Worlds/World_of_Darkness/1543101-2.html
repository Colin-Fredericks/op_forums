        But what defines the potency of the effect?. Rolling Arete it's the normal way, I think Avatar could work as a replacement in this case (after all, recent Awakened don't have strong Arete).

        What about if we add Paradox to it?. That way there could be an incentive to actually have some dots on your wheel, in spite of the risks, because it could allow one to have a lot of "instant" power (that unprepared mages normally lack).

        Although it's not a power that you can control very well, I do think that at least there should be some way to determine some degree of general Intent. Otherwise nobody it's going to use it, considering it's risks. The wizard on the "Last Unicorn" couldn't know what magic would do, but he really trusted magic wasn't going to do something useless or anticlimactic, like dropping an anvil on the unicorn, or NOT doing anything at all to protect her, or the movie would have ended on a "Magic, WTF!?" , note ... and that wasn't the idea

        I would take the mage's intent. Then use Ressonance, Avatar's Essence, and the Avatar's own goals and relationship to the mage, as things that could deviate from it (or the "Will" of Magick, as it may be).

        *With "ridding Paradox" I was thinking along the lines of Vampire "ridding the Beast". A way to have some degree of control in exchange for a different risk.

        As an idea Taking the "Black Hand" as basis:

        On a backlash, there could be a mechanic (perhaps tied to your Arete or Avatar rating) to suggest one or more "aspects" of the resultant spell. An "aspect" could be something from the magickal effects tables, such as Damage, Duration or Targets, could also be things like removing one Sphere from the Effect, or determining which Ressonance it's going to be used to colour the backlash (providing that these do matter, and that you have more than one)
        I.E: you could determine that Paradox isn't going to cause damage directly. Or you could declare more than one targets + no Correspondence, and suddenly your Paradox becomes a potential problem for everyone arround you

        Perhaps this could be restricted to (or facilitated by) some kind of Focus. Like how Dataweaver suggests "Wild Magick" for Faith based Focuses, it could be the special thing for a fourth category of Focus:

        I'm thinking about some Akashicks and Extatics here. These are typically regarded as "mystical", however a lot of the belief systems they embrace could be considered the opposite of that. Mystical thinking it's all about the magick comming from the inside, it's centered about the "self empowerment", or cultivating a very direct connection to "the divine" to the point you don't need intermediaries at some point. But a lot of ideas about "enligthenment", especially oriental ones, could be regarded as the opposite of that, as the "self" it's an illusion, the work it's centered not arround "inner power", but rather about something that could be better regarded as "freedom". Freedom to such an extreme extent as to want to be free from "causality" and even "continuity".

        It could be cool if these types of belief had something "paradox related". As you free yourself from the illusion of the world in different ways, you can flow trough it's mechanisms more skillfully

            Some references:

            1. What I've been referring to "Wild Magick" is the Optional Rule: Wild Talent on M20 pp.527–528. Rules are very light: for a scene or two, the character commands a dice pool that's one to three higher than usual, accesses Spheres she doesn't have, and access more ranks in her existent Spheres than she has; the drawback is that the Storyteller makes all the decisions.

            2. M20 p.534 gives us the Paradox acronyms. It stays that by default, Mage assumes HAB and PBD. Wild Talent is, almost by definition, an exception to this, and is much closer to RBD: the player states the results he's after, and the Storyteller decides how, or even if, the Magick achieves those results.

                            Indeed, there are a number of contributing factors to Paradox.
