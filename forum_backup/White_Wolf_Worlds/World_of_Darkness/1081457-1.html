                For what its worth our group has recently house ruled that 1 additional language is learned at Academics 1, 3 and 5 for free. The basis being that to graduate from high school on a college prep curriculum you need to take a foreign language, which is more or less the 1st dot of Academics. While the XP "bump" between the first and second dot does mean that if you're buying languages with the Linguistics model and starting at Linguistics zero (and a character with a native language, characters that aren't born human do not always get the default free fluent language), that your third language is slightly cheaper than your second language, the scaling causes further languages to continually cost less:

                                            First language: free.
                                            Second language: 3 XP
                                            Third language: 2 XP
                                            Fourth and fifth: 2 XP per language
                                            Sixth through ninth language: 3/2 XP per language
                                            Tenth through seventeenth languages: 1 XP per language

                                            So the old model doesn't follow your own statements about the rate of acquisition where there's a plateau effect, as languages get cheaper and cheaper in XP value.

                                            Neither model is actually realistic, because XP isn't realistic to real learning in general.

                                            All major meta-studies demonstrate that languages with a historical or syntactic relationship to ones own are acquired faster.
                                            Meta-studies are about confirming the results of individual studies for the key scientific goal of ensuring that results are repeatable. They show that despite any variation between individual studies, that they all support (or don't, as the case may be) a scientific consensus on the theory involved. A meta-study that shows a large amount of consistency in supporting individual hypotheses to support a broader theory doesn't mean anything to whether or not the results are universal. A meta-study just increases the confidence that the consensus result is the most accurate estimation of how prevalent the effect is.

                                            I've never read an account of such a disability, how does it affect their acquisition of closer languages vs further ones? What do you mean by closer, other Indo-European languages?
                                            As I already stated, my father and sister have trouble with muddled rules in systems that are too close to each other. For my sister, learning French after Spanish was extremely difficult (she literally failed her first French class which lead to her being officially diagnosed) because the commonalities between the two languages only obscured the differences. She aced Hebrew (which is a radically different language from English or Spanish) because it was easier for her to keep the rules separate. Language A follows rules A, language B follows rules B, and language C follows rules C. Learning the languages as discrete entities was easier for her than learning them as one building off the other; negating any speed up that would have happened.

                                                            Restating, what linguistics says is:

                                                            (a) Acquisition of an L2 imparts skills than can be used for L(n) acquisition, improving the rate of that acquisition. The improvement does not seem to vary across syntactic or historical relationships, i.e. there is a "constant" improvement, coming from acquiring language acquisition skills.
                                                            (b) Acquisition of L(n)s are affected by the historical relationship of that L(n) with one's own L1.
                                                            (c) There is an observed "confusion" effect when learning similar languages for some people. However studies do not show this "overcoming" (a).
