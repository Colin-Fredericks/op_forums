    "WoD 20" portrayal of the real world

    07-12-2014, 04:22 PM
    Preface.

    Ah, Anniversary Editions of cWoD gamelines! To say that after their announcement a few years ago “there was much rejoicing” would be an enormous understatement. All die-hard fans who, so to speak, have kept the light of the original “World of Darkness” bright through all these years really expected a lot from the various “20s”. Not just another chance to revisit well-known settings – there were enough books for that already. No, I will be so bold to suggest, that all those people wanted “Their Game” in pure, platonic ideal sense of the word – without all numerous flaws and inconsistencies of the previous editions, up to date for our modern rapidly changing time and, yet, the same in spirit and essence.

    I first learned about WoD long time ago – in 2001, via my elder brothers borrowing from friends an obvious Diablo-clone known as “VtM: The Redemption”. While I won’t consider such CRPG sub-genre as “Hack’n’Slash RPG” as some sort of deep, thought provoking art piece, I’m also find it enjoyable sometime. But this very game tore all stereotypes and thought-patterns that I’ve always associated with such games. There was a feeling of belonging to some even bigger game universe, gleaned through often convoluted way the game tried to present its own plot and background. But most shocking of all (at least for the younger me) was the unavoidable fact of your hero’s transformation into the vampire (and how!). I still remember, how I asked my brother not to sell “human” health potions that he encountered during his further playthrough. Why? Because I was dead sure that, maybe in the middle of the game, or a little bit further, our brave Kristof would be transformed back into mortal. Oh, my naivety!

    We’ve played the game for some time and then my brothers returned it. I’ve forgot about all this vampire business until 2004. Prior to the launching of “VtM: The Bloodlines” one of the magazines I’ve bought regularly published an article about the WoD universe. The “Big Three” game lines were discussed at length, others got lesser attention and there were also some very nice illustrations. But for me most awesome of all was that on CD that came with the magazine there were a “VTM Quickstart” pdf, some charsheet scans (of all Dark Ages gamelines!) and MET version of WtA (2nd ed). It was 2004, I was 17 – a very romantic age, they say. What I’ve read blew out my mind. I’ve never played DnD (but knew what it was), and started playing table top RPGs only in 2002. WoD became my second game system that I’ve ever played.

    Of all the infatuations that I’ve acquired in 2004 now only 3 remains – Cthulhu Mythos, WarHammer 40k and WoD.

    My first “paper” WoD book that I’ve ever read was “The Ends of Empire” for WtO. I own WtO cores of both eds. Personally, I’ve always loved the 1st ed core more because of it’s illustrations, awesome setting history piece and feelings of sweet melancholia and nostalgia. The fact that my copy of WtO 1st ed bears the marks of its previous owner(s), like stains from some unidentified snacks didn’t devalue it in my eyes.

    The latest addition to my collection of printed WoD books occurred a year and a half ago under very unusual circumstances. Knowing that I like WoD (after all, he was my player for a long time!) one of my friends managed to buy me a special present for the upcoming End of The World of 2012. He and his wife, plus another of my friends came all their way to the military base where I at that time was serving my stint in Army. They presented me with garishly wrapped bundle and asked me not to open till The End of the World. I’ve managed to restrain myself for 3 long days. After that I unceremoniously exhumed my presents from their paper grave. What I’ve found was just incredible – Mage 1st ed Core and Mage Storyteller’s Handbook! As I’m printing these they are lying safely on my bookshelf unaware of where they have spent several months and what kind of journey it was for them.

    I’m writing all these bits of personal history just to explain one thing – I really, really love WoD despite its numerous flaws. And I really, really, really hoped that this new “Anniversary Ed” thing would transform “The WoD that Was” into “My Game”. I’ve hoped that a lot of system flaws, cranky rules, plot holes, batshit crazines of some authors that was allowed to assume printed form would be made “poof!”. But most important for me, was the desperate hope that this time all those fine people that are working on WoD-20 products would avoid hamfisted, unbelievable and often quite offensive portrayal of real world countries, their histories and people that live there.

    You see, I’m Russian, from Russia who lives in Russia. To say that the portrayal of all things Russian in cWoD was horrible, lame, prejudiced, biased and full of stereotypes would be like saying that “a lot of people died in WW2”. But, you know what? I actually found some excuses for all those authors who had the misfortune to mention Russia and its neighbors in their books. “It's not their fault, really. Well, they didn’t have Google or Wikipedia in 90s, or anyone Russian speaking on hand while they had to write about things they don’t know. And, besides, other countries/ethnicities often suffered the same poor treatment. Or just author’s laziness.”

    Naturally, I was really hopeful that now during our Global Age with its near limitless opportunities and all-pervasive Internet new cWoD books would give my country a justice and portray it realistically, not like some backward Ruritania. Also, I really, really hoped that this time around, in this form of art that is writing RPGs, our good developers and authors would avoid some widespread stereotypes and misconceptions that are often presented as “well known facts” and “common knowledge” about Russia, that previous books of cWoD were literally choke-full.

    The main attributes of Russian stereotyping in all forms of fiction are:
    1. Vodka. In Russia everyone drink it like water, from newborns to the elderly.
    2. Bears. Thousands of them! In Soviet Russia (tm) bears wander freely on the city streets in ushanka’s, drink vodka, ride unicycles and kick the crap out of everyone with their trusty balalaykas.
    3. Balalaiaka – every Russian owns one and can play it. It’s like “Natural skill” for all Russians.
    4. Matryoshka – the only toy Russian children play.
    5. AK-47. The weapon of “Team Evil” (tm), dastardly stolen by the Soviet evil genius from the bright and talented German engineers.
    6. Kirillic alphabet – is just the Latin one, only more primitive. All complaints about grammatical inaccuracies are promptly ignored.
    7. KGB. A sinister Order of Evil that have spread its tentacles all through the Free world. Its agents are fanatically devote to the Motherland and Party, are spiteful, crafty and nearly indestructible. Nearly all Russians are connected with KGB in one form r another.
    8. Winter. Russia is always cowered in snow, no matter what time of the year or place. Russians can bring snow and chill with them even abroad – a well known fact widely believed by the Western Enlightened Public at least since the Great War.
    9. Ushanka (and other “typical Russian clothes”). Russian are either too poor (or too stupid) to wear anything else. With the communist simbology.
    10. Churches with onion domes and wooden huts (izbas). The must have in any portrayal of Russia. Without them audience may not even believe that it’s Russia (even with bears in ushankas drinking vodka in winter).
    11. Ivan the Terrible and Oprichnina – another proof of the “unique bloodthirstiness and brutality” of all Russians.
    12. Cossacks – Elite of the Russian Army (even in the Soviet period), the bearded papakha wearing bloody bastards good only for staging pogroms.
    13. The Red Army – consist slightly less then entirely of wearing winter-uniform (despite climate and time of the year) poor sods of all ages (as if people here serves in Army their entire life) riding either Bear Cavalry, “Mammoth” tank or “Kirov” war zeppelin. Russians ultimately don’t know how to fight and are using exclusively “zerg-rush” tactics.
    14. Red color, “Hammer and Sickle” – must be everywhere. Even in portrayal of modern Russia – otherwise, how would enlightened Westerner realize it is Russia?
    15. Everyday life – all Russian men are hulkish brutes that work either for KGB or for Mafiyah or for some combination of the two. Russian women are either a seductive femme-fatales with the top-model looks, or just ugly as Hell. Naturally, all work for KGB, but constantly dream of defection.
    16. Beard. All Russian men must be bearded. Period. Nothing, even facts and logic, can go in the way of it.
    17. “Comrade” and “Nazdaroffye” – Russians really say that all the time – if Hollywood is sure of it, then why doubt?



    These and some other no less silly stereotypes were often found in cWoD books, but, as I’ve already said – I won’t judge them.

    What I want to talk about here is how the “Anniversary WoD”, i.e. the cWoD produced by the Onyx Path team deals with national stereotyping issue and all inconsistencies in the portrayal of real world and history (Dark Ages are coming…) on the example of Russia. For me – it’s a big deal, because the book may be even well-written, or contain some really strong or tear-jerking message – but it’s all momentarily lost for me, when I read something extremely stereotyping (or just plain untrue). As I’m sure on this message board there are users and WoD fans from all around the globe, I would like to hear their own “WTF I’m reading about my city/country/subculture/people?!” moments, they’ve got after reading new “20s” books.

    In my next post I would discuss what I’ve found in new “V20” books concerning Russia and environs and what I think about it.

                  VtM-20
                  1. Dust to Dust.


                  After his uncompromising, brutal and violent “Tribebook Red Talons” and “Breedbook Rokea” Matt “Black Hat” McFarland became "Chuck Norris of WtA gameline". The only things I’ve ever heard from the people who read his Wer-books were “Hell yeah! That’s the way to portray Wers! Matt is a great author!”. And, honestly, I cannot disagree with them – Mr. McFarland is really very good WtA author. Also, his advises and short essays on how to run/play games that appeared in various books and on his personal blog (like “What makes a beautiful player?”) are often well-written and useful. And the parts that he’d written in “Spirit Slayers” for the HtV are another proof that he still can deliver an excellent game materiel.

                  Well, at least when he is writing for gamelines that:

                  a) Suit his quite specific style
                  b) He is actually interested in and knows a lot of stuff about.

                  The first cracks in the nearly perfect visage of Matt “Chuck Norris” McFarland that I had for a long time appeared after reading Dark Ages Werewolf. Well, I understand perfectly, why one of the main WtA authors was asked to not only became one of the authors of this historical corebook for the Werewolf line, but also as the developer of all Dark Ages game line, i.e. kinda guy in charge of all crunch/fluff things. Matt has his own distinct writing style so you can unmistakably notice where the parts that he actually wrote are. And one of the lines in the Circle of the Red description (written, supposedly, by him) about the attacks on the greatest Warder of the Men city septs/caerns goes:

                  “As the years passed, caerns in London, Kiev, Moscow, Beograd (sic!), Cologne and Paris came under attack and were claimed by this new foe”
                  Wait, what? And I’m talking not about the typo here. I’m talking about mentioning Moscow in the same line as the other large medieval European cities. I understand that Moscow is probably one of the few Russian cities that the average American has ever heard off, but I was under the impression that game developers and authors of the game set in the historical time period maybe should do some research beforehand? Yes, I know – that’s sounds crazy! Mentioning a developing but still pretty much frontier town that had been founded some years prior to 1147, that had often changed owners and that had a total population of about 5000 max (if you count nearby estates and villages) along with some long established and more populous cities (Kiev as the most populous Russian city had 40k citizens prior to the Mongol invasion) makes no sense. Why mention Moscow, but not, say, Berlin? It was founded approximately in the same time as Moscow and had, probably, the same demographics and status as well. Mentioning Berlin will also make no sense at all and, AFAIK it’s never mentioned in any of the Dark Ages books.

                  If you think that mentioning of Moscow in this instance was some sort of odd mistake, an easily forgotten case of a bad research that has nothing to do with the rest of the book, then how can you explain this passage from the Lithuania’s description?

                  “Lithuania sits to the south of Latvia, between the empire of Tartars and Muscovy and Christendom”.
                  Erm, should I unnecessarily remind everyone that the game of DA:W is set in 1230? Long before the Mongolian invasion? And Moscow at that time was just another small town of Vladimir-Suzdal princedom? The way the author here (I’m not sure if it was Matt, but he, as the chief developer, is nonetheless responsible) describes Lithuania is correct… for the 14 centaury. Also, what’s the matter of singling out “Muscovy” as not being part of the “Christendom”? Russians, what, have experienced some kind of unexpected and un-documented pagan revival while no-one was looking?

                  And that was only the beginning of downfall of Mr. McFarland from his pedestal of the “Unsullied WoD Writer”. Then someone decided that it would be such a swell idea to invite him to write something for the “Infinite Tapestry” book for the Mage… which he did with all the subtlety of a wrecking ball while treating the old material. Finally, he finished off the CtD line in the “Time of Judgement” book with his signature style, i.e. pretty much ignoring nearly everything that was written before hand (i.e. don’t givin’ a damn to all this “Game of Thrones meets War for Independence”… thing) and just gave us a story about Palpatin-Milege, 2 “whimpers” and 2 “bangs!” that are, despite all his attempts to deny it, were really just a “FeyHammer 30k”.

                  And this worthy individual was asked to write an adventure for the “reborn” VtM20. After all, he’d written… some stuff…as one of the co-authors… for VtM in the past! Long, long time ago, nothing memorable, but he did!

                  And “Dust to Dust” probably (just probably!) might have become a good well-written adventure if the good people of the “Onyx Path” hired someone with the nerves of steel, who’s job would be to use a cattle prod on Mr. McFarland every time he goes bat-shit off topic in fluff or starts to imagine that players need the WtA level stats for antagonists.

                  And of course, enough electrically charged motivation would have done something to this character:

                  “Lazlo Varga
                  Lazlo Varga died years ago in Detroit, but that didn’t stop him. A mortal necromancer of some skill before his death, the bullet in his heart did nothing but force his hand. He activated a ring he had stolen in Kiev some years back — a ring that supposedly belonged to Rasputin himself. According to reports, the ring allowed its wearer to survive any wound.”
                  Oh, how original! A typical Eastern European Evil Character (tm) named after some real-life person! And look – this book is so faithful in its early VtM editions references that it even makes Rasputin homage! It’s another “well-known fact” for the Enlightened Western Audience that Rasputin was “night indestructible Russian Mad-Monk/Evil Sorcerer with dreadful powers and enormous libido”. That he was so hard to kill had nothing to do with
                  1. The low-grade Cyanide dissolving in pastries that Rasputin ate in Yusupov’s house.
                  2. The first “mortal shot” was dealt by prince Yusupov (a well know transvestite) with his lady’s Derringer.
                  3. The final shots were delivered by the leader of rabid anti-Semitic and chauvinistic party Purishkevich who had very bad eyesight.


                  No! It was magic! Speaking of which how did the (supposedly!) Rasputin’s ring turned out in Kiev? Or, what, it was just another way to show the vast geographical knowledge of Russia by the author which he already demonstrated in his Dark Ages books? Hey, more importantly – would the plot of the adventure itself suffered if all this “Rasputin connection” or mention of Kiev were left out? Nope, not at all. So… what was the point? To convince readers even more of this character sinister Eastern Europeaness by making references to obvious stereotypes? What was the point?

                  And how should I understand this:

                  “If the characters wish to study his necromancy, they find that it has its roots in Eastern European magical practices. Though it’s different in theory and basis from most forms of Necromancy that vampires practice, it all works the same from a game perspective.”
                  Uhmm, Mr. McFarland, I don’t know if you will ever read my post, but, nevertheless, I’d like to ask you about all these “Eastern European magical practices”. You see, as an Eastern Europe (as if it’s some sort of homogeneous territory!) resident I can’t recall any necromantic practices in the region countries’ folklore. Yeah, shocking, I know, but myths and fairy tales here dodn’t mention rituals for raising undead legions of marauding zombies or making restless souls of the departed to make your bidding.

                  How did our power-hungry, brutal and merciless Eastern European necromancer (honestly, I can’t say this aloud without laughing) looks like – or more importantly – on which real life person is he based? Hungary, after all, is a country where all kind of people live, so his appearance may be just anything the author decides to give him! Did he look like the great Hungarian poet, patriot and revolutionary Sándor Petőfi? Or Karoly Takacs (they have this nice Badass of the Week strip about him)?

                  Naah – are you kidding? Did you, after reading so far, expect anything but even more clichés?

                  “Lazlo is a muscular, solidly built man in his late 40s. He shaves his head and wears a full beard… He dresses in simple clothing and expensive, sturdy boots. He speaks with a thick Hungarian accent.”

                  So, basically he is just another lame “Rasputin” from the “Hellboy” movie, which is also just another “hulkish standard-issue Eastern European Bad Guy”. Was it done deliberately by the author, to save all future Storytellers that would run “Dust to Dust” some time, by allowing them to say: “Ok, players – you see Rasputin from the “Hellboy”” “Ooooh! He must be a bad guy!”

                  What was all this point of creating some stereotypical “Eastern European” character for the VtM adventure? In what way does the fact that he is a Hungarian influence the story? In no way whatsoever – he could be just as easily made Portuguese, Albanian or Armenian. Lazlo Varga is a walking stereotype, a two-dimensional NPC that is just a collection Eastern European tropes, bringing home to players and storytellers alike ages old idea, that “Eastern Europe is Mordor of the Earth! Everything is baaaaaaad here!”. This kind of portrayal is either a demonstration of laziness and/or of unshakable beliefs of the author in all stereotypes he is referencing here. Strangely enough, but I don’t consider all these kind of cheesiness as a mark of “doing justice” and “reviving the spirit” of VtM. All these makes already far from perfect (to put it mildly) adventure even worse.

                  But Mr. McFarland’s “outstanding” material just pale before another VtM20 product I gonna discuss in my next post.
