                        Originally posted by Cogliostro View Post
                        The original Gaki and Bushi were pretty much Kill Whitey and "why don't they act like Cainites" even back in First Edition. And the point was that the First Editons were a horrifying mess of preconceptions, stereotypes, racism and utter cluelessnes about the world beyond America.
                        I don't think you're using the term first edition correctly. The Bushi, for example, are already second edition material -- the 2nd ed corebook came out in 1992 and Dark Alliance: Vancouver was published in 1993.

                        Likewise, the original writeups of the Independent clans in the first edition Players Guide are, as a general thing, less cringeworthy than the various second edition clanbooks. The Ravnos, for example, were not originally conceived as "gypsy vampires" -- while the writeup mentions that Ravnos had latched onto the cultures of various traveling peoples (the Rom among them), they tended to get rejected by the people more often than not. The original Ravnos weakness was not compulsive lying and theft, but an eldritch aura of unease -- much like the nWoD Nosferatu weakness. What's more, the initial Ravnos writeup implied that some of the injustices done to Romani people throughout history happened because they were exploited by the Ravnos parasites among them.

                        Similarly, the Giovanni were originally the modern Borgias, not "Italian mafia vampires." Their writeup doesn't even mention the mob -- that's material from the second edition Giovanni clanbook. The Assamites and Followers of Set were described as originally hailing from a specific culture, but having long since diversified into something else than just the "Arab clan" and the "Egyptian clan." It's curious how all of the Independents became more stereotypical and sometimes outright racist during the second edition run, leaving the revised edition to clean up the mess made during the previous iteration of the rules, rather than what was originally presented in the first edition.

                                    Requiem (mostly during the late 1st edition and 2nd edition) got a lot of things right, and I wouldn't feel the need to occasionally jump ship to Masquerade for some chronicles, if Requiem paid more attention to fleshing out a concrete toolbox setting. The Wicked Dead book, for example, was great and useful, but it feels like a D&D Monster Manual, that doesn't do the fourth wall any favors... They were a minor template without a powerstat, and there was no organization or culture inherent to these creatures. For some reason, a lot of nwod creatures are comfortable with voluntary isolation for no apparent reason whatsoever...

                                    The corebook and the Secrets of the Covenants slightly departed from the city crawler recipe, by mentioning the Sons of Phobos outside their respective book (Blood Sorcery) in the Jiang Shi write-up. Still, that game needs more of what Masquerade has too much of, namely some basic continuity (don't need 4 slightly different versions of the Strix across the books, but would love some fluff that plays down the birdie aspect and more the vampiric demon aspect and how they plot to bring down Kindred society for example) and more of the Requiem clanbook love in the form of concrete NPCs who do more than just try to hold some petty, seasonal title in their respective domains and nothing more. King of the playground doesn't mean much to adults after all...

                                    Anyway, enough of me derailing the thread. From my (scarce) WoD 1st ed experience, the books got the formula right. I agree with Elphilm, second edition often went gonzo stereotypical for no good reason, while some of Revised (and early Requiem) felt like it hated itself.

                                    I like the idea of non-Kindred eastern Vampires, but doing such a concept justice would mean digging deeper than the cultural surface, into philosophical, theological, and metaphysical differences between said cultures. How can you possibly teach damnation to a person who does not believe in sin?

                                            Originally posted by Marcus Smythe View Post
                                            Is it possible to write a game set in other cultures that would not anger people?
                                            Yes. Look at the CofD. One of the advantages of the clean slate it had, was to head off this sort of thing from the beginning. Which isn't to say that the CofD never angers people, or is batting 100% on this, but the culturally problematic stuff is by far an exception. Not all if this is research (though that helps). A lot of it is not linking general character groups to specific real world cultures in the first place; instead the rules allow for specific adjustable and/or optional groups that can be used for modifying the game to a local culture. When the authors want to right settings for other cultures, they have all the tools they need to do so in the extant material. It is designed to be adapted to locations (and even eras) of choice.

                                            What level of scholarship and research do we expect of people who are, ultimately, writing pretendy-fun-time game books set in a world similar, but not identical to our own?
                                            Enough to treat the material with respect. Nobody's actually looking for perfection, but enough knowledge about the subject matter to actually portray a world similar but not identical to our own, instead of a world populated by misinformed stereotypes from our own.

                                                I wrote a really long post, so I thought I'd summarize instead.

                                                There is a fine line between realism and racism, sensitivity and bland back story.

                                                It's been difficult for both worlds to strike perfection, so I don't suggest going into either extremes.

                                                RPGs need realism and stirring critical thought in the minds of players to compete with modern forms of entertainment, IMHO. So I like my NPCs racist or prejudiced, but clans, sects, etc. require a well-based understanding of the cultures and philosophies they are dealing with.

                                                The solution is not removing Malkavians, it's studying psychology, psychiatry, watching how mental illness really works, and at the same time how in the past some sufferers managed to be regarded as geniuses or oracles.

                                                Bottom line: The world is shit, the world of darkness is shittier. As long as writers understand what they're writing about in detail (and can become passionate about it), I don't think certain topics should be off limits.

                                                The Giovani may have been intended as modern Borgias, but according to Wikipedia, the Borgias operated much like mobsters of their time. So in the 60s through 90s, it's likely that the clan focused on mob business. I suggest watching I cento passi, to see how much the mafia had infiltrated everyday life even in rural Italy.

                                                    Originally posted by Lian View Post
                                                    NO they didn't.
                                                    Note I said original corebooks and not First Edition material. Things were only described in a very sketchy way in the corebooks since page count was limited, and it is infodumping a lot. Since it was an American company producing things for a predominantly American market, of course it's going to present things from an American perspective. But precisely because it was kept vague, one could work out much details on their own if they wanted to set a game elsewhere.

                                                    The Camarilla and Sabbat were presented as worldwide organizations. They were not limited to only the West. In the very early sourcebooks, you ever had examples that their members were worldwide to support that. Chicago Primogen were dominated by a Muslim Nosferatu and African Gangrel despite being a very American city. Werewolf had some prominent Tribes based on European lore, but Native American Tribes were also predominant, and the descriptions of other Tribes tied them into the Middle East, Asia, and India. For those areas without wolves, they brought in other shapechangers more appropriate for those lands. Mage presented its Traditions in very large archetypes. Some were more Western in orientation, while others were more Eastern or other. Several Traditions were obviously much more heavily based on non-Western ideas and culture.

                                                    As these things were developed, it got worse - mainly because a bonehead mistake or ideas early on had to be perpetuated throughout the game line because of "canon." Or that the authors wrote what they knew about or thought cool, so they ignored a lot of things that could have been included. So if you want to complain about how some/many of the supplements were bad or bad in certain aspects, I will join you. However, I still feel the original corebooks (1st and 2nd edition) were mainly good, and their ideas could be applied practically anywhere.

                                                    As the game line developed, WW started backing away from the idea that the societies introduced int he corebooks were worldwide. So instead of Asia including Camarilla vampires, even India became dominant by one Clan, and mostly excluded from all of East Asia. The Garou of China seemed to have all become Stargazers and were involved in a very different set up (the Beast Courts) than the rest of the Garou Nation. Dead people's souls in Asia and Africa were explicitly different and reflected in game mechanics, instead of just adhering/beloning to some different Lord of the Dead.

                                                    Later editions were explicitly different than how I imagined things when the original corebooks first came out, and their development was not intrinsic to the original set up.
