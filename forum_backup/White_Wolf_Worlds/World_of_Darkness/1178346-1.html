                Hence, yes, there's "equivilant to the likes of yog-sothoth" in China, it's just that the folklore and legens don't speak about them (Just like they don't speak about them in Europe nor in America)

                Or, to put it other way: Why do you think that Occident can have yog-sothoth cults that date from Cruzade (at the very least, because k’lashaa are mentioned in the book about Infernalists), in spite of the fact this is a "modern invention", as you say, and China can't?.

                I think most of WoD misrepresentation of other countries has to do with this weird idea that makes people to think that "non-western" places are supposed to be immersed in their own cultures, so to speak. It's borderline racist to think that most Chinese Nephandi have to be "culturally appropiate", when you don't demand the same for "the West".

                People in Asia read Lovecraft too, you know.... They may want to use k’lashaa concepts too.
                For further evidence, just look at Bloodborne. A Japanese interpretation of Lovecraft, and on the Western genre of Gothic literature (which I see as being a heavy influence on Lovecraft itself). Just like Dark Souls was a Japanese interpretation of Western high fantasy lit.


                Edit: Turning back to China, I'm reminded of an expression about Lovecraftian literature, and how it compares between America and Britain. It reads: in America the Old Secrets are very secret, while in Britain the Old Secrets are very old. Primarily, this difference stems from America having large space to hide "Old Secrets", but having a relatively short history (that is, if you only count that history recorded by whites; Native American history is often ignored, what of it hasn't been lost). Britain, meanwhile, is a confined space, but with a long history, meaning Old Secrets go back very far.

                Now, imagine what sort of "Old Secrets" might arise in China, a land that is very large AND very old. What sort of horrors hide in the Middle Kingdom, that fall into deep cracks, and predate even the earliest of empires?

                                                That’s more or less addressed in the books. It’s stated that malfeans may not all necessarily believe in the wyrm, but rather consider the wyrm a concept, an anthropomorphic personification of all the putrid waste that is mankind. They don’t serve the wyrm so much as acknowledge that mankind is the wyrm, and that anyone trying to ‘ascend’ from that is acting out of sheer arrogance.

                                                Extrapolating from that, infernalist nephandi might not be religious. They might actually be nihilists or atheists. They see the world is already hell, so they might as well act the role of the demon. Everyone considers themselves the good guy of their own story, that level of pride must be punished.

                                                And the k’lashaa may not even consider themselves beholden to a god, but rather a philosophy so twisted it resembles a creature outside of understanding. After all, the cosmic horrors of Lovecraft was born out of Lovecraft’s own feelings of fragility and insignificance in the wake and resolve of the Great War, where powerful nations act as beings out of time, caring little for the people that crawl across its face.
