In which case the 100lbs for Strength 2 aren't bad, really.
        Remember that you can always lift more! It takes a Willpower roll diff 9, but each success will move you one point up in the chart. I presume that this at least means carrying around stuff will have a considerably shorter limit.
        I considered how the feats of strength factor in, and I decided that the different levels of "Strength" described have to mean that that's the amount you can "lift" (whatever that means; more on that below) without a feat of strength. Otherwise the entire scale becomes meaningless, because there is literally no one with Strength 1 and Willpower 1 that cannot "lift" 100 lbs. With that in mind, it's clear that people who hold the world records for competitive athletic lifts are either using feats of strength, or roping in Athletics somehow (for the more technical lifts like clean-and-jerks, or snatches), if only because the world records for the more powerful lifts (dead lift and squat) both exceed 1k lbs. There's just no other way.

        With that in mind, I think the difference between "lifting" and "carrying something around" is fairly vast, and the books unfortunately limit their description to nothing more than the word "lift". I used competitive, few-second-lifts because they are the absolute floor--in other words, we can discuss all day what the book means by "lift", but there's no intellectually honest argument that for the words "lift" meaning anything less than what a person can dead lift. Though, I will say again, the dead lift is not some impractical olympic lift that can only be done in an athletic context with a barbell (I'm looking at you, snatch); on the contrary, it is one of the most functional forms of lifting that a person can do--every able-bodied adult ever has probably dead lifted something, because it's the most natural and intuitive way of picking something very heavy off the floor ("Lift with your legs" -every dad ever).

        Obviously the rule can be interpreted as expansively as we want--"lift" could mean that it's the amount the person can lift from the ground and straight over their head; it could also mean it's the amount someone carry on their back on a 10-mile march. But if I go beyond the text, I'm necessarily just making a policy/value decision at that point--because if I'm not going by the book, or some reasonably defensible interpretation of the text therein, I'm making an arbitrary decision to make human beings, on average, more strong in my game world (or less strong, depending the call), without much or any support from the text. I'm not saying there's any fundamental problem with that, because it's basically the ST's job to make calls where necessary and fill in the millions of holes that a book is never going to be able to cover. But this isn't exactly a "hole," so much as it's a quirk of where they decided to draw the bright line that divides Strength 2 from 3, which was never going to be fully satisfactory by dint of the exercise being pretty reductionist and absurd to begin with.

        Even if we're going to evaluate Strength 2 more narratively, I think it still support the idea that you'd need Strength 3 (i.e., a single notch "above average" strength) to "employ" a great sword, if you just consider that "average strength" (which is what Strength 2 is) for human beings is probably pretty low. Human sexual dimorphism means that about half of the world's population has a markedly lower capacity (compared to the other half) for the creation and maintenance of muscle mass; then there's the incidence of malnutrition and poverty, on side of the socioeconomic scale, and (on the other side of the scale) luxury and indolence, both set of which are going to make it unlikely that many people will build appreciable functional strength; and finally, the fact is that for every Hafþór Björnsson in the world there are dozens or hundreds or thousands of kids.

                        Originally posted by Warpwind View Post
                        firearms hit over much greater range then depicted
                        Yeah, okay, but is that a description of the literal maximum range of the round, or the maximum effective range (by which I mean not only deadly, but reliably accurate) that can be achieved? If you look at the footnotes, it reads, "Range: Within listed range (in yards), difficulty is 6; at twice listed Range, difficulty is 8; within two yards, difficulty is 4." So, for example, they don't list an an AR-platform weapon on the list, but they have an AK-47 (which is the stat that would invariably be used for an M-16, even though I know at least two people who would blow all the gaskets at hearing that), and the max range is listed as 150, which means the weapon could be used at up to 300 yards, but at a diff 8 (the weapon is regularly diff 7). As I understand it, U.S. Army basic training requires some high degree of proficiency and reliability out to about that range--and these are professional soldiers; the Marines do a little more. But the fact is that as ridiculous as it might seem to use the exact same stats for AKs and ARs, it would be just as, or perhaps even more ridiculous, to suppose that because a U.S. Marine fresh out of boot can grab an M-16 and reliability make 500 yard shots, that a person who has taken a basic firearms safety course, only fired a gun on handful of occasions (i.e., Firearms 1), should realistically be able to get the same out of the gun. Even though differences in skill ability are accounted by the character's dice pool, it makes sense to me that the stats for range are based on a part of the firearms skill bell curve closer to the middle, than the tail end of the curve where Marine Scount-Snipers and Annie Oakley live.
