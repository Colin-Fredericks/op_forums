            Mascaras (Mexico, 1955 - 1970)
            This is the zenith of Mexican lucha libre culture, that of masked professional wrestlers with their larger than life personas, and especially that of the luchador film genre, in which these characters would battle criminals, spies, vampires, and other monsters. El Santo is the most famous of these wrestlers, appearing in over 50 films and having his own comic book (back when that actually meant something). The entire genre ended up inspiring a number of Hellboy stories, many of which were collected in the trade paperback Hellboy in Mexico.

            Primary Games:
            • Hunters Hunted - In which the premise behind all of those films are more or less true. The PCs are masked luchadors, with sacred masks which evoke the powers of ancient heroes, saints and other forces, doing battle against the dark forces that prey upon the people. Sometimes that means mobsters or spies. Sometimes it means mad scientists or vampires. Sometimes it means the Devil has built a robot. I'd honestly consider adapting the rules for Shih martial artist demon hunters from World of Darkness: Demon Hunter X into something that fits lucha libre, augmenting it with some aspects of True Faith and/or some of the Shaman material scattered around books like Dreamspeakers Revised, The Spirit Ways, and some of the Werewolf Kinfolk material.
            • Mummy: The Resurrection - The Teomaliki of Mexico take up the mask to carry out the tasks the ancient gods have set before them, protecting their families and their people in an effort to guide them through the trials and tribulations of this strange new era.

            Secondary Games: You can use this with Werewolf and Mage fairly easily. Vampire is maybe a bit more difficult, given how Mexico is generally considered Sabbat country and vampires tend to be the villains in luchador films.

                The Baron's Shadow (Haiti, 1957 - 1986)
                Haiti has a rough and bloody history. Even before Columbus landed, the Taino peoples had already displaced the original inhabitants a few hundred years before. Its revolution against the French was one of the most violent in the Americas, which was followed by various civil wars, conflicts with the neighboring Dominican Republic, and various US military interventions. But the absolute strangest period in the nation's history has to be the dictatorship of the Duvalier dynasty, Francois "Papa Doc" Duvalier and Juan-Claude "Baby Doc" Duvalier.
                Papa Doc was an actual doctor and the former Minister of Health. He came to power with the help of both the backing of the military and a lot of popular support that owed more than a little to the fact that he was "black" and his main opponent was both mulatto and from a very prominent family. He presented himself as a champion of the poor rural blacks and the black middle class against the urban mulatto elite who had dominated Haitian politics and economics for a long time. In truth, he established one of the most repressive and corrupt dictatorships of the late 20th century.
                His main weapon of repression was a paramilitary force, the Volunteers of the National Security, but they soon became better known as the Tonton Macoutes, named after a Haitian boogieman (the name translates to Uncle Gunnysack, a monster that carried off bad children in his sack and then ate them). The group was responsible for countless murders and rapes (which were used as a tool of political repression and punishment), including burning people alive. It's estimated that they killed 30K to 60K people.
                Vodou had come back into prominence in Haiti in the 1950s, and Duvalier drew on it a lot during his reign. A large number of houngans/Vodou priests served in the Tonton Macoutes, and the group used a lot of Vodou imagery and iconography. Duvalier himself even began drawing on the faith, and was reputed to possess sorcerous powers. He deliberately started modeling his public persona on that of the loa Baron Samedi, and allegedly even started claiming he was the Baron. He kept the severed head of one of his enemies in his closet. Later, be believed one of his enemies had the power to turn into a black dog and so ordered all black dogs in Haiti to be killed. He claimed that the JFK assassination was the result of a curse he'd placed on the president. He also had the Lord's Prayer rewritten to refer to him instead of God.
                Baby Doc, who came to power with his father's death in 1971, carried on with the violent political oppression and extortion, but pretty much abandoned any pretense of being a "man of the people". He lived a ludicrously lavish lifestyle while the rest of the nation's population slipped into greater and greater poverty. The US considered him at least nominally useful for his claims of being anti-communist, but by the mid 80s, the US had had enough and when a revolt broke out, the Regan administration told him to leave.
                Graham Greene's novel The Comedians is set in Haiti during Papa Doc's reign, and delves into the political repression and terrorism of the period. The book apparently sent Duvaliar into fits of rage. Wade Davis's nonfiction book about Vodou, The Serpent & The Rainbow, was researched during the end of this period. (Both of these books have movie adaptions, the former in 1967 and the latter in 1988.)

                Primary Games:
                • Vampire - Early Vampire had a fascination with Haiti. This may have been a personal thing of Andrew Greenberg. But he wrote a section about it for the original 1st edition A World of Darkness sourcebook, and was developer when the Sabbat books, which included the Serpents of Light, came out. So, naturally, the island has always been presented, at least in the early material, as some sort of Setite stronghold. However, this was before the Samedi came along in the 2nd ed. Regardless, this is a time and place where local vampires play a very dangerous game. You have competing Setite cults, plus the local Samedi, and those vampires from other clans (such as Camarilla coteries or Sabbat packs, or independent factions). While it might be tempting to try to use the Macoutes as a tool against one's enemies, they'd be a dangerous double edged sword that might easily turn against you.
                • Wraith - Haiti is one of the central strongholds of Les Invisibles. While political murder certainly isn't a new thing in the country, the activities of the Macoutes is going to generate a lot of new wraiths. And more than a few are likely to be very angry and wanting revenge. Existing Les Invisibles will have to try to control these new members, as well as deal with those that have become Spectres. The Macoutes may also become a front for certain Vodou themed Spectre cults.

                Secondary Games:
                • Mage - Haiti is one of the strongholds of the Bata'a Craft. However, the heavy Vodou involvement with the Macoutes and Duvalier means that the members of the Craft may be at war with themselves during this period.
                • Werewolf - The Caribbean isn't really a stronghold for the Garou, though the black dog story from above can carry interesting connotations for the Silent Striders' long war against the Setites. The country probably also has its share of Mokole, Ananasi, and Ratkin.
                • Hunters Hunted - The idea of Vodou practitioners as vampire hunters is an interesting one.

                    The New City (Carthage, 550 - 340 BC)
                    Carthage - the name apparently derives from the Phoenician term for "new city" - is kind of weird. It really was one of the great empires/civilizations of the ancient Mediterranean, with a history spanning about a thousand years before it was destroyed by Rome. They were mainly a seaborne empire, interested mainly in trade more than anything else, with most of their client states/cities having a fair degree of autonomy. And their ships apparently managed to go out into the Atlantic (which they worked hard to keep their Greek rivals out of) as far north as Brittany and as far south as Gambia (and likely further). Culturally, they were Phoenician, starting as a colony established by the city state of Tyre. This means they apparently worshiped a number of the Canaanite gods. Because a few of those gods get mentioned in the Old Testament, usually in a less than flattering light, they ended up getting associated with demons. Which transitions us to the first bit of weirdness.
                    Early White Wolf had a very strange obsession of demons. (Not just in Vampire; more than a few Ars Magica fans apparently find the Infernalism obsession in that game's Spain/Iberia book a bit off putting, to the point I'm kind of surprised the current property owners have never made a new version of it.) So Carthage has always had a weird demon thing hanging over it in the World of Darkness. Which pairs a bit strangely with Carthage's other big WoD legacy, that of being some sort of Brujah utopia.
                    I'm generally confining this to Carthage's Magonid dynasty of kings/tyrants/dictators (exactly what their leaders were called seems to depend on who is telling the story at the time), as this is effectively the peak of Carthage's power and prestige, barring their brief period of looking like Hannibal ("The Grace of Baal/Baal is Gracious") was going to win the second Punic War.
                    This is a very rich and prosperous trading empire, with a lot of money and wealth coming into the city. Carthage itself produces a lot of agricultural products, including olives, fruit and nut trees, wine, apiaries, and ranches, usually as part of small estates. And the city is fairly advanced for the time, with multistory buildings and a city wide drainage system. In addition, their harbor is one of the most impressive of the ancient world.
                    Carthage's chief gods are apparently Baal Hammon and Tanit. Baal Hammon is of the sky god archetype, controlling weather and crop fertility. He's depicted as a bearded man with ram's horns. Beyond that, scholars apparently can't really agree on much about him, especially just what "Hammon" is supposed to mean. (The name translates to "Lord of Hammon", so it would help if someone could figure that out.) But he was apparently sometimes called Baal Quarnaim (Lord of Two Horns, referring to the ram horn thing). The Greeks thought he was the Titan Cronus. This may have something to do with Greek writers also describing the Carthaginians as sacrificing their children to him as burnt offerings. So, yeah, he's probably Moloch.
                    Tanit, meanwhile is Astarte, also known as Ishtar, a goddess of war, fertility, and sexuality. (In a bit of historical strangeness, Astarte was likely imported into Greece to become Aphrodite, which the Romans adapted into their own Venus. Meanwhile, after the Punic Wars, the Roman's rebuilt Carthage as a colony and Tanit's worship continued under the name Caelestis and was sometimes tied to Juno. This is the sort of thing that causes people on the Scion forum to pull their hair out in frustration or end up trying to settle arguments in steel cage matches.)

                    Primary Games:
                    • Vampire - Of course. This is one of those settings where everyone is probably pretty low Generation. And we have this very strange dichotomy of the Brujah utopia on one hand and the Baali Infernalism party on the other. My own opinion is that the ancient Bronze Age Brujah's concept of a vampire utopia is a bit different than that of most of the post Dark Ages Brujah who hear this story and try to imagine it conforming to their own ideas of some sort of Christian or Marxist or whatever perfect society. Carthage's vampire population is probably ruthlessly kept at a level where it doesn't endanger the food supply. Said humans are likely fed upon freely by their patrons, especially any kept as slaves. Canaanite religion seems to have a strong ancestor worship component, so it's entirely possible that the city's vampires keep their living descendants as semi-willing herds, with blood sacrifices given in exchange for support and patronage. Outside of the Brujah, you've probably got a fair number of Lasombra (owing to the maritime empire aspect), Cappadocians (again, the ancestor worship thing), Salubri (one of the other city gods was Eshmum, a god of healing, so there's likely a Salubri claiming that mantel and his childer), and maybe Toreador. Also the odd Assamite, Setite (pay no attention, we're just visiting), Malkavian, and others. In my own mind, I see the Baali as actually being extremely rare, holding just a few places of important influence, and probably devoted to Baal Hammon.
                    • Demon: Earthbound - This is during the high point of the Earthbound's influence and power within the ancient world. Baal Hammom - and even Tanit - may be such beings. And their cult will be prevalent in ancient Carthage. One way to play this is as Thralls to the ancient Earthbound, "blessed" with the mighty powers of the gods and effectively being Bronze Age "heroes". Just Carthaginian ones rather than the Greeks we're more familiar with. The PCs go out, have adventures as explorers, traders, mercenaries and pirates, kicking ass, fighting monsters, and bringing back big piles of loot in order to establish themselves as VIPs in the city.

                    Secondary Game:
                    • Mage: The Sorcerers' Crusade - Wait, isn't that set in the Renaissance? Yes, but hear me out. As I mentioned, Carthage is one of the more advanced civilizations of the ancient world. They are skilled explorers and navigators and have a really massive mercantile empire. In other words, its the sort of culture where a bunch of the old Order of Reason factions can fit in very easily. The Explorators and the High Guild most especially, but it's not too much of a stretch to fit the Cosians, the Artificers, and the Razors in as well. Probably the Celestial Masters as well. Maybe also the Gabrialites adapted to fit with Baal Hammom. Just dial the technology back to Bronze Age stuff akin to Daedalus, Archimedes, and the like, and it makes an interesting alternate Mage setting.

                                    Sunset People (LA, 1972 - 1984)
                                    Los Angeles in the 70s and early 80s was a weird and fairly gritty place, where various levels of entertainment industry decadence crashed headlong into rebellious countercultures and violent crime. The center for a lot of this was the (in)famous Sunset Strip. The Strip had been a popular hang out for Hollywood types during the 40s and 50s, but had fallen out of favor. But in the 60s, it became a big counterculture hangout, with its various hotels, go-go clubs, and bars. This culminated with the Sunset Strip Riots of 1966. 1972 saw the opening of the English Disco, which catered to the glam rock movement. Other places of note included Gazzarri's, the Rainbow, the Roxy Theater (an upscale joint backed by music industry heavyweights, including David Geffen), Jewel's Catch One (a black gay dance bar/disco), the Whiskey, and comedy club The Comedy Store (which was run by Mitzi Shore, who became sort of a den mother to a bunch of talent that included the likes of Robin Williams, Richard Pryor, and Sam Kinison). The hotels were infamous for the behavior of its rock star patrons, including members of the Rolling Stones and The Who tossing things - including TVs - off the balconies and members of Led Zeppelin riding motorcycles in the hallway. The Continental Hyatt House hotel earned the nick name "The Riot House", whose coffee shop was the main hangout for the "LA Queens", a clique of young groupies who would eagerly wait for rock stars to show up and invite them to party. The Tower Records store was also a big deal, and its been half-joked that everyone who worked there was in a band and would constantly be stuffing their gig flyers into customers bags at check out. Glam turned to punk turned to New Wave and eventually turned to glam (hair) metal. This shift in the early 80s coincided with a major change on the Strip: clubs started adopting a "pay to play" policy in which bands had to pay the club up front in order to play a set there, or else had to go out and presell a certain level of tickets to the gig ahead of time (with the club getting all that ticket sales money). This system clearly benefited bands who were already famous and had industry backers who could front the money for them (especially to show them off to critics or other VIPs). This effectively killed the Strip as a place for up and coming bands and turned it into an industry showcase.
                                    While all this was going on, LA continued to be zoo it had always been. 1980 saw the so-called Sunset Strip Killers, where Doug Clark and Carol Bundy killed at least six people as part of some sort of insane sex kink. This period also saw the activities of The Hillside Stranglers, The Westside Rapist, The Toolbox Killers, and at least three totally different serial killers who were all dubbed The Freeway Killer by the media. Also the rise of the Crips and the Bloods street gangs (including the infamous 1972 murder of Robert Ballou Jr outside of a concert at the Hollywood Palladium on Sunset, in which 20 members of the Crips beat him to death just to steal his leather jacket). Then there was the Wonderland Murders in 81, where five people were murdered at a drug house hang out, allegedly on the order of nightclub owner and crime boss Eddie Nash. Making this weirder was the involvement of adult film actor John Holmes with pretty much everyone involved. This period coincides with the so-called "Golden Age of Porn", when it was a big business, especially in Southern California. West Hollywood was also a hot bed of gay/LGBT culture in the 70s, including numerous clubs, and the Different Light bookstore, among other things. This community joined forces with the local senior citizens and Russian Jewish community to organize West Hollywood's 1984 incorporation into its own city separate from LA. This was largely motivated to preserve local rent control policies which LA was trying to phase out.
                                    All in all, this is just a very uniquely weird time and place, especially in the context of the influences of GenX culture that would eventually inform a lot of the original World of Darkness material. Personally, my favorite story from this period is Alice Cooper talking about his friendship with Groucho Marx. Marx, in his old age, apparently suffered from insomnia, and would apparently invite Alice over in the middle of the night to sit up and watch TV with him. Honestly, the only way that could be more perfect is if the pair ended up involved in some sort of adventure in which they fought vampires and/or a Satanist cult. (And I really want to try to do that with They Came From Beyond the Grave.)

                                    Primary Games:
                                    • World of Darkness: Outcasts - As I said, this is a very strange time and place, and because of that, I think it works exceptionally well as a setting for the WoD's various Pariahs. Caitiff, outcast Garou, and Orphans (and Hollow Ones) fit into this sort of weirdness very nicely. The Sunset Strip and the rest of LA's 70s countercultures - glam rockers, punks, teen runaways, gays, groupies, stand up comics, and the like - is largely made up of people who just don't fit in with "normal" society. And I think that carries over well into the supernatural world. While yes, you're going to have your various Anarchs, Garou/Fera, Traditionalist Mages, Changelings and what have you running all over the place, there's going to be those who aren't part of that scene and really don't want to be. But they are part of their own scene, and may find they have more in common with other Pariahs than they do "their own kind". It's a great excuse for a mixed group chronicle, as the PCs have to navigate a nightlife full of humans who may be stranger and more dangerous in their own way than any vampire or wizard they come across.
                                    • Hunters Hunted - I mentioned They Came From Beyond the Grave, and that's because this period of LA is perfect of classic Technicolor Gothic horror campaigns. Blacula and Fright Night kind of book end the period for vampire hunting. A lot of the Kolchak: The Night Stalker plots can easily be moved to LA. The Howling can give ideas about werewolves. Plus ghosts and hauntings (the original Poltergeist), witches, demons, and anything else you can think of for classic horror/monster hunter films. These characters should, ideally, not be part of any of the major witch hunter organizations such as the FBI-SAD, Arcanum, or Society of Leopold. This is the world of hunters in the vein of Deborah Rose Keller or The Uncanny Danny. (Also the sort of characters in Marvel's Tomb of Dracula series.) Lone individuals - cops, reporters, students, night shift nurses, would be rock stars, officer workers, suburbanites, etc. - who have run head long into the things that go bump in the night and are only just starting to find others who believe.
                                    • Twilight People - As a bridge between the above two, you have the WoD's assorted collection of second tier supernatural-tinged mortals. Mediums, sorcerers, shaman, psychics, revenants and what have you (some of whom may or may not be estranged Kinfolk or Kinain). They're the sort of characters which can fit into either of the above, or lead to some sort of team up/mixed group of the two.

                                        Heroic Bloodshed (Hong Kong, 1982 - 1996)
                                        This is a focus on Hong Kong action cinema's world of violent crime films. Hong Kong, before its turnover to Communist China, was a very unique city and culture. Southern China's Cantonese culture mixed with British law and lassie faire economics to create a dynamic and fairly prosperous society. However, it also made for a fair amount of crime and corruption. The 1974 Independent Commission Against Corruption helped curb a lot of this, but like most cities, it never really went away. At the same time, the early 80s saw a boom in Hong Kong's entertainment industry. The Walkman made Cantonese "Cantopop" (or HK-pop) music ubiquitous, and a rise of newer independent film production companies created a new wave of Hong Kong cinema. This included a lot of violent crime films, such as A Better Tomorrow, The Killer, and Hard Boiled. These tended to deal with morally incorruptible cops and criminals with heroic codes of honor, usually with tragic endings. It's a world of noble but flawed protagonists, stylistic - at time almost graceful - violence, black humor, mixes and clashes of certain cultures (not just Cantonese and British, but also that of Tanka and other Chinese ethno-cultural groups, immigrants from India and other places, and so on) and economic strata, and questions of loyalty vs morality.
                                        Primary Games:
                                        • Kindred of the East - One of the reasons KotE appears so wonky in hindsight is because a huge influence on its development was what Asian media was actually available in the US at the end of the Millennium. One of those was the aforementioned Hong Kong films, and so this would be something that just leans into that. Most of the Dharma can fit into the genre fairly easily, but this is the sort of world that should focus mostly on younger vampires who've only been around a few decades at most, with elders far in the background, save the occasions when one or more of them are involved in events at the climax or when PCs have to decide which side to take. (It also allows for a slight amount of crossover with Vampire if one wishes.)
                                        • Beast Courts - Werebeasts are no strangers to violence, and so fit into this genre equally well. This is more the world of the more urban shifters, including the Chinese Glasswalkers and Bone Gnawers, Ratkin, and certain Khan, Kitsune, and Mokole.

                                        Secondary Games:
                                        • Demon Hunter X - While the likes of Strike Force Zero are more the realm of Japanese Anime and so fit much better there, more "normal" hunters - police, criminals, priests, and the like - work well here. Also, it's not that difficult to work the Shih into this sort of thing.
                                        • Mummy - Finally, this setting can work with the Chinese Wu T'ian immortals, especially the Celestial Arrows of the Wu Feng.

                                                    Originally posted by Penelope View Post
                                                    Who’s Rudolf II and why is he famous? And I really like the one about the Sunset People.
                                                    Thank you. (Sunset People probably owes most of its existence to my seeing Penelope Spheeris's The Decline of Western Civilization documentaries - the original about LA's punk scene and the sequel about the Metal Years - as a teenager and the They Came From Beyond the Grave KS for reminding me why I love a lot of the films from that era.)

                                                    Rudolf II was Holy Roman Emperor around the end of the 16th century (the same time Elizabeth I was Queen of England), famous as both a patron of the arts and his devotion to astrology and alchemy. His court included the best occult scholars he could get, and I have long thought it would make a fun Mage and Changeling setting.

                                                        Harlem Nights (New York City, 1916 - 1935)
                                                        New York has always been a fairly dynamic city, and there's a lot going on there in this two decade period. Institutions such as Sacks Fifth Avenue, the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, The New Yorker magazine, the New York Giants, the Holland Tunnel, the Museum of Modern Art, the Apollo Theater, and Radio City Music Hall all started during this time, as did multiple publishing houses and radio stations. It was also the period of the 1920 Wall Street Bombing, the mysterious (and still unsolved) disappearance of a NY State Supreme Court Justice, and the real life murder (of Joseph Elwell) that inspired the creation of the "locked-room murder" sub-genre of mystery fiction. But our focus for this is what's going on in the neighborhood of Harlem.
                                                        Harlem was originally a little Dutch farming village on the northern part of Manhattan which got absorbed by New York City as it grew. By the turn of the century, it was a major population center for immigrant Jews and Italians (and their local organized crime outfits), but by the time of the first World War, more and more African Americans were living there, and this is when the neighborhood would become synonymous with black culture and life in New York City. This period also coincides with the Great Migration of southern blacks to Northern cities, including New York. This actually created a lot of tension between the newcomers and the Northern black people already living there. Northern blacks had tended to be middle and working class, and better educated, and so looked down on their poorer, less education, and much rowdier "cousins". (Black culture in the South is nigh identical to that of poor whites, and this influx of black "rednecks" into Northern and Midwestern cities is what prompted the mass wave of Northern segregation in the early 19th century. Blacks were acceptable when they were well dressed, polite, and few in number; not so much in large crowds of "deplorables". )
                                                        At the same time, Harlem was seeing a massive cultural explosion in realms of art, literature, music, political thought, sports, and other areas. The political side of things was known as the New Negro Movement, led by people such as WEB DeBois, Marcus Garvey, A. Philip Randolph, and Hubert Harrison, among others. The cultural aspect became known as the Harlem Renaissance, with such luminaries as Langston Hughes, Duke Ellington, and Jessie Redmon Fauset, among many others. A mild irony is that part of the reason the writing and music of African Americans from this period became so well known, both then and in a historical context, is because during the 1920s it was the hip and trendy thing for upper class whites in New York and surrounding areas. They talked about black writers in the major literary magazines and attended shows by black jazz and big band groups in Harlem and elsewhere.
                                                        Regardless, this is a pretty exciting and dynamic period to set a World of Darkness game. There's a lot of interaction, be it conflict, cooperation or otherwise, between a number of different racial, ethnic, cultural, and economic groups. And as the Roaring 20s gives way to the Great Depression, the societal changes can easily echo a number of the themes in the various games.
                                                        If you have any interest in running a game during this period, I highly recommend the Call of Cthulhu sourcebook Harlem Unbound, which goes into a massive amount of detail about Harlem during the 20s and the various people who live there, as well as discussions about racism during the period and how to deal with it in a game. Also a major recommendation for Victor LaValle's The Ballad of Black Tom.

                                                        Primary Games:
                                                        • Vampire - So much to work with here. I mention the New Negro movement, and it had more than its share of radicals, including Pan-Africanists, Socialists, Communists, racial separatists, and so on. This is the sort of thing that could easily attract Brujah, as well as various Anarchs and even certain types of Ventrue and Lasombra, among others. The Harlem Renaissance will be a play ground for Toreador and any other vampire with an interest in art, culture, or just having a good time at night. Prince Hall Masonry (a branch of Freemasons established by African Americans in 1784) is active in Harlem, and that offers an interesting twist on a certain type of Tremere stereotype. There's also an influx of black immigrants from Jamaica and other parts of the Caribbean (as well as Africa), and this combined with the "hoodoo" culture of Southern blacks most likely serves as a front for more than one Setite group. A lot of Italians still live in East Harlem at this time, so even the most stereotypical Giovanni isn't that out of place here. For Kindred in general, this is almost like a giant Rack, at least until the Great Depression puts the breaks on things.
                                                        • Changeling - The creative output of the Harlem Renaissance and the dreams of the New Negro movement offer a feast for the children of the Dreaming. This is the height of the Jazz Age and everything that goes with it. Much like the Kindred, this is a massive party which kind of lurches to a halt with the Depression, and then slowly starts to shuffle forward again.
                                                        • World of Darkness: Mafia - While Mafia tends to deal mainly with (a somewhat cinematically stereotypical version of) Italian organized crime, there's also a lot to be mined in Harlem in terms of criminals, the moral choices they face, and possible encounters with the supernatural. This is the time of Prohibition and gangs warring over territory for both liquor distribution and other vices. Stephanie St. Clair was one of the most powerful criminals in Harlem during the 20s and 30s. If you've ever seen the 1997 film Hoodlum, it stars Laurence Fishburne as her chief enforcer Bumpy Johnson during his mid-30s conflicts with Dutch Schultz and Lucky Luciano. I also recommend the 1989 Richard Pryor/Eddie Murphy film Harlem Nights, which inspired the name for this bit.

                                                        Secondary Games:
                                                        • Wraith: The Great War - This is during that game's time frame, and New York makes for an interesting setting. If you're at all interested in African American soldiers during the Great War, I highly recommend Max Brooks and Caanan White's The Harlem Hellfighters graphic novel, about the 369th Infantry Regiment.
                                                        • The Arcanum - This is just sort of a bit of personal weirdness on my part. There's a lot of highly educated people in Harlem during this period, including those doing studies of African American folklore and the like. The Arcanum is possibly racially segregated to a certain degree at this time, and I just find the idea of an all black Arcanum chapter based in Harlem investigating local paranormal activity and mysteries based around Afro-Caribbean and African American folklore, aspects of Harlem's Jewish and even Dutch history, or the classic tropes of Gothic monsters or Lovecraftian Cosmic Horror to be an interesting basis for a chronicle.
