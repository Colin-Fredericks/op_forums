    Mechanics Vs Narrative

    12-28-2015, 04:05 PM
    Does anyone else feel slightly urked about how cWOD will sometimes through narrative under the bus for it's mechanics and vise versa?

    Like when in vampire all "Plot" NPC's have similar numbers of backgrounds and disciplines regardless of whether they are a powerful elder more ancient than the roman empire or a neonate that was embraced 4 years ago(mechanics screwing narrative). Or when they don't give stats to NPC's because they're "Too powerful" to be measured(Narrative screwing mechanics).

    It all rings of an attempt to "Balance" things that don't really need it, in doing so limiting both the storyteller and players in what they can achieve. While I know I can house rule this stuff out, it still bugs me when the "Official" rules do this.

    What are your guy's thoughts? How do you handle your stories when a splat has mechanics that don't allow you to do what they story says they should or vise versa?

      I revise or ignore the stats. The rules exist to facilitate the story, not vice versa.

      But the story should never dis-empower players. I don't have any objections to saying an NPC is too powerful for stats, but I'm not going to use them in direct contests against PCs, run scenes where the PCs aren't supposed to do anything but gawk at how great and powerful the NPC is, etc. Some of the CWoD's adventures were pretty bad on that front (lookin' at you, Transylvania Chronicles). If I do use a Methuselah or uber-powerful elder directly on-stage, it'll be under circumstances where the PCs can still make meaningful choices that aren't limited to "do what the uber-NPC wants or get Dominated/die horribly." It's not impossible to do, either. Lair of the Hidden was an awesomely-written adventure that prominently featured two entities too powerful for game stats, yet put the outcome of events squarely in the PCs' hands.

      Blood and Bourbon, my New Orleans-based Vampire chronicle.

              When you're writing a story, the narrative trumps everything. There might be setting rules, a "bible" for the series, but those rules can be changed if necessary. Look at all the ridiculous plot holes in TV series and movies that exist because, if people acted reasonably, or the McGuffin from last week's episode still existed, the story couldn't be told. Or look at comics and how in one issue, Captain America can lift a car, but in the next he's trapped under a 300 pound wooden beam. The story needs to get told, so the story gets told.

              Roleplaying games, though, are not just about writing a story. They're also a game, played by multiple people, working together to have fun. The Storyteller is there to challenge the players and present them with an interesting setting to engage with. So you run into a conflict. If story is all that matters, then rules don't matter... except they do, because that's how the players interact with the setting. They rely on knowing that when they roll their dice, a success means success and a failure means failure. They rely on knowing that if they can lift 500 pounds today, they can lift 500 pounds tomorrow. The Storyteller can't say "Well, yes, I know you could bench press a car last week, but this week I need you to be trapped under a wooden beam. So you can't accomplish that feat of strength right now, or my story is ruined."

              The rules provide a framework that the story must exist inside. A well-written game does that. It provides a consistent baseline framework that everyone, NPC and PC, adheres to. And, hopefully, one that is fair. Not fair to the characters, but fair to the players. That means that if your character is a 20-year old marine with badass combat capabilities and equipment, you can expect to face down an NPC who is a 20-year old marine with badass combat capabilities and equipment and have it be a fair fight. It means that if the Storyteller wants to have one single NPC take on an entire party of PCs, the NPC needs an advantage that makes sense inside the framework. They need more resources, more experience, more powers, more goons.

              A lazy way of handling this is to make NPC-only-powers that handle this issue for you. Want an elite NPC to thrash your entire party of PC's? Give that NPC abilities that the PC's can't match. Ones that are intentionally, absolutely stronger than the equivalent abilities that PC's have access to. Unfortunately, White Wolf tends to go this way. A lot. Vampire is full of Disciplines that are just flat better than others, because they were originally designed for use by NPCs only. They were meant to trash entire groups of PC's. Quietus, Vicissitude, Chimerstry, Serpentis and Obtenebration are all great examples. They work fine, if they are only ever used by NPCs to advance the plot. They give the Storyteller immense freedom and latitude in what can be accomplished with them (especially Chimerstry and Vicissitude) because they are specifically designed as plot advancement tools.

              It requires more creativity to have only one set of tools for both NPCs and PCs, because it means that everyone is on the same playing field. Then you're stuck with boring things like giving the NPC's lots of experience, or more resources, or more goons, as noted above. Which doesn't always make sense. Sometimes it'd be a great story if there was just this one bad guy who, all alone, presented a challenge to an entire group of PCs, but was still kind of on their "level". About their age, about their standing in society, from a similar background. Except he needs more power than the PC's have, but there's no logical way to explain why he should have that power. He just needs it for the plot to work.

              To me, that kind of story is a bad story. The rules should be fair. NPCs and PCs should use the same toolkit, and that means that if the Storyteller wants to challenge the PCs, the Storyteller has to use the same tools. She might give her NPC's more money, or more contacts, or they might be older, more experienced, better trained.. but they shouldn't simply be stronger without logic behind it.

              If you have to change the rules just so your plot works, maybe you should consider changing your plot instead? Because, hey, what if your players outsmart you? What if they have a perfectly good reason to learn whatever magic your NPC is using? What if they obtain the magical device powering her army? What if they convert the NPC's overpowered minion to their side?

              Basically, the setting might be unfair, but the rules should not be. And the rules should stay the rules. They're your limits. Your plot can bend them a bit, but they exist for a reason. They make sure that what happens this time on As The Dice Roll is consistent with what happened last time. They're the glue that keeps the bottom from falling out of your plot.

              If you want a necromancer to threaten your party, that necromancer should be built using the same rules as your players used to build their own characters. If he's stronger than they are, why? Is he older? Is he better trained? Does he have a mentor backing his play? Because if you can't answer that question, you have a problem. And I have a problem with you, too. Isn't it fair of the players to ask "Why is he better than me?" and expect an answer back? If the bad guy can master Necromancy by age 22, why can't my character? That is not a silly question.

                      Originally, in VtM 1st Edition, the Sabbat Clans, Independent Clans and all the Bloodlines were not playable options. They were for NPC use only. That continued to be pretty strongly encouraged until well into the later half of 2nd edition. Even Revised had a really strong vibe of "these are not for you" when talking about anything but the Camarilla Clans.

                      The idea behind Experience is time. Sure, you might know people, but if you haven't spent the XP on acquiring them as a Contact or Ally or whatever, then you just haven't gotten to the point where they're comfortable giving you that help. You just not to each other as you pass on the street. Or they can't make time for you. Maybe in the game you talked about taking time out to learn kung fu, but if you didn't spend the XP, maybe you never really learned your lessons, or you procrastinated and flunked your Kung Fu Exam. Experience buys traits that your character has actually earned. Even if it might make sense for them to have a trait, if they never burned XP on it, they obviously didn't bother to invest time in it.

                      I've been around IT professionals for years, but haven't picked up any IT-related skills because I haven't invested the time in listening to them or helping them out. Sure, it would make sense for me to be able to draw on those contacts and improve my own IT abilities... but I'm not interested, so I haven't done that. I've been doing other things.

                      XP makes sense, if you bother to work it into the story.

                      Giving away free points seems silly. Some people didn't finish high school, some people didn't learn to drive, my mother barely knows what a werewolf is. I know a fair number of people that don't have Resources 2, even as adults. There are people that don't have Resources 1. There are homeless people and people living in extreme poverty. If you spend all your traits on something else, then your character just never did those things and earned those dots. It's resource management. The character creation system does account for your character's mortal life. It just lets the player choose what that life was. There are plenty of concepts where Academics 0, Drive 0, Computer 0, Occult 0 and Resources 0 make sense.
