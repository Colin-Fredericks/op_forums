        Yes, you can. Although I do think it requires a good ST who is willing to put into the work needed to create a comprehensive setting.

        The original five game lines are largely compatible. They were meant to be. There was even meant to be crossover in terms of PCs. Mechanically that was never really the case since there were too many game line specific mechanics for many powers. Those always had to have fudges. But in terms of the setting itself, it's fairly consistent. There are some seeming inconsistencies, but they can be easily resolved by a single Storyteller. The only reason it broke down in the Revised era is that the various game line developers established their feudal fiefs and couldn't work together. But a single ST doesn't have that problem. They just need to decide what they want to take precedent. And most of those issues are high metaphysical stuff that won't come into play for 99% of games. The other issues can be resolved simply by damping down on the actual # of supernatural creatures out there.

        I only utilize the first game lines as that was comprehensive outside of small 1 book WoD books like the original Mummy or Sorcerer. I considered the additional game lines (the new Mummy, Hunter, KotE, Demon) etc. to be desperate attempts at cash grabs and not part of the original vision. But since even those were built on the earlier foundations, I suspect they too could be made to work.

        I think the real issue is how an ST should present the setting so that all elements are (theoretically) present, but for the most point don't interrupt what players expect from the game their playing. In other words, Vampire players want a game where they mainly interact with mortals and other Vampires. They don't want to be interrupted and dealing with werewolves or strange umbra creatures... most of the time. Those same players may still have lots of fun with a specific plot involving werewolves or ghosts or whatever as long as it doesn't seem to hijack the game, and they still want to see the setting mainly through the eyes of vampire genre expectations. They typically don't want a Monster Mash that detracts from vampire specific themes. They want their vampire game to FEEL like a vampire game even if somewhere in the background are lupines, mages, ghosts, and fae.

        How an ST blends everything in will be specific to them and their playing group. I'm fairly confidant that I run a single coherent World of Darkness universe in my chronicles, and can probably answer 99%+ of the questions asked of me OOC either immediately or with a little thought. It just will likely be different than someone else's conception who emphasize or prioritize certain things.

        One hang up is that certain players insist that their favored splat is correct because they actually know how X works. But in fact, other splats also know that they are correct as well. Very often, if you actually pay attention to the books, each splat only knows so much through direct evidence and they simply ASSUME a lot of things. But if you change those assumptions, it won't contradict their direct evidence.

        What is mainly needed is a skilled ST that can introduce other elements into the game without disrupting the genre expectations players of that game want to experience.

            While I already outlined my “many layers” answer, one thing I think is worth asking too is “How often do you have players using more two of the supernatural types in the same campaign?”

            I ask because the fewer “layers” you have to have interact for your campaign, the easier a consistent world becomes. Merging just Vampire and Wraith practically writes itself. Merging Changeling and Mage is similarly easy. Vampire and Mage can also work reasonably well. HtR and Mage can also be pretty interesting if you let the Imbued recognize “purple auras” are another type of mortal who help them.

            Basically, nearly any two splats* can be merged relatively easily (minor splats like old-style mummies and mortal hunters almost don’t count in this regard), its when you start adding a third or fourth splat that problems can start to occur.

            And in my experience, unless you have an exceptionally large group (6+ players) and explicitly open it up to multiple splats, you’re probably not going to see more than two splats mixing. The biggest campaign I ever ran was 14 players (it worked largely by using an entire college classroom to play and treating it as a mini-LARP where I only stepped in to RP a particular NPC or resolve something mechanically) and we had 12 mages, a vampire and an old-style mummy for PCs.

            Everything else was typically a case of a lone player and “I wanna play with the group, but just can’t wrap my head around playing X... can I play Y instead?” and even then it was more typical to go splat adjacent (ex. playing a Dhampir or old-school Mummy in a vampire game) than completely cross-splat.

            The biggest outlier I’ve found in “does not play well with others; mechanically, socially or metaphysically” are the werewolves. Their Crinos forms just break the mechanics of everything else with ridiculous dice pools and how their rage interacts with willpower and social mechanics can make them practically unplayable outside of combat in a mixed chronicle unless the player is willing to build a “non-combat” garou (in quotes because even a 2/2/2 in physicals becomes a 6/3/5 that soaks agg, regenerates lethal and deals 7 dice of lethal damage in melee just by entering Crinos).

            Similarly, their cosmology is the most dogmatic. You can explore the main themes of Vampire without ever involving an Antediluvian or even a Methuselah or mentioning Caine being cursed by God as literal truth. You can explore the main themes of Mage without making any assumptions about the cosmological basis for vampires and werewolves existing or why they can’t be allies.

            Werewolf though is centered around an apocalyptic conflict in a very specific cosmology that labels just about everything that’s not another Garou as some perversion of nature that must be destroyed to appease their insatiable rage. Mages are, at best, thieves of spiritual essence that “rightfully” belongs to the Garou to puppets of the Weaver and/or Wyrm at worst. Vampires are Wyrm-tainted abominations. Wraiths and Mummies are loaded down with entropic Wyrm-taint due to their ties to the Dark Umbra.

            This is why the Lupines in my Vampire campaign aren’t Garou, but mortals under a separate divine curse (for their progenitor’s hubris and cannibalism) who have similarly twisted divine power into disciplines and that is passed on by an infectious bite. It both fits better thematically (since everyone’s playing either a vampire and it’s all about dealing with a curse you didn’t ask for) and mechanically.

            No one is playing one now, but depending on where things are at in the sandbox when a PC death occurs, a player could choose one of these Lupines and not be unbalanced with the remaining vampire PCs because they’re built using disciplines, they must kill/feed to power them and struggle with their humanity and, while they can be active in daylight (a compensation for no ghouling or blood bonds) their disciplines and other supernatural abilities only function at night (i.e. they’re a mere mortals during the times a vampire can’t be active and would have to act through ghouls).

                        You misunderstand... Lupines aren’t built like WtA with disciplines on top.

                        They’re built like vampires.

                        There is no rage or gnosis or stepping sideways, nor being able to change into any of five forms.

                        They have the standard vampire special abilities modified by their “clan weakness” and 3 dots of disciplines, one of which must be in their custom discipline; Lycanthropy;

                        Lycanthropy
                        1) Wolf Form: as Protean 3, but wolf only.
                        2) Heightened Senses: as Auspex 1.
                        3) Feral Claws: as Protean 2.
                        4) Wolf Hide: mechanically as Serpentis 3, but no willpower cost and can’t squeeze (appearance-wise it’s your classic horror film wolf-man).
                        5) Hybrid Form: mechanically as Vicissitude 4 (appearance-wise it’s a hybrid form akin to Crinos).

                        Their other “clan” disciplines are Celerity and Potence. They can also learn Animalism, Fortitude and Presence (though instead of being paralyzed and unable to act out of fascination, it’s due to supernatural terror), but not other disciplines. They power their disciplines (including attribute boosting and healing) by feeding on the flesh of mortals (gain “blood” points equal to half the health levels dealt by their bite attacks).

                        Their “Clan” weakness is that they cannot blood bond or ghoul, nor can they spend “blood” or use their disciplines during daylight hours. They also take aggravated damage from silver, but do not take damage from sunlight.

                        So yes, they can move around during the day, but they’re no stronger than a mortal and actually weaker than a typical ghoul that vampires will use to protect themselves and carry about their daytime affairs (for example, a ghoul can use its disciplines during daylight hours... the werewolf cannot).

                        In other words, my Lupines are on par with a vampire of similar experience and generation and are man-eating monsters, not noble warriors for Gaia (who, again, doesn’t exist in this campaign... or if she does, is another biblical demon masquerading as a pagan god).

                        You don’t have to like it, but my players do and that’s all that matters.

                                                    I am also a fan of a comprehensive World of Darkness, with Cainites and Garou and Awakened, etc. In trying to assemble a cosmology that is at least roughly unified, I've found the best approach to be a willingness to trash some of the assumptions of each gameline without ripping them up entirely.

                                                    For Vampire and Demon, the Abrahamic God does exist, but is not quite equivalent with The One, instead being one of the largest identifiable aspects. The Demons are remembering a lot of particulars wrong, though a lot of it is at least vaguely accurate.

                                                    For Werewolf, the Garou are essentially correct in their belief that Gaia and the (conflicts of) the Triat are one of the most important features of reality, in that they are another crucial aspect of The One. But again, not everything; there is more between the Gaia Realm and the Umbra than is dreamt of in their philosophy.

                                                    Mages are correct to a degree about Consensus Reality, in that they theoretically have the potential to rewrite Reality in almost any way they like. But in practice, Reality has its own existing opinions on the matter (e.g. the Triat), and is generally capable of putting arrogant Mages in their place.

                                                    What is The One? My current theory is essentially pantheistic: The One is The Tellurian, the multiverse of interconnected realms that make up the World of Darkness, bounded by the primal chaos of the Deep Umbra and the absolute nothingness of the Abyss. If you make the analogy of The One being a single human being, every Bad Thing in the World of Darkness boils down to an Internal problem, the equivalent of a physical or psychological dysfunction that needs to heal or work itself out, or an External problem, some kind of Eldritch Horror capable of 'attacking' something on that scale. Generally speaking, God and Gaia don't 'concern themselves' with the plights of individuals because, by analogy, the concerns of individuals are the concerns of cells or atoms; they care about the fate of limbs and organs, not the condition of individual cells. Mages Ascending, meanwhile, is the equivalent of a reproductive process, the splitting off of a portion that has grown to the point of becoming its own Reality (or Eldritch Horror....).

                                                    There are obviously a lot of details that get trampled in such an interpretation, particularly the teleological aspects of most gamelines, but that's intentional. In a lot of ways, it's about accommodating the mechanical realities of the games while being willing to play fast and loose with the more open and intentionally-mysterious aspects of the lore. There's a lot of particular details I haven't quite decided even with the framework has been figured out; I dunno whether I want to make Oblivion an Internal or External problem, for example.

                                                    While I enjoy the exercise, and like the answer I've come up with, I do think that Heavy Arms' initial response was probably the best: depends on how much thematic inconsistency you define as "a bunch," but mostly no, lol.
