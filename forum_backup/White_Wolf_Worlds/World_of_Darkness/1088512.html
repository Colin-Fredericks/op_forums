    Has anyone played Dark Ages: Inquisitor?

    05-05-2017, 06:16 PM
    I'm not in love with a lot of the WoD, but honestly view the whole Dark Ages line as a separate thing and I absolutely love it. Inquisitor stands out as one of the more unique books in the bunch, and I adore the concept behind it, but I don't think I've ever seen it even mentioned, let alone played. If you've ever been in an Inquisitor chronicle, I'd love to hear about it!

      I ran a convention one shot for it, which didn't really endear me towards wanting to try to recruit for a bigger campaign.

      Player feedback and my experience was basically that while it's an interesting premise, and it plays well, it's kind of listless. There's not quite enough meat in the Inquisition as written to really get a lot of mileage out of: hunt monsters, Church politics, hunt monsters, more politics, repeat. Between the concessions to the metaplot, and to not create a disparity between what the Inquisition is doing in the other DA game and things happening in Inquisition, it can feel very pinned in with what to do with it beyond those two core play modes.

      As well, it's a bit caught between the much more mechanically simple Society of Leopold as presented in the Hunters Hunted books, and the more mechanical invested Hunter the Reckoning. Pulling from both doesn't really combine as well as it could in practice. The recent preview of Hunter the Vigil 2e's new Endowment system to me seems like something that could be stolen to address this better than DAI ended up with (HtV 1e's Endowments had a few similar issues to DAI's faction powers).

      Even if you treat the DA line as completely separate in continuity from the main WoD metaplot, it can open space for DAI to be much more enjoyable, but you end up having to fill that in yourself. Which might have something to do with how it doesn't feel like DAI got played much (though a lot of other factors probably played into this).

                  I've been playing Dark Ages: Inquisitor a lot over the years, and I'm getting more and more committed to the system after every adventure. At this point, I have a lot of practical experience about running the game, and I feel it would be wonderful to talk to fellow enthusiasts. But alas, searching the internet reveals very little discussion about the game. This thread is one of the only things that can be found, and I feel I have a duty to say at least a few words here, if for no other reason but to benefit possible future enthusiasts.

                  I see doubts about the scope of the game here: hunting, burning and church politics in between hunts. I don't think it needs to be that narrow at all, and here's why: inquisitors are people. They have some exceptional gifts and powers, but they are first and foremost human beings, ESPECIALLY when compared to other WoD (or DA) splats. Not only do they fit in the crowd of the setting biologically, but also culturally. They are Christians, like most other people in the setting. These simple facts open up several approaches to adventures that can be utilized. Inquisitors, being human, can take part in human politics (not only church politics). Matters of economy might interest them. New technologies adapted in society can affect their lives. A biological hazard like a disease is something they can not ignore in their work, even if they are hunting incorporeal enemies. Any human confilict resulting from greed, misunderstanding, neglicience, love, immigration, ambition, heresy, stubbornness, or any other reason, that affects their flock, can become a central element in the game. For a part, Dark Ages: Inquisitor IS actually Dark Ages: Mortal.

                  That said, the name of the game remains Dark Ages: Inquisitor, and those general elements of human conflict must be mixed with elements unique to the life of the inquisitors. They must hunt, and they must burn. But the hunting and the burning can happen in a theatre filled with elements relating to general medieval life. They can be more than window dressing; instead, they can play a major part. For example:

                  - Politics: Guelph / Ghibelline conflict in North Italian city-states spreads over to vampires, and the inquisitors realize they can root out the blood-drinkers if they follow the human conflict.
                  - Natural disaster: fishermen in Cyprus face desperate times as the fish are diminishing (due to natural causes), and some of them return to pagan rites to bring the fish back. Demons or spirits appear, and the inquisitors have to deal with those, but they have to understand the root of the problem, or else it will just re-appear later.
                  - Psychology: a cell of NPC inquisitors is disintegrating rapidly, due to the fact that they have lost a key member to mundane illness. This member was the psychological center of the group, keeping the different personalities tied together. Now no one is dealing with the supernatural problems in the area. The NPC group will remain ineffective until someone understands the cause of their ineffectiveness.

                  In all of these examples, supernatural adversaries don't operate in a vacuum, but always in the theatre of medieval Europe. It's often much more satisfying to present the adversaries surrounded by human society, rather than in the creepy cabin in the woods.

                  Another aspect that I must emphasize is the amount of "screen-time" that the adversaries will have. Lovecraft knew that the strongest fear is the fear of the unknown, and this means that as a ST, you should probably limit the "screen-time" of the monsters severely. Not only will this make the monsters more scary and more exceptional, but also the remaining "screen-time" will be given to the characters' own society of medieval Christendom. By hinting at the presence or possibility of evil, tension will remain high in the adventure, even with limited actual exposure.

                  Then there is the more ambitious approach of the philosophical or theological adventure. By drawing the focus towards these abstract concepts, players won't be feeling that they just hunt and burn demons. How to do this in practice? Present the PC's with patterns in happenings and/or NPC characters. These patterns should also cast their own actions in a different light. At best, they will be re-considering the beliefs of their characters. These beliefs can be on the theoretical side, or on the practical. Examples:

                  - What is heresy? Present them with a spectrum of heretics, and also a spectrum of orthodox believers. Or present them with reasons for being a heretic or orthodox. Someone might be heretic, or orthodox, for all the wrong reasons. The PC's might develop more nuanced attitudes towards heresy (while still remaining orthodox).
                  - A virtue's or value's relation to another virtue or value. Does someone in the group value only decisive action? Thorough research? Rigid morality? Absolute orthodoxy? A sensitive conscience? Take two virtues and make a 2x2 matrix with them. Then present the PC's with NPC's with each combination of these traits, and watch their attitudes change. They will ecounter traits that they valued in isolation, and they might learn that it makes more sense to look at them in context (What good is morality if it is coupled with laziness? What good is decisive action if it is coupled with immorality? etc).
                  - Attack or defend a theological concept or dogma. It makes more sense to me to defend them in the contex of this game, but you could play the devil's advocate if you wished and attack them instead. Most people who want to play this game do care about what their character thinks and feels about religion. For example, you might a have a group of NPCs who refuse to practice the sacrament of confession, preferring to confess to God directly. First the PC's might learn that this is due to corrupt priests abusing their knowledge. But then you could turn the tables around and have the heretic group fall victim of pride and hubris.

                  How to hunt monsters during a philosophical / theological adventure? Simple - make the monsters one side in the debate. They do not actually have to join any debate, but if you just present them and their actions in a certain light, your point will be noticed. Monster hunting will feel fresh then, because the adventure is dealing with the questions of WHY the monsters (or the things they do) are bad (besides them being evil demons).

                  My final point will be the monsterless adventure. I've done a couple of those, and I'll be the first to say that they are best used sparingly. People are playing this game because they want to fight monsters, so this adventure will be a surprise. One of the reasons of why to do this is that they will not take monsters for granted. Anyway, you should probably have hints and rumours of monsters in every adventure. Now, when they finally find that there is no monster, the reason might be:

                  - There was a monster, but it is dead now (or in torpor...) (learn about it).
                  - There was a monster, but it went away (go after it in the next adventure?).
                  - There was no monster but a setup (catch the culprits and turn them to the secular authorities).
                  - There was no monster but a misunderstanding (this one might be a bit dissatisfying, but it might do as a warning about quiotism).

                  The monsterless adventure is always a warning about being trigger-happy. If the PC's have interrogated or even tortured suspects, it will be a grim lesson when it all clears out. I recommend it every once in a while, just to keep the PC's on their toes.

                          Originally posted by Xaltotun View Post
                          Lovecraft knew that the strongest fear is the fear of the unknown,...
                          A small nitpick, but Lovecraft's horror is based in the unkownable, not the unknown. His stories rarely lean on suspense in a meaningful way because he did not think that the ultimate horror was something that would stop being scary once it came into view. There's a reason his stories consistently deal with truth so beyond human comprehension that simply trying to fathom a fraction of it risks destroying a person's mind, or beings of such great scope that rule over the universe that simply do not care about you, because you do not matter to the universe.

                          Beyond that... your post is good but it largely amounts to the same advice most people that run whatever game about hunter monsters end up developing in order to keep the Monster of the Week cycle interesting and fun. You can learn the same lessons watching the X-files and how it intersperses mundane, unique oddities, and the myth arc threats the heroes face.

                          Note how you never actually discuss the game itself? Everything you said would be just as applicable to running any other monster hunting game set in Medieval Europe. There's no praise for the mechanical side of things, how Inquisitor fits into the wider WoD (for good or ill), or why DAI is appealing over the many other options out there. There's little about how myopic the DA line is in general regarding how much Medieval Europe had cultural exchange with other areas of the world. DAI lacks tools for a basic conflict: Catholic Inquisitors and Muslim witch-hunters targeting the same enemy. In modern WoD rules, True Faith is explicitly not Christian only. Any faith can have hunters that are dedicated enough to wield True Faith as a weapon against evil. I can use the sheer variety of hunting groups in Hunter: the Vigil to get variations between different groups of hunters that might come into conflict with each other.

                          The reason so many of us didn't stick with DAI is simple: it's not really that special. It's not bad. It's just not offering anything other games either do better, or do just as well while doing more.

                                              Originally posted by Jackob View Post
                                              I ran a one-shot right when it came out, but honestly, I don't revall anything about the game.
                                              I also reviewed DA:I for my podcast.
                                              Jackob, I listened to your podcast review of DA:I. It is so wonderful to witness at least someone discussing the game - it has mostly been ignored all these years. I have a feeling that people have run one-shots with it, more than anything - full-fledged campaigns using it must be a rare thing.

                                              You correctly identified some of the problems of the game in the podcast - for example, the almost complete lack of support for Holy Relics. And yes, it always makes sense to choose a holy weapon relic instead of a non-weapon relic (this is bad). I've been trying to find ways around that; one that I'm considering is banning the use of a holy weapon in Praying. This might sound harsh, but the powers of holy weapons make up for it, I think. Still -- I haven't implemented this yet.

                                              Otherwise, you raise valid concerns all throughout the podcast, but most of the time, the answers to your concerns a) are either in the game book itself, or b) will be revealed through the practice of playing. My perspective is of course different, as I am someone who has played the game extensively. I understand that the game will look very different to someone just reading the book and trying to extrapolate how actual play will be going.

                                              To give an example, I think your point about Conviction, and the uncertainty element that risking Conviction gives, does not represent the reality of actual play. First of all, you can only risk Conviction once per scene, so that alone is a severe limitation to the mechanic. Second, and more importantly I think, the vast majority of Conviction points that the PC's will be getting, will come through Prayer. That's the true source of the power of the Inquisitors. Prayer is reliable, and although it does not give every PC the exact same amount of points, the very fact that it is repeated so often (usually at least every second day during an adventure), it will level out the discrepancies. In the end, all PC's will be getting roughly the same amout of Conviction, so they will advance at a similar pace.

                                              Now, what risking Conviction is, it is just that - risking. Because Prayer will give reliable Conviction points all the time, people will risk Conviction only in dire straits. Moreover, risking Conviction is very important, because it is one of the only ways in the game that can actually push your Conviction over your Piety, and thus make you Callous. Prayer does not make you Callous - only risking Conviction, or gaining Conviction points due to a victory over an enemy can make that. The important thing is that both of these are optional. You can always choose not to risk Conviction, or not to gain Conviction due to victory. The third option to become Callous would be to have maximum Conviction, and then commit a sin, and fail the Conscience + Faith roll, and thus lose Piety. But again, that sinning is again a choice.

                                              What I am trying to do here, is to give the point of view of an experienced DA:I player. And I restate, I just love that someone actually likes this game enough as to actually make a podcast about it - or to post to a forum about it. Every post in this thread makes me happy - the game is at least acknowledged.

                                              What I would like to do in this thread in the future (since I seem to be the only one who is really into this game, so that I cannot find fellow experts), is to answer any questions others have about the game, the setting, the rules, and especially actual play itself.
