    Per the FBI Uniform Crime Reports, in 2008 African-American youths, about 16% of the youth population, accounted for 52% of juvenile violent crime arrests, including roughly 58% of youth arrests for homicide and 67% for robbery.

    Urbanization and the drug trade are HUGE factors, which make easy demographic and morbidity/mortality comparison to other countries difficult. Also compounding this is the reluctance of some countries to publish complete statistics (looking at you, UK) as well as using different definitions of certain crimes (i.e. any crime in the US where the perpetrator is carrying a gun is a gun crime, whether or not the gun was brandished or used -- In the UK, it is only reported as a gun crime if the gun was used, the victim injured or killed, and the perpetrator found GUILTY of the gun's usage).

        Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post
        We did a discussion of this in a political science class and discussing demographics and studies being used for political gain.

        There's a certain skewering of law enforcement elements that makes the issue a "if, and, or but" when dealing with the question of, "Do minorities commit more crimes than the majority?" The short version being, "Yes and no."

        What are the complicating questions?

        * Are we talking per capita?
        * What sort of crimes are we talking about?
        * Are the crimes violent crimes or are they crimes like casual drug use (maraujana) or prostitution?
        * Are the police responding to crimes by minorities more than they are the majority?
        * Are they the minorities or the POOR who minorities are dispoportionately the membership of?
        * Which minority group are you talking about?
        * Where is the sample being taken from.

        Generally speaking, the numbers start getting very skewed if you remove nonviolent crimes from the issue and compare the averages of minorities to majorites on the same level of income.

        In Kentucky, for example, poor whites are disproportionately more likely to commit violent crimes than poor blacks.
        But the numbers even out elsewhere.

        Am I making sense?
        In short: yes. These things are always complicated.

        Though, in my eyes, speaking about these problems and trends are important. Speaking about them like "this and this minority is predisposed to this and this crime, because it's 'in their blood' "or something like that is abhorrent and I'd never, ever stand behind something like that. But if there's a problem in certain demographical groups, it's doing no good to anyone to pretend it's not there, but we have to talk about it honestly, with considering all the social and economical causes and working for improving on it.
