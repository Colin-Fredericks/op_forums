    Gehenna Games

    12-04-2023, 09:05 AM
    Hello everyone,

    I'm currently working on my PhD and in the context of my chapter on World of Darkness (more specifically its allusions to apocalypse within the setting and how they manifest narratively and ludically) I'm trying to get an understanding of how players who were around at the time experienced the storyline that ended the whole setting, as well as the experience with the end of the product line as it were.

    In light of that it would be super-interesting to me to hear accounts of how different storytellers realized Gehenna games, what approaches were used. I wonder whether people typically stuck to the storylines proposed by the Gehenna book, or went a different route with it, and how players felt about these games.

    It would be really cool if you could share your stories about the challenges, flaws or upsides of running Gehenna games. I couldn't find a thread in the search that dealt with this specifically, if I missed something let me know. I hope this doesn't break any forum rules. If I were to try and use any of the data I will of course contact you personally via PM regarding consent and of course anything I use would be anonymized and not utilize any personal information (which I won't attempt to gather here in any way anyhow).

    Would be cool to hear your stories, cheers,
    carpresearcher

                      I thought the entire idea of a company ending its own game was ludicrous. By publishing an RPG, you are putting out your product so your consumers can do whatever you want with it. I disliked the entire metaplot idea of the Revised edition. Instead of giving me tools to run my own game, it seemed to me that White Wolf wanted to hijack my game and run it for me, tell me I'm doing it wrong, and elevate their own tastes and preferences over my own.

                      In short, I want to run my own chronicles and want to play in the chronicles my friends created with their own interpretation of the setting. I don't want to play in or run someone else's vision of the setting.

                      I stopped buying World of Darkness product long before Gehenna game out. When at some point I did scanned through a copy of a friend's book, long after the line ended, I wasn't impressed. I already had my own ideas and conception of what Gehenna would look like, and for the most part I thought its scenarios failed to do the concept well. I thought it was weak and pedestrian. I know many other players who were frustrated by the changes White Wolf was making (which they felt was ruining the setting) was finally just glad that by "ending" the setting, it just meant they would no longer be destroying it. It gave them a sense of mental freedom they didn't need to follow the metaplot anymore, and could revert back to what they felt the setting should be like (for those who hadn't already done it, or who had already left the setting behind for some other game or hobby).

                      I think metaplot always destroys games and game companies. I understand why companies do it. By treating the setting as if it is a form of serial fiction - instead of an initial game setting where everyone can do whatever they like - it allows game companies to publish new supplements long past the time once they filled out the game world. It is a lazy way to keep the revenue going and increase. Unfortunately, such an approach only increases sales in the short term and hurts medium and long term revenue.

                      1) Eventually existing fans get tired by having to buy new editions of the core rules, new editions of existing sourcebooks, and so on. They already paid for those. It seems very exploitative but not truly giving them new product, new tools, new ideas - just repackaging old ones. Eventually it causes existing fans to stop buying out of resentment.

                      2) Metaplot tends to invalidate existing chronicles and games that players created according to the previous version of the setting. If all the new sourcebooks now refer to events that not only did not happen in your own game - or even perhaps directly contradicts them - they aren't usable. If in your games, the Loyalist faction of the Sabbat managed to regain their influence within the sect, then the Revised era's presentation of an extremely centralized sect strictly controlled by the Ultra-Conservative faction has little relevance. If in your games your gaming group really likes the Gangrel being a member of the Camarilla, then sourcebooks describing the Gangrel as not being members are useless to you. If you already developed your own solution to the mystery of Saulot, who cares about what the new, official version is? No reason to buy. You drop out of being a customer.

                      3) The Revised era had a big problem insulting certain aspects mentioned in the previous books. Instead of just not repeating them or providing additional information or concepts that was more in line with what you think is good, some sourcebooks deliberately want out of the way to specifically invalidate certain aspects or even insult them. I get it. The writer really didn't like some previous idea, statement, or fiction. There are things I didn't like in the original material too. My solution was simply to ignore it or change it. I didn't insult people who did like them, or go out of my way saying their approach to their own games was wrong.

                      4) After a while, increasing metaplot becomes so complex it turns new readers away. They want something simple enough for them to understand since they are brand new to the material. They have to digest concepts. Requiring them to master some kind of encyclopedic knowledge is offputting.

                      5) The real issue is that metaplot treats game books as if they are serial fiction. It's not for people who actually want to play the game. It's for people who consume metaplot as if they were passively reading or watching serial fiction. But gaming is not only interactive, not a passive form of entertainment, it is only collaborative between every game master and their players. It isn't like someone reading their favorite author or comic book character, or watching their favorite TV show.

                      Gehenna, for me, represented everything I disliked about the metaplot way of developing new product for consumers. Every RPG setting has the issue of once you initially describe all aspects of the setting, how do you keep new revenue increasing? After all, you have headcount and overhead. So instead of paring things down, relying on new customers, expanding the customer base, or figuring out how new sourcebooks should be providing new tools for STs to create their own chronicles, it's just so much easier to produce metaplot or new editions to get the existing customers to keep buying more. But mostly what it does is drive existing customers away and not attract any more new customers than they would with another approach, and possibly more.

                      Managing an RPG line is hard after the initial explosion of new material that covers all the rules, introduces all the concepts and options, and describes the setting in general. What to do next is hard. But I don't think changing rules and making new editions or turning to metaplot is the solution. Boardgame companies sell plenty of new Monopoly, Clue, Risk, and other classic games every year without the need to come up with new editions, different rules, expansions, or by ending production of a perennial favorite. (Granted they also don't have a headcount of employee who must produce new product or they lose their jobs).

                      If you are really interested in showing how cool a centralized, militaristic Sabbat that launches a Crusade to take a Camarilla city, the best way to illustrate that is to create a city sourcebook that takes that concept and runs with it. Give players examples and tools of how to run such a game. By limiting it to one city sourcebook you get the same thing across and have new product without invalidating existing chronicles. Players can incorporate that at their own game table if they want without the burden of saying this player's Loyalist character no longer fits, or that their character on the Path of Harmony must have perished in a purge, or any of the other number of things that Revised explicitly invalidated. Then you also have the opportunity to create another sourcebook that shows the other side of things and a very different conception of the Sabbat in another local setting. That's how I would have gone about it at least. Higher quality products that help translate the often vague ideas introduced in earlier sourcebooks into something practical for the game master to run at their game table.

                      RPG games are not serial fiction. Sourcebooks should be a salad bar of tools and ideas that game masters and players can pick and choose and from them.

                              Been running WoD since 1st edition VtM came out, yes I ended up collecting a large amount of the books and no my bookcases have not forgiven me.

                              When the end times books were released, I took a somewhat different approach to Gehenna. I mixed the cosmology of the Kuei-jin with the plot points of the Kindred. The 6th age was one of darkness and terror, but the wheel of existence never stops, so eventually it keeps turning until it comes all the way back around to the 1st age era where the planes of existence were closer together, magic was at its most powerful and pure. Then this was where the Exalted line comes into play since the original concept for Exalted was that it was a prequel to the WoD setting, with each exalt type being a splat analog, Abyssals = Cainites / Lunars = Shifters / Solars = Hunters / Dragon-Blooded = Kuei-jin etc. The idea was that only by surviving that long could the characters actually perceive the cyclical nature of the universe. Not that I made surviving the 6th age a walk in the park.

                              We had at that point already played around with max rank exalted who survive the second age and the coming third age, but also survived until the 5th age to interact with the WoD setting. The Baali pray to eldritch things sleeping beneath the earth, most of those would be low to mid level spirits from the Exalted setting. Caine would be an abyssal Half-Caste who never quite exalted and his children all carry the potential to awaken their abyssal blood ties but everything is so weak that only a sharing of cursed blood can activate it in the modern age and even then, the result is pitifully weak.

                              All in all it was fun, though the tricky part was changing the game play up to ensure that things didn't get stale waiting for the next era to switch over.
