            Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post
            I also point out the fact the D&D movies were subpar also is why they're subpar.
            I think that's the only true thing said until this point about what to expect.

            D&D and WoD both have the trait of being settings/styles, not stories. It doesn't mean that making a show based on then isn't feasible or that it is easier, it just means that you need to come up with a good story to tell. The D&D movies failed royally because they were badly done, nothing exceptional or inherent to D&D.

            And you can make any kind of story using them, which is also both a feature and a bug. The expectations of the fanbase are a problem only if you commit to fulfilling them, then you'll put yourself in a tough spot. If you make it reasonably clear that your story isn't the story of the setting, but just a story in the setting, then you can attract people based on your genre and proposal and avoid that trap.

            Going right for reference comedy is as much a trap, if not an even bigger one. Yes, it can be done and work, but it is just another genre and will only work in its own context, like any other genre.

            Complaints about corpo-speak and ESG scores are void of merit on themselves. Everything is profit-based. Onyx Path doesn't work for the art more than Warner Bros. do. Artists have families to feed, bills to pay and stakeholders to please if they want to keep making their art. The more your art brings money, the more you can devote your time and energy to it instead of to a main job. The shows, books and comics we love are as much made for profit as the ones we see as sterile and mass-produced. And they may very well have those problems, it is just that what caused those problems wasn't the search for profit, but just sheer stupidity.

            Except, of course, in some cases where we call them like that just because they're meant for a broader audience and stop being interesting for us. Then it is just too tempting to blame corporate interests instead of accepting that there are other people in the world that also deserve to have things they like, and adapting to cater to them is a perfectly valid choice. Fans have a tendency to feel entitled to the IP to the extreme. They're not. We're not.

            Of course the corpo-speak does worries me a little, but just because they're avoiding talking about the show itself, and that's what should be worrying. And ESG scores can be as much a downer or encourage creativity. The whole Silver Age of comics came from the limitations imposed by the Comics Code, and it was far more creative and interesting than the Golden Age (have you ever read a GA comic? I did, and it is mostly lame with a few gems).

            Originally posted by Thoth View Post
            If I was to give credit to what was revolutionary about V:tM I would say it was the Storyteller system, where XP was gained by playing rather than butchering X amount of monsters for Y amount of xp to gain a level and steal all of their stuff. The D10 dot system with difficulty 6 can be learned in a few minutes, where as explaining THAC0 still causes mild forms of PTSD.
            Not even that, not by a mile.

            D&D wasn't the only thing before WoD and the RPG market already had everything they put in the Storyteller System and a lot more by the dawn of the 90's. Shadowrun did the whole 'add two traits, roll that many dice, state a target number for your difficulty, count on successes' in 1989, if with D6, and other versions of this predated it. We had games that used advanced calculus in the rules for real, and others that ditched dices completely.

            Tracking Backgrounds and increasing traits by playing the story was already in Traveler in 1977. Call of Cthulhu had Sanity and rules for how your actions affect you personally at least since 1981. Simple and highly lethal combat exists since Boot Hill, 1975. Point-based character creation is as old as Superhero: 2044 in 1977. Rein-Hagen himself brought the rules and concepts related to having support characters from his previous years with Ars Magica. Even more specific things like rules for characters climbing an in-game rank ladder like Generation and Garou Rank can be traced to sources like Paranoia in 1984. Most of those games had simpler rules than D&D and actually than the Storyteller System. Some have less flaws, too. Many others were more robust and modular, GURPS being from 1985. VtM simply wasn't all that brilliant from a mechanics standpoint.

            If there's one thing I give WoD credit for, is simply for capturing the spirit of the 90's better than any other game at the time. It was the right game at the right time and the right place. Not for any extraordinary merit of its developers (it wasn't bad by any means, though), but simply by happenstance. Yet, it is a merit, an important one, to be in tune with a decade and a specific generation of teenagers. It is a remarkable feat, but one to be understood in its own confines.

            Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post
            Which...makes it sort of not punk, doesn't it?



            (Also annoyed with Solarpunk)
            Blame people not getting Cyberpunk for both. And I personally blame this on Cyberpunk 2020.

            Cyberpunk was maybe the last true punk literary genre. But thanks to things like Cyberpunk 2020 people thought that it was just Sci-Fi through a given aesthetics, and that lead to other aesthetically-bound Sci-Fi to be called "-punk" by association regardless of any relationship to actual Punk.

            Also,

            Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
            The majority of the OG punk bands were adamantly against the idea that their music was nihilistic. Rather they were calls to action because the systems are broken and need fixing, and punk was a way to express that discontent in a fashion relatable to people.
            That.

            Punk literature may seem nihilistic because it plays against a background of the worse the system has to offer, one way or another. On top of that, as it is Punk, it heavily plays with the idea that mainstream moral isn't just flawed, but a part of the system that causes this suffering, so the heroes aren't nice, actual goodness doesn't look good. Yet it is in the end hopeful that the system will eventually fail, catastrophically, setting people free.

            No, there are Posers who'll market the revolution, but the revolution will eventually drown them. Violently.
