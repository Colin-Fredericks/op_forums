                            Originally posted by Herr Meister View Post
                            But now you made me curious, what do you mean by "tweaked sorcerers"?
                            Basically taking sorcerer revised rules and boosting sorcerers in several places to make them in line with other splats. Main tweaks are:
                            1. Having them start with the same stat arrays as other supernaturals(SRev has them start with less dots in almost every category than your standard starting splat).
                            2. Altering some paths to actually function off the aspects system. For some reason the writers thought it was fine to leave some paths functioning on a different mechanical system than others.
                              1. Prime examples: Mana manipulation. Has no aspects and functions like a blood magic path. So iseparated it's powers into aspects that get stronger if you invest more successes into them. The "power" aspect of it being the things you can pull mana from or push it into(level 1 only allowing a sorcerer to pull mana from themselves to save in outside objects and pull mana back from those objects, level 2 allows tapping nodes, etc.)
                            3. Making one of the Aspects determine difficulty. Example for clarity: A sorcerer using hellfire 5 can inflict 10 damage dice with the path as long as they get 1 success and only needs to spend successes if they want a longer range, to effect more targets, or to use a fancier damage type.
                              1. Base sorcerer is weird, it makes a player declare at what dot rating a player is using a path at before the roll to determine the difficulty and then divides the successes to invest into different "aspects" to determine the actual effects. The dot rating you use the path at determines the limit of how many successes you can invest in any one aspect.
                                1. As you've probably guessed, the above system ends up making the system convoluted and frustrating. The player with a decent dice pool using a lower level path rating ends up with more successes than she's allowed to invest in anything and the same player using the higher levels of the path end up with nowhere near enough successes to actually reach the higher aspect ceiling.
                                2. If the Example: I showed was using the normal rules, the sorcerer would be casting at difficulty 9(have 1-2 successes average) while needing to spend 4 successes on top of the initial success to actually cast the spell to do a full 10 dice of damage. That 5 success spell would also be limited to 1 target at touch range and couldn't do anything fancier than lethal damage because the sorcerer would have Nothing to spend on other aspects.
                            4. Making the dice-pools for casting Ability + Path rating and paths cost current rating x5 to lower XP costs because the above tweaks still leave sorcerer paths generally weaker than the equivalent of a splat's abilities at the same rating. This makes it so sorcerers can be more generalist with a lot of tricks up their sleeves even with generally weaker powers.
                            5. Use lost tower resonance rules from mage 2nd edition. Resonance raises the dice-pool of path casting by +1 for one type of action and subtracts from the dice-pool by -1 for the opposite type of action for every dot in that resonance. A person with a Fiery resonance would be Really good at spells dealing with fire, renewal, and purification, but would Suck at spells dealing with Ice, water, calm, and temperance.
                            6. Actually giving Enchanting and Alchemy Rules rather than handing the player a list of example and going "Figure it out".
                              1. Enchanting allows a player to make an object that either A. Gives [path rating used] dice to a specialist ability roll, B. Gives [1/2 path rating rounded down] successes to a roll, or C. puts a pre-made spell or ritual from another path onto an object for instant access[maximum path rating of the spell or ritual must be less than or equal to the enchanter's path rating]. Aspect are split between how long the enchantment lasts, number of uses, "Price" of use(success added lowers the severity, 1 success may make you kill a person between every use, 5 would be something trivial, 6 would be free), and if enchanting a spell from another path, any aspects used in that spell(enchanting is an extended roll, so a player can still make something stronger than they could normally cast on the fly).
                              2. Alchemy is allows a player to transmute substances, allowing more complex effects at each level, higher level allowing a player to transmute supernatural substances, which allows a user to copy other splats abilities to a limited extent(or fully become one of them at path level 6). Aspects are the same as enchanting, only instead of copying a "Spell from another path" a sorcerer copies a supernatural ability at a rate of 2 successes per level of ability(maximum half path rating rounded up). As a materials-based path, alchemy can't transmute fully intangible things like like ghosts, spirits, demons, or raw mana, and can't copy the powers of such entities(basically same limitation as Srev).
                            There are other changes, but this post is long as-is.

                                        Originally posted by Herr Meister View Post
                                        So, how do you feel about these issues?
                                        I think the Plot Device in those books is less the issue and more a symptom. It could have been done well.

                                        Hades has a point in that the old saying holds true: if you stat it, it can be killed. I know you can create really powerhouse sheets for the NPCs, but there's always the chance that a combination of combo and lucky/unlucky rolls will settle matters in unexpected ways.

                                        But then you can fudge rolls behind the shield as easily as you can Plot Device shit, so whatever. You can also Plot Device in constructive ways instead of just killing your PCs, so whatever again. As I said, that's not the real problem.

                                        The real problem is that they didn't gave STs any advice on how to use those characters outside the strict lines of the railroad adventure.

                                        Lair of the Hidden stays as my favorite adventure for WoD bar none, and virtually every character in it should be capable of disposing of the coterie by themselves individually. But if the adventure simply said that things will go this or that way, and the Methuselah in the castle will just kill the characters if they "mess up", it would be just as poorly made as Nights of Prophecy, regardless of it having their stats.

                                        What made LotH better as an adventure isn't the stats themselves, but the fact that the adventure focus much more on presenting the character, goals, passions and frustrations of the casting, and let the PCs deal with them however they want to. It has extremely limited boundaries, yet it lets you explore those boundaries to your heart's content.

                                        Do the stats help? Yes. Are they necessary? With enough info on the kind of thing you can expect from them, hardly. They're there less because they're integral to its quality and more, again, as a symptom of the mindset behind it.

                                        Originally posted by Prometheas View Post
                                        The worst thing about Samuel Haight is that he and his skin-dancers could have been a great concept.
                                        The problem with Height was less that he had every splat power, and more that this basically surmised him. Even his creation of the Skin Dancers as a whole became a second thought and he went on to just be a bogey-man with all the powers.

                                        It holds true for the Mage powers, too. The whole process of the Skin Dancer was interesting, becoming a Ghoul is ok on itself. But the story on how he obtained the powers of a Mage isn't bad because he did it, but because it felt cheap. Plot Armor isn't a problem, but it can't feel like it for the reader, and it felt. Likewise, giving a source of power to the character just because you want them to have it isn't at all a problem, but the reader shouldn't feel that the whole thing only exists for this purpose. We don't have this feel with the Rite of Sacred Rebirth and how Height developed it, but we do have with his Mage powers.

                                        Now, for the current Skin-Dancers... I actually find them ok. I think they actually fit better in the setting now than before.

                                        Basically, the problem with the original version is that it is too similar in role to the Black Spiral Dancers. Between Fomori, Spirits, other monsters like vampires and the BSD, they would just end up being one more antagonist in a game that has no lack of antagonists. It had no reason for the Garou or the players to ever see the Skin Dancers as anything else than another manifestation of the Wyrm, and Kinfolk that became one of them would necessarily be villains, either by disposition or necessity.

                                        W20 brought then to a grey area that I feel is a lot more conducive for giving them their own niche, despite being mostly undeveloped. They're a new tribe carving their place in the world and seeking for a purpose, they're a too varied bunch from many distinct tribes and situations trying to build a community and culture without the support of shared traditions, they aren't common rejects lost to the tribes, but outsiders that make themselves Garou by sheer drive and want to be accepted by the others while rejecting the Wyrm. I think this is far more compelling than just making them another antagonist, and at the same time they remain a cautionary tale
