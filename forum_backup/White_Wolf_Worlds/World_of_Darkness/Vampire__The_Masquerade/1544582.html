    Chronicle of Ages

    10-23-2024, 03:21 PM
    In all my years of experience in the WoD setting, I've always liked the idea of building our own canon world through the ages. Most of my tables usually start somewhere in the Middle Ages and then advance slowly (often times faster or slower depending on the taste of my players and what I envision for a story,e.g. some players and sometimes I myself feel not inclined to actually get into the current era, like years 2000s, etc.) through the years. As expected many things end up different in the setting, like the year of formation of Camarilla and sometimes even entire clan devlopments are utterly changed, I had a story where the Tzimisce Antediluvian was "destroyed" for the second time during the 1300s and another clan was founded in its stead, some other times the Giovanni ended up being totally subjulgated and the Capadocian clan was still around in the 1800s. Other times, more fantastic things happened as tradition mages had some astounding victories over the Order of Reason and technology advanced much slower than in our own world.

    All of these examples I give in order to ask you all, have you ever had something like this in your games that made the world totally different than the "normal setting"? I ask this because it's to be expected that some things are going to follow a totally different path, as for example, it would be normal if some important Canon NPCs are destroyed in our setting, as it's hard to expect things to follow exactly as they did in Canon, but I think it's interesting to consider these changes.

      All of these examples I give in order to ask you all, have you ever had something like this in your games that made the world totally different than the "normal setting"? I ask this because it's to be expected that some things are going to follow a totally different path, as for example, it would be normal if some important Canon NPCs are destroyed in our setting, as it's hard to expect things to follow exactly as they did in Canon, but I think it's interesting to consider these changes.
      There are two main ways that the setting changes.

      The first being that the PCs change a historical event and the resulting timeline is altered, the PCs prevent Lincoln from being assassinated or they assisted the Confederacy, so they won the civil war and thus world history is altered because the US took a different path. This can be fun to think about, but unless you are running a very complicated historical simulation, your average ST will just run history as it was, with the PCs contributions or alterations lost to history since the public never really knows what actually happened.

      The second way is for the PCs to have agency in the current time. This is where Player ingenuity has a head on collision with the setting over plot holes. The very game design states that the neonates can never out do the elders, they are eternally oppressed and at best can hope to survive for another night. But the reality of the situation is that PCs have far more potential agency than most groups use because they never consider the options. So, let's look at a few examples.

      1. The vampire population is canonically relatively low, meaning that once you assign 10 - 50 cainites per city, there are hundreds of cities out there which are unclaimed by vampires. So rather than playing politics, being under the thumb of the local elders, any coterie can simply travel across the country and stake a claim on an unclaimed city or town. They are now the Prince & Primogen of the place, with no need to overthrow the existing power structure. Of course, they will have to fight to maintain their control over the place, but they can sire as they wish and make the rules as they wish. So why do so many coteries stick around their city again?

      2. The character creation process means you are not restricted to only making vampires who were janitors, mechanics, cops, or gang members in their mortal lives. You can be a captain of industry or other business mogul from the start. If you go with Resources 5 / Influence 5 / Contacts 5, you are making Elon Musk or Jeff Besos look like underachievers, especially since in all the fluff the richest and most influential mortals can't compare to vampires in the same situations. So, what happens if a coterie is made up of kindred with those kinds of stats? Rather than getting into a turf war with the local gang or the established kindred in their city, they just buy a city sized plot of land in the middle of the desert and build a company town from the ground up. Every aspect of the city would be designed to isolate the residents and meet all vampiric needs. Once again, the coterie becomes the ruling class of vampires rather than fighting against them in the previous city.

      3. Given the rise of technology in first world countries, it is becoming an increasingly problematic plot hole to explain how vampires function in those areas without breaking the masquerade by existing inside human society. So, vampires need to hide away more than they are currently portrayed as doing in the fluff. What happens when vampire coteries start building doomsday bunkers like converted missile silos? They can have a live in herd, built in hydroponics, a solar and wind farm for power, and all business is conducted over video calls which makes Presence and Dominate useless. There is relatively little reason to maintain the Lost Boys method of existing, but then about 90% of the plot hooks wouldn't work.

      Breaking or changing the setting happens almost automatically if you are not artificially enforcing the outcome.

          Because of the issue that given enough XP, even neonate NPCs can overshadow powerful elders over the course of a long chronicle, I typically prefer to have chronicles that last many decades although I have never run a game where it lasted centuries. So I always start them in an interesting historical era. The 1920s are a favorite because I already have a lot of historical sourcebooks from the Call of Cthulhu RPG that can be quickly adapted. I did have an ambition once to run a game set in France that started at the "standard" 1197 era of Vampire: the Dark Ages and last until the end of the Hundred Years War which would have covered the time of the Anarch Revolt, but it ended far earlier than that.

          My view on things is that the PCs are important, but aren't that important. They can play vital roles that may impact the setting materially, but can't fundamentally change the historical forces driving change. That does mean I would keep them away from any fundamental events like a diablerie of the antediluvians or Tyler's attack on Hardestadt. The setting will still be recognizable, but important departures can take place.

          For example, a Lasombra PC who joined the Camarilla would not be able to stop most of his remaining clan from eventually joining the Sabbat, but she could become an important member of a much larger Lasombra anti-tribu, perhaps one even with a seat on the Inner Circle. Likewise perfectly fine for the Cappadocians to have a much larger presence that survived the Giovanni purge, but much of that is because I never found the purge to be that credible and the game ended up revealing all these surviving Cappadocians (though called by different bloodline names) anyway. Maybe a bloodline survives that perished, or perished that officially survives. Bloodlines aren't as relevant to the game than the main clans are.

          Individual NPCs are totally open to being destroyed because they are there to serve a purpose in the chronicle I am running, not to fulfill some historical destiny. I don't find any canon NPCs to be so precious that I could not tolerate their destruction. If they needed to serve some special role in canonical history, some similar NPC will work just fine. To be honest, while I do like some of the signature or canonical characters, I'm just not that impressed with Vykos, Anatole, Lucita, etc. that I would feel the need to keep them alive. They'd be treated just like any other NPCs in a city sourcebook. Sure, if some NPC really tickled my fancy, I'd do what I could to keep them around and play smart, but none of the "major" signature characters fit that.

                          I think we have to address the fundamental difference between what the players want from the setting verses the setting default as law.

                          Using video games as an example, some Players want a Dark Souls experience where survival is the accomplishment, some Players want a fantasy game where they can be characters with different problems than they do IRL, some Players just want to play an RPG rather than going to therapy, and some are wanting to experiment with a different persona. All of which can be done quite easily with the rules and setting as written.

                          But what does an ST do if the Players want to do something more scripted? The default situation is everyone makes a character and is dropped into the game world to interact with all the NPCs and each other. But what does the ST do when the Player already has a beginning, middle, and end planned out? In Narrative terms this could be the rags to riches storyline but achieving the wealth is only the middle game since they want to actually play around with it. Now the middle and end game can be about anything as long as it allows the PCs to make a difference.

                          To use the Count of Monte Cristo to frame this issue, your average storyline in a game is the PCs are betrayed and sent to prison, the game is how to escape. The sort of Players I am talking about are also wanting to play out the treasure hunt, building the influence network, setup the plan for revenge and then enact it. From there then they will probably want to play out how the characters would contend with the setting now that they have all the power and influence to be proactive rather than reactive to it.

                          This more scripted character design seems to be common if the Player is an ST. This gets more common if most if not all of your group are STs, since their minds are essentially trained to think in story arcs more than the experimental "lets see what this does" style of character design.
