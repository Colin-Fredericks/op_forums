    Dangers to Vampires - New Systems

    06-18-2026, 04:16 PM
    I'm looking at V5 Core pp 221 and trying not to die of disgust and sadness.

    The worse part, and reason I'm not marking this as [V5], is that the bulk of the problem isn't new to V5, it's old staples of VtM that should have been killed with prejudice 3 decades ago.

    So, Five entries total in my ramblings. Each will start with a problem, followed by a long wall of me dishing out frustration, and then something I hope to be a solution that helps people without the need to actually read or agree with the frustration. So: Section -> Diagnosis -> Fury -> Proposal. Suggest all to skip Diagnosis and Fury unless wanting to engage in pointless debate. The proposals should be good even if you find RAW fine.

    Overall philosophy: my proposals focus on identifying how each rule affect the game and give the ST an informed choice, not to just say I know better than the book.

    Except Extreme Cold, that was just ridiculous.

    INDEX
    - Sunlight and Daysleep
    - Fire and Application
    - Decapitation and Metaphysics
    - Wooden stakes and Combat
    - Cold and Extreme Threats

    Sunlight and Daysleep

    The problem: the system tries to cover three distinct takes on it, but ends up favoring one so much it drowns the others.

    1. Sunlight is dangerous and Daysleep is incapacitating. Kindred society simply can't afford to not be exclusively nocturnal.

    2. Damnation adds nuance, not all monsters are equally at danger. Those closer to mortals can afford a longer stay.

    3. Indirect exposure matters. You can be tactical about it, you can take calculated risks.

    But the actual damage rate is so brutal it just flattens everything to "sunlight kills, don't go", the many details become mostly irrelevant and few tables ever put the rules to the test. There's no point. The rules could just be "a vampire exposed to sunlight dies in 3 turns if don't get out". Staying awake is a sequence of boring, pointless rolls to still work capped.

    PROPOSAL

    - Main Rule (simple)
    A vampire suffer 2 Agg dmg each turn exposed to sunlight. The ST is the final judge on what counts as exposure, but there's no middle ground.
    Keep rules for awakening, but if damage is taken it's instant success for the scene to stay awake.
    REASONING: this is already how it works in practice, as the factors that change damage aren't enough to make it less urgent to escape. Take agg per turn, in a couple minutes tops you're toast.

    - The Mercy (optional rule)
    The purifying light of the sun punishes the Beast, not the Human beneath. If being moral protects against the sun, the rate of damage is as follows:
    - Humanity 0: 5/turn, no Fortitude applies
    - A Wight has no place under the sun, ever
    - Humanity 1-2: 2/turn
    - Regular, pretty harsh, run or perish
    - Humanity 3-4: 1/2 turns
    - Mild? This is still Agg, but you can brave a short run without it being a death sentence
    - Humanity 5-6: 1/scene
    - Still free Agg, but once in a while you can make a painful sacrifice to take care of business
    - Humanity 7-8: 1/day
    - Don't get confident, this will burn through your reserves (and skin) fast if you abuse, but you can actually function during day despite the smell of bacon
    - Humanity 9-10: Superficial/Daywalker
    - Whatever it is that a saint does during the day, be my guest
    REASONING: Aggravated damage is a big problem regardless of amount, most vamps won't start wandering by day just because they can survive the burning pain. But that does make for a setting that highlights Humanity vs the Beast, and what the later take away from you.

    - The Aura (optional rule)
    The stronger the Curse, the harder to escape judgement by the sun itself. If proximity to Caine is relevant in the matter, use an Exposure Score:
    - Exposure 10: summer clothes, sun high in a cloudless sky.
    - Exposure 0: total darkness, absolutely no visibility.
    - any layer of protection, from heavy clothes to covers or shadows, reduce Exposure, but...
    - add Bane Severity to Exposure.
    - define a Threshold (default 5), if Exposure ≥ Threshold the character is exposed to sunlight.
    - Idea is solid, but numbers need tweaks. Take notes of what you try, what works, what don't.
    REASONING: this makes Bane Severity affect the damage in a way that actually matters as it gives you strategic decisions rather than slightly slower or faster certain destruction. It also adds to Kindred society a calculated benefit for the Embrace, as Neonates can more easily navigate the day even if everyone prefers the night still.

    For now that's what I can focus enough to make. I have ideas to hammer those and for the others, and would like the feedback.

    Keep in mind the important part is the structure, the precise numbers are just dials.


    Fire and Application

    The problem: as a recurring theme, the game never settled for real if it is about realistic combat, overly dramatic combat, tactical combat or cinematic combat. Trying to mix is valid, but means making decisions on how conflicting premises will resolve.

    Instead WoD mostly chose to not choose and the rules are all over the place without much attention to how they tell stories, causing not versatility, but incoherence.

    And few places felt this incoherence more than fire rules (or lack thereof), which is particularly egregious when it is the #1 method of causing Agg to everything that moves and especially pains vampires through Rötschreck.

    You see the results in the many, many, many discussions about how using fire is OP, fire powers are OP, fire this and that, despite fire IRL being at the same time everything the game says, yet most definitely not a common weapon in real conflicts.

    Why fire is a big deal?

    1. It bites deep, no way around it. Any rule that tries to "balance" things by making fire cause less damage is missing the memo of why firefighters are firefighters, not just "rescuers".

    2. Fire is almost uncontrollable by nature. Yes, the Pyro is a fun unit in Team Fortress 2. IRL no one uses flamethrowers as weapons because it's stupid: they require extreme care and attention, are horrible for fine control and "aim", and the user is basically stuck in place without being able to react to an actual combat as they are instead focused on applying fire without dying in the process, preferably also not destroying everything else than their target.

    3. Fire isn't subtle. You don't cover up a fire, at most you cover up with fire. It causes a mess and leaves a mess. You can certainly destroy evidence with fire, but it's an admission that you destroyed evidence in the first place. Trying to quietly off vampires with fire is like putting silencers on frag grenades.

    4. Fire is a known, constant danger. Our houses and apartments are built with fire safety protocols almost whenever you live in the world. Everything that uses fire is also full of warnings and safety instructions. And fire still causes accidents all the time, everywhere.

    5. With all that, fire is easy to produce. You see discussions about how a given fire power is broken, when the PC could simply dump gas on something and put it on fire for same or worse results.

    6. IRL fire-based weapons and ammo simply aren't what the hype says because they either:
    - Don't put anything on fire. Most phosphorus bullets and similar are just tracers without any remaining fire on them when hit target (and frequently less lethal because of deformation)
    - Work, and get all the same problems in the process. Dragon Breath ammo is an excellent example of a casing that robs your gun from all the benefits of being a gun and make it work as a glorified flamethrower. It has good technical reasons to exist, but they put it back squarely in the "sensitive tool" category of regular flamethrowers.
    - serve specific tactical purposes instead of being general use weapons, usually when the lack of control is the goal. Molotovs aren't for targeted destruction. They're for starting fires.
    - only exist for show. A lot of very cool weapons are actually bad at combat, but good at demonstration.

    Characters that use fire as weapons in media usually subtly cancel everything out by skill or fiat. What makes them cool is that they use fire, but what makes their use of fire cool is that they avoid all the problems. Is that the best approach for VtM?

    PROPOSAL

    - Control Fire Action (Wits + Resolve)
    Whenever you want to use, feed, start or put down fire, this is a Control action, no matter how big or small, no matter your purpose. It doesn't always require a roll, but it is always its own thing with its own time.
    - Crucially, that means you can't make attack or defensive rolls with fire, period. You can use it in combat, but only as a Control Fire Action. In V5 that means you don't participate in the regular combat contested roll, you forfeit.
    - Mechanically it's a decision tree where you start small and slow, then "buy" your outcome out of this paying with the risk of a fizzle or a backfire.

    Step 1: state your goal. Lighting a cigarette is a goal, putting the building on fire is a goal. Throwing a match in a tank of gasoline is not a goal, the goal is if you want it to catch fire or fizzle.
    Step 2: choose between Realistic and Dramatic fire.
    - Realistic is slow and safe, and has to be. Any resistance, hindrance or failure at planning means you back down. If everything is ok, no roll, you just do it.
    - Dramatic is risky, but is faster and don't back down. Roll Fire Control, if you fail, the fire gets out of control and you're probably the first/next to get a taste of it.
    - You can add Tactical rolls to plan ahead how the fire will spread, or Cinematic uses where the fire glows bright, but most of the heat is lost.

    Open to discussion, but that's the gist of it.

      I have to admit, since V5 was cribbing from Requiem so much anyway, I have no idea why they didn't copy VtR 2e's sunlight damage rules. You can port them over with almost zero effort (well, the damage numbers might be a bit high but not that much). Humanity dictates how much damage and what kind, Blood Potency dictates how often you take damage. Sufficient precautions reduce your effective BP by 1. Supernatural damage mitigation doesn't apply.

      IME it better balances the three main "takes" well, and reinforces the stratification of vampire society. Ghouls, thin-bloods, and fresh vampires can operate with relative ease during the day and thus have a significant place in vampire society by being able to do so. As vampires get older and stronger, their grip on Humanity slips, and they're forced more and more into the night. The lords of vampire society are powerful enough to demand whatever they want, but they can only really exercise that direct power when the sun is down; thus they have to be judicious and built up a vampire society that keeps them at the top, and forces conflict into more indirect arenas so younger vampires don't get ideas about burning down Havens during the day.

              In time I will consolidate everything in the first post and add decent formatting.

              Decapitation and Metaphysics

              The problem: sometimes the game undersells how and why some aspects are important because it just flows with intuition without question if it should.

              Vampires get specifically rules for decapitation and it is an instant death sentence. All well and good, nothing weird, except for three things:

              1. Why decapitation was singled out as the insta-kill method if there are other forms of non survivable trauma? Why not heart removal, crushed head or torso, struck by lightning, or a lot of other extremes?

              2. Given that the fact is at first self-explanatory, the game really need decapitation mechanics?

              3. If vampires are supernatural undead beings, is "intuition" really the correct approach here? It seems easy to run with gut feeling, but should we?

              Our greatest problem is less the game having rules or if they're good, and more that it doesn't usually dedicate nearly enough space to explain why they are there and why they matter.

              Why decapitation is relevant?

              1. It's not for the intuitive reason, and this is an important info that shouldn't be glossed over. Vampires are animated corpses. Their hearts and brains don't work. A vampire's mind is not housed in their head. Observe that only beheading is dangerous to vampires no other trauma to the head is.

              2. This applies to all their organs. Everything a vampire does is because magic, not any physical property of the biological substrate. This substrate is dead and could just as well be rocks and stuffing. In fact, it's perfectly possible that a vampire would be mostly unaffected by brain removal if it's not a beheading.

              3. Removing the head instead has old history as a ceremonial action, because the head is a symbol of identity. It is where your face is, and what matters metaphysically isn't your brain, but eyes, mouth, expression. After all, for most of history it was clear the house of thought and emotion was your stomach.

              4. Beheadings aren't just effective executions, they are ceremonial. Exposing the head of the deceased is a powerful symbolic act be it in a stake as a warning or in a glass chamber as an object of veneration. Beheadings are ritualized and full of rules even when just a secular execution. The Guillotine was and is divisive as an execution instrument because of how it impacts the significance of the act.

              5. Crucially, those many aspects converged in the rituals to explain or deal with the unquiet dead. What causes someone to come back from the grave? A corpse to refuse rest? The ghost to linger or suffer or find peace? How do you correct the problem? While the answers are not universal or anything, they frequently include the state of the head.

              6. In particular at the time VtM was written we have two important influences: the rituals of the Vampyr (which is not a vampire, but the influence is kinda obvious) and Revenant in Europe in recent centuries, where it was usually recommended to behead the body, stuff the mouth with holy offerings and place it carefully back to give rest to the deceased. And Highlander because it was seriously big on VtM creators at the time. The whole Diablerie is pure Highlander influence.

              Only after this we can examine if the rules are needed or good. And that Highlander influence left the questionable default of beheading as a combat action rather than ritual performance.

              PROPOSAL

              - Enduring Corpse (new baseline)
              Ditch the part of the rules that say you meet Final Death if you keep taking damage after going into Torpor. Full Agg is the limit of harm a vampire suffers. The real extension of how this body is now a supernatural abomination rather than a normal corpse is visible at this point: sometimes it resists hacking as if made of unbreakable metal, sometimes it repairs when no one is looking, sometimes it's just that no matter what punishment you dish, enough of its integrity always remains.
              Under this rule killing a vampire for good isn't necessarily hard, but is a deliberate ritual and not something that just happens. You must find and chose an actual execution that works, and here beheading becomes important.
              - Final Death (Intelligence + Occult, Dif 1 + Bane Severity)
              Not even the Banes of vampirism will just put one to rest eternally by themselves. You have to use a proper execution like beheading, stake or burning, but you also have to follow procedure. Sometimes setting for torpor and sealing is the only option against the most powerful monsters.
              RATIONALE: this makes the distinction of what things actually kill vampires more relevant, and add rules for actual execution scenes where the destruction isn't just the result of combat, but a deliberate dramatic action. It also drives home why a given method is a "threat to vampires" rather than just a flavor of violence.

              - Cursed Integrity (optional)
              The ST can always decide the vampire suffers the normal constraints of having an organ missing, and if you don't want a mauled corpse as a functional PC you can just keep them alive but torpid. But whenever you want, this rule is simple: a vampire only depends on its body for physical actions in the strictest sense. Since no organ has actual biological function, no function requires an organ. When the vampire lacks a sense like vision, for exemple, it's an effect of their particular metaphysics and not a lack of functional eyes, since no vampire has functional eyes anyway. The character suffers no ill effects of the state of the body, even if that means seeing, hearing and sniffing things without a working head. Rules work as normal, but description of their physical integrity has no bearing on anything.
              RATIONALE: that helps remove the sensation that something should hinder a vampire because it should hinder a mortal. It's not a hard rule, but a remainder that those monsters don't work through biology whatsoever.

              - The Catalog of Destruction (optional)
              The ST can and should mess around with what is needed for a vampire to truly die. It can change by chronicle, or within chronicle depend on area, era or individual vampire. Not always a beheading or immolation will do the trick even if they remain general Banes, sometimes they may even make things worse.
              At the same time, previous Final Deaths can establish reliable methods, and maybe replicating an entry in the chronicle's Catalog do bypass the need for a roll.
              RATIONALE: the Catalog is where the ST make the decision on what to keep as certain Final Death if they feel the need. Instead of ambiguous rules, you curate your own authoritative guide on vampire killing.

              What about beheading in combat?

              Do as thy will, it cease being the important rule, just that. If your game needs a beheading rule, keep it. If not, drop it. Beheading instantly fill anyone with Agg +1 dmg, with results covered by the rest of the system, and your Catalog is the authority on if it is Final Death or not.

              Removing the rule doesn't remove the descriptive existence of beheadings just as you don't need specific rules for every conceivable injury.

                              I think the ceremonial execution actually covers it better than a combat beheading rule. The last one throws away all the actual scenes of corpse trials and exhumations to focus on Highlander.

                              The adjustment is to get it out of combat mechanics as a ninja action to bring to a rule that will make beheading the forefront of the scene. The combat mechanic is still there for Highlander scenes.

                              Wooden Stakes and Combat

                              The problem: the game says it is more horror than action. It says narrative and drama take precedence over combat. It says combat rules are there to reinforce desperation.

                              It delivers a cinematic rule based on action stories that use goth aesthetics for impossible testosterone-ladden violence rather than an actual horror-based rule.

                              1. Stakes are crude pieces of wood by design. They are not weapons and don't work as such. They're not adequately sharpened or hard or anything to work as such. They have no aerodynamics, they are bulky, they're intending and soft. And have to go through a reinforced ribcage to reach a heart.

                              2. The traditional depiction of a Hunter driving one through a vampire chest isn't about aiming a bizarre stake with a medieval-stylized Tommy Gun in horror literature. It's a careful work, attentive to the intervention of servants or the sudden awaken of the monster, while a strong man position the stake with care and hit it with a fucking maul. Repeatedly. Because you're trying to break through bone plating.

                              3. Combat delivery IRL would result on success with splinters at best, while the main stake bounces, scratches or breakers because that's what a actual stake does when its treated as a bullet or dagger rather than a wooden stake.

                              This is a similar problem to the beheadings, except way worse because rules focused since old times into making this even easier to pull in combat as it is a common trope. It recognizes the importance of the trope, but acknowledge only the Blade delivery.

                              PROPOSAL: the rules above cover a lot, and the combat stake is still an option for cinematic hunters. That's for games that want to lean less on combat for any reason and cash in the tradicional staking.

                              - Mystically Inconspicuous (Strength + Medicine)
                              Stakes are not weapons, and few people have any reason to think otherwise. Even those few who knows about vampires are more likely to think you want to go camping or build a fence than try to kill someone with this thing.
                              This is not a supernatural effect by itself, you're not going to be ignored as if your stake is Obfuscated, except against torpid vampires.
                              In this rule, wooden stakes work as charms against the vampire itself:
                              - Carrying the stake gives a -1 dice penalty to the vampire on rolls to notice invasion, or +1 dif if applicable.
                              - Handling the stake gives a -2 dice penalty to those rolls if the vampire is still sleeping. You'll give no doubt of your intentions if seen, but have a slightly better chance at not being seen.
                              - Hammering the stake down takes a Strength + Medicine roll, dif 2 or more. Success paralyzes de Vampire immediately for a round, while extra successes accumulate for the more permanent effect. You must achieve Bane Severity in extra successes to finished the job.
                              - Failure means the stake is dislodged enough for the injured and pissed-off vampire to revide.
                              RATIONALE: this is not an easy endeavor, but a stake is rendered indeed the simplest way to paralyze a vampire without engaging in combat at all, which should be priority. It isn't fast and requires access to the vampire, but reliably getting 2 successes is enough to remove the undead at least from combat as long as you keep pressure on heart, not stomach.

                              Beheading in combat at least is a thing. Stake in combat, only if you really want the cinematic flavor. For horror or realism it's not a good option .

                                      We're never going to have an objective accounting of how folklore developed. It's rare that you can reliably trace the development of these concepts. Did stakes start as some idea that certain woods could impede the flow of blood in an animate corpse and then get conflated with nailing corpses into their coffins, or was it an expansion of the concept of nailing corpses down? We'll never really know. We don't have the records for anthropologists to tease these things apart. The best we can do is look at more recent pop-culture iterations on folklore. We largely know what Bram Stoker was pulling from - and it wasn't some attempt to create an "authentic" vampire - in different parts of creating Dracula, because we have is notes from research for the novel. So we can say, "he knew about this story, and that legend, and this myth," and generally have a solid idea of why he picked what he did. We don't have that knowledge for older stories.

                                              Cold and Extreme Threats

                                              Problem: everything. Not a single line there is good. The idea is and a lot, but the execution has all possible errors, including scientific ones, which is always a wonder in a game where you can magic away your problems.

                                              1. The rule kicks in at -30⁰ (-22 in Freedom Units). You start to run against freezing that was ignored before.
                                              2. Despite described as an insidious danger, it's a temperature only seen in nature in very few times and locations, and even in industrial settings only when you leave the realm of "efficient refrigeration" for "flash-freeze or more radical stuff".
                                              3. The torpor is kind of lame here. The vamp is already immobilized. How cooler is a frozen undead that still projects thoughts to nearby targets than just "you have 1 hour to telepath your friends, then torpor"
                                              4. It's overall bad because it attacks the trope of the arctic vampire without offering anything in exchange, it seems to simply state "if a mortal can't survive there, you can't do much better".
                                              5. I'm unaware of any interesting story about a vampire being frozen to harmlessness.
                                              6. The last paragraph on water is a barrage of stupidity. At -30 water is frozen. Water below the ice cap of a river or something is liquid precisely because it's not at -30, it's around 0.
                                              7. And vampires don't sink in cold water more than warm water, which depends because the whole argument is bullshit. The human body has lots of low density tissue and averages close to water density. Float or sink without air depends on person. And vampires have lungs, they just don't need the oxygen, but taking a breath to float is kinda an option.

                                              PROPOSAL:

                                              - Cold penalties, no roll
                                              Below 0⁰C - cosmetic changes, -2 dice to pass for mortal as your skin won't thaw ice.
                                              Below -20⁰C - undeath fights brittleness, but movement becomes visibly stiff (no penalty in rolls, but can't be hidden). Suffer 1 Superficial Health dmg when reaching it, but is not a recurring damage. Instead, cycling thaw/freeze is worse than stable freeze.
                                              Below -40⁰C - Brittleness kicks. Add +1 to any damage suffered. -2 to physical actions, though you can accept 1 Superficial Health dmg to forego the penalty.
                                              Below -60⁰C - if the Curse's magic fails, the body is too brittle for any action without taking damage, the vampire is living, but immobile.
                                              - There's no saying when the magic will be overwhelmed, below -60⁰ and without special powers nothing the vampire can do about it. It may also forego the Night Waking Rouse Check and the Daysleep, resulting in a body paralyzed without rest, forever.
                                              RATIONALE: greater range of activity and I hope better scientific grounding while using the magic of undeath to simplify stuff. A real body won't get full solid 'till close to -70 because it isn't just water, but will get brittle in a nonlinear progression. Particularly, the ST is free to hold things together indefinitely if they want.

                                              - Cold References (that was greatly missing)
                                              2-4⁰ - Pelagic temperature. Deep waters don't get colder than that. It's possible at that pressure to be liquid way below 0⁰, but it's not what we see in natural water bodies.
                                              0⁰ - freezing water. It may be either liquid or solid if pure, it's transitioning.
                                              -10⁰ - most temperate coast will hardly get colder than that. Commercial refrigerators. Ice film can form on surfaces.
                                              -20⁰ - this is where tourists seek extreme cold, the litmus for comfortable life even with house heating. Some lab equipment and industrial freezers that need extra safety but are still freezers work at -18⁰. Rubber and plastic lose flexibility, even metal is noticeably brittle and most fuel start to thicken or gel.
                                              -30⁰ - the coldest inhabitable environments for most people. Deep freeze warehouses and cryogenics test chambers work here. It's the limit of cold storage before changing the very meaning of cold storage.
                                              -40⁰ - in inhabited areas this is possible, but catastrophic. This is a pharmaceutical ultras freezer, used for regular cryogenic facilities and some vaccines. The aerospace industry has chambers that cold to search the worse conditions they face, and some materials labs to study changes in matter.
                                              -50⁰ - natural cold like that isn't just rare, demands deliberate search for real. Some cooling chambers fed by liquid nitrogen can reach it. Otherwise, facilities this cold are for research purposes, only.
                                              -70⁰ - the coldest regions on Earth, period. The most specialized cryogenic facilities work at this. Others are labs working at space conditions or on absolute material failure tests. The most resistant oil is starting to freeze while some metals start becoming superconductors.

                                                          I studied it some time ago, so no sources. Then yes, if you have an expert that would be a good call, better yet if they can point a good free source.

                                                          But long story short two things change the effects of pressure: uniformity and how much the fluid around you compresses under pressure.

                                                          Most of our experience with pressure under air is uneven and dealing with a medium that compresses a lot. That leads to squishy results as the fluid in question moves violently to weak spots.

                                                          Hydrostatic pressure is the opposite. Very hard to concentrate, tending to get uniformly around you, and water is stupidly resistant to compression. A drop from the surface and one from the Mariana Trenches have almost the same density.

                                                          All in all the result is no damage. Other things do happen. Without magicking physics away, a vampire in deep water would be pinned by the weight of the water column in a way air doesn't do. Think being unable to raise even if less dense because the pressure is literally forcing you down.

                                                          The problem with speed is chemical. Pressure changes reactivity and solubility of a lot of things that we have in the body. In this case the main culprit is your blood's Nitrogen. As you compress it will suffer some changes, if you then decompress too fast it will form bubbles in your veins.
