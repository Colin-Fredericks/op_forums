                  The Storyteller System is great for its intended purpose, which is to be explained in a couple of sentences and intuitively used. This in turn allows people to pick up the game very quickly compared to other systems which require a lot of reading and memorization of granular mechanics. As such, there is generally little need to convert the entire setting to a different mechanics setup.

                  But I will agree that the Storyteller System has holes in it, so rather than a full conversion I tend to use "chunks" of other systems to patch the areas where it falls down. Usually this means I use a different system to run sub-game sections, such as running a Warhammer Fantasy or Warhammer 40K minis game with custom point costs to simulate battles and sieges. This is especially useful when you have PCs which are minion heavy builds or rely extensively on using Backgrounds from a distance rather than fighting in person. Once the mini's game is resolved, I take the results and use that outcome as the baseline for the next section of normal Storyteller System use.

                  VtM starts as a street level game about a coterie doing their best "The Lost Boys" impression, but after a certain amount of XP is gained it more or less becomes almost a Real Time Strategy game where you use influence and minions to achieve your goals rather than directly punching things. But the Storyteller System doesn't really have rules for that transition beyond a lot of hand waving and abstraction.

                      For a long time, I basically ran a simplified version where you aimed for a fixed target number of 6+. 1s cancelled successes and 10s counted twice. Specialisations let you re-roll one die (usually a 1, since that could net you two successes if you were lucky). Even one success was a win, but it might be a partial success in tricky situations.

                      Combat was always the most involved part. I think everyone ignored the "declare in reverse order" thing anyway, and probably used the same initiative across subsequent rounds.

                      We were never that bothered by all the rolling for attack/damage/soak (no one ever used the defensive actions, preferring to save up their turn for their own attack), but we did try leaving the damage and soak unrolled and just subtracting the latter from the former. It works, so long as you apply a minimum damage for lethal and aggravated attacks. But it can also be pretty devastating for those attacks that get through. Or you can roll one and take half for the other.

                      CofD2e works much better, so you can just use those rules (or the Exalted/Trinity ones) if it makes more sense. I'll be stealing what I like from Curseborne, probably, but I don't think I'll ever be able to love it as much as Classic WoD.

                                      I actually like the sustem 'as is' but I'll explain why with a couple of points.


                                      Originally posted by adambeyoncelowe View Post
                                      I think everyone ignored the "declare in reverse order" thing anyway, and probably used the same initiative across subsequent rounds.

                                      We were never that bothered by all the rolling for attack/damage/soak (no one ever used the defensive actions, preferring to save up their turn for their own attack)

                                      It's a fairly common option taken by many groups but there are some potential pitfalls.

                                      If your init is the same across multiple turns then that causes the 'no-one ever used defensive actions' problem because you have no choice but to risk being hit in order to attack.

                                      Frome one point of view WoD system is designed for _duelling_ i.e. 1v1 combat. Which is why you need to roll init each round and why you only get a choice of attacking or defending and why the reverse init declare is important.

                                      In the situation where A is figting B and A wins init then B has to choose between attacking and leaving themselves open, or defending but skipping an attack, because they don't know what A will do. When the situation reverse in subsequent turns A has to decide. 1v1 this is quite fast to resolve and is pretty good for dramatic tension.

                                      The problem is that this is very slow when it's not 1v1 which is why so many compromises and house rules are created.

                                      My 'house rule' is that the ST goes round the table and resolves combat as a series of 1v1 duels. This does demand a certain amount of 'fixing' by the ST to provide suitable opponents but is overal, in my opinion, a better solution.


                                      The other 'misconception' is that WoD is actually a _superhero_ system... obvioulsy one that leans heavily into the 'dark and gritty' theme but the system itself is all about people with fantasical, superhuman powers. The dice system also tends towards success for the characters, pools as low as three dice regularly succeed whereas in other systems low skilled characters tend to fail most of the time. Once you understand this then being an ST is remarkably easier - you expect the players to succeed which means you can guide the story more reliably.

                                                  Originally posted by Iguazu View Post

                                                  In the Masquerade-days, I often thought that other supernaturals, should be run in the game-system your running; ie, Werewolves should use disciplines, vampires in WW: tA should have gifts, etc.
                                                  I considered the same, but I think it's because those games were similar but not actually written to be compatible, causing multiple issues when using the powers of one in a game of the other. And even on mundane things like maneuvers in combat there were contradictions, like the same maneuver working differently in VtM and WtA, but not just because of obvious difference between vampires and werewolves: humans in a VtM game would be using that same maneuver as written in VtM, while humans in WtA would use it as written in VtM.

                                                  With CoD2 there is much less of that because all the splats use the same mundane system and their supernatural features are generally compatible and comparable. Of course, some are stronger than others by design (mummies trash vampires, for example), but they are still written to operate in the same world.
