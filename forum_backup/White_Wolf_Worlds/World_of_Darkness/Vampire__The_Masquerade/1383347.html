                  As elmerg says, there are no specific rules for this. But the sidebar on page 180 talks about removing Flaws through gameplay—maybe you're Destitute, but you get a boon from a Ventrue, and ask for a minor source of income (Resources 1).

                  The sidebar mostly focuses on gaining and losing Backgrounds, but does mention Flaws as well, so I'd apply the same rules: if you remove chargen Flaws during play, you assign those Flaw points to something else. Maybe you can no longer feed on animals, but you still have ethical concerns about feeding on humans, so you get rid of Vegan and replace it with Feeding Restriction (Must Consent) and something else to add up to the same number of dots. Maybe you establish a Haven in play, so the No Haven flaw no longer applies, but that new Haven ends up being Haunted. Etc etc.

                      Most games I play say that removing of Flaws depends on ST approval (based on your PC's roleplay and events in the game) and costs a number of XP equivalent to the flaw x2 (or maybe more). Obviously certain Flaws can be more easily removed than others, and some Flaws are likely to be permanent. Sometimes there needs to be a special story involved, and other times it just requires passage of time or certain experiences in the routine course of game.

                      I don't think people should take Flaws with the idea that they'll be removed later. As an ST I would not be look well on that. But there are plenty of occasions when the Flaw no longer makes sense, and the PC should work with the Player on how to remove it, and then pay for that with XP.

                                              Originally posted by nobby View Post
                                              Yeah sure, but if the player doesn't want to play the flaw, why pick it in the first place? Picking a flaw basically comes down to saying hey ST this is something I want explore, play with, have in in the story. Design wise the necessity to pick flaws under V5 character creation is there because flaws create drama, crate conflict, lead to character driven story and also force players to make multidimensional characters that are tied to the world around them.
                                              If you don't want that you can always change things so that your players start without flaws.
                                              It's a good point that players should be careful of the flaws they choose, or risk being stuck with flaws that are more restrictive than they realized. I have a lot of sympathy for that sort of strict interpretation of the rules. Yes, it is collaborative storytelling, but at some point it is also a game system, with dice and win/loss/mostly draw conditions. If players don't want to put in the time to learn the rules, well, playing a sub-par character with all the settings set to "difficult" might teach them the importance of doing so. (see also: choosing Nosferatu in VtMB)

                                              There are a few small problems with that position, which make me pause before being too hard-ass of an ST:

                                              1. Some players, being inexperienced, may blithely take a (literally for the pc) fatal flaw without realizing what they are doing. Now, this is why STs should review characters before play starts, but no one can catch everything. Given V5 is still a relatively new system, and the rules presentation is... let's charitably say... "not designed with usability as the main goal". Some flexibility is called for when the player's vision of their pc's story-arc doesn't work with the mechanics, or when unforeseen synergies arise.

                                              2. It is not necessary for a character to have additional flaws to be interesting in V5. Vampires, by definition, have a giant pile of flaws. Arguably quite a bit more than most fictional characters. Hamlet may have been indecisive, but he was able to be indecisive at brunch without bursting into flame. Scarlett O'Hara may have been oblivious to the suffering of others, but she never had to make a roll to resist frenzy. If nothing else, every V5 pc has clan or thinblood flaws already built in. The reason it still works and doesn't feel frustrating for the players is because all the pcs have the same massive flaws. Few VtM sessions start with, "The next day, at brunch, you all go fwoosh!"

                                              3. Requiring the flaws freely chosen at chagen to be permanent closes off story possibilities, not opens them up. Fictional characters with flaws that cannot possibly change are less compelling than those who might reform. Even if the character never reforms, the open question of their fate maintains interest. A very reasonable plan for an ST running a sandbox campaign would be to just drop pcs off in a city and challenge them to remove their flaws. You could run a whole chronicle of complications out of that. But, not if removal is not a clearly available possibility under the rules.

                                              4. Some flaws, aside from being overly constraining, as in CajunKhan's situation, are just nonsensical in some stories. Let's say you have Stalkers as a flaw because you are a world-famous KPop star. You go into torpor in a hidden cave for a hundred years. How does one justify screaming fans greeting you when you awaken? It's nice the system says the flaws can be changed, but by not giving us a mechanic for doing so they have really done nothing the golden rule doesn't do.

                                              5. As for the vague mention of being ability to alter pcs in-game, there is a major problem. It's fine we here agree that a reasonable solution could be homebrewed when we are STing. What if you're not the storyteller? Having published canon, RAW rules exist so STs can run games, but also so players have some idea what they can expect during the game without having to convince their ST. If there were a published mechanic for changing flaws, it would be reliably available to non-ST players. (Plus, STs wouldn't have to constantly homebrew stuff on the fly.)

                                              6. The entire concept the player has for the character's story-arc may involve removing their flaw. Often such a desire is the basis for a whole quest. If there is no mechanic for removing the flaw, then what's the point of the story arc? If such a character has no clear path to success, at best the whole thing can devolve into nihilism. "I am vegan, and so I know I am not immortal. I shall die the final death when I reach BP 3." At worst, it can breed wangst.

                                              7. Around a table where the ST takes such a hardline, frustrated players may notice there are game systems where it is nearly impossible to build a character which is straight-up unable to survive in the campaign world. D&D 5e may have it's flaws, and some builds are marginally more or less effective in play. It's nearly impossible for a new player to accidentally create an entirely ineffective character using the standard rules. It does not have the equivalent of Vegan just sitting there, looking all innocent, waiting for inexperienced players to choose.
