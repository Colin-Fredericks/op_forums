                            Originally posted by higgipedia View Post
                            I think it's interesting that the TRPG IPs with the deepest lore are all somewhat contemporaneous (World of Darkness, Shadowrun, and Cyberpunk all released within 3-4 years of each other).
                            I'm not sure if this observation is accurate.

                            D&D started with the idea of being lore agnostic, but fans requested from the get go to have more of it, leading to the creation of the Greyhawk setting. Tékumel had huge lore from the get go in 1975 and was a great success (it didn't got far due to internal issues at TSR). Chivalry & Sorcery also had a profound investment in lore in 1976. Champions brought deeper lore to the Super Hero genre in 1981. Chaosium brought more lines with careful lore, either from scratch or advancing from previous literature, with games like RuneQuest, Stormbringer and Call of Cthulhu. Even Paranoia got a lot of lore starting from it's first core book in 1984, and most of Mage lore started out from Ars Magica in 1987. All that without including those who already start from lore heavy franchises despite not advancing them much, and games who tried to start a deeper lore but didn't went far for other reasons, who existed at least since Star Trek in 1978.

                            RPGs with deep lore existed from the beginning and kept coming up after. In this regard, I don't see World of Darkness, Shadowrun and Cyberpunk as special (despite the first two being really good, but I find the third unimpressive). WoD is a special case in how much momentum it got during the 90s, but that's about how many sourcebooks and stuff it churned out than being different in concept.

                            Maybe the perception that they stand up to players from the 90s and later is a more relevant investigation to begin with, instead of an assumption to start a research.

                                                Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post
                                                The recent thread I made on how the Second Inquisition could end had me thinking about what the intentions were for the organization versus the expectations of fans.

                                                I had a problem with THE SECOND INQUISITION book and why I realized that I'd been reading the writer's intentions all along. Basically, for a lot of fans, the Second Inquisition was this massive police state organization that would be able to track down and systematically eliminate vampires across the globe via anonymous military strikes during the day and burning the buildings down around them. It's a horrifying situation that means that vampire society has to cease to exist.

                                                It's not that.

                                                Even if it isn't, that seems to be how they're portrayed in table top. I have yet to find a single live play session where the Second Inquisition wasn't presented in a way where the vampires are completely screwed as soon as they show up. This problem also existed in Swansong, as you never get the option to just go buck wild and slaughter them. It's always you having to sneak your way around their forces, or in the case of Emem, stealth killing them from the vents. Caleb can't even mind control them, because they're wearing sound proof visors or something.


                                                Also, it's laughable that the SI only started to get its act together after the breach of Schrecknet. Because yeah, that's why the Society of Leopold failed to wipe out the vampire menace... lack of government support. Even through they were a church funded organization who had agencies all over the globe.
