    Tips for Running Touchstones

    04-11-2021, 04:34 AM
    There's been a couple threads on Touchstones lately. I’ve used Touchstones in my games since a while before V5 came out. Some have been awesome. Some have been meh. When done right, they can add a lot to a game. These are my tips on how to run awesome rather than meh Touchstones.


    1. They should be compelling characters. The whole premise of Touchstones is that they’re people PCs care about, so the GM should get players to care about them too. Touchstones should have interesting histories and fun personalities. The GM should enjoy roleplaying them and players should enjoy interacting with them. It should be possible for the GM to run a fun conversation between a PC and their Touchstone where nothing dramatic or consequential happens. (This is less important in one-shots or shorter games, but in long-running games, it’s important to have low-stakes scenes in between the high-octane ones.)

    There are lots of ways for GMs to do this. There are as many ways for Touchstones to be interesting as there are for people to be interesting. This one might seem like a no-brainer, but Touchstones don’t work as a concept without it. If the GM can’t make Touchstones compelling characters, it’s a waste of time to include them in games and all of the below advice is useless.

    2. They should be relevant to the story. This one also seems like a no-brainer. Anecdotally, I’d say most VtM games (or at least most of my games) are about vampiric plots and politics. If a PC’s Touchstone is a grocery store clerk, it is very easy for them to fade into irrelevance and to only come up when the player goes, “Hey, I want a Touchstone scene.” Or to just never come up at all.

    Touchstones should have a stake in the chronicle’s narrative. For example, if a storyline revolves around multiple vampires jockeying for control of the same hospital, make the Touchstone an employee there. Alternatively, if the PC has authority or influence over a sector of mortal society, make the Touchstone someone attached to it. Maybe the PC runs their own business and the Touchstone works for them as an employee. Maybe the Touchstone is a mortal with authority or influence who the PC regularly deals with to enact their vampiric agenda. Maybe the Touchstone is a key figure in a mystery the PC is trying to solve. Maybe the PC actually lives with the Touchstone. (Good luck maintaining the Masquerade.)

    The bottom line is, the Touchstone should not exist in a vacuum. They should play a meaningful role in storylines and they should come up on their own. Players can request Touchstone scenes, but that shouldn’t be the only way that PCs get to see them.

    3. They should be more than morality pets. In my experience, coming up with NPCs purely to be PC Touchstones leads to worse Touchstones. I’ve had better results coming up with a cast list of mortals, sketching out their personalities and roles in the setting, and then letting players pick their Touchstones. Design independent characters first, who don’t owe their existence to the PCs, then let players pick which ones they like most. Don’t design a character expressly for players to like most. That’s back-asswards.

    On that same “more than morality pet” vein, Touchstones should have flaws and foibles. While they are more likely to be decent people than your average guy off the street (hence why the vampire chooses them as Touchstones: they’re often reminders of the best in humanity), saintly characters are boring and unrealistic, and most people have their share of emotional baggage and inner demons. Let Touchstones struggle with those, let players root for them, and let the PCs help them (or make things worse).

    4. They should have something to offer. Players tend to most appreciate NPCs who do things for their characters. This can be material aid. This can be social connections. (Touchstones often work great as Allies.) This can be good advice. This can be emotional support. This can be letting the vampire feed on them. Whatever it is, if the player feels the Touchstone does something useful for their PC, that goes a long way towards building goodwill—especially if the PC needs to make sacrifices to maintain the relationship. Which segues into…

    5. They should be more than damsels in distress. The V5 rulebook repeatedly hits you over the head with using Touchstones against PCs, but IMO it gives fairly bad advice. There is nothing that sours players to giving their PCs loved ones than needing to continually bail loved ones out of trouble, especially if there’s no reward for it beyond not losing a relationship they’ve sunk time into.

    GMs can use “Touchstones in trouble” plots. They’re iconic for a reason. But the GM needs to earn them. The GM first needs to establish the Touchstone as a compelling character and get the player to care about them. When the Touchstone gets in trouble, it should happen as a logical development of the story and have narrative buildup behind it. It should not feel out of the blue or (worse) like a consequence of the Touchstone’s own stupidity.

    It’s also crucial that the resolution to any “Touchstone in trouble” plot feel like it moves storylines forward. For example, if a PC’s rival kidnaps a Touchstone, and the PC kills the rival as part of getting them back, that’s cool and satisfying. “Forward” doesn’t have to mean in the PC’s favor: if killing the rival makes their more powerful sire set out to ruin the PC, that’s cool too.

    The bottom line is that something should happen. The player should not feel like they are bailing the Touchstone out of trouble purely to return things back to the way they were, or like they’re paying a cost to save the Touchstone that has no further impact or bearing on the plot. When they do that, all they’ve done is clean up a mess.

    Speaking of Touchstones in trouble, just killing them is boring. There’s a lot more interesting stuff that enemies can do with them.

    Finally, GMs don’t have to use “Touchstones in trouble” plots. They seriously don’t, if that seems like it would go over badly in their group. Relationships need conflicts, but there are lots of conflicts GMs can use beyond rival vampires trying to weaponize Touchstones against PCs. Vampires are perfectly capable of causing drama in their Touchstones’ lives all on their own.

    6. Their relationships with the vampire can be twisted. Bad guy vampires can have Touchstones too. Vampires can abuse and do bad things to their Touchstones. They’re people the vampire cares about, not necessarily people whom the vampire is nice to. Dracua’s stalking of Mina Harker and Strahd von Zarovich’s obsession with Tatyana are a good examples of how inhumane vampires can relate to their Touchstones.

    In practice, most PCs (and a sizable chunk of vampires) will be nice to their Touchstones. They are the mortals who the vampire cares most about. But the slide towards monstrosity is one of the game’s core themes, and most vampires will hurt the ones they love sooner or later. If a player genuinely cares about a Touchstone, then later has their PC do bad things to them of their own free will, player and GM can give themselves a pat on the back.

    7. They can break the Masquerade. Some vampires tell the truth about their condition to their loved ones. Why wouldn’t they? Keeping up all the lies can get very tiresome. Why you’re never around during the day, why you never eat anything, where you work, who your significant other is (or why you don’t have one), why you don’t have kids, and ten thousand other questions the vampire will have to answer with lies. The closer the relationship with the Touchstone, the more lies the vampire will have to tell.

    Some vampires just say “fuck it.” They tell the truth. In my experience, this works best as something that happens in-game, rather than made a feature of the Touchstone relationship from the outset. It usually happens in response to two things: the PC deeply caring about the Touchstone, and keeping up all the lies becoming an increasingly big pain in the ass. (Which they should! Maintaining the Masquerade takes work and games can benefit from reflecting that.) Also in my experience, Masquerade-aware Touchstones who accept the PC’s vampirism tend to be really, really big hits with players, especially if the Touchstone uses their newfound knowledge to make unlife easier for the PC. There is a reason the idea of just telling the truth is so seductive. Everyone wants to be accepted by their loved ones for who they are.

    Is it risky? Yes. There are ten thousand and one possible plot hooks that can result from a Touchstone knowing the truth and getting drawn into Kindred society, perhaps even ghouled or Embraced.

    But here’s a dirty secret: for all the Camarilla’s propaganda, sometimes no consequence comes of it. If the PC is careful, and the Touchstone really is trustworthy, it’s possible this Masquerade breach might never come to light. People get away with crimes all the time IRL. Vampires get away with plenty of crimes in the WoD. The setting tends to portray Masquerade breaches as inevitably getting found out (and with good reason—that’s usually a more dramatic outcome than “no consequence”), but is it so implausible that in all the history of vampires, some have broken the Masquerade and got away with it? The Touchstone isn’t immortal, so the secret has a shelf life.

    Breaking the Masquerade isn’t mandatory. PCs can have fulfilling relationships with Masquerade-ignorant Touchstones, but it’s another tool in the GM’s box.


    What other tips have helped people run Touchstones well in their games?

          If a GM doesn't want players to care about Touchstones as characters (or can't get them to), they might as well scrap Touchstones altogether. The point of Touchstones, which were originally introduced in Requiem 2e, was to get PCs emotionally invested in mortal NPCs and to make mortal interactions a bigger part of the game.

          V5 obviously wanted to copy that, but where they dropped the ball was in presentation. Requiem had several pages of compelling sample Touchstones and good advice for using them in games. V5 has a much shorter and more generic sample Touchstones list, then hits you over the head with how rivals will use them against you. Followed by an example scenario where a Touchstone gets kidnapped by rivals, a PC rescues them, and then forces the Touchstone to drink poison so that can't ever happen again. All of this sends a message to new players that Touchstones are liabilities it's pointless to care about, rather than characters it's worthwhile to develop relationships with.

          I don't use V5 rules, anyway, so I'm more interested in storytelling than mechanics advice for this thread. Touchstones can exist under any game system as "mortals who are close to the PCs' hearts."

                                      Pretty well.

                                      The problem a lot of players have with Touchstones is they assume they're going to be a trap for them. They assume that the ST is going to use them to be held hostage or killed off to angst them or something else. This is why so many players don't have families for their PCs or otherwise are orphaned and single.

                                      I've found if you let THEM be in charge of it, though, your Players are likely to be sadistic psychopaths and set up all the tragedy their own. One thing I've found with my players is that they LOVE to overact and if you give them a Touchstone as a prop and say, "Here's someone for you to angst over and act. Describe them and your relationship to them" then they're likely to taje the ball and run with it.

                                      Generally, I've found they tend to be love interests or tragic characters the PCs have wronged in some way like the daughter of a family the player murdered in a frenzy. Sometimes both. However, it works pretty well -- especially if you are doing text based roleplay. Players love writing out little scenes where they get to exposit to their Touchstone or have the Touchstone reassure them they're not monsters.

                                      Some examples we've had:

                                      * A TV reporter the PC used Blood and mind control to make fall in love with them to get them to kill a story before they "broke up" but she remains a contact. It is a source of immense guilt because, obviously, they mind fucked them. In the end, she broke free, became a hunter, and the PC had to kill her. It was all the player's idea, btw.

                                      * The player character's sister who is now the slave of the Prince and ghoul/blood vessel because they broke the Masquerade during one of their first nights. He wants to free her but is terrified she'll be killed if he does or hate him.

                                      * A Thin Blood (I broke the rules there) who the PC kept as their lover but who actually was just using the PCs for protection. It was a toxic and ugly relationship where the PC reassured himself he was a good perosn by protecting her while the Thin Blood just needed a patron. This was one of my PC's. I played her as an EXPY of Lily from Bloodlines.

                                      * The PC's ghoul he rescued from human trafficking who wanted to be a vampire but he was using as an emotional crutch. She was basically portrayed as a budding psychopath due to the blood warping her little brain. The player played them as a hyperactive psychotic Knives Chou meets Renfield. Humorous and yet sometimes disturbing.

                                      * One particularly sick one was the player kept their abusive dad crippled and paralyzed but alive as well as aware in a nursing home to pay him back for all he'd done to her.

                                      Like I said, Players love getting DARK if you let them control it.

                                                  I don't play V5 and therefore don't know the rules for touchstones, but it seems to me that this should primarily be a responsibility for the players, not the ST. If players want an NPC that their PC cares about that drives some sort of mechanic or is part of character creations, that seems much more a player issue than an ST one. The ST should help the players brainstorm interesting characters and ask open ended question to define their relationship and character traits, but the ST already does a whole lot of work to create the game. At some point, the players need to take ownership. Any of the NPCs related to PC traits like Allies, Contacts, Retainers, Influence, etc. are a PC responsibility, and they should be working to meet the ST's satisfaction in order to gain their benefits, not the other way around.

                                                  Some players thrive with that - they are very creative and can think about their entire "troupe." Others find it much harder to think outside just the PC to expand their horizons to all the other ancillary characters under their control. So STs may still need to put in effort to coach some players.

                                                  In my experience, the players expect a lot more from the STs than they do from themselves. So whether it is things like "why are the PCs part of the same coterie" or "we have a new PC that needs to be introduced to the party" or "you should care about this NPC", players will constantly shut down efforts by the ST to come up with reasons. They're just not good enough because "my character wouldn't care about this person", or "trust that new PC", etc. But the minute the shoe is on the other foot and the burden is on the player, all of a sudden these objections stop. Players find that ideas that are "good enough" is all they need.

                                                  I'm very jaded and insistent on this. I ran a game long ago when a player had one PC die and created a new one. At the time, I was still of the mindset that it was a GM responsibility to get the party of PCs together. However, rather than roleplay it out (and waste a lot of time as people came up with reasons why they wouldn't accept/trust the new PC because of a minor roleplay issue) I wanted to discuss OOC how to quickly get the new PC into the party. What would work and make sense, and then we'd just say it would happen and start the roleplay after the acceptance. So I still gave a reason I thought would work. All but one player agreed, but the last one was very insistent the reason wasn't good enough. Fine, so I offered a second I thought was also strong. Nope, the same player shot that one down too. So I gave a third and fourth idea, but no, those also weren't good enough. I then had had enough. I said, that's fine if what I came up with didn't meet the party's needs. I was only helping. What were their ideas? Everyone but the one guy said, everything was fine and they were willing to go ahead. They offered some minor modifications, but mostly kept to my original point. But the one guy still kept insisting it wasn't good enough. So then I put it explicitly on him. Everyone else was fine with what was offered except him. That meant the rest of the party could go ahead, but it was his character who would be left behind not the new PC. So if he had a great idea that would satisfy his objections and would be accepted by everyone, it meant his character could then accompany the party. Then suddenly after he stammered for a minute, he had no problems whatsoever. Mister "You're ideas aren't good enough" had no ideas of his own.

                                                  At that point, whenever the issue of player motivation came up, I've put a lot more burden back on the players. I'm there to help, but I'm not a mind reader and can't determine their motivation for them. Whether it's to trust another PC, create an emotional connection with an NPC, explain why a Merit or Background relationship exists, or anything else. If it is something you want on your character sheet, or involves common player etiquette to each other that all PCs are together to play the game, the burden is on the players.
