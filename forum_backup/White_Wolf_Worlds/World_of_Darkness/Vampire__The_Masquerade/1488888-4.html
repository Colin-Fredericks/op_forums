        Originally posted by Mizu View Post
        I don't see why you are struggling with it, you already seem to understand the answer when applying it to an RPG.
        I thought I was pretty clear:

        RPGs are an actively collaborative process. The GM/etc. and the players need to have a baseline agreement on what the setting they're playing in looks like so they can mutually participate in it.

        When I run a VtM game, I need my players to understand the VtM setting on a level; and having a solid lore for the game helps communicate that setting to them.

        If I want to write a short story set in the WoD, I can diverge from the setting canon as much as I want (and WW has repeatedly given novel authors freedom to do exactly that for the sake of evocative stories rather than worrying about forcing the stories to fit to a single continuity), and either other people enjoy it or they don't. There aren't player that have a role in the process that need to understand what events from the books I'm ignoring, or that I'm playing really loose with this rule or that rule, for the sake of the narrative I want to convey.

        If someone is doing an adaptation of an existing work, they're only as beholden to the original as they want to be (including, of course, the potential to want to profit off their work and thus appeal to fans of the exist material by not straying too far). If the work is good, generally speaking for most of human history, nobody gave a shit about taking creative liberties while doing adaptations/reimagings/etc. For most of artistic history taking an existing story, and retelling it in a way people like rather than being "purist" about the origin was a laudable activity. The only place where "purists"had a real argument was in translations because when you're translating there's a serious balancing act of directly translating the body of the work at the risk not the new audience not understanding things that don't communicate well in direct translation vs. translating the spirit of the original while sacrificing some accuracy to it's exact state. Amusingly there are "purists" on both sides of this because there are people that want to be true to the text, and others that want to be true to the narrative; and both groups have people that consider their priority the true "pure" version of the source material.

        The nature of needing consistent lore doesn't apply like it does to RPGs.

        The lore keeps writers making works in that setting on track in regards to how to make a work that belongs in that setting.
        According to who? White Wolf green light lots of stories for novels/comics/short stories/etc. for the WoD because they felt the spirit of the stories belonged in the setting even if the specifics of the stories didn't actually work with how the gaming books were written. So clearly there isn't some objective "true" version of the setting to weigh this against. Legally the IP owners get to do this however they want. Fans can disagree, and will voice it loudly if they do, but fans saying that a wok doesn't belong in the setting doesn't define things with any real authority.

        Lots of franchises eschew your concept of setting purity for numerous reasons. In the end, works get judged far more on quality than on setting purism (esp. considering nobody can even agree about literal purism vs. narrative purism).

        For an example of what happens when they don't just take a look at The Last Jedi by Rian Johnson.
        You're talking about Star Wars. The franchise that at one point - under George Lucas mind you - has a six tiered canon classification system to sort which sources to give precedence to because George Lucas notoriously (and quite explicitly) did not give two shits about how the EU folks followed his canon or not. Do you realize how big of a mess the old EU was if it needed six different levels of canon in order to figure out whether or not an event "happened" one way or another if you want your future stories to comply with one level of canon instead of falling into a lower tier of canon?

        And canon tier was no indicator of quality. The G-Canon, the tippy top George Lucas stamp of approval canon, including every last still hated moment of the prequel trilogy, while much beloved material like some of the video games and comics are stuck at the 3rd,4th or even 6th level of canon (the sixth being non-canon including things that were originally S or C canon but the prequels rendered them N canon as Lucas didn't care if his stories made fan favorites stories incompatible with the G canon).

        So yeah, spare me the idea that "purists" are what keeps stories good. There were bad SW stories of high tier canon, and good SW stories of low tier canon, well before Disney got in the picture. Or at least make me a good argument that G and T canon are clearly superior to S and C canon in SW simply because those tiers are about "purity" to Lucas's vision and not anything else (very literally according to the man himself, it's only about what matches his vision of the setting and what strays from it).

        I don't care about getting into a debate about the flaws of one entry in the SW franchise, but the sheer amount of stuff people seem to love to forget was in the old stories never ceases to amaze me. For every flaw you can find in the sequel trilogy, I can point out a dozen in the material put out in the old Lucas days (hey remember when SW had a fighter sized space ship that could fly into a sun and cause it to go nova and survive, and then everyone acted like since the prototype was destroyed it was impossible for that to ever be a problem ever again? I do, so I can get past hyper-space ramming and nobody following up on that). Star Wars was never about high art and strong setting consistency, it was about cool iconic action fantasy stories in space; and the creatives that have worked on it over the decades have all understood that was more important than worrying about there being three different origin stories of Boba Fett out there (and if you think "guy making a kid clone of himself to raise as a son" is the best, and the best fit for the rest of the Mandolarian stuff... I have no idea what to say to that).

        Very rare seeming is the adaption that makes significant changes while feeling respectful of the work its adapting as opposed to it being a sign they had no faith in an original story putting butts in seats and so hastily spray painted over an unrelated story with an existing popular IP without the slightest care for making it really fit as an adaption of that more popular IP.
        Or, you know, it's just the elevation of "fandom" as a thing that really matters, and thus needs to be catered to. Fans are the source of this perception. I say this as an avid fan of many things. Look at me go on about WoD5... that's only happening because of how attached I am emotionally to the older stuff. I can try my best to come up with as many objective or rational reasons for what I'm saying and why it should matter, but in the end Paradox doesn't need to care, and I have no claim to say they have to care. The quality of the WoD5 books isn't going to be judged by history on how much they listened to fans like me vs. other fans. At best we might be considered people that read the room right. We'll have no more importance than that.

        Seriously, if you look at any form of art it's the fans (or various other "insiders" that came before) that make a big deal about this,not anyone else. Because fans are the people that care about this sort of "literal purist" respect for previous works. Most people just want a story that matches the mood they're in, and if it positively reminds them of works they liked before? Even better.

        Yes I know he did do a translation of it from old English to modern English, not the same thing as a re-imagining.
        Linguists, of which he was one, would definitely disagree that you can do a translation that doesn't also count as a reimaging on some level. As is frequently said: "translation is interpretation."

                                            I've just watched the first episode. I wasn't expecting much, but I genuinely loved it.

                                            I loved the movie. It was camp and dramatic and lots of fun. Kirsten Dunst was the best actor of the trio, but it didn't require much for the other two: Cruise delights in being the foppish villain; Pitt gets to just act awkward and silent, which is the best sort of role for him.

                                            So I didn't think this would offer much. But Louis is much less whiny and Lestat, though not as flamboyant as Cruise, has a dangerousness about him that feels very vampiric.

                                            Initially, I also wasn't sold on the shift of the time period, but this really works. It makes it feel new in a good way, rather than just a "changing things for change's sake" way.

                                            It was violent. It was sensual. Like the movie, it was very, very gay, but less stylised, and more overtly queer while still feeling like there's real risk and horror.

                                            The first movie felt like Anne Rice's prose style -- often purple, portraying even violence in a poetic, very artistic way -- but this iteration feels like it occasionally punches through (ahem) that facade with a dagger-nailed fist.

                                            I'm very pleasantly surprised.
