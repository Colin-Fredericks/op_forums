    Originally posted by Hermeticus View Post
    Apparently the heaviest draw weight used by the strongest English long bowmen was 200 lbs. Let's assume that's our value of reference for Str 5. 200 / 5 = 40 lbs per dot of Str + Potence. Our 4th gen vampire with 9 in both needs 40 x 18 = 720 lbs draw weight. The heaviest draw weight I heard of, but it may be legendary, is 496 lbs for Guru Gobind Singh Ji's bow. After that, it's the 200 used as reference above.(source: https://weightofstuff.com/heaviest-b...w-weight-ever/)

    I need to see if there are ways to raise the weight to 720 lbs without making the bow bigger or too big.
    ​​​​​
    Did more research. Medieval hand-held crossbows reached a draw weight of 1500 lbs. The difference with bows is the size (crossbows have shorter limbs, which increases the draw weight) and the mechanism (crossbows are pulled through a mechanism that makes it possible for a human to draw that much draw weight, though that makes it slower to reload). Since neither is a concern here, because the vampire would have the strength to pull by hand a crossbow instead of using the mechanism, a customised bow for supernatural strength would be smaller than normal bows but entirely possible and proportionately more damaging.

        You're taking one part of the physics here and not really factoring in lots of other things. You can't just take the steel prod off a crossbow and turn it into a short bow with the same draw weight. The stock plays an important part in keeping the whole thing from snapping under the stress and providing leverage. Keep in mind that the center of a bow, even a steel bow, is the weakest point because a human hand needs to comfortably grip it. The more draw weight you put on the arms, the more stress you're putting on the joint between the arm and the grip. Eventually either just trying to draw to maximum, or over multiple uses, there's a maximum amount of draw weight you can apply before the whole things falls apart. Crossbows, by keeping the prods on the stock, can have much stronger joints and thus withstand much higher draw weights since they never have to narrow for a grip.

        Steel is also a less effective energy medium than wood for bows. A steel bow transfers less energy to the missile than a wood one does; a 150 lb wooden long bow isn't actually ten times weaker than a 1,500 lb steel crossbow. Depending on the specifics of design and materials, the actual joules the missile has when loosed could actually be even because there's that much of a difference in energy loss when you store potential energy in a steel spring like that. That energy is also lost as heat. If a supernaturally strong vampire tries to use a high draw weight crossbow and just hand pull it to fire as rapidly as a standard wooden bow, the arms are going to start heating up fast. That heat will ruin the temper on the steel, and ruin it's ability to function at all.

        The big reason to go with the crossbow for normal humans is because very few people are fit enough to pull such a large draw, hold it to aim, and then do it again, and again, and again. Crossbows hold the weight for the marksman, and a crank/winch is slower (though that also means no overheating the metal) but less maximum output to draw it.

        There are a lot of factors at play when talking about trying to push the extremes of even what feels like such simple technology.

                What year is it even? what's considered the medieval period lasts around a thousand years, and a good amount of progress was made in this time.

                From a gameplay mechanic standpoint, I'm hesitant about giving any mundane melee weapon Strength+5 damage. If anyone wants to use a 100kg maul, I'd argue they' be wasting strength trying to handle the thing and a 10kg maul would be what peak performance looks like. I was arguing with myself if using ward against vampires on a weapon should add to the weapon's damage or be resolved in a separate roll. Maybe the former for Ward but for a lot of magic I'd use the later. On a macro level, if mundane attributes and abilities peak at 5, perhaps melee weapons should too, although I wouldn't hold armour to such a standard simply because thickening steel does indeed scale really well.

                For non-meleeweapons, like, IRL humans have made some fucking terrifying stuff. Weapons made for use against concrete and steel create haunting results when used on flesh. Garand thumb released a video where a support vehicle, Tank and Howitzer shot gelatine dummies: The first is closed-casket stuff but the latter two burst the human aproximate like it was a water balloon. With that in mind.. the system has never really been keen on simulating high damage weaponry. Anything beyond 8 dice is an inconsistent land. It could do it I think, but things like 30mm canons usually have rules like -big but not crazy damage, half soak values-

                For non-gunpowder projectile weapons, nothing has that destructive power.

                If you want a more simulationist game, a lot of rifles will do more than 8 damage. the joules needed for every dice after the 8th increase exponentially. I calculated that WW1 rifle rounds would do between 10 and 12 damage, which makes sense given that they were using what can be referred to as "full sized" rounds optimized to kill distant human targets in a single hit. Given characters have seven health levels, assume half the dice become damage, and add a few dice due to degrees of success, and that works out excellently. But when it comes to bow-type weapons... there's nothing with anywhere near that force. Strongest modern crossbow is comparable to an intermediate round as I said earlier. Crossbows back then had similar strengths but with higher poundages because the technology was far less effecient.

                For throwing weapons, I'd use that +5 rule I'm using for melee weapons. In DAV20, there's some armour ignoring weapons, I'd count the armour ignore in that +5 limit.



                From a narrative perspective
                I don't think vampires are going to appreciate someone swinging a symbolically big hammer around. Elders want peace and stability. Someone going out of their way to be an unstoppable badass, even if it's a sheriff, is a threat Massive weapons don't blend in well, and the silence of the blood must also be observed. Similarly, ignoring shitty game mechanics, this is the main reason vampires wouldn't wear a lot of armour even though they, not needing to breath, worry about exhaustion, and having super strength, should be able to comfortably wear and conceal more than ordinary humans could.

                    Originally posted by Hermeticus View Post
                    So, is it impossible to produce a bow or similar weapon with a draw weight of 720 lbs (I'm not really looking for more than that) with medieval technology?
                    Probably possible, but you're getting into the "obviously designed for an inhuman monster or shonen anime protagonist" territory of what it would look like.

                    Couldn't the handle issue between bow and crossbow be solved in some other way, for example by loading and releasing a crossbow by free hand (if one has enough strength,as in this case), like with a bow, but keeping the stock to avoid the snapping issue? This to ensure stronger and yet more frequent shooting? The strength of a crossbow, and the speed of an archer.
                    Again there's problems that arise when you start pushing these boundaries. If you make a crossbow like this, you're invariably sacrificing accuracy to keep up that speed. A normal bow has a strong advantage because you can keep the weapon aligned with a target while knocking and firing fresh arrows. A crossbow requires you to adjust the position of the weapon to pull the string, meaning you lose your sighting each time. So you can make a weapon that fires as fast a a bow, with the strength of a crossbow, but you'd be sacrificing a huge amount of accuracy in the process. There is such a weapon that was used by the Song Dynasty in China, but it was explicitly a mass volley weapon where the archers would fire laying on their backs with their feet bracing the bow. As you can imagine, with a few hundred archers they could volley lots and lots of arrows, but nobody was accurately picking off specific targets like that.

                    There's just a basic issue of physics were no matter how strong you are, you can't hold a crossbow (or rifle) stock with one hand and keep the weapon aligned with your sighting mechanism. The distant end of the weapon is going to move around. You have good instincts to think about range increments instead of other aspects of the weapon here, because when the end of that crossbow is shifting around a few centimeters it doesn't really matter at a few meters, but it means you're not hitting whatever you're aiming at 50 meters away as the deflection compounds with distance from the target.

                    After all, I assume a bullet penetrates faster and more harmfully than an arrow, and yet it does not inflict tons of damage in the game, unless it hits vital points.
                    This is again... complicated. The bullet from a firearm is definitely faster than an arrow, but it's also a lot smaller. It's entirely possible for a bullet to blast right through a target and just miss any major organ or blood vessel. A broad head arrow on the other hand, due to its size, is vastly more likely to cause a hemorrhage or a gut perforation without having to accurately aim for a specific part of the body.

                    That's why modern bullets are meant to deform in the target's body: they flatten, growing larger in surface area so they hit more stuff as they go, and tumble around more, to maximize the odds of causing lethal wounds.

                    I may have to scrap the ideas on the customised bow and let my character stick to thrown weapons, which already benefit from increasing character strength.
                    The Francisca is probably a good place to start. They were a well known design in medieval Europe, and the Franks were notorious for using them to shred enemy shield walls before closing into a melee. Some designs were large enough to be a fighting axe as well as a throwing axe, so they scale up fairly large without losing their primary function.
