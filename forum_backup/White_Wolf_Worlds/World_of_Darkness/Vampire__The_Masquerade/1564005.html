                              I think we all understood that (may be wrong) and our points stand.

                              Your idea of "traditional" board game seems to come from having quite some experience with them, but the point is that this "tradition" isn't as universally accepted as you think.

                              Myself, I hate anything with long setups. I think the limit of my patience may be Diplomacy, and it's already stretching it. My problem extends to games that are "traditional" only as far as they have boards and pieces.

                              So, is a "traditional" game something everyone will enjoy? Idk, but I wouldn't bet on it without looking at a concrete project. Have the already made games enjoyed success? It seems so.

                              Gaps don't exist in a vacuum. Making a game or any product because you think a category must be filled is a fast lane for bad decisions. In business and development a "gap" means a demand going unaddressed, so by definition it can't be just "this doesn't exist yet", it is "there is demand or need to supply this thing that doesn't exist yet".

                              Just to explain why I wouldn't call it a gap, what I thought you meant and that I think a more proper use of the world. Your clarification is valid and noted.

                              But the biggest problem I tried to point is, you can't just say a certain boardgame should exist and that's it. Board game design is hard, with few professionals in the industry. You don't say it, but you want competently made rules and assets, and those take a lot of work and money to even prototype.

                              Each board game you like is a greater initial investment than TTRPGs or videogames cause those two are more conducive of the idea of "we'll think it as we do it", even if development is long and costly you can start with a rough idea and whatever.

                              Not to say it isn't feasible. Just demanding, and that means you only have two options: either someone has a great idea and want to invest their time and effort in that particular idea, or a company pays a competent professional team to make it happen.

                              Paradox doesn't seem interested in this for the time being, and as the IP owners they're the only ones who would do the second option. We depend entirely on the first, then, and as such on the whims of fate making a designer have an idea they want to execute with VtM.

                                                      Kids come into understanding where the money is going, and this community highlights the problem of talking only from personal experience. Different ways of looking for board games and players will surface different segments.

                                                      Children are the lion's share of the board game market. There are several reasons for this from gift-giving culture to parents that need to include the whole family. One important caveat is that usually a game will be classified by the youngest audience supported, so you can see a lot of games who are loved by adults classed as for children.

                                                      As a result, games for children are where the big gaming companies are willing to place the bulk of their investments, and seek IPs that can help improve sales. Other markets will receive less attention and effort. And that's us.

                                                      That's why everything has a Star Wars version, a Marvel, a Dr Who and so on. It's not just that those are famous IPs, but crucially they make for games a parent don't have to chose if they'll treat themselves or their children.
