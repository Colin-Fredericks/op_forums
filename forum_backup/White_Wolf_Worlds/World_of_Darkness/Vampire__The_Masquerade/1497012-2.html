    IIRC it wasn't even the creator who removed the socialist rules from The Landlord's Game, it was the thieves. It's removal had a political purpose in addition to not being how most people wanted to play (if that's even true, capitalist rules monopoly is a horrible and unfun board game that knocks most players out far too early). The removal of Sabbat play is probably just as much of a Monopoly situation as the swing from an Anarch focus to a Camarilla focus

    But I'll agree that the majority of players probably aren't in it for the angst, or even the hot goths. At the same time they're probably not in it to be conplete monsters, Sabbat/Brood* games tend to be the exception. Most players probably want somewhere between vampire politics and vampions, and some people want to play Vampire for the other big draw.

    Vampires live for a long time. Few other games allow you to pick a historical culture and play through to the modern day. That can be an appealing prospect, and one that shouldn't be overlooked (of course Mages can also nab immortality, but have to deal with Paradox, and Dreaming allows you to do Middle Ages to the modern day with reincarnating Fae souls).

    * 1e Brood, not the 2e Ascended Draugr version.

    Blue is sarcasm.

                            I'm always a bit amazed at the variety of experiences we have here when we discuss not just V5 play but also interpretation of V5 as a product, its key aims etc.

                            V5 Core, when introducing the concept of the game (pg. 34-36), states that:

                            "This is not a roleplaying game where you play good guys. Perhaps your character tries desperately to hold on to vestiges of human morality despite the sordid demands of vampiric existence. Or maybe they have already adjusted their morality to their new condition, telling themselves that they’re no worse than other vampires after they accidentally kill someone.

                            Whatever the character’s approach to their morality, it’s very likely they end up doing things the player finds morally repugnant. Using this game to explore moral questions and immoral acts can be interesting and emotionally meaningful. After all, the character is not you, and the game is not real. You can use it as a fictional space to explore terrible things, and perhaps even have a little fun with them.

                            How long does it take for your neophyte vampire to start getting used to hunting for blood? Do they lie to themselves, insisting that they’re a good person, or do they believe their self-flagellation and guilt somehow makes the killing okay? You can explore these questions through a character and seek out parallels to real-life issues in your own life and the world at large. [...]

                            Stories of redemption are within the scope of this game too, but redemption is hard. How do you stay the moral course if you literally have to force your fangs into the neck of a human every night? Other tales exist, with different choices. If your character is a working mother trapped in a vicious, abusive marriage, being Embraced as a vampire can give her power and options she never had before. For the disenfranchised of the world, becoming a bloodsucker is like making a deal with the Devil – you get a chance to escape the hell you were trapped in, at the cost of your humanity."

                            Some pages later, when describing Kindred society, the book states that "For the first time ever, a neonate can take over a domain if they play the game well enough. What’s more, many of the elders have left their estates, fortunes, and resources essentially abandoned, tended by a few confused blood servants. A sharp neonate can hijack a fortune accumulated over the millennia and use it for their own purposes. [...] It’s a time for a young vampire to make their mark. Why not be like the neonates who spearheaded the capture of Mexico City from the Sabbat? The elders and Justicars of the Camarilla long thought such a bold act impossible. What else might be possible, if you can seize the night first?" (pg. 38).

                            The premise is still a very broad church, in which Vampirism as a condition can be bemoaned, and redemption pursued, but you can explicitly play it as a power fantasy, or as a descent into monstrosity. In fact, you are explicitly encouraged to test your morality and enjoy your monstrosity, as a metaphor and as an exercise, and redemption is described as very hard, not as the default mode of the game. Nasty politics are still very much the rule of the land here.

                            Pg. 337 introduces advice for running games, under the label "Chronicles": the "Styles of Play" section tells us "So, Vampire is a roleplaying game in which joyless monsters wrestle with morality in the shadows of suitably gothic architecture. Right? Well, it can be. But it can be fun too. Past editions described Vampire: The Masquerade as a “Gothic-Punk” game. Punk is still as good a word as any to describe the sordid, transgressive joy of playing in a good Vampire game. But Vampire can accommodate many different play styles, and they are all valid, as long as everyone in the game has a good time".

                            No particular One True Wayism, here: pg. 343 even states that "The job of a Storyteller or a player is not to morally judge the characters in the game. Vampire works best in a world where everything is in shades of grey and there is a little bit of a sinner in everyone. While the characters can have moral opinions inside the story, you get more play from them if you let them act according to their personalities, motivations, and personal logic".

                            The section also presents various Campaign modes, such as "Inheritors of the Illuminati", "Judgement Night", "Fun with Evil", "Hunting Party", "Fang Gang", "Nomads": again very different chronicle types, which all connect to the various playstiles of traditional Vampire. And playing an Ancilla is one of the default options of the corebook.

                            Hunger as a mechanic reinforces a central concept of the game, but nowhere the corebook states or supports the idea that V5 characters should just spend their unlives decrying their transformation and striving to purge the world of more powerful vampires. It also nowhere stresses that the game is best served by one shots, or that characters shouldn't accumulate power and influence.

                            Mechanics here may have unintended or hidden aims here, of course, but I think that V5 gets often misrepresented in its design aims and stylistic choices, should someone judge it by the book (as it will be for most of the new players).

                                I don't doubt V5 had fucked up PCs and darkness in mind, because otherwise there is no way in hell the Setites and the Giovanni would still be around in any form. But 5th edition has also been thrown around like a hot potato in new directions every now and then. The core, and what it was intended to become, may no longer be what it's heading for now. In regards to "the one true way" the game is being designed for, I read it as "you can be a bad guy, but RAW firmly encourages you to play a specific kind of bad guys and not those Bad Guys."
                                You're not supposed to play the Second Inquisition because they are the Man and this is Punk. No Alphabet Agencies!
                                On that note, you're not allowed to be part of any organized hunter group. They are also the Man.
                                You're not supposed to play a sect that's been a playable part of the game for, what two decades? The Sabbat are comfortable and exalt in their vampirism and That Is Not What This Edition Is About. (Also touchstones must be human, you can not be totally indifferent to humanity or else you will probably wassail. The benefits of high humanity make you more human. Embracing is stains, ghouling is stains, etc. The tenets can be whatever they like, but the levels of humanity are described as though having it high means you're a "good vampire." There is a strong undertone of intended play which makes the mentions of other themes feel like lip service.)
