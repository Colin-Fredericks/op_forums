    Originally posted by Ragged Robin View Post
    Big advantage if mass embraces is it really opens who is in what clan. Last sabbat lasombra was a cheerleader gone bad who actually did quite well for herself.
    My thinking about the Sabbat is firmly based on the impact of mass embraces and the example pack found in second edition Chicago By Night.

    For Camarilla and Anarch vampires, the emphasis is usually on what was it about their mortal life that interested their sire to embrace them. The reasons might be trivial or profound, but usually there is a reason. When designing their character, most players will think hard about that and why and how their PC will fit into their clan based on their mortal history. There is a continuity between their mortal life and their existence as a vampire.

    For Sabbat vampires though, the emphasis is how they reacted after their turning. Instead of continuity, there is an abrupt departure.

    This is what we see in Cameron's Nomad pack.

    Cameron himself was a scrawny hypochondriac loser. His Panhandling Talent implies he was likely living on the streets when he was embraced. Yet somehow he survived the creation rites, became the only surviving member of his pack, and eventually rose to be ductus. Of course, to his clanmates in the Lasombra, Cameron remains an embarrassment and they can't understand why this guy survived. So they send him off on a mission that is mean to fail and probably get him killed. Yet by looking at his stats and history, there isn't much evidence that he's been a failure as a vampire. It's just that to other Lasombra he doesn't fit the mold. Yeah, he's relying on Tyler to tell him what to do, but it's his first experience with being any kind of leader. He has to grow into it, and it wasn't like he was groomed or prepared for these responsibilities. Even if he fails at being a leader, it's not like that is the sole criteria by which to judge a Lasombra. He has minimal Leadership Talent, but has a very high Manipulation. That implies he would do well in other scenarios.

    Lolita was an orphan who spent her whole life in the foster care system being passed around from one family to the next. Now she's aiming to move up in clan and sect Status, having confidence and opportunities as a vampire she never had in life.

    Margarite was fresh out of high school looking forward to finding a future husband so she could have kids in a house with a white picket fence. Now she cares absolutely nothing about those dreams and is completely dedicated to being a vampire and member of the Sabbat.

    Of course, we already had some of that with Wendy Wade who went from being a young, successful lawyer who was embraced only because she was at the wrong place at the wrong time. But with Wendy, we can see why she was being successful as a vampire based on her mortal history. With Cameron's pack, we're seeing some surprises. Now the nomad pack as a whole is supposed to be a bunch of misfits, but I don't see why any of them could not have been considered a successful Sabbat member if they were integrated in a more successful pack. Nor do I think putting together "misfits" into a new pack and sending them off is anything unique. This is probably standard modus operandi for the sect.

    To me, this is the big thing distinguishing the Sabbat from all the others. The others are still concerned about getting the "right" childe and use their mortal life as an indicator for their fitness. The Sabbat instead has the attitude that their mortal life is unimportant and doesn't reflect how well they might adjust. Just embrace anyone and see what happens - people will surprise you. It is a good way to distinguish the sects.

    The examples of the nomad pack cemented certain ideas of what Sabbat vampires were like, and that did fit how the sect was described in the Players and Storytellers Guides. It's a big reason why I dislike the Lasombra and Tzimisce clanbooks. They were written from the perspective that those clans were actually more like the Camarilla in clans in how they embrace new vampires. I don't recall any of their PC templates/examples reflecting that a substantial number of them would be random embraces. I didn't mind to see that some embraces would be "traditional", but completely ignoring the creation rites created a very bad impression in players. And that is why we saw the erosion of distinctive Sabbat elements in Revised (other than a general splatterpunk). The mass embrace should be the typical while the traditional embrace should be the exception within the Sabbat IMO. (The many examples of traditional embraces in the clanbooks would often make good Lasmobra anti-tribu or Old Clan Tzimisce (if not for the Viccisitude), but are out of place for the Sabbat.)

                    Originally posted by Ragged Robin View Post
                    Something a GM I know who runs a lot of Sabbat does is they create a distinction between people who just happen to a member of the clan vs those who 'fit in'.
                    I think that is very appropriate for the Sabbat. The Sabbat lack the NCO core of ancillae as very few Sabbat neonates live that long. So clan culture is something that elders perpetuate among themselves. Whether Sabbat neonates "fit in" or are "misfits" is almost immaterial. Very few will live long enough. Only those few who become ancilla would be worth time and effort to cultivate. And likely only those surviving neonates who fit into the culture would get any kind of support or mentoring that might help them survive until then. The worst misfits might even be assigned dangerous tasks ("It's a great opportunity!") that would likely get them killed.

                    Those misfits who do survive and gain power in the sect likely gravitate as minority clan members who either become Loyalists, or perhaps join one of the other factions where clan loyalties are not a block to advancement like the Sabbat Inquisition, Black Hand, become a secret infernalist, or become a templar/paladin.

                    An emphasis on clan culture is really something that the hypocritical elders are concerned about, it's not really important to the Sabbat as a sect. It actually represents something the sect - supposedly - rebelled against in the Anarch Revolt. Clan divisions are just another way the elders divide and conquer the neonates. At least that is the unstated, but generic attitude of the sect that I try to present as ST. That the elder leaders of the sect still perpetuate its importance is unacknowledged, but present. So clan culture is acknowledged but downplayed in public, while being important behind closed doors but not stated outright.

                    My main concern as an ST in regards to the Sabbat is to present a very different sect culture and feeling than among the Camarilla or anarchs. So emphasizing pack culture, the paths of enlightenment, and the unique institutions of the Sabbat is more important to me than the clans of characters. I've seen too many Sabbat games where characters are not distinguishable from the Camarilla except that they are much more bloodthirsty and use different titles. I think that does a disservice to the ideas behind the sect. I want the portrayal of the Sabbat to be truly alien.

                                    Multiple editions' diluting the Sabbat's identity is the issue. I mean, when you say Sabbat, which Sabbat do you mean?

                                    1. Losers from the first Anarch revolt who just wanted a vampiric society of their own, but locked themselves and their descendants into cycles of violence and revenge against the Camarilla?

                                    2. The mishmash of heretical and eschatonic cults based on major world religions and Noddism?

                                    3. Vampire separatists and supremacists who embrace the Beast and their own monstrosity?

                                    4. Those (mostly neonates and ancilla) who are just kind of along for the ride, and amount to slightly edgier and more ultraviolent Anarchs?

                                    5. Serial diablerists and other degenerates who are for all intents and purposes wights, but retain enough of their "higher" selves to function on a pack level?

                                    Each aspects' focus changed depending on edition, and it never quite seemed like writers could decide who or what the Sabbat really was at its core. Ultimately, the Sabbat is a dysfunctional society held together only by the threat of their own elders and methuselahs, the threat of the Camarilla, and the vaulderie.

                                    [Yes, you could say the same of the Camarilla being a dysfunctional society held together by the threat of their own elders and methuselahs, the threat of the Sabbat, and prestation...and the Anarchs are a dysfunctional society held together by the threat of the Camarilla and Sabbat.]

                                    When it comes to my games I like to emphasize points three and four, with points one and two being the Sabbat's connective tissue, and point five being its (unintended) self-regulatory mechanism. To me, at the end of the day the Sabbat became what it intended to fight: a tyrannical elder supremacist sect that maintains control by keeping neonates and ancillae distracted with external threats. Sabbat elders have more in common with Camarilla elders than the younger vampires of their own sect, and are more than happy to throw an entire city to the fire in a manufactured and curated conflict to preserve their own existence and station. All else -- religion, politics, whatever -- is simply kayfabe and delusion.

                                    To me, that makes Sabbat and Camarilla more interesting: Camarilla vampires on Paths of Enlightenment (or even Roads), hypocritically enforcing Humanity on their lessers as a mechanism for control. Sabbat elders carefully hiding and preserving Humanity, going to elaborate lengths to make themselves appear monstrous while never actually being such. Elders and even ancillae with alliances across sect boundaries, engaging in quid pro quo or even manipulating entire sieges for mutual benefit. The careful dance of a manufactured sect war that really isn't.

                                                    Ultimately, I believe the purpose of the Sabbat is to allow the World of Darkness to have pre-Anne Rice vampires. The kind we all know from movies and literature where the vampire shows up, turns random people into vampires, and devours the community until the hunters destroy him. The Camarilla was for post-modern vampires where vampires could be protagonists, perhaps even heroes. So the game covered both angles. The Sabbat also played the role of the bogeyman in a setting dominated by the Camarilla, but increasingly under threat. As long as you do that, you've succeeded at a general level.

                                                    The issue is how do you distinguish the Sabbat from the Camarilla so they are truly different? If you just make them like the Camarilla, it opens the question of why those anarchs at the Convention of Thorns just didn't just join up anyway? Why go through the long centuries of initial defeat when they were routed from most of Europe to flee to the Americas only to self-destruct in three civil wars? The terrifying Sabbat we know from the sourcebooks only really began in 1957 when the Third Civil War ended, or at best 1933 when the Second Civil War ended and the revised Code of Milan was established.

                                                    That Sabbat is very different from the original anarchs who defied the Camarilla by leaving the Convention of Thorns. The Sabbat has always been a big mess. That's part of its appeal. It gives the ST a lot of different tools to tailor her chronicles. Emphasizing Paths of Enlightenment, pack cultures as an institution, internal Sabbat politics of radical freedom versus warfighting needs, and so on provides the tools for the ST to distinguish the sect from the Camarilla. An individual Sabbat domain could be vastly different from another one. I think the trouble occurs from a setting design perspective whenever the publisher decides only ONE way to portray the Sabbat was the one true way, and invalidates.
