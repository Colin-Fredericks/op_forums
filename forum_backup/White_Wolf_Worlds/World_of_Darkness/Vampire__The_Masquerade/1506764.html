              Having been on both ends of player betrayals (plenty of times), there's nothing more frustrating than losing a favorite character, but also nothing quite like it to spice up group dynamics. It can produce some fine drama for a chronicle and only becomes a problem if the players take it OOC. Which happens too much, unfortunately.

              The Vampire LARP group I played in was pretty much political pvp. After all, you play egocentric, power-hungry monsters in a society in which the ability to backstab somebody and get away with it is seen as an admirable trait. When characters got too cozy with each other, it took away a lot of the thrill and the game started to get boring.
              I took that mentality back to TTRPGs with me, and inter-group squabbles remain one of the most fun things for me, even when I'm STing.

              tldr: if all parties involved can accept it as part of the game, it's part of the fun; if someone can't, it needs to be discussed before it causes OOC drama.

                  I can't say I've had this particular situation happen to me... mostly because backstabbing in this situation makes zero sense (if you're good enough with a gun to injure a vampire with one badly enough they can't flee a fight... you were good enough to shoot the wight's leg instead).

                  I've been on the losing end of PC on PC betrayals before. I personally don't get the appeal when it gets to really high stakes stuff like character-death. I've never seen it actually be fun for everyone involved. Rivalries over Backgrounds, competing for favor from NPCs, who gets to Embrace a high value potential childe, jockeying for position within the coterie? Sure, bring all that stuff on. But vampires form coteries because there's strength in numbers - esp. for younger vampires aka the PCs - and it's in everyone's best interests not to take internal coterie bickering too far, or some more unified coterie will take advantage and take you all out. If the wider vampire community knows a wight can destroy the basic trust of mutual-self interest in your coterie, then your coterie is going to get torn apart so other people can have all your group's stuff.

                  As PCs get more powerful (both with XP and more narrative things) and less dependent on their coterie for mutual protection from the greed of vampire society, the conflicts still tend to keep away from PC on PC killings, because they're going to be able to send proxies to fight proxies instead. You persisted that far by working on making sure you're not in a position to be killed off that readily by others.

                  Of course tastes vary, and has been noted, this is really something groups need to discuss to keep things fun. Some people like really back-stab heavy games, and some people hate PvP play completely. Whatever is fun for the group is more important than appeals to how vampires "should" be played.

In biology where this comes from and all, fitness is generally measured by ensuring your genes are perpetuated in the population (which means applying the concept to most RPGs doesn't work because very few RPGs get into that).                               I've never had this happen to me, but issues about intra-party conflict affects a lot of RPG games. It is usually justified as "I'm just playing my character", but if it is causing your fellow players to not have fun IRL it just destroys the gaming group. Players don't want other players crapping all over them and destroying their ability to enjoy the game. It's just not fun.

                              And in my experience, the player who acts like a jerk and says "I'm just playing my character" tends to get really pissed off if NPCs or even other PCs react with outrage to their actions. Then getting another PC killed is just "playing their character." Their PC getting killed in response becomes unacceptable (as is the idea that the other PCs won't accept the other PC into their group anymore and the ST telling that person he needs to create a new PC).

                              Depending on the genre, what constitutes acceptable IC conflict can change. Players expect very little betrayal in heroic fantasy games, but expect some amount in a game like Vampire. In my gaming groups, players tend to be OK with a rare and skillful betrayal provided it allows them to continue playing their PC and the rationale for the coterie to continue exists. In very rare cases, the actual destruction of another PC may be acceptable, but the story behind that decision must be so good the other players are willing to accept this. But there is still a point when it crosses the line. Usually there is an unwritten agreement between members of the gaming table of what is acceptable or unacceptable. Sometimes that needs to be discussed or made explicit.

                              Players having fun is an OCC issue. It is not an IC issue. You can never resolve these debates by handling them IC. The gaming group needs to determine what is acceptable behavior. IMO the excuse of "I'm just roleplaying my character" for ruining another player's fun means that player needs a new PC.

                                                  Backtracking a bit, have I ever had a situation where my character got betrayed by another character, with possibly lethal consequences?

                                                  Well, yeah, twice in fact, nearly three-but here's the thing, it was part of the group expectations when we made characters and gave the Storyteller ideas of how we wanted those games to run. One of them had it explicitly clear that the goal was for every character to have goals that they heavily prioritized, because that was a theme we all wanted to explore, just how much we were willing to sacrifice for those goals. I've had a lot more games where the group agreed at the start that, even if some inter-party conflict was fine, at the end of the day our characters were there for each other, and kept to it.

                                                  RPG's, as a creative endeavor, are just as subject to the fact that they are games of setting and managing expectations, heavily weighted in a certain way because of the direct human interaction. It's important for enjoyment and exploration to make it clear what players are in for, what they're not in for, and checking for how those can change as the story progresses.

                                                          Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
                                                          None of this addressed what "Survival of the Fittest" actually means. There is no correlation I'm aware of between pre-nups and biological fitness in humans, because fitness doesn't actually care if you get divorced and how resources are divided during divorce as long as you continue your genes.
                                                          If you are too stupid to see the betrayal coming and take steps to protect yourself, you can't pass on your genes. Keeping with your limited view of what "survival of the fittest" means, if you lose most of your resources in a divorce, then your ability to procure another high quality mate decreases, which means even if you find another mate then they most likely will be of inferior quality, which means the offspring will be of likewise inferior quality, thus not the fittest.

                                                          You are correct, nature does not care how you produce offspring, be it forming social bonds or using force to ensure offspring are produced. But the breeding stock that you choose is a concern. If they have diseases, bad genetics, or any number of other issues, then they might not be worth your time to attempt mating with. But since humans are social creatures, we create societies with artificial aspects that throw a curveball into what is an important trait or behavior. This means that behaviors or traits that would be a detriment in the wild are actually a boon in our artificial existence.

                                                          A good example is the fact that in big business sociopathic tendencies are ten times more prevalent than in the general population. The lack of empathy and cutthroat behavior would get you exiled or ostracized from a tribe, but they tend to make you successful in business. This normally wouldn't be a problem, except that being successful in business means you attract a lot more potential breeding partners just due to the higher level of status and resources.

                                                          But since we are talking about vampires, other than Clan & Generation, there are no aspects of "genes" that really enter into the conversation. Instead "Survival of the Fittest" refers to a given vampires ability to be adaptable, to learn, to be cunning. In short to ensure their continued existence and defeat their opponents, or at least negate their enemies ability to do them harm.
