            In the old supplement, "Dark Colony" the chronicle in the book was an attempt at incorporating Lovecraftian elements into Vampire in a fitting setting for both: New England. It even featured a Tremere antagonist summoning an eldritch entity ( a Nexus Crawler of werewolf). I suggest that you don't define it in character or out of character; don't put the entities in an understandable framework or define it in a manner that allows the players to neatly provide context to it or quantify it ("Oh, it's a Type VI demon!"). For example, there's no need to ever use the term "Nexus Crawler" or connect the entity to any particular mythos. Don't provide exposition. Neither confirm nor deny; and never give them "the answer". I'd also make it so the worse atrocities are committed by the very human cultists who serve the "dark powers" or whatever, keep the vampire remote, requiring work to see what lurks behind the mortals, and then what lurks behind the vampire..

                            Originally posted by No One of Consequence View Post
                            Two of the recurring themes of Lovecraft's works is the general insignificance of human beings and that his protagonists tend to detached, reclusive and socially isolated...
                            Pretty much this. Gotta remember the eldritch horrors were never really the point. Running a game where PC's are Tremere neonates simply learning to navigate the Pyramid is plenty Lovecraftian at its core. Remember the nature of the clan and the Pyramid.

                            Literally everyone hates and fears the Tremere, especially the Tremere. For most Tremere neonates who don't get the privilege of working with external coteries, the Pyramid is all they have. Of course, the Pyramid is a cult of personality working under a perpetual siege mentality, which is a serpents' nest of backbiting, lies, betrayal, and utter obsession with the will to power that well surpasses even that of the Lasombra, all bound by supernaturally-enforced oaths of loyalty, propaganda, supernatural gaslighting, and indoctrination, when the reality is the only real rule is "don't get caught". In fact, in a certain point of view the Lasombra are the Tremere's moral superiors, because at least they're honest about their internal strife and constant backstabbing, and make it well known from the very onset with zero room for misinterpretation that in the final analysis, each and every Lasombra stands alone.

                            But, the linchpin is, the Pyramid mandates all Tremere maintain their Humanity to preserve the Masquerade. For any vampire, in the long run this is a fool's errand, but in this regard the Tremere are uniquely set up to fail, and catastrophically at that.

                            Common restrictions as mandated by Tremere siege mentality, upon neonates and apprentices at large, tends toward limiting contact with mortals -- after all, a Tremere outside the walls of their chantry is a vulnerable Tremere. Meanwhile, to simply use Thaumaturgy requires one unlearn everything they may or may not know about metaphysics, and put in its place a Medieval worldview that is foreign and alien to the post-Enlightenment mind, and this process of changing one's worldview is readily supplemented by mind control, torture, and other abuses should it not proceed to the Regent's liking. This is a discipline all Tremere are required to learn to the near-exclusion of all else, with exceptions few and far between for Tremere whose sole decreed purpose is to manage the clan's assets and act as liaisons to the mortal world. Then comes the actual practice of Thaumaturgy, which can and does require activities like theft, vandalism, graverobbing, consent violation, torture, even murder, at its upper echelons of use. Make no mistake, to learn and practice Thaumaturgy regularly is a strain on Humanity that cannot be easily borne in the short term, and in the long term becomes impossible.

                            And, as I alluded to earlier, this is all by design. The Tremere are only as strong as their weakest link, and as such they have zero tolerance or sympathy for weakness. Every last aspect of the Pyramid is purpose-built by some of the most intelligent and strategically-minded beings to have ever existed, to destroy everything neonates ever were, and may have ever been, to recreate them in the clan's own image in the face of an entire world that would destroy them to their last without hesitation or remorse given half a chance.

                            Is it any wonder so many go rogue, turn to infernalism, eventually go mad, or degenerate too rapidly and have to be reprogrammed or destroyed? In an entire game and setting awash with Lovecraftian themes, the Tremere are a cut above.

                                    Mind you, there's also the Revelations of the Dark Mother where God is just first among the Elohim and thus not omnipotent.

                                    The reconciliation for "a benevolent God in a world that includes evil" is one of the denser problems in theology.
                                    Yes and no. It depends on whether or not you accept the answer.

                                    But I include a lot of Lovecraftian mythology in my games, sometimes while including Judeo-Christian and the following answers have been used:

                                    * The Great Old Ones of the setting are the fallen Archdemons in the Underworld. I.e. the take from Demon: The Fallen. It's a very Christian take but there's the possible horrifying fact God has left or died which leaves only the alien Cthulhuoid insane entities.

                                    * The Great Old Ones are the "things before the Light" and are chaotic horrifying beings that are not part of God's law or universe. God defeated them with Lucifer leading the host against them.

                                    * God in the V:TM world is a product of human faith and not the reverse. The GOO are the true gods of the universe.

                                    * The GOO are the Wyrm's various faces. "God" is either the Patriarch or a somewhat more benevolent face of Jupiter or Helios.

                                    * God is one of the "young gods" who was created by Gaia and the Elder Gods are the things before Gaia (which is actually the history of the Marvel Universe's magical entities by Roy Thomas).

                                    * The GOO actually are simply aliens from another world that have come to the Earth. They have their own gods and are far older than our own.

                                    * God is actually Azathoth, purely driven by wild and animalistic urges, but humans have ascribed mortal ideas to it. His power is real but it answers to whoever who channels it, good or evil.

                                    * God is one of many gods like Zeus, Odin, and others. Their numbers dwindle now and the fight is only kept up by Oracles of the Mages, Changelings, and others.

                                        Generally speaking, as others have pointed out, Lovecraftian Horror is about Cosmic Horror - the insignificance of humanity (and especially of a single human) and the alien and inscrutable creatures that populate the world but are beyond any sort of human understanding.

                                        I've found one of the easiest ways to do that is to actually pull from stuff outside of VtM, such as grabbing Umbral spirits from Werewolf. They're peripherally part of the VtM cosmology, but are extremely alien and unknowable since outside of a small handful, vampires generally don't truck with those sort of creatures. Many of them are also totally inhuman and operate along concepts that are well outside of what humans might want. You can drop a corrupted Wyrm spirit into a game and boom, you've got unknowable cosmic horror represented by things that even supernatural monsters like vampires would find frightening.

                                                Methuselahs can be used to deliver Chtonic Horror pretty easily. Not every Meth is "human" like Mithras, or "relatable" like Helena and Menele.
                                                Yorak, Byelobog, Ur-Shulgi, Enkidu, the Hierophants, The Baali founders,Odin,the sire of Esperanza Lucifer,Lamdiel, Nissiku, the Word Eater, Tiamat and so on...they are all good examples of really out of touch creatures, inhuman and fathomless, and powerful beyond imagination.
                                                A thing like Yorak can be pretty much alien, and the Chatedral of Flesh doesn't have much to envy from Ryl'eh; Nissiku is so strange that he just warps reality and madness follows him like a plague; Tiamat is a Demon-Vampire that lies in the depth of ancient and forgotten tomb; Odin is an old necromancer that lead a cult of blood whose legacy still lives in the darkest nights of Norther Europe.

                                                Let your players being caught in the threads of the Jyhad, put multiple layer of complexity and horror, let them feel little and powerless in front of an old feud between alien beings that control society and history. Let them glimpse the horror beneath the city, ancient crypts lost in ages, blood rituals to appease the gods of old and so on.

                                                It's really easy, since the 2nd edition Vampire moved from personal horror to chtonic horror pretty quickly. You really don't have to use things from other settings, you already have everything you need.

                                                            Another recurring theme in some of Lovecraft's work is that of inherited guilt, or the idea of the "sins of the father being visited upon the son". This is a really common trope of classic Gothic literature and so is already a component of Vampire. The most common take being that Childer end up getting caught up in the machinations of their Sires and paying a pretty steep personal price. It also works really well with Ghouls and especially with Revenant families, and the Ventrue make for fertile ground as well. Back during 1st ed and part of the early 2nd, you'd see more than one Ventrue who was strongly tied to a single mortal family, be it his (it was usually a he) own or one where someone back in the day had become sworn to his service. The price for this is that, in addition to being the vampire's pawns or source of resources, the family may serve as a herd (especially for a Ventrue who can only feed on that bloodline) or young members discover that they're being groomed for the embrace. (The Giovanni are practically based around this idea.)

                                                            Also, Lovecraft didn't exist in a vacuum. His own works were strongly influenced by the stories of Edgar Allan Poe, Arthur Machen, Robert Chambers, Algernon Blackwood and Lord Dunsany, all of whose works offer up potential ideas and themes that can be incorporated into Vampire. Dunsany's stories, which influenced Lovecraft's Dreamlands, can be inspiration for a group of Auspex-possessing vampires - especially Malkavians - astrally projecting into a dreamlike realm of strange beings and cryptic gods. Perhaps even bringing something back with them. I also recommend looking at some of the work's by Nathanial Hawthorne, especially for anyone who wants to set a Vampire game in New England.

                                                            There's also Lovecraft's contemporaries who each built upon the Mythos in their own way. I find that Robert E Howard's works, especially his horror stories, work really well for Vampire games set out in the American West and for those that involve barbaric anachronisms released from torpor into the modern nights. Clark Ashton Smith's works tended to involve protagonists striving, often in vain, to recover some lost and idealized part of their past. This is perfect for Vampire.
