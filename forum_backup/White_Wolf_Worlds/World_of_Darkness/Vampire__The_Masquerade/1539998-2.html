    Most of that framework exists to justify how the character feeds (and how feeding preferences would influence dot allocation in the backstory phase), while providing a baseline ability to feed in the first place. The quiet part out loud here is predator type doesn't just influence disciplines, advantages, and flaws -- players still have to allocate attributes and skills before predator type, and once they've put everything together they need a respectable dice pool in their feeding roll of choice, and in the case of some predator types and clans a discipline dot to supplement feeding strategy.

The categorization-exercise was meaningless therefore.

        That seems to be a common mistake a lot of GMs make. They love to make any characters connected to a PC as potential hostages or victims of their antagonists. They think it will make players more involved in their campaign plots. However, this only annoy players because this NPC they supposedly care about is hijacking the game and diverting them from the things they are actually interested in. So not only to players resent it, they tend to do things IC and OOC to eliminate the problem which is to entirely ditch anyone they care about. So instead of a richer setting and supporting cast for the PC - which is often the intent of such characters, it just results in even less setting detail and no supporting cast.

        In a drama, it makes a good scene for Hector Salamanca to put the squeeze on Ignacio Varga's dad because it creates a lot of tension and provides for character building. In a roleplaying game, all it usually does is make the player of Nacho not have a father (or any family or friends) at all and eliminate any kind of roleplay opportunities.


        So as ST, I go out of my way to not have such characters become important to the chronicle so they can remain exclusively part of the player's roleplay, and avoid NPCs targeting them in any fashion. It doesn't always make sense from an IC perspective, but if you want to encourage good roleplay and a supporting cast, it's what needs to be done. So NPCs just ignore the supporting cast of PCs UNLESS the players themselves decide to utilize them in some sort of scheme against the NPCs. That makes them fair game, and players recognize it was them who put their supporting cast member (whether they are just NPCs they interact with or actual Backgrounds/Merits that are on the character sheet) in jeopardy.

        So in the RPG campaign of Better Call Saul, Hector Salamanca never asks Nacho to have his dad's business become a front. Instead, the GM can only have Hector comment he needs one and see if the player of Nacho bites and offers his dad's business. If he does, he then finds out his dad never agrees and threaten to go to the cops. Because the player created the situation, he cares about the dad instead of being angry at the GM for screwing him over.

        Having that guideline has improved my games a lot.

            Originally posted by monteparnas View Post
            Predator types are certainly interesting, although I think the way they’re implemented could improve. Attaching so many “points” to them muddies things more than help. It is like teasing at being a Character Class without committing to being a Character Class. Which is made worse by the fact we already have Clans for this.
            I think Predator types and similar mechanics like Backgrounds in D&D 5e have the same benefits and drawbacks. The benefits are that they provide the means to further customize the character. And in certain ways they prompt the player to think more carefully of their character's backstory. The drawback is that there are only a certain number of them and thus can be overly restrictive and not accommodate certain backstories, and the desire to provide specific benefits for each one can lead to certain features be too good or bad from a mechanics perspective.

            I usually prefer some sort of method of defining some quick and easy way to allow PCs further customization and build points, but not in ways that break the game. So I let the player describe whatever it is they want (My vampires often feeds on the homeless, or my fighter grew up on a farm and worked as a pigkeeper) and collaborate with them on specific benefits. For Vampire that might be the first dot in some Discipline they haven't put points in and the first dot in some Ability. For D&D 5e, that can be two skills they gain proficiency in, a proficiency in either a tool or vehicle, and some extra cash and gear worth a certain amount. That allows for extra customization, but all players get the same benefits. I just use the existing types, backgrounds, careers, whatever they call it in the game, as examples.

                                                    I could break down V5 versus previous edition character generation step-by-step, but in the end V5 has one fewer step overall by way of rolling backgrounds, merits, and flaws together, and replacing freebie points with predator type. Freebie points were by far the most problematic step of previous edition character generation -- freebie point-buys had wildly divergent experience cost discrepancies, were the biggest decision paralysis invoker and stumbling block for newer and less rules-fluent players. The difference between an experienced player's, and a newbie's, starting characters could be massive to the point of disrupting game balance for easily the first hundred experience points' worth of play.

                                                    That's without going into the storyteller headache resultant from potential merit/flaw munchkinism, having to audit at char gen to make sure someone didn't accidentally spend too many or too few freebies, and checking for odd, inappropriate, or hard-to-justify freebie expenditure. That's without assuming negative player intent -- sometimes a player just gets overexcited, loses focus during character generation, suffers from decision paralysis, or slips into old habit. I do it too -- a recent V20 character I made was a Nosferatu ancilla recently out of a long torpor, and I as someone who likes to play characters with the drive skill, had to consciously remind myself this is not a character on whom points in drive would be appropriate.

                                                    Can you justifiably call something unnecessary bloat, when it overall reduces the number of steps to character generation and directly replaces a far more problematic step? Commentary thus far is just restricted to character generation.

                                                    In play, predator type recommends predetermined dice pools for feeding. That's one fewer decision point for a storyteller to make, while establishing a player expectation of how they might be able to approach a circumstance outside character specialty or expertise. It amounts to less time and mental bandwidth to be spent on game mechanics over the course of a session, story, or chronicle, which can then be spent on story and narration -- the things that really matter in a narrative-focused game. And most importantly, it reduces the potential for squabbling over which dice pool might be most appropriate for a given situation by keeping everyone on the same page.

                                                    Is it perfect? no. But "imperfect" is not synonymous with "bad", least of all when the systems replaced are even more problematic.
