                What Outburst and Beast Frenzy do is similar to V5 Clan Compulsions. Well, for now Beast Frenzy is literally the V5 Clan Compulsion. You get a guidance on how to behave, penalty when trying to do something else and a condition to end the state. Then you slide in the scale.

                On the marks, they're in the Beast and Nature tracks, not the scale. The tracks are to time the Frenzy/Outburst rolls. They only trigger when a track reaches 5, you can try to resist or Ride the Wave, if you roll and resist it erases 1 mark, going back to 4. If you Frenzy/Outburst, at its conclusion you slide in the scale.

                Marking both makes sense. They're both drives you carry within, they are not really opposite and they can be both neglected, so you can have a balanced vampire that fulfills regularly both without inner conflict, and a balanced one that lives from Frenzy to Outburst interspersed with a refusal or denial of who they are in both senses.

If they decide to go back to a more traditional take, I'd say they could let both options in the book as equally valid play styles.

                        Originally posted by GreatHornedRatkin View Post

                        I think codifying what Humanity is has been a problem from the onset. Especially for neurodivergent people, who are biologically wired to view the world in a completely different perspective.

                        Refocusing your Humanity to be the remaining vestiges of your mortal self is a far better approach to the situation, in my opinion.

                        Also, what you said about balancing the two extremes provides an interesting possibility for how they might reintroduce Golconda in the future.
                        The problem is that what the game was trying to encode is not exactly intuitive to explain in its context, and frankly, they chose the words for it poorly.

                        Humanity with a Hierarchy of Sins is a key part of the Gothic side of the game. Not as in Goth counterculture, but the original two waves of Gothic Horror literature in the 18th and 19th centuries.

                        One central element of Gothic is a naturalistic take on morality. It's not moral in the sense we usually understand of socially accepted morals or individual beliefs, but rather the notion that the most fundamental moral principles are so basic and intuitive we can say they exist as a fact, even if we can't explain why.

                        Killing is bad. Stealing is bad. Violence is bad. Nuance matters and circumstances change everything, but no one with a healthy mind will deny those basic ideas. The Romans were a warlike people, as by the way the modern US is, too, and many other nations. But if you asked a Roman, just as if you ask a US citizen, war is a necessary evil, not a good thing. So to Gothic the first point is this: Good and Evil are real, but not in the way we rationalize and rather in their basic principles.

                        Gothic's second point is a naturalistic reward to Good and Evil. The villain will terrorize and horrify. They'll harm, kill, maim. Yet in the end they'll pay. The villain will fall. Their victims will suffer, but those who escape and are good will have the opportunity to live a happy life. Those who are like the villain will suffer torment in life even as they torment others, and in the end will meet a horrible and solitary end.

                        Again, though, Gothic is naturalistic, and this is key: these who live doing good will have good lives because they don't make unnecessary enemies, because they don't obsess over their whims, they don't live in frustration. They do good to others band that increases the chance that they'll get goodness in return. Isn't perfect, but is a positive balance. The villain will suffer because the natural consequences of their actions will get them. They're despised. Their obsessions can only grow. Their failings will follow them. Not because the world wants to punish them, though, but because their actions are building up to this.

                        Finally, it's a morality play about choices, this is the third point that closes it all. Gothic Horror is based in the premise that most of the time the difference between Good and Evil is clear. It's not always big, but it's usually clear. And while choosing Good may be hard or inconvenient, it is ultimately feasible: you'll have to give up on something, but you can live without it. You may have to suffer or face hardships, but they're not unsurmountable. The Gothic villain is evil in the end because they chose evil, and they kept this choice again and again. Good people don't live easy lives, they choose to do right rather than convenient and the reward isn't instantaneous. But in the end it's the villain whom doomed themselves.

                        In this interplay, the Gothic plays complex and traditional morals and adages as frequently being excuses to chose poorly. The villain will rarely recognize themselves as a villain. They are victims of the circumstances, they're just upholding honor, or proper norms, or science in the second wave. They are full of legitimate reasons to have chosen what they did, because no horror is greater than to face the fact you're the one responsible for your choices and their consequences. Society in the Gothic is full of false maxims about how hard it is to do good, just to release people from guilty over their bad choices.

                        Legacy VtM plays this trope, and one of the main mechanics for it is Humanity. Having it high feels good. Your reward is mostly to be satisfied with yourself. Lowering it gets you ever closer to a terrible end. The undead are all the time telling each other about how the Beast gnaws at your mind and slowly draws you to the bottom. But the actual rules tell another story by design. Avoiding Frenzy is hard, but doable. Feeding safely just requires that you do frequently in small sips, which is inconvenient, but sustainable. You can avoid all the sins in the Hierarchy if you simply chose to do so, and while that'll make your life harder, rarely will you be really required to do any of them.

                        None of those are problems with the rules, they are exactly the intention: staying good is meant to be feasible, it was in your reach all along. The fluff on Humanity being a slippery slope? It was an unreliable narrator for a reason: he is lying. To you the reader, probably to himself, to all Kindred and with all Kindred. The punchline is that Vampiric society isn't horrible because they're damned, damnation is just something they keep parroting to live with their choices. Their society is horrible because they're selfish assholes in a purely mundane way, even if the consequences, not the cause, are magically amplified.

                        Within this proposal, this vision of gothic horror and dramatic theme, Humanity had a specific role that was never about morality in a social or religious sense, it was about the price of choosing power and convenience over basic decency. The game was too obtuse, though, and failed terribly to convey this goal. Which is not a problem unique to VtM in the WoD. And this new Humanity Scale covers another theme entirely.

                                    I wouldn't say it can't be worse, but would definitely take an extra effort to do so.

                                    The only splat I'd say that really manages to do inhuman evil outside the Evil Forces™ of Baali, Wyrm and Nefandi is Changeling. Of all lines, the Fae are genuinely build to facilitate a player that does utterly despicable shit for reasons a human would not have an analogous drive.

                                    All the others aren't just human evils in the sense of being literary metaphors, the games literally rely thematically on their failings being entirely human despite their negation of such. It's just that the choice of first person narrator, while immersive, makes it kinda obtuse as you get the in-character evasive justification rather than the direct explanation of the themes.

                                    So, in a nutshell:
                                    Vampire: the Masquerade has as main conflict selfish decisions justified by a Beast that's real, but has only a fraction of the strength they say.
                                    Werewolf: the Apocalypse has as main conflict a military leadership that's too proud of its role and prestige to accept they messed up and must admit their failings to move forward.
                                    Demon: the Fallen is about how war fucks up a soldier, literally. The whole problem is Torment, and behind all the poetic descriptions it's "just" regular PTSD. The main conflict is about how those powerful spirits are trying to cope with good old trauma, with all the regular responses to trauma, including misanthropy, reliving, negation, dissociation, and even their own way of seeking therapy.

                                    Mage and Changeling are harder to point one main conflict because they're more thematically open, so while all lines have other conflicts and perspectives to follow, those two are hard to fix any option as being the main one.

                                    Wraith, Mummy and Hunter I don't know enough to say much.
