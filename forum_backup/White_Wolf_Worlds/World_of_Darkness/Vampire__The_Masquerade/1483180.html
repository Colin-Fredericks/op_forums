          Well, it could be a similar situation to writing for a show or book series. You can't, or more accurately shouldn't, view fan made content. There is a legal reason, and a narrative reason for this BTW. Legally it's to help avoid copyright/trademark issues. We humans take information we have observed and naturally remix it when we create anything. This is an issue for writing as it can cause accidently problems with plagiarism and such, or at least the accusation of such things. As such, many writers for ongoing projects will willingly, or be obligated contractually, to avoid fan content so they can have some plausible deniability if a coincidence does happen.

          Alternatively, if writers see feedback or a theory that is almost 100% what you were planning to do, the writer's naturally reaction is to change it in order to maintain an element of twist in the story. Obviously, this often makes a story feel cheap, just look at recent Star Wars and the Game of Thrones.

                                          So, to paraphrase someone that I don't feel like citing because he can get attention from someone else:

                                          Fans - as a group - are great at noticing when something isn't right, but horrible at telling you what's wrong.

                                          That's why I think the OP was right to phrase it as "read." Yeah, Paradox should have someone that's reading stuff here. People don't talk about these things so much if they didn't care about them. If they care about them, they're a resources for increasing profits because we're cheap labor. Again, the whole point of the reference above, is not to just do what we say; the fans as a collective highlighting something that needs work doesn't mean you take sides in the debates on how to fix it based on forum arguments. You get your designers to focus on that topic and see what they can do; that's what you hired them to do. If you're even mediocre at iterative design, your game gets better in a fashion that increases sales.

                                          That doesn't mean you're going to make everyone a fan of the game in the end, just maximize from the pool of potential fans. "Not the change I would have made, but I'm glad they did something here," goes a long way with a lot of people that are on the fence about a product. You maybe shift a few people on the "still nope" side of the fence to the "maybe" fence sitters too, and then some future iteration gets them to buy.

                                          Or, to put it another way: you build goodwill with your fans by using the huge amount of free research into what areas of your game need adjustments to show that you are listening to the fans, and then finding solutions. They don't have to be groundbreaking. They don't have to even work all that well. Trying shows acknowledgement, and a willingness to expand the game (aka appeal to more people and get more sales!) rather than be overly dogmatic to one vision of what the game should be.

                                          One of the biggest corporate-level mistakes I think Paradox made when buying the WoD/etc. IPs from CCP, was letting the WoD fans in their ranks confuse brand loyalty and company goodwill. CCP kept a lot of goodwill from the oWW because they kept a lot of the current oWW people on staff during the buyout. Our favorite development teams weren't getting canned for new ones with the new company. We could trust that, at least for awhile, the products would be the same even with some hiccups in the transition. Yeah, this lead to some silly things like CCP hating the idea of a new "edition" of the CofD instead of just patching it like you would a video game. But pretty much right up until they cancelled the WoDMMO and then canned most of the oWW holdovers, the fans had a decent amount of goodwill towards CCP for letting the oWW games continue on as best as possible in a new economic reality.

                                          Paradox really boggled that process. They were in a goodwill deficit before they got a single RPG book out, because they leaned way too heavily on brand loyalty, and didn't consider that they had done nothing to establish goodwill. Ever since Paradox ending the nWW as its own subsidiary, it's clear they've been trying to reverse that. There's been a lot more attention to community outreach (even if not here), there's been some shifts that at least show that they recognize some of the early mistakes they made. But if they want to keep that trend going, they need to pay attention to what people are saying. Again, not to do what someone like me says is the best thing to do, but because a couple people like me harping on the same things over and over is a reason to say, "Do they have a point? If so, can we do something about it that doesn't make five other things worse?" etc.

                                          Even with D&D in your equations, the TTRPG industry is a tiny niche luxury market. There isn't enough money in it to do the kind of market research for future products like Samsung or Ford can afford to do. But TTRPG fans are also willing to talk endlessly about TTRPG in public spaces, meaning you don't need to do traditional market research: you just need a couple designers that can translate fan discourse into actionable design changes that lead to a better overall product.
