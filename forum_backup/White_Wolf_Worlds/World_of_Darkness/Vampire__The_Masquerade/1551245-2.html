            Personally, I take a bit of a dim view of the sci-fi/fantasy affectation of "this character is so elevated that they perceive all of these matters of identity as beneath them". All well and good when used in terms of including characters with diversity, but I think it rises too much from a milquetoast viewpoint of "these are matters in which I have no stakes, so I can afford to be distantly intellectual about it".

            I'm not making any assertions about the people who wrote those things specifically, just that it's how I view the attitude it tends to connect with. It's a few steps above outright bigotry, but I think still falls short of really getting it or having good ideas of how to portray it. And, particularly, not exactly great at providing a framework to recognise underlying biases.

            I hesitate a bit to use the term, but I think it's one of those instances where "neoliberal" as a pejorative is warranted; that form of later era liberalism with an unearned confidence that not being actively, overtly hateful and repressive means that the issue has been resolved.

                                Not even a hint. The same with the Dracula / Beckett and the Tremere / Goratrix shipping mentioned upthread. Onyx Path Vampire had a thing for adding gay relationships between established characters where, not only had there been any prior indication of the relationship, but where said relationships are actively inconsistent with how those characters have been represented in the past. A way of thinking about how Vampire has changed over time is that between the first edition and revised edition, relationships between characters were primarily conceived of in terms of power relationships; you would have the Kindred behaving in the manner of the characters of Succession, and, because of this, romance would almost always be corrupted by power. It was a remarkable representation in gaming at the time, and became one of the compelling reasons to engage with the game.

                                Onyx Path Vampire (and, to a lesser extend, Paradox Vampire) tends to see relationships as companionate and, in particular, romantic. The writers struggle with the notion of Mithras and Camden (or Tremere and Goratrix) making common cause in the political sphere, and building trust from that, so they become romantic partners. This is Vampire where the cast behave in the manner of the characters of Twilight. I feel that it comes across as a not terribly compelling and, indeed, fairly juvenile view of the world...
