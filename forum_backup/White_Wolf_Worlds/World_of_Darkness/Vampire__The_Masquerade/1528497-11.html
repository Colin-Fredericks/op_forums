                There's an important distinction which I think gets lost when most people see texts like the Canon Episcopi. Demons can't grant inherent magical abilities, but they can do things themselves that people aren't capable of. As an alleged example, one of the possible origins of the idea of witches riding broomsticks is that demons couldn't give someone the power to fly, but they could lift and move objects including those people were riding on. How accurate that is, I don't know, but some of the spell books of the time included spells to summon demons and make them do things.

                Part of why we know this is a phenomenon from the Middle Ages known as clerical necromancy and a lot of the spellbooks and other occult works of the time were written by priests and exorcists. Now in one sense that shouldn't be too surprising because priests and clerics made up a disproportionate percentage of the people who could read and write. But they also compiled books of occult knowledge using information they got from demons during exorcisms. Or sometimes they would summon the demons directly for the knowledge because they wanted to bypass the unfortunate element of someone being possessed. And sometimes they also wanted to skip the knowledge part and just summon a demon to do something directly.

                Now this was not an authorized Church practice. In fact, none of this was okay with the Church. These clerics were forgetting several important things, including that demons are lying bastards, are more intelligent and conniving than humans, and that working with demons is dangerous to the soul. This practice likely did a lot more spiritual damage than we will ever know. Nevertheless, the writers and readers of these books could often think that they were doing a good thing by invoking the power of Christ to learn from demons or even unleash their power on the world.

                    On the denial of magic (or the denial the devil could grant magic), one of the fun things is reading how many of the church's authors would tie themselves into knots in order to try and explain things. Like, the Old Testament is filled with stories of Israel's enemies and rivals having sorcerers that can cast magic, and that they do so without drawing on God's power. For example, one of the most famous examples is in Exodus where Moses calls of God to turn his staff into a snake. Then the Pharoh summons his sorcerers and they use magic to turn their staves into snakes. But since Moses god was stronger than Egypt's gods, Moses snake eats the others.

                    So this necessitated the argument that God was actually the one that supplied Pharoh's sorcerers or the sorcerers of other groups with magic, and God was doing this as a way of testing the faith of the Israelites. Sometimes God would grant miracles to Israel's enemies to punish the Israelities, but then he'd turn around and grant even better miracles to the few Israelities who remained faithful since the whole goal was to test their face. So people who refused to accept the existence of the Christian God could cast magic, it's just that God was the one granting them that power as a way of testing the people who were actually faithful to him.

                                That's not what happens in 1 Kings and is a later development.

                                Elijah doesn't seek to prove other gods aren't really. He seeks to prove Ba'al weren't real. And he did so (or rather, this part of 1 Kings was written do claim so) for a specific reason: at first YHWH was a storm god, but Ba'al was the storm god worshipped in the area at the time. That was not about a broader challenge, it was a dispute for the portfolio of sky and storms.

                                1 Kings is among the most recent books to be written down, mid to late 6th century BCE, and even then it's not monotheistic unless you brute force the interpretation that a dispute against Ba'al is somehow against the concept of godhood.

                                It's by this time that IIRC Josiah initiates the reforms that establish monolatry, which is not the same thing. The bible has books written as long ago as the 14th century BCE, 8 centuries before Josiah, and through all this time they were polytheistic even if El, and later YHWH, was the national god, and the Hebrews were there before, as all that was first oral tradition.

                                So we're talking at least about a thousand years of polytheism before monolatry is established and the worship of other gods forbidden. It would take at least 400 more years, possibly 600, before this monolatry got changed into actual monotheism, or something that resembles it.

                                We have no sign of monotheism in the whole OT when we look at the old texts and the context of the time, only things that can be misinterpreted when looked at by people from completely different places and eras. And keep in mind that neither is the bible presented in chronological order, nor were the stories written like that. As an example, Deuteronomy seem to take place by the 14th century BCE, has at least some passages that probably were oral tradition by then or a few centuries later, but only got written a few decades before 1 Kings.

                                                            Originally posted by Tjalei View Post
                                                            How many races are there? Elves, Man, Dwarfs, Ents, Dragons, Orcs & Goblins, Trolls, Intelligent animals, missed anything?
                                                            Besides Hobbits?

                                                            Spirits, Giants, Huorns and unique beings, though Tolkien gave very little information on those.

                                                            Giants are cited briefly in The Hobbit when the group is trying to cross the Misty Mountains, before the Orcs take them. They hear in the distance the collisions of the rocks Giants throw at each other. Current editions of the book were slightly modified to fit the LotR, so this part remaining means it is as canon as we can say there's one. It's also during the day IIRC, so they can't be Trolls.

                                                            Huorns are a very specific ambiguity. Ents look like trees and were created by the Vala of trees, but they're very specifically are not trees, but treelike Free People. Huorns are either what happen when an old tree becomes animated or an Ent becomes too static, partly an equivalent to intelligent animals, but tinged by Tolkien's love for trees. The Old Willow-Man is probably a Huorn, and in the book the Huorns are the true saviors in the Battle of Helm's Deep. Weird, scary saviors everyone is thankful for and no one is feeling like going say hi.

                                                            Spirits of many types are cited, but they're also given little to no explanation. Goldberry is a river spirit, the Barrow-Wights are evil spirits possessing corpses, not their original owners. Ghosts. Through all books we actually see a lot of mentions of spirits of things that can be spirits, but they never get much of many explanation and no one ever told anything about where spirits come from, which is itself strange for Tolkien and his many treatises on absolutely everything even when it is to conclude we can't know.

                                                            Unique beings generally fall in the same overall category, but add an important distinction: the mystery is the point. Most races have known history, if not their origin, then at least when and in which circumstances they appeared and theories in what they may be. Spirits are mostly just stated as such or something to that effect and it seems to be explanation enough. But Tom Bombadil, or the strange statues in Rohan are specifically depicted as something that do begs for an explanation, not just unexplained to the reader but active holes in how even the most wise understand the world. Something about them is a contradiction, a challenge to how things are phenomenologically supposed to be.

                                                            You can count some variations, too, besides just ethnicities. Among humans we have the Drúadan and Númenorians. The first were just between the size of a Hobbit and a Dwarf and were the very first human tribe the elves found in Beleriand. Their eyes glowed red sometimes. Númenorians are technically humans but are actually elf-touched and blessed. Aragorn is one of them and he's more than 70 when Frodo meets him, dying with more than 200 years, more than a fucking century after the end of the story.

                                                            Entwives are another mystery for the reader. If on one hand they seem obvious, they seem to have some fundamental distinctions in their nature besides gender

                                                            Saruman made the Uruk-Hai and the Half Orcs. The first bigger, meaner, braver and actually more militaristic for real than Orcs. The second capable of passing for ugly humans despite being orcs.

                                                            Finally the Olog-Hai, the new breed of Troll that Sauron created but we get very little about them, contrary to quite the thorough depiction we get from Saruman's creations.

                                                            Honorable mention for Istarii and Balrogs. They may be Maiar, but did form each a semi-race of their own even if limited in number.

                                                            Note: by the mid 20th century of Middle Earth it is confirmed that Elves, Dwarves and Hobbits are still around, while Dragons, Orcs and Trolls are confirmed extinct. Many fans know about the importance of the Appendix in LotR, but no one tells you about the preface. It changes your understanding of the book drastically if you pay attention.
