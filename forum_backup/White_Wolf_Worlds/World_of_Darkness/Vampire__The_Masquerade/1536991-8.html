        Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post
        He's always been useful in my games as a Worf figure.
        Basically the only use I can find for him.

        I’m unconvinced that he was even needed for the Schism, the tension between the two factions could have been solved another way with the same effect. And even granting that point, the possibility of his coming awakening was far more relevant for the Schism than the actual happenstance.

        The Web of Knives is by itself unnecessary baggage, too. Not only does it fills a niche that’s kinda cluttered already, Ur-Shulgi removed the only thing that made them unique. All the Web did was keep the problematic aspects of the clan still existing.

        My opinion persist that the best way to show the power of the Second Inquisition in V5 would have been to bombard the Allamut instead of the Vienna Chantry. Cleaner, more sensible, it would greatly impress the vampires all the same, and we wouldn’t lose anything of real value to the game.

        Originally posted by Theodrim View Post

        The same could be said for any methuselah, frankly. Methuselahs exist on a level never really intended for players to interact with over the course of normal chronicles
        I disagree, if not greatly.

        Methuselahs are characters. However you see them as humane or not, they can fill a lot of narrative niches.

        Decently written methuselah can fill niches that don’t relate to direct confrontation. They may have plans and minions that carry some sort of narrative signature, they may be shadows looming in the distance that have some color and personality to them, they may even once in a while interact with the characters.

        Lair of the Hidden is a prime example, if not the most popular scenario around, of how you can have a whole story with the PCs interacting with Methuselahs without any need for them to ever try to do harm to those ancient vampires.

        Ur-Shulgi’s greatest crime isn’t that he is too powerful. It’s that he is too boring. The guy is a freaking Villain Sue with less personality than a spoon. He comes to change the status-quo with his pinky and then just sits there being invincible.

        And it is important to make it clear: Methuselahs aren’t invincible. While they can’t be beaten by the PCs, the narrative is all about how they constantly maneuver against each other, So there are theoretical limits to what they can and will do. Ur-Shulgi simply doesn’t care.

                                Ur-Shulgi has to be vague and unseen to be effective, I think.

                                In V20, I think he was merely "stirring", rather than actually awake. That wasn't a bad idea. Once he's awake, that's when it becomes a huge problem—because, realistically, he would act, and the result would be devastating.

                                Personally, I don't think you have to kill him, but you do have to play up the doubt if you don't. He's allegedly awake, but isn't doing anything, so there has to be a plausible reason for why that is.

                                Is he in a fugue state and unable to act? Is he wandering the Earth to find his lost sire? Is he trapped between worlds? Did he sacrifice himself to remove the Tremere Curse? Or maybe he used Daimonion 9 and is now just some cosmic horror with alien drives and plans?

                                Or maybe he's just a ruse by elders with high-level Obfuscate? Or maybe it's not really Ur-Shulgi at all, but one of the Aralu/Children? Or maybe he just woke up, did a Ravnos, and got blasted back to whatever Hell he crawled out of? (Cue the Week of Nightmares, Mk. II, but targeting the Web of Knives. Probably an unpopular thing to do setting-wide, but it would be fun for individual tables.)

                                What if Alamut was actually built on the original Well of Sacrifice? Maybe that's why the Schismatics have left. They know something awful is forming down there, from whatever remained of Ur-Shulgi's soul.

                                Who really threw off the Tremere Curse? Or could the clan always have opted out (like the antitribu did)? Maybe it was actually just Tremere losing his body that ended the curse? In which case, Ur-Shulgi might never have been involved at all.

                                If the Aapilu are a thing now, maybe all that congealed vitae the Banu Haqim have been gathering at Alamut has just gained sentience and become a nightmare spirit of blood? Heck, it could even be Ur-Shulgi (or an echo of him), albeit without a body. Maybe that's why he can't act yet? But people can imbibe portions of him to perform incredible magic—such as breaking the Tremere Curse.

                                But nearly all of these ideas hinge on Ur-Shulgi being a concept and not really an active entity that poses a wider threat.

                                    Really good analysis there adambeyoncelowe, regarding Methuselahs as story hooks rather than immovable obstacles. Not for nothing, my favorite Methuselah is Sybil and we can count the facts we "know" about her one one hand. She's (Lasombra)'s only direct female childe, she's Marcus Vitel's sire, she's manipulated the North American Tremere for centuries, and she wants to use the United States nuclear arsenal to nuke the Antediluvian's.

                                    This kinda parallels a conversation I'm having on reddit about how the "Content treadmill" of White Wolf (and the RPG industry in general) during the 90's seems to suffer from the same problems as late-stage MCU. Each new product is mostly concerned with (a) dropping easter-eggs for previous/upcoming projects or (b) setting up the next "episode" (weather it be a movie or a streaming show) to the detriment of the plot of the movie or show you are currently watching.

                                    The reveal of Ur-Shulgi in Children of the Night suffers from the same issues. His entire entry is an easter-egg hunt or an additional puzzle piece of the Salout/Baali/Haquim clusterfuck. Its written in an impenetrable and mythological style. Which, hey, you get points for adhering to a theme and mood. And I appreciate the compliment to my intelligence that I'm supposed to infer all these great stories that could be told using this character but a bit of plain text like @adambeyoncelow laid out above about how you could use not only his emergence but RUMORS of his emergence to motivate actions in your story.

                                    Since this tread is already massively off topic I may start another thread asking folks to do just that with their favorite Methuselahs. Weirdly, for one who's opinions on the V5 plot are rarely positive, I think that V5 has managed to do this right at least once with the Master of Ravens. No one is quite sure who he is or even what clan he is from. His powers and abilities are not nearly as important as the uproar he causes and the affect he has on younger kindred social movements. The fact the Inner Circle was vetoed from making him Anathema is a great plot hook as long as we don't know who issued the veto.

                                    And, dang, the more I think of Children of the Night the more I see the MCU parallels. CotN gives us some mysterious and evocative Inconnu members, the various Monitors. Seemed like we were finally getting some breadcumbs about the most secretive of Sects. What we finally get in Lair of the Hidden is a letdown on par with what Secret Invasion accomplished with the fantastic set up it had been given. Jalan/Karsh is just another easter egg hunt. But I digress...

                                                Thanks for your kind words! It's a tricky line to walk, for sure. The WoD benefits from the fact that it isn't a novel or movie, so dangling loads of mysteries and setting up things to come is perfectly fine — but tables need options, not railroads.

                                                When laying out lots of potentially conflicting information, it's better to treat the metaplot as a guideline rather than the law. But also, when you keep stuff vague, don't backtrack and give it a definite answer later on unless you're prepared for how that will upset the games people have already been playing.

                                                I think this is something V20 actually does really well. We get all the usual IC "unreliable narrator" stuff, followed by chunks of OOC lore, and then a bunch of bullet points for how this could be explored in a chronicle. Being metaplot agnostic is actually the perfect fit for this type of game, because the point isn't to lay down canon but to provoke creativity.

                                                People actually end up engaging with more of the metaplot if they have more leeway to make it fit their stories (and there's less risk their games will be invalidated by future supplements).

                                                The problem comes in, say, Revised or V5 when you say or imply there's a particular story unfolding and that you have a particular set of answers to the great mysteries of the setting which may or may not be revealed. How can you plan ahead in that context if you want to use the metaplot? You can't.

                                                So it becomes a story you passively consume and you maybe sprinkle a few bits into your story, but it's mostly something that doesn't affect your tables at all. Then you either stop buying the books, or you buy them and feel alienated from the same setting which previously drew you on.

                                                Think of the Beckoning. It felt like ParaWolf wanted it to be a big mystery, as it's a big part of the setting, but it was unclear if it would ever be answered. So people mostly ignored it, because otherwise, you risk establishing things as facts that would be later contradicted by the books. (And also, dangling a mystery without much development is just kinda boring. Those few suggestions in the Gehenna War supplement should have been there in the CRB's Storytelling chapter.)

                                                If you have hard points (things that have to happen) and soft points (things that might happen but don't have to or could happen in a different way) in the metaplot, you should signal which is which. Soft points are generally going to be better than hard points, because every hard point is going to risk alienating people or invalidating parts of the setting. When you explain whether they're firm changes or optional suggestions, you should also give some ideas about how they may affect individual games and some options for how to make it fit.
