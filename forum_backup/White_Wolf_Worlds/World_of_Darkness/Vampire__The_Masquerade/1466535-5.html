    Recapitulating:
    Originally posted by monteparnas View Post
    The following clans are deeply affected by changes in outlook from the authors. A.K.A. retconing racism out. They all start off as heavily racist and simplistic stereotypes of entire religions.

    So, for them, you have actually two versions that seem the same in the surface, but work differently, and applying the same argument to both isn't constructive. For all of them it is also extremely recommended that you take the retconned version.

    Although no one will stop you from using the first version on your game, the first version is extremely racist, must be acknowledged as such and is unlikely to be well received in the forum as acceptable canon.

    None of those retcons happened at once, and many started early in the WoD publication history but only fully developed at Revised, so it is a process.
    Followers of Set

    One interesting aspect of the FoS is how subtle the transition went. That is a strikingly good work at storytelling, but had the downside of being hard to notice the transition and getting the new form without a lot of attention.

    What made this possible was their choice at new core religion for the Setites: Gnosticism, a dualism philosophy that teaches the separation between a Real world of Pure Knowledge and a False world of Fictional Matter. For this philosophy the goal of humans is Gnosis (Enlightenment), but Gnosis can be obtained only through personal experience. The goal is freedom from the material world and its bindings, and ascendance into the sphere of God.

    For Gnosticism strict doctrines are traps for the soul, and gods that teach a ritualized life are jailers for their followers. It doesn't mean that the teachings are wrong by themselves, but that following them out of blind obligation keep you from achieving spiritual knowledge over the matter. Some gods, though, offer guidance to achieve Gnosis instead of seeking blind obedience.

    This theological construct is at the same time flexible in form but distinct in its implications. Conflicting myths, for example, aren't a problem, as no doctrine is far from the truth if it leads you to seek personal Gnosis instead of claiming to be Gnosis in itself. But any teaching that deviates you from seeking truth on your own is heretical.

    So the actual Setite religion has nothing to do with the trappings of Egyptian Mythology, no more than it have with any other religion. Yet at the same time they have no reason to abandon it, either. They may accept freely variations that use other trappings, other names for entities, and even discuss openly if the gods act out of malice or ignorance. At the same time, they combat heresies whose teachings seem like almost the same to outsiders, but just because they have the same trappings, but not the principles.

    Practically speaking, what does that means?

    1 - Followers of Set aim for enlightenment and ascension. Some only for themselves, some for the clan, others for everyone;

    2 - Corruption is a looser term than it seems. They differ on what they mean by it and on what they do with it;

    3 - Set is seen as a teacher more than a lord. Although he is important to them all, nothing else about him means the same for every Setite;

    4 - There's a difference between institutions with clear rules and purposes, and institutions you follow just out of tradition. This isn't lost on all of them;

    5 - Most of them see indulgence as a means to free yourself from desire. Other paths may exist and the goal is asceticism, not vice in itself;

    6 - Roads and Paths are just sample guides to Gnosis. A faithful Setite may follow almost any Path, even Humanity;

    7 - Denouncing tradition, institutions, religion and morals isn't the same as saying you should be an evil bastard. Some Setites think so, others don't;

    8 - You can iterate those teachings in infinite ways and add whatever trappings you want and the end result will still be an "orthodox" Setite;

    9 - Not all of them are orthodox, though. The clan's disposition towards an actual heresy depends a lot on context, going from indifference to war.

                                            Originally posted by Elphilm View Post
                                            It seems to me that your primary objections fall under the umbrella of "disagreeing with the original concept and scope of the Assamites," which is fair enough. As a consequence, if you hate the original Assamite concept, or the original Ravnos concept, or whatever, you are without a doubt going to hate the first clanbooks for them as well.

                                            In later years I've come to terms with the way the early writers approached 'clan' as a concept. Although the 13 largest groups of vampires in the setting were referred to using the same term, they were not designed to be used equally by players and GMs alike. That is to say, 1st and 2nd Edition Vampire: The Masquerade did not assume that players were as equally likely to play an Assamite as they were, say, a Brujah. There was a fairly sharp distinction between the original seven clans, which expressed very broad and instantly recognizable archetypes, and all the additional groups that did not fit established vampire society. The independents (and later the Sabbat) were deliberately designed to be much more narrow in scope.

                                            I would rather leave aside the cultural and religious issues of the original Assamite depiction. Talking about the matter is nearly always an endless swamp of minute disagreements and accusations of bigotry. In general, anonymous users disparaging the work of authors who have since moved on from the franchise and thus have little chance of responding to the points raised tends to leave a bad taste in my mouth.
                                            Although I understand your grievance against talking about someone's work without that person being able to defend themselves, being too cautious about not judging their writing ability just hinder our ability to discuss their work meaningfully.

                                            I'll say what leaves a bad taste in my mouth:

                                            1 - Stating that a new work isn't responsible for furthering the problems of previous work just because "wasn't intended to change original concept". If the original concept have a problem, it must be addressed as soon as possible, not deepened;

                                            2 - Assuming that going for a stricter concept justifies stereotyping things that do have real-world equivalents. The 4 Independents didn't needed to be better developed just because too strict a concept is a bad choice, but because they have clearer RL counterparts than any other clan. The 2 Sabbat ones too, but to a lesser extent;

                                            3 - Leaving aside matters of depiction that affect RL people when defending a work, dismissing it as not worth the amount of nuance it brings up, given that it brings nuance precisely because it affects real people. The whole phrase "endless swamp of minute disagreements and accusations of bigotry", despite not being necessarily wrong on some conversations, push no less than 3 red buttons for me.

                                            4 - Putting the well being of the author above the well being of the people directly or indirectly depicted. That leaves a terrible taste in my mouth.

                                            You are right that talking low of other's work isn't always fair. But bringing up the author's credentials isn't doing it favors. For what matters, it was the early 90's and it was one of the worse periods for design theory on RPGs ever, so I do accept the idea that doing such limited scope for the new clans probably seemed like a good idea, even for the best authors of the time.
