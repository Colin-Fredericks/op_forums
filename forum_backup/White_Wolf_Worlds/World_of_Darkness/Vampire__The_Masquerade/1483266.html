    Thoughts on Plot Device

    06-06-2022, 09:41 PM
    So, I was rereading Nights of Prohechy and came again upon Vasilisa and what was in my opinion a not very well-developed story conclusion (I remember years ago I was very mad because of this mediocre end they gave to " Old Granny", who certainly deserved better, but then I just moved on lol), but that's not the point of the thread. I remember in the old days when players have complained about this idea of NPC that have what they'd call a basically "You lose button" and they naturally didn't like it.

    By the way, this happens in many stories in Vampire, to cite one example, we have it happening in Transylvania Chronicles as well, where if the character mess with Etrius or Goratrix, they just lose, period and in the perhaps more justifiable case, with Tremere, the text just says something like "if they mess with Tremere they're basically going to explode or something", completed with the statement "Don't mess with gods or whatever". While I feel in Tremere's case it's somehow justifiable, what I want to bring to the table is the general vision of this "mechanic" or lack thereof. How do you feel about using Plot Devices to determine the outcome of conflicts involving PCs in your stories? Don't you think it would be better to have a basic idea of such characters traits, etc? I mean it always sounds a bit strange, especially to players that came from other games, like D&D, where even gods are given stats etc. Wouldn't it be easier to give these characters traits, than simply stating "You explode, you burn, you can't move, or whatever"?

    This question is not only for the purpose of discussing perspectives on this given device, but also because when the books don't give us traits for characters, (what happens VERY often in vampire books), it gives a lot of work and headache to the ST as well. I mean, when you create a story centered around a city or region etc, we as ST have to create most things from scratch and this is a hell of a job. I have many friends who gave up STing for this very reason, one friend of mine who is a GM/ST for more than 40 years (naturally among different RPGs) said once to me, when I was about 14 or so, that he gave up ST vampire because it's too much work, you have to keep track of way too many things and D&D is much easier because the books "spoon-feeds you with basically everything".

    So, how do you feel about these issues?

      I think it was a deliberate attempt to basically try to discourage the idea of pigeon-holing characters into stats and making it more and more storytelling and narrative based. Etrius being statless is meant to be a sign that he's something FAR beyond the character's levels and they should not be trying to figure out a way to kill him.

The thing is that a well-made character sheet is not going to be beaten by a simple plan, I mean if the character in question is supposed to be powerful in the first place, as is the case with Etrius, Vasilisa or Goratrix, even though the latter had his stats thankfully finally published for V20 Dark Ages France.

          I find it easier to avoid important NPCs destruction by making good character's sheets that are not easily "exploitable", besides the natural benefit of making the characters feel more complete and thus "feel more real", so to speak, what is in turn better for the overall narrative. Also, we have the benefit of giving the PC's the feeling that the NPC's are "not cheating the system". That, not to talk about the other issue I pointed out here, that is the hard work it is to give traits to so many characters in some stories when the book don't provide us with those.
          All in all, I feel that the benefits far outweighs the disadvantages here.

                      I think this is more of an issue for "historical" games and "metaplot" adventures because those books NEED to have certain developments happen. Can't risk Etrius being killed in 1198 because he's supposed to be alive when the game timeline begins in 1991. Can't have Baba Yaga survive because the metaplot requires her to be eaten. Can't have this NPC be defeated because this railroad adventure needs him to survive until the end to show how clever he is. So they stack the deck to make sure their predestined end matches what is supposed to be canon.

                      The Revised era was really, really bad with having these kind of chronicles or sourcebooks. (Although honestly, many WoD adventures - as opposed to City/Rage/whatever books - had this problem, as did many nineties era RPGs.)

                      Well written adventures don't have one single destined resolution. They empower the ST to roll with whatever the PCs do and provide the ST the tools to handle their decisions and actions.

                      Originally posted by Herr Meister View Post
                      we as ST have to create most things from scratch and this is a hell of a job.
                      Yep. The entire point of buying a premade adventrue or setting is to reduce the amount of work a GM needs to do. But there's a lot of poorly made sourcebooks that leave too many things for the STs to determine on their own. This is why I hate sourcebooks made on metaplot. I would rather have toolkits that provide me with things I can actually use in game rather than being told I'm "doing it wrong" by running chronicles that end up running counter to the metaplot's predestined ends.
