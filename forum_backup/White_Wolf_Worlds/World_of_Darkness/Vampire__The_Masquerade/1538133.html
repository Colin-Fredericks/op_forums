                              The first edition of Vampire: the Dark Ages remains the most intellectually and aesthetically cohesive. Reading the book there is a lot of material which is more or less a cut and paste from the second edition of Vampire: the Masquerade. Nevertheless, it still presents a very strong view of the world of Cainites prior to the Anarch Revolt, and a medieval world in change with the rise of the Tremere, and with conflicts in Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean. It's worthwhile noting that some features of the book, such as including level six Disciplines, Merits and Flaws in the main rulebook, or alternate moralities which are viable for a conventional group of characters, were a departure from established practice at the time, despite how normal they feel now.

                              The story goes that Mark Rein-Hagen wanted a vampiric Dungeons & Dragons, whilst Jennifer Hartshorn, the developer, wanted a more historically accurate game. Hartshorn won out (though some of the prospective material on vampiric roses and fae cattle did appear in The Book of Storyteller's Secrets). I personally think that Hartshorn's focus on gothic horror (she had been the first, and best, developer of Wraith: the Oblivion), and the subsequent development work on the game's supplement's over the following couple of years made the game a success, and is the reason why we are still talking about it now.

                              Dark Ages: Vampire is far weaker in theme and aesthetics. One of the aims of Philippe Boule in this edition was clearly to push the game away from its routes in second edition Vampire: the Masquerade. The results are, I would argue, somewhat mixed. There are some clear successes, such as the re-imagining of Mortis, or the updating of the Road system into something more detailed and coherent. But the notion of High Clans and Low Clans doesn't really work, nor does the War of Princes (as presented clearly and painfully in Under the Black Cross, the worst chronicle without exception published by White Wolf). There are mechanical interactions which clearly have not been thoroughly considered. For instance, with weapons and combat there was no consideration paid to the addition of overage and division between bashing and lethal damage. The index is terrible (core rules are not referenced, but illustrations of signature characters are), and I am still resentful of the plagiarised map on the opening endpapers.

                              The twentieth anniversary edition of Vampire: the Dark Ages fixed some things, and broke others. Overall, this presents the most functional and robust set of rules, a fairly mixed bag in terms of art (having it all in colour is a bit unforgiving in places), and an indicator that editing and development are important; from page to page you can go from well-written descriptions of the setting and rules, to paragraphs which are barely coherent. Clearly, the underlying quality is solely down to the strength of the original authors, which shows through in the final product. And there are some bizarre design choices - did anyone want the Ahrimanes, or the Niktuku as bloodlines?

                              The chapter on Roads is a complete mess, and the weapons table only partly fixed, but then you have some very good guidance for Storytellers. And the book has a chapter on the medieval world which focuses on Italy and Eastern Europe, has multiple factual errors on most pages, and seems to think that places like Hungary are a mystery to players of the game.

                              If only there was a good edition, to combine the strengths of the various editions, and to get rid of at least most of the obvious flaws.

                                              Originally posted by James_Willoughby View Post
                                              The first edition of Vampire: the Dark Ages remains the most intellectually and aesthetically cohesive.
                                              Excellent post which summarizes much of what I believe. My only real complaint about the original VDA is I would have liked a more detailed comparison between how things are done in the modern Camarilla and how things were done pre-Camarilla. There are huge differences. While the books did cover this to some degree in the differing Traditions, in practice I've seen most STs run Dark Ages domains almost the same as they do to modern Princes, and that should not be the case. Both the Anarch Revolt and the establishment of the Camarilla should be seen as revolutionary in terms of how vampires interacted with each other, and unfortunately we didn't get that.

                                              I also would have liked a bit more fidelity to how earlier editions portrayed vampire society at this time (more lone vampires, more rural based) rather than the big city based chronicles of the modern age. The kind of anonymity modern cities provide with their large geography and millions of people is vastly different than a small neighborhood with some 10,000 people. More thought should have been put into that and how to still make a good game from that setting. The types of city settings most people like and play in are still doable in some cities of the era, but mostly not found in Europe but elsewhere.

                                              But in terms of aesthetics and feel of a medieval Europe beset by vampires, VDA really nailed it.

                                              Originally posted by James_Willoughby View Post
                                              There are some clear successes, such as the re-imagining of Mortis, or the updating of the Road system into something more detailed and coherent. But the notion of High Clans and Low Clans doesn't really work, nor does the War of Princes (as presented clearly and painfully in Under the Black Cross, the worst chronicle without exception published by White Wolf).
                                              I don't think those innovations worked out well. The distinction between the High Clans and Low Clans don't make sense for various reasons given the canonical history, and feels like an attempt to introduce the conflicts of the Anarchs into the setting at a time when that couldn't be a thing, and the War of Princes feels like an attempt to introduce sect wars at a time when there were no sects. In other words, just a tired rehash of the Revised era setting instead of dealing with the themes of a society blithely nearing the edge of ruin and revolution.
