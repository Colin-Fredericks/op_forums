Even Baba Yaga herself would not allow that. But since she is not around anymore...? Bad News

Always clever, Gregornous laid low, hiding in the wilderness to figure out what was going on in the world since he fell asleep. During this time he secretly watched over Baba Yaga's resting place until she finally rose. After the Hag is gone? Safe to say he want REVENGE!

                                Originally posted by Herr Meister View Post
                                One of my main gripes with some people who criticize Haight (beyond the fact that I love to play the "Devil's Advocate" lol) is that some of the criticism he got from some people I knew, imply that a "recurring villain" is somehow a bad thing for the setting and I'm of the complete opposite idea.
                                You are completely right, and that should be obvious. But this is the thing with such situations, characters like Haight grate on our skin and then we start trying to explain why they're so awful and it's easy to inadvertently blame aspects that are obvious, but not the actual reason.

                                It is a lot like the whole "Mary Sue" concept: people try to build lists of Mary Sue traits and diagnosis of Mary Sue characters, but actually the problem is just that the story is badly written and the character comes out as unconvincing. Likewise, Haight has a lot of traits attached to him that became synonymous with bad character, but they're not actually the cause of him being bad, we just try to avoid another Haight.

                                Haight is a bad character for three reasons that aren't visible traits of him. Actually, they're not characteristics of the NPC whatsoever:

                                1 - Things happen around him with cheap storytelling. The problem isn't that they happen just to give him power or something, but that the writer makes a bad, lazy job at trying to convince the reader that they're organic happenstances;

                                2 - His presentation is completely geared for the ST, not the players, which results in adventures that make him seemingly pull stuff out of nowhere. The players don't know about his plans, about his powers, about his contacts, they get no hints to any of those before or after the facts, so they only see Haight coming out of nowhere, make some impossible shit up, and leave;

                                3 - Worse of all, all of his pre-W20 appearances are railroad adventures where his fate has little to no possible input from the players. Every single one of them has a scripted ending accompanied by a huge amount of instructions for the ST to sabotage or outright impede any and every PC action that may result in things not going by the script, and to not stop Haight even after because he'll appear again in an official book.

                                Similarly, Ur-Shulgi isn't a bad character because he can't be written well. He's a bad character because he's writing in such a way that he pulls shit out of nowhere, destroy the setting by waving his pinkie and shows less personality than a chair. And all of those can be solved. But solving then won't change the impact of the failings in his initial depiction. See that Lore of the Clans already goes a long way improving him with very little effort, but unfortunately has to do so by retcon. Curiously, not by retconing the character itself as far as anyone can tell, but retconing the bad story itself instead.

                                Originally posted by Father Enoch View Post
                                Sam Haight is just one guy
                                I loved your ideas for Pentex as a crossover villain, but I disagree that Haight couldn't do the job.

                                Haight could have done it, even doing everything he did in the other adventures, if the above problems were addressed. #1 is the hard part and would require serious rewriting, so lets leave it alone and just assume better writing for his shenanigans. #2 being addressed means players would be given more info and agency during the adventures, and be able to engage with Haight's story and actions even if not always with the man himself. And #3 would mean that interacting with him have actual stakes on the setting.

                                A major aspect of dealing with a recurring villain in an RPG is that you must actually do his job and prepare ahead of time for the plans the players may come up with, so his escape doesn't feel forced, and give the players something for their effort, so even if the villain escapes his next appearances show the lasting impacts of the previous ones. But above all, you must be prepared for an untimely demise.

                                Now, just imagine if every adventure after the Valkenburg simply had a side box telling you how Haight's exploits cause ripple effects or inspire people, in such a way that if Haight has died before, you can exchange him here with his legacy. Followers, copycats, offended parties trying to recoup their losses or going insane with the repercussions of his actions. You could have all the same stories being told with different characters whose actions are a direct result of the life of Samuel Haight, or just with the man as long as he actually manages to live.

                                Originally posted by AnubisXy View Post
                                To Grandmother's House really should have been a Werewolf adventure and involved the PC's helping to lead the shapeshifters and other supernatural creatures of Russia in murdering the hell out of her and her Zmei.
                                Not necessarily.

                                The bigger problem with her ending is less that it was a Worf Effect for a thing that wouldn't even stay around to impact the setting in any way (although this is a huge sore point), but that the module itself unceremoniously makes the PCs into passive witnesses to the story of Baba Yaga and "Vasilisa". Few if any module depowered the PCs so thoroughly, and that has nothing to do with splat.

                                I think her demise should be a crossover book, because she touched every splat. It should be something anyone can witness or even cause. And instead of making the PCs lead the charge that kills her, the story itself should shift focus to her forces, to the many things her presence and actions cause in the land and why that's everyone's problem. And let the PCs have lasting effects on the setting by their actions, not because they killed her (or failed), but because their actions will shape the aftermath of her fall, the power balance and the size of the leftover threat.

                                With this, getting hints of the Nictuku and maybe having a hand in its finding and murdering the Hag would be fine. The scene of her death would be a symbol of the changes they brought instead of the actual purpose of the story.

                                                I agree with Asmodai here, while the full end of time metaplot package was a bad idea, Revised was also the source of a lot of good material.

                                                Personally I think of it as a new stage of growth for both the setting and its communities of fans and developers. Quality of writing and mechanics largely improved, despite some serious hiccups. Not perfect, but a far better and more stable average than the wild days of 2nd edition, specially early 2nd. A lot of improvements were made, a lot of corrections that worked more often than not, a lot of excellent supplements and some very good modules.

                                                Revised is as much To Grandmother's House and Lair of the Hidden, the culmination of the changes brought by late 2nd Edition, with the writers dismissing crossover and "gonzo" concepts as they learned to focus and think through their stories. The game we have today owns too much to Revised for the whole edition to be dismissed, even if you want to dismiss its metaplot's development towards the end.

                                                End, by the way, that was also a part of this. It's easy to look back with current eyes and see how to fix the game of them for the tastes of now, but the reality at the time was that White Wolf had in their hands a game that was broken, and ever more obviously so. And the fact that it can be fixed now doesn't change that they didn't thought so back then. They experimented with mechanics in other games and learned what was wrong with the setting, but for them the construct of WoD was too big to be fixed from within. Not that anyone has to agree with their conclusion (I don't, even for the period) or like what the metaplot did in Revised, but I think it's always important to keep things in perspective by considering their reasons, and that destroying the setting was the plan since 1998.
