    Kurdish "freedom fighters" in Ashirra

    04-09-2020, 10:49 AM
    This passage is from Camarilla sourcebook v5 (I had a look at the earlier version with Chechnya part, so if this part is edited please let me know!):

    "thinking the ashirra is a single thing, a single culture, is a prejudiced idea that will get you killed in the courts of the future. consider this: among our new allies we find saudi princes dining on the blood of women stoned to death for infidelity, a liberal ıt-millionnaire from jordan, turkish nationalists dreaming of the ottoman empire, kurdish freedom fighters and persian nobles longing for the glorious days of disco in 1970’s teheran. they are all ashirra. they all believe in allah’s pillars. yet none of them think and act in the same way."

    To my knowledge, freedom fighter is a relativistic term and usually, it is said if a person is sympathetic and supportive towards the goals of the so-called freedom fighter. While all other examples are more or less neutral (like Persian noble, Ottoman wannabe Turkish nationalist, and Saudi princes that drink stoned women’s blood), why a Brujah (Albertzen) who chose to remain inside Camarilla, would be picking sides in a mortal conflict by calling them freedom fighters? You know how the saying goes: Someone’s terrorist is another’s freedom fighter (in this case the other way around).

    Kurdish freedom fighters who are referred here, if you consider the timing of the release of the book and the emphasis on Syrian Civil War in some other parts, are the YPG that     shares an intellectual lineage and certain overlaps in memberships and militant recruitments with the PKK, which is designated as a terrorist organization by EU and USA (if you have any other ideas that who might be those 'freedom fighters' please share your thoughts!). I mean, of course, kindred manipulate and take sides on kine conflicts to their own ends and goals but I just can't imagine a supposedly neutral Cam brujah would sympathize with them. I don't think anybody in WW would intentionally promote violence but honestly, it just seems like some shitty propaganda between the lines. What do you think? Personally I really hate words like "freedom fighter" that, in a way, glorifies the violence.

                                                  Originally posted by malkav19 View Post


                                                  "thinking the ashirra is a single thing, a single culture, is a prejudiced idea that will get you killed in the courts of the future. consider this: among our new allies we find saudi princes dining on the blood of women stoned to death for infidelity, a liberal ıt-millionnaire from jordan, turkish nationalists dreaming of the ottoman empire, kurdish freedom fighters and persian nobles longing for the glorious days of disco in 1970’s teheran. they are all ashirra. they all believe in allah’s pillars. yet none of them think and act in the same way."
                                                  I have no problem with an in-world character having a specific worldview which bleeds through into their comments on in-world topics. My litmus test for whether a passage is well done is whether it is internally consistent for the speaker's world-view.

                                                  And it is...

                                                  thinking the ashirra is a single thing, a single culture, is a prejudiced idea that will get you killed in the courts of the future.
                                                  The speaker clearly believes in a multi-cultural, citizen of the world ethos. Other in-world characters may or may not agree; indeed, the fact that the speaker emphasizes multiculturalism, and warns that parochialism will get one killed, implies some in-world characters are not so cosmopolitan. In real life, you or I might agree with one side or the other... or neither... but the passage is meant to tell us something of the tensions that exist for in-world characters. It has not been easy for Eurocentric Camarilla to adjust to a wider world of vampires.

                                                  among our new allies
                                                  Not "among the Ashirra", or "among the A-Rabs", or even "within the Umma"... all of which would have communicated a different world-view... but "new allies". The speaker is not afraid to stake out a position, while almost daring the listener to contradict the implicit assumptions that are signaled. Classic Brujah.

                                                  we find saudi princes dining on the blood of women stoned to death for infidelity
                                                  This passage is interesting, because there are several coded statements in it. The most obvious is "those wacky Saudis, with their morality laws"... a view which has become more complex and more nuanced just since the book was published.

                                                  There is a subtler dig at the Saudi vampiric leaders, though. Read more carefully, the speaker is saying that Saudi vampires have princes so weak-blooded they can partake in the revolting practice of drinking the blood of mutilated corpses. Mechanically, this would require the Iron Gullet advantage, and likely a blood potency of 2 or lower. There is a pile of generational-classism in this insult, with its dismissal of Saudi princes as simultaneously nouveau riche and barbaric.

                                                  Never mind that it seems unlikely someone could make such feeding a staple of their diet.

                                                  a liberal ıt-millionnaire from jordan
                                                  Think about the subtleties of how this line could have been structured, and how different the implied meaning is in each:
                                                  "a Jordanian it-millionaire, who is liberal"
                                                  "a liberal Jordanian it-techie, who is a millionaire"
                                                  "a Jordanian liberal millionaire, who made their money in IT"

                                                  In the original, the traits "liberal", "IT", and "millionaire" are all run together, as if they are self-reinforcing rather than contradictory traits. Splitting those traits with a conjunction from the remaining trait "Jordan(ian)", implies there is some surprise to be had with somebody from Jordan running a tech startup. The speaker is trying soooo hard to be inclusive, and stumbles into the false stereotype of Arabs living in technologically backward countries. (PDF: Jordan is quite tech forward.)

                                                  turkish nationalists dreaming of the ottoman empire,
                                                  I'm guessing the speaker sees this as a series of insults. "Nationalists!" "Imperialists!" "Dreamers!" As written, there is an implicit warning that this is a movement that must be monitored, especially since the speaker is alternating in a bad-good-bad-good pattern in their list. There could have been a more neutral description like, "Turkish vampires who hope to advance the fortunes of their country, using the Ottoman Empire as inspiration".

                                                  kurdish freedom fighters and persian nobles longing for the glorious days of disco in 1970’s teheran
                                                  True, the passage could have been written with "terrorist" or "nationalist militiamen" in place of "freedom fighters". Then again, "glorious" could have been replaced with "decadent" or even "cheesy". None of these substitutions would have been consistent with the world-view portrayed in previous parts of the text, though.

                                                  As written, there is an internal consistency to the passage. Same worldview, same prejudices, same tone, even similar vocabulary. As is usual with a fictional character's statement of opinion we learn as much about the speaker as about the topic they are discussing.

                                                  I kind of think it's cool that Kurdish freedom fighters and Persian nobles both share nostalgia for the glory days of disco. Even freedom fighters need to boogie down once in a while.

                                                  Or, was there an Oxford comma in the original text?
