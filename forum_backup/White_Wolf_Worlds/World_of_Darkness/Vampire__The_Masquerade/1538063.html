    Setting | Parallelism or Divergence

    07-03-2024, 08:59 PM
    Parallelism & Divergence: A Question of Setting Philosophy in the World of Darkness


    The World of Darkness is a world very much like our own but where the shadows are somewhat darker - this is well known. What does that mean for you in practice? One example I find illustrative is a common description used on these forums to describe the general rules of thumb for distribution of territory to the major sects. Cities that are considered prosperous/influential & generally non-violent are Camarilla (Classic 90s example: Chicago), cities that are in decay and violent are considered Sabbat (Classic 90s example: New York City, the Bronx; Detroit), cities that are considered in the middle, such as prosperous but violent (Classic 90s Example: Los Angeles). Now the old sketch of sect territory was certainly made with artistic license, a certain amount of Great Lakes provincialism, contemporary popular attitudes & cultural depiction, with certain exceptions, but this largely held true.

    One of the reasons why New York City was considered Sabbat territory (outside parts of Manhattan) in the early 90s was due to the cultural image of New York City that had pervaded since the 1970s in films like Escape From New York, the Warriors, etc. This suited the general treatment of the northeast megalopolis as a vast field of crime-ridden urban decay which presented a vivid and exciting backdrop for the Sabbat to flourish (although one could imagine carveouts for non-Sabbat in certain areas).

    However, as we all know, the New York City of 2024 is not the NYC of 1991. Hell, even the NYC of 1991 isn’t necessarily the NYC of 1991 - one could argue the forces of gentrification and redevelopment were already in the midst of transforming the actual New York City by that point, even if cultural depictions lagged behind.

    In the revised era New York City fell to Camarilla control by 2002, which would match with a Parallelist reading, but alongside this came the East Coast Sabbat Crusades, most notably the Fall of Atlanta. These are metaplot elements that I know some have found controversial over the years. So the logic goes: in a period of gentrification and urban renewal, with crime and violence on the decline, how can we justify the retrenchment of the Sabbat in US cities like Atlanta? While one can offer Doyalist answers to these questions , ‘All those Sabbat displaced from NYC/energized by Polonia had to go somewhere’, it still wrankles those who are more inclined to Parallelism.

    These leads me to the cruxes of what I’m struggling with: the temptations of Divergence.

    Divergence for me is anything which occurs in the plot of the setting which is independent of irl phenomena as inspiration. When the Anarchs flourish in the counter-culture of the 1960s, a resonance between life and storytelling, that’s an example of Parallelism. When the Sabbat sees a resurgence in the era of gentrification, a dissonance between life and storytelling, that’s an example of Divergence.

    Divergence is often rooted in plot or metaplot. Something happens in the plot of your chronicle or the overarching metaplot of the franchise which causes the setting of the WoD to diverge from our world. For instance: Pollonia’s Crusade is successful in re-entrenching the Sabbat on the East Coast in the aughts. The cases of metaplot divergence are often controversial but I’d be interested to hear about people’s attitudes towards plot divergence in their chronicles. If, let’s say, a coterie of PCs effectively caused or prevented an irl development project (or lack there of) in their city, how do you handle that as a Storyteller?

    However, not all Divergence is a case of the Metaplot taking on a life of its own. Sometimes, in fact, the Metaplot Divergence can occur in response to another factor. I call this factor: ‘The Gotham Problem’.

    The Gotham Problem compares Divergence in the World of Darkness (or any loosely real world setting really) to the case of Batman’s Gotham, (I’ve recently had the thought that metaplot resembles comic books in a few ways, but that’s beside the point. The Gotham Problem, in a nutshell is this: many Batman writers and readers want Gotham to be a certain type of setting, a stylized Gothic-Noir hellhole of urban decay and art deco glamour where gangsters, socialites, and scandal can easily rub shoulders. The problem: if Gotham is supposed to reflect real world cities, whether Chicago or New York City, then the issue is that those cities have changed. Times Square today is massively different in aesthetic, appearance, connotation, and tone than it was in the 1970s. The old-school Mafia is largely defunct, most of the old political machines are either defunct or significantly changed, gentrification has redesigned the look of cities with new images of glass, stainless steel, and silicon. To realistically depict these modern cities as they are today is to forfeit much of the glamour of past eras in favor of the verisimilitude of modernity. But that isn’t always satisfying to reader or fan. For example: a relatively common complaint of modern Batman readers, is that Gotham doesn’t feel like Gotham anymore, it feels like any other city that just happens to have a caped vigilante in it.

    There are a few responses to this. One is to put on your cultural critic hat and survey the modern landscape in search of those contradictions and shadows which can be used as the levers of telling a good Gothic-Punk or Neo-Noir in the setting you have while meeting the present moment. This, however, is hard. The present is a constantly moving target, highly chimerical, even with liberties granted for artistic license. Even when done well, what you get can be compelling, but it’s still not going to be Classic Gotham.

    The alternative to this is classic Divergence: instead of adapting to meet the times, simply continue using Classic Gotham to tell your stories! This can easily be accomplished by making your story a period piece (notice how many period pieces you see in contemporary media) or by keeping the clock running forward but keeping the city the same. Now, this also has its own problems. It can feel stagnant and navel-gazing to see a setting that never develops or can’t meaningfully interact with the present. Again, there’s nothing wrong with it, but it has drawbacks.

    As a storyteller and a fan of WoD, I often struggle with myself on where to fall on the scale of Parallelism and Divergence. I want to keep the setting of my games moving forward and meaningfully interacting with the current moment but I miss losing classic features of the Gothic-Punk world. Similarly, I fear having the story appear as a mechanical retread, where the events are dictated by real world history, where the vampiric characters essentially become mere vessels for the movement of some sort of Hegelian World-Spirit. I suppose in practice I lean towards Parallelism but try not to go to the fullest extreme, leaving room for places of divergence and for certain cultural aspects to be different. Familiar yet with twists, or something like that.

    The purpose of this post wasn’t simply me writing a short essay to share my thoughts on setting philosophy. I’m curious to hear how others handle this question in their chronicles and in their perspectives on the WoD. Furthermore I’m curious to hear any thoughts that anyone has on the ideas of Parallelism & Divergence themselves.

    Thank you all for reading and best regards!

      I think a point you brushes, but misses, is that Parallelism is actually an illusion.

      You never reflects, directly or by symbolism, the real world in the setting. Instead you're always reflecting an interpretation, a filtered version that tells about how you read the world at a given moment, coming from your knowledge as much as your lack thereof, your opinions, your biases of all types.

      Heck, it isn't even your understanding of the world per se, because this artistic interpretation depends on emotion, aesthetics and goals, things that pertain to your moment, not your information on the subject.

      The best parallelism fits the real world only on the broadest strokes. Both real life and fiction are full of complex nuance, but never the same nuance.

      On top of that, Parallelism is a starting point, a means to take from life the context and inspiration for your game. To stick to it beyond the starting point is to deny your fiction to take hold and grow as its own thing. It's to let your story aside to forcibly correct the fiction in a direction that don't belong to it.

      My own take on this is: take from the world what you want to start things, but then let Divergence be king. Change the world to fit your story, not the other way around.

      And you'll notice that sometimes it's even easier to fit Parallelism into the story when you accept it as just a weak suggestion, incorporating and embracing any deviation as a feature of your WoD.

              The WoD setting can't function without Divergence due to it depending on technology not advancing or being put into application. Yet its main selling point is the Parallelism that it offers by being set in our world.

              In the modern day anyone with an amazon account can purchase a smartphone attachment which allows you to see in the infrared spectrum for around $300 and have it shipped to your door without any special paperwork or licenses. With such technology readily available to the public, there is no real way for vampires to hide in plain sight unless they are at events where phones are banned. Don't get me started on the realities of vampire hunting thanks to advances in drone technology.

              Basically, after the year 2000 vampire society would have to either go subterranean like Nosferatu warrens & that vampire city from the "Seraph of the End" anime, or they would go extreme high society where they are the movers and shakers but never go on street level adventures. Alternatively, vampires could flee first world countries in general and only setup shop in extremely rural areas or third world countries with very low levels of technology or little to no access to things like the internet.

              So, you have to balance and mix Divergence/Parallelism create a functional gaming space, in the same way that the Care Bears setting says that the fluffy guardians of friendship exist and modify human society but things like WWI and WWII still happened.

              Personally, I favor accurate modelling of function first, then adding divergent flourishes for flavor. If you do the reverse, then you are just playing in a fantasy setting with somewhat accurate modelling of things like titles or technology, which last I checked was called Ravenloft and Dungeons & Dragons in general.

                  I think the setting in general works best with high scale parallelism. The World of Darkness is set on Earth. So it should look like Earth at high levels. The major historical events are the same. The major historical people are the same. The same countries and cities exist and are roughly at the same level of socioeconomic development. The world should be recognizably the same because otherwise why would you set it on Earth? That's the entire point.

                  But any fictional setting is always going to have its own fictions unique to it. In Quentin Tarantino movies, there are going to be references to Big Kahuna Burger which doesn't actually exist. Even very realistic movies are going to have fictional people and organizations to tell the story. The 2011 film Margin Call wants to explain how the 2008 financial crisis happened. Its unnamed fictional investment bank and fictional characters is how they did it. Sometimes that can even extend to roman-a-clef versions of real people. In James Clavell's Shogun, you don't have the real historical personages of Sengoku Jidai Japan. Instead of Tokugawa Ieayasu, we have Toranaga Yoshi because that gave the author more artistic liberties to take. Likewise in the Godfather, we don't have Frank Sinatra but Johnny Fontaine.

                  Then you have divergences that are based simply on the idea that you need antagonists or need certain genre tropes. An RPG about supernatural creatures from Earth's folklore and legend set in a gothic themed story is going to have stuff that meets the needs of such a world, which would be different than if you have an RPG about Cold War super spies, superheroes, the Golden Age of Piracy, or another genre of horror.

                  As an ST, I want the setting to start very parallel to the real world except for a variety of fairly low scale or setting important divergences. Left by itself, my setting will continue to be more or less parallel to the real world. However, the PCs are always going to create divergences based on their actions. I'm willing to allow the local setting change based on that, even if the larger setting remains intact. Otherwise that robs players of their agency, and that tends to not be fun.
