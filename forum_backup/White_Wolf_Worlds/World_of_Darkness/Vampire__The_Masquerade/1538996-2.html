    I will note that Vampire has always had this weird mix of influences.
    On the one hand, it is an early 90s "street level" urban fantasy RPG, and so is built with a lot of the same assumptions as things like Cyberpunk 2020, Kult, Nightlife, and TMNT & Other Strangeness. This being that the PCs are essentially the protagonists of some sort of Hard-Boiled Detective or Film Noir story, individuals with some useful skills who exist on the edges of normal society, tend to get sucked in to dangerous situations, and (usually) have at least some sort of degree of morality that puts them above the predators they encounter in either the gutters, the working world, or high society. Sure, you may be an outcast, a jerk, or even a freak in the eyes of normal society, but you are (probably) better than the amoral sociopaths you find yourself confronting.
    On the other hand, it is a setting that is almost entirely based off of a trilogy of vampire novels which start with a fairly grounded account of one man's experience of being a vampire and ends with a 4000-year-old blood god trying to take over the world. So certain aspects of the setting clash with certain aspects of the RPG design, running the risk of PCs being reduced to helpless bystanders or having to engage in some sort of arms race to create PCs who can actually compete with the blood gods. In addition, that particular style of vampire fiction and its fandom tended to lend itself to very social-oriented settings, scenes, and play styles (especially LARPing). And that can clash with some of the more "traditional" focus of some players, the "find the monster, hit the monster" formula.
    I don't think there is really a wrong way to play Vampire, although my own preference is essentially Film Noir with certain moral and psychological overtones (probably because I saw the film Angel Heart at such an impressionable age), sometimes veering into more horror or conspiracy areas.
    (In hindsight, I really do wish Vampire and the other games had done more books devoted to specific genres and how to do them for your game. Like, this is the Horror genre, its tropes, and how to incorporate them into Vampire. And this is the Political Conspiracy/Mystery genre, this is Gothic Romance, etc. I think the closest we got was Wraith's Love Beyond Death and Mage's Dark Adventure.)

        Originally posted by No One of Consequence View Post
        I will note that Vampire has always had this weird mix of influences.
        I agree with a lot you say. Even in 1e, the game is all over the place. It's explicitly about some kind of personal horror, but a lot of the supplements concentrate on the War of Ages between neonates (frequently Anarchs) and elders (frequently the leaders of the local Camarilla). The Players Guides and ST Handbooks not only have stats for certain types of firearms, but give different stats to different individual weapons within those firearm categories. So we have a half dozen examples of revolvers, another half dozen of light pistols, a half dozen heavy pistols, a half dozen rifles, etc. all with unique stats for damage, difficulty, rate, clip amount, range, etc. We're given stats for grenades, military grade machine guns, artillery systems, antitank weapons, armored fighting vehicles, attack helicopters, and even an F-14 Tomcat. Yeah, those are the type of things I expect in a game of "personal horror".

        And of course, a lot of that stuff did get used in the games I played in because my play group was mostly a bunch of guys in their late teens who were used to D&D.

        And to be honest, while they were mostly used in ridiculous ways, there will be good players who ask why they can't pull off a scene like they had in Godfather Part III (which just seems to be an ordinary helicopter with multiple guys with submachine guns).


        Lots of violence. Of course, some of that was simply us trying to figure out how different roleplaying games would need different play styles. As we played other games than D&D like Vampire and Call of Cthulhu, our playing styles slowly shifted as we realized we could have fun outside of combat. If you ever look at some of the earliest Call of Cthulhu published scenarios, even the original creators had that problem as well. It took a while for them to figure out a better way to approach their game adventures.

        As Vampire attracted new kinds of players and they joined our game group, they were often the ones who first approached the game from different angle because their exposure was not from D&D but because they liked Anne Rice or vampire movies in general.

            I think part of the reason that many games did either take on the form of Noir Style Mysteries, ‘Superhero’ Beat-em-Ups, or even Political Thrillers is that, ‘personal horror’ as a stand-alone concept, is kind of tricky to conceive of in a game-able fashion. I’m a fairly regular reader of the Alexandrian blog and his posts and game structure is something that I’ve thought about a lot re: Vampire (and other WoD games).

            I think one of the things that makes Mysteries, Action, & Politics so attractive for vampire is that they each provide a tentpole structure which is useful both to player and ST. If the game is about Mysteries then the PCs need to investigate and the ST needs to write mystery plots for their game. If it’s about Action then the PCs need to fight and the ST needs to prepare conflicts for their game. If the game is about Politics then the PCs need to conspire and the ST needs to prepare intrigues for their game. Those are all easier to grok then Freeform ‘personal horror’ and one can even weave personal horror into each of these pillars. But if one doesn’t have a pillar and is just going for personal horror, what does that look like in play?

            Interview with the Vampire can keep us invested in Louis and Lestat’s inner turmoil, whether in the form of novel or film or show, far more easily then we can construct frames for such narratives at our tables, at least in my experience (although LARP and play-by-post has such advantages in this regard, imo).

            These things aren’t mutually exclusive of course but I think part of this discussion has to do with how one uses personal horror storytelling while also including game structures that facilitate play and storytelling.

                                    Originally posted by Matt Corleone View Post
                                    I'm just going to go ahead and say it...

                                    In my humble opinion, I feel Justin Achilli and all of his imitators did a lot of damage to the Vampire: The Masquerade IP in the long run
                                    On the other hand, the third edition of Vampire: the Masquerade is, I would still argue, the best single rulebook for the game, presenting a strong and cohesive view of what Vampire is supposed to be, and about 85% of the book was written by Justin Achilli.

                                    There are some serious problems which arose from his tenure as developer of the game; I would suggest that these were the following:

                                    The One True Way: The starting point for revised edition was to pull together all the disparate odds and ends which had accrued over previous years. So, you have a rulebook which contains rules for all thirteen clans, all main disciplines, Merits and Flaws, Paths of Enlightenment, and then supplements which take the same approach, leaving you feeling that you're playing the same game, regardless of which books you are reading (pick up A World of Darkness and Ghouls: Fatal Addiction, and read them alongside each other; they don't read like they reference the same universe, let alone the same game). But this desire for cohesiveness ends up in rants about trenchcoats and katanas, and the implication that there are wrong ways to play the game. Compare Chicago by Night with New York by Night and you can see how this approach risks just making everything boring.

                                    Metaplot: When the concept of metaplot was introduced in Nights of Prophecy it had to be explained to the reader, because the average player or Storyteller had no idea what this represented (it had been discussed, often forcefully, on Usenet for a few years by that point, but most gamers were not on alt.games.whitewolf). Metaplot is a bad idea for lots of reasons, but primarily because it exists for people who are readers and spectators, rather than for people who are players. And it feeds into...

                                    The Supplement Treadmill: This one wasn't Justin Achilli's fault, but a significant issue during his era in charge. There was, at this time, a great pressure to push out more game supplements, with more regularity. Vampire, during the revised-era, was seeing a book per month. This led to all kinds of junk being put into press. Firstly, you ended up with books coming into print which seemed good on paper, but were worthless in practice - I don't think there is a single worthwhile, usage sentence in Succubus Club: Dead Man's Party. Secondly, the volume of working coming out is too quick for the development cycle to properly manage, and has a demand for the written word which exceeds the talent pool available. One massive issue coming out of this was verbiage; there were many books that were twice as long as they needed to be, ending up filled up with words for the sake of words. It would be an interesting exercise to see how page count crept up over time...

                                    Rock Star Writers: Prior to White Wolf, RPG writers were uncool nerds. White Wolf was in the right place at the right time, surfed the wave of the emerging Internet to put writers and creators in touch with fans (alt.games.whitewolf again) and created personal fandoms around creators which never really existed before or since. Writers, and more crucially developers would be told literally every day how great they were, and how every decision they were making was the right one for the game (until Jess Heinig took over Mage: the Ascension; and, again, this was not his fault, but no-one knew that at the time). This created a toxic fandom, which did not accept any dissent in relation to the rock stars, nor exposure of their misbehaviour (one of the great scandals was the Matt McFarland cover-up of 2003; before the "me too" movement, it was incredibly difficult to reveal the misconduct of particular individuals in the RPG industry). And, of course, an industry where criticism is actively opposed is going to end up with quality issues.
