        Originally posted by Black Fox View Post
        I think a major difference between vampire texts and historical works is that all of the historical works were meant to be published and disseminated.
        Actually not necessarily. Many books in the past were guarded for one reason or another, and the difficulty in copying them added to the problem.

        But ultimately we're not talking about books from the past in the past. The modern forms of those books are exactly that, modern, compiled by scholars all the same and with mostly the same tools and techniques despite being vampires.

        That's why I think those texts are public. A single scan or transcript, even if incomplete, hitting the internet is enough. Once the thing is in the net, what will those Archons do? By the way, they're a nice idea that I do think make sense, congrats.

        Each person that knows one of those books may put what they know in the web. Anyone with access to a copy can scan or transcribe it. Nosferatu was almost completely destroyed by a judicial decision for being plagiarism on the original Dracula, yet it became an astounding cult success that informs the Vampire genre to this day due to a single copy that survived (the accompanying soundtrack was lost).

        It is particularly good to do so from an academic standpoint. Scientists work better by sharing information so everyone can pass on ideas, and many Kindred working on those texts are scientists. If a Tremere text is copied enough for each two chantries to have a copy, how much time until one gets scanned? What about texts that are common knowledge among the Kindred?

        On my view there are, indeed, books that aren't publicly known, but that requires that every copy be accounted for. Either a copy is lost somewhere and so no one scanned it (it happens even in a library), or it is locked and access to it tightly controlled. The more copies, the harder to keep everything under lock and key. Any copy unaccounted for is a matter of time (a random amount of time) to become public.

        Finally, given how little most of those scholars actually know of the past directly, I think that just as fun as to think that mortal scholars may have access to Kindred-related texts and know nothing of what they really mean, the Kindred may also have a lot of wrong conclusions and not actually know as much about those texts as they pretend to.

        Does the Book of Nod have all the pieces in place? Is every fragment really about the Kindred, or from the same book? Is there some key piece lost in some library, known to a working archaeologist who know nothing about the Kindred, yet understands the text better than any of them? Such is the cost of not sharing information, after all. The pieced text may be very good, but as the work of a single person, it can also be very wrong.

                                    Originally posted by Black Fox View Post
                                    I do think that various bits of the Book of Nod, Lilith Cycle, etc. probably has been part of the academic scholarship for a while. But they may not be organized as such, and interpreted it as various things. Maybe this piece might be believed to be part of biblical apocrypha like the Book of Enoch. This other piece considered to be a Gnostic text. This other thing a medieval heretic text. This other one a forgery. Cainite scholars may recognize them for what they are, but to mortal academia they might be odd text fragment found in the ruins of a monastery in Turkey and stored in the archives of the Vatican Library.
                                    Worth noting in this too is that it’s not like Cain was “Caine’s” actual given name, any more than Adam was really called Adam (which in Hebrew just means Man) or Eve called Eve or Abel actually called Abel. It’s translations of translations that, if actually true, are written down from oral histories thousands of years (with all the associated linguistic drift) after the events occurred.

                                    The Book of Nod and the associated works were compiled and translated from fragments… which probably means in one fragment it’s Akkadian and is referring to some immortal blood god-king or father of night by a fitting Akkadian name and due to various cues the text compiler decides it is a reference and translated that name as “Caine.”

                                    Basically, take each separate sub-section (or set of events in a longer narrative) of the Book of Nod, make each a separate document with different names for all the figures in that fragment… and that is what the mortals have; a bunch of unconnected fragments of folklore from dozens of cultures spanning thousands of years.

                                    It’s all there, but only someone who’s literally spent centuries trying to study the material would ever be able to compile it into a single connected narrative (and only in line with their personal theories and biases… they could have absolutely thrown out a key fragments that were actually true just because it didn’t fit with their own conclusions of what it should look like… and suddenly some unrelated nomad whose name was pronounced like the English word “Keen” or “Qin” who was cursed by some blood spirit* gets conflated by the scholar with the biblical Cain (because no one in history has ever had the same name**, much less just a similar sounding one) and Lilith.

                                    * And to top it off this story fragment isn’t even about the first vampire, but an early fomori.

                                    ** Person 1: “I bet you can’t name 23 popes.”
                                    Person 2: “John.”
                                    Person 1: [beat] “Dammit, that’s not fair.”
