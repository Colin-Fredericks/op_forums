    Part of me is inclined to jump on the 'Cappadocious is insane' bandwagon, but I'm also reminded of ToJ Gehenna and how it reinforced all the different ways in which Antediluvians simply changed into things much more than bloodsucking vampires. Ante's by definition play by a different set of rules and are supposed to represent truly alien, unknowable minds so it's really hard to justify appending 'human' labels like sanity or insanity. I mean in a way I could see why he might think it would work - spirits devouring other spirits to grow more powerful isn't exactly an unsuual thing in WoD as a rule from what I recall, and if he can gain enough power he might be able to assimilate a deity. I mean we saw something similar proposed with Voormas in ToJ:Ascension, and Revelations of the Dark Mother suggested the masculine 'God' may not have actually been the creator-entity (in that sense it would be analogous to Cappadocious trying to consume Lucifer. And if you accept the premise of the Ante being insane how would he know the difference between an Elohim and a true deity?)

    Really how you try to explain it will depend greatly on how you piece together the cosmology and what assumptions you're willing or unwilling to work with.

                    My interpretation is that Cappadocius's supposed conversion to Christianity and his diablerie by Augustus Giovanni was linked. This is a bit different from my original post on this thread in 2016 and reflects some growth in my ideas.

                    I don't believe Cappadocius was ever an orthodox Christian. He most likely believed in an extremely heretical Gnostic version of Christianity (which may not even reflect one of the real world versions, but be based on his own ideas). He sacrificed himself in order to gain special spiritual knowledge that would have released a "divine spark" that already existed within him (and possibly everyone else according to Gnostic views), but trapped in flesh. In this theory, the destruction of his physical body was a necessary precondition for that divinity to be released and Cappadocius to ascend to godhood (in some form). Allowing Augustus to betray him may have been an attempt by Cappadocius to mirror Judas' betrayal of Jesus for important symbolic purposes.

                    And if he was some kind of Gnostic Christian, he would have a very different view of what "God" meant. Union with the Monad would mean becoming more powerful and wise than the Demiurge. This might be translated as "diablerizing God" or "becoming God" by people unfamiliar with Gnostic religions - such as members of the Giovanni family who grew up in a culture dominated by orthodox Christianity.

                    Although this sounds crazy to us, it would be within the tradition of actual Gnostic religions. Incredibly, it would also fit the idea that Augustus only absorbed the vitae of Cappadocious and not his soul.

                    Cappadocious struck me as the kind of person who thought he was playing 4th dimensional chess when everyone else was playing checkers. It's entirely possible he disdained the Jyhad and came to the same conclusion as WOPR. in the movie WarGames - the only winning move was not to play. So he may have been trying to circumvent the need for the Jyhad entirely and define his own victory conditions.

                    I'm not saying this was the intent of the actual writers/developers. It's just my interpretation of what they gave us.

                                                AFAIK Cappadocius was confirmed at first to be a Gnostic, or at least heavily hinted to be. That's already similar to the Setites, with the distinction that he wouldn't be of a "Gnostic-based fictional faith", but an actual Gnostic (yes, with some idiosyncratic beliefs in the middle, but that's also pretty standard for Gnostics). But the "Diablerize God" part isn't stated as a metaphor in the books, and that's my problem.

                                                That, and given age and whatnot I really don't think that madness is unlikely. Actually I find it extremely ableist to assume that you can only be smart and effective if you're completely rational and mentally sound, specially when most "normal" people are already perfectly capable of acting on completely irrational beliefs.

                                                But I do think he may have succeeded at the basic Gnostic goal, it's just the goal to eat God that was unfeasible. He may be more powerful than ever by having transcended beyond Ialdabaoth, and it may even be that his Gnostic beliefs are mostly right and through that he reached the Pleroma and joined the Aeons above the very "God" that curse Caine.

                                                It doesn't have to deny his Diablerie to be truth, either. Whatever spark is eaten during this action, it isn't necessarily the soul just because the experience of eating it seems like it. What's a soul for us to pinpoint when it is present or gone? Augustus' actions also have a powerful effect on the lore, and denying him that doesn't make for a better story. It reinforces the theme that Diablerie is a gamble, a mysterious action that won't have the same effects every time. He did it, be it by merit or dumb luck, and won.
