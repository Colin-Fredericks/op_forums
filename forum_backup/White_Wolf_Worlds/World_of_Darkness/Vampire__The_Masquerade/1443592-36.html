    I am reiterating the points about V20 because of the context of this thread being ‘V5 vs V20’.

    Why is it silly?
    To be succinct: The point of combat is to remove the direct physical threat your character is facing (either beating them in combat, or escaping combat). The point of most social actions is to alter another character's course of action. There is an overlap where altering another character's course of action is because they are a threat to you that you need to remove, but everything else one might expect to do with social actions will never work properly if modeled after combat.

    I think that is a misinterpretation of what an ‘Advance' is.
    V5, p. 296-297:

    "Advance
    Working directly towards the goal(s) of the conflict: this could be cozying up to an elder when vying for a seat on the council, destroying evidence before getting over-whelmed, gathering influence in a housing project, hacking a security system before being discovered, Kissing students, etc. Note how-ever, that advance is not applicable to all conflicts. The prime example being combat, where there are no goals except hurting the opposition and/or getting the hell out of there. Depending on the conflict, a prior Maneuver (see below) might be required to advance effectively..Advances are usually run as an extended test (see p. 293) with the player rolling to accumulate as many wins/successes as possible."

    Emphasis mine: one of the examples is explicitly trying to impress a third party for political gain rather than engaging your opponent for that political position in a debate, or something of the sort.

    It clearly is part of a narrative that involves head-to-conflict, because the rule is listed and stated as being “An Additional Conflict Option” right before Maneuvers.
    V5, p. 296:

    "Additional Conflict Options
    All conflict actions need not be straight attacks on the opposition. In fact, most engagements involve a goal that does not solely rely on removing all enemies, and there is no reason why players should be forced to confront their opponents head-on when they can achieve their ends with other means. Consider the following action variants when engaged in a conflict of any kind:"

    Emphasis mine. The Addition Conflict Options introductory paragraph directly states that one of the goals of the additional options present is to give players methods to avoid head-on confrontations.

    A 'prior maneuver’ is exactly what you were describing as lacking before - it is something you can do in preparation for a conflict and as, it does not impinge on any action during the conflict itself.
    V5, p. 297:

    "Maneuver
    This option involves getting into an advantaged position, either in order to be able to take other actions at all or get an advantage, such as flanking a foe, making sure you are alone with the subject of your desire, getting access to important people, finding cover from oncoming shots, etc. Depending on the situation, a successful maneuver should result in one of these benefits:
    ...
    In some cases a maneuver is required to make an attack or advance towards the goal in the first place. Only after a successful maneuver can the character attack the intended target or advance towards the goal. Example: finding the target in a city-wide conflict, arranging to be alone with the target in a conflict of seduction and intrigue, getting into position to take over a company."

    Emphasis mine, again. There is no statement in any of these sections that state you can start a Maneuver before the conflict beings.

    This isn’t me trying to give you house rule, this is actually what is written in the rules of the text.
    Please cite a passage that states that any Additional Conflict Option can be taken before a Conflict begins.

    In your story example, you would make an extended set of Advances towards the blackmailing goal, and these would potentially accumulate dice to add to the final roll.
    Please cite a passage that allows the extended roll from the Advance option to be put towards a different Conflict action in the forms of a dice bonus. Just to head it off, a Cascading Extended Test, is a variant on a Series Test. The ability to roll successes over into the next roll is contained with the Test, it does not extend beyond the Test. While it is actually directly referenced for being a way to handle blackmail attempts, it is not included in Extended Conflicts, and thus isn't for when you're targeting someone significant enough to roll back at you instead of being abstracted into a Difficulty.

    To that last point, V5, page. 304:

    "Social combat requires an opponent: someone who actively doesn’t want you to succeed and who attempts to defeat you on the same ground on which you attack them. For social actions without such an opponent – persuasion of a disinterested audience, seduction of a victim, fast-talking your way past a military patrol, etc. – use normal resolution."

    The impact of Audience isn’t really in context, because it is a different type of social conflict.
    V5, page 305:

    "Willpower damage hurts most when others see your weakness. Add a damage modifier depending on the audience (just being present doesn’t cut it, they also need to be interested in the outcome)."

    Please cite where this different type of social conflict is detailed that changes the context of the above quote.

    With the WP thing, you can simply rule it that the stakes are higher than a single WP loss - if that is agreed previously as an outcome.
    V5, page 304:

    "As in One-Roll Conflict, it’s important to set the stakes ahead of time; what happens to the winner of the social conflict and what happens to the loser?"

    V5, page 299, under One-Roll Conflict:

    "The more player characters that win, the better the results. If the majority of the player characters win, the opposition might for example break, fall down wounded, storm off in a fit of pique, surrender, or whatever else makes dramatic sense.
    ...
    Each player character then takes damage equal to the difference between their successes and twice the Difficulty."

    Please cite a passage that indicates that the setting stakes for advanced social conflict override how WP damage is applied. Please cite a passage that says stakes can be set as mechanical consideration such has who gets the most successes instead of the stakes being in-character goals each side has for the resolution of the conflict which is resolved by the number of successes rolled.

    The Audience factor really is for a particular type of social conflict where a character’s confidence, reputation or face is at stake - and where the Audience clearly raises that stake - like a public debate as an obvious example. It doesn’t necessarily apply to every type of social conflict.
    Please cite a passage that supports this; esp. considering the Audience chart includes +0 for no audience.

    Though while we're here, V5 p. 305:

    "Combatants roll their respective dice pools and compare numbers of successes. The combatant with more successes subtracts their opponent’s successes and applies the result as damage to Willpower."

    Since social conflicts - once rolled, always result in Willpower damage if one side wins a roll, and Audience is always considered (even if +0 because nobody is there, or any other people present are not engaged in observing), then the assumption must be that all advanced social conflicts handled by the system are ones, "where a character’s confidence, reputation or face is at stake." Considering how vital WP is to vampires keeping their Beasts contained, draining a vampire's Willpower is definitely a risk to their confidence and reputation as they might not have the WP necessary to avoid Messy Crits and Beastial Failures if social conflicts don't go their way.

    Though this touches on a whole other issues with V5's social mechanics where engaging in social conflicts in Elysium is a ridiculously bad idea because of the risk of taking a big WP hit in a situation where you need WP the most to avoid breaking the customs of Elysium... which are there to provide a safe place for vampires to engage in social maneuvering without breaking out into violence; which means the social rules my Elysium a self-defeating concept.

                                                        Originally posted by Chris24601 View Post
                                                        You seem to miss how probability works. More dice REDUCES randomness by pushing the outcome towards a bell curve distribution where relative attributes matter MORE not less.

A d20 and 3d6 both have an average result of 10.5, but d20 has 1/20 chance of a 20 and a 1/20 chance of a 10. A roll of 3d6 has a 1/216 chance of an 18, but a 1/14.4 (15 times greater) chance of getting a 10.

                                                        V20’s multiple opposed dice tests may be slower, but they serve to push the results closer to the median outcome where the more skilled will defeat the less skilled more regularly.
                                                        Well, this is wrong - because you are rolling contested dice pools through a chain, with each individual die having a fixed target. If you roll more dice you are changing the range of the potential number of success, not increasing the likelihood of a median point average You are not rolling a bell curve of results - you are rolling dice pools that are weighted towards accumulating lower numbers of success rather than higher number of success. You are citing the wrong mathematical model, because we are not talking about rolling 3D6 like in D&D or GURPS, so this is not how the maths work in dice pool rolling.

                                                        You can talk textbooks if you like, but this statistical anomaly has been analysed mathematically before a number of times before. Here is a spreadsheet link: https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets...jHI/edit#gid=0

                                                        Unlike other rolls in V20, you roll for initiative, then an opposed attack roll and then an opposed damage roll. So, for example, you may have a big attack pool of 8, and might roll 3 or 4 successes, but this will be reduced by the number of successes that the opponent will roll, and then you still have to roll again for another contested roll for damage. It is the number of opposed rolls in the chain that increases the randomness, compared to a single roll. The roll tends towards smaller amounts of damage being incrementally applied despite rolling loads of dice. it means that combat outcomes - ie damage - generally moves forward in small increments. This levies the overall chances of winning towards somebody with less skill but lucky rolls, as opposed to more skill but unlucky rolls. The advantage of having bigger dice pools is reduced by the number of tiers in which you roll them, so the overall outcome is more randomized - and means that the combat is more likely to be protracted regardless.
