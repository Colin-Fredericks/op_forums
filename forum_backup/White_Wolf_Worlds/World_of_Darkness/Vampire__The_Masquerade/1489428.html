    What is the future of VTM, and the WOD as a whole, within the videogame industry?

    08-07-2022, 05:30 PM
    Paradox seems to be growing their videogame portfolio, with everything from interactive fiction to a battle royale, it seems like Paradox has done a good job of increasing VTM's, and overall, the WOD's, popularity. However, how long do you think this is going to last for? Almost nobody talks about their interactive fiction games anymore, Swansong doesn't seem to have been well received, Earth blood looks to be a disappointment, and Bloodlines 2 is going through issues of its own. Blood Hunt seems to be the only game that's doing well, which is a mystery to me seeing as how unless you were playing from day one of release, you're going to be dying constantly.

    I actually deleted the game because of how unfriendly the matches were for new players, like seriously, you rarely make a kill, and you will never win a game. Most of the time I would have my Nosferatu just crouch and go invisible, I would eventually get killed, but it would let me outlive many of the other players. Playing like that isn't fun though. I'm pretty sure I'm not the only one who has had this issue either. Rant aside, Paradox seems to be churning out one videogame after another, not all of them seem to be that great, but enough are doing good numbers to seemingly justify making more games.

    What do you think?

              The primary problem with Bloodhunt is that it's a Battle Royale game, which are designed to heavily favor skilled FPS players by removing as much as possible that would let another factor dominate a match. It's the Souls franchise approach to multiplayer shooters: die, a lot, until you either give up or get to a competitive level. That's a hurdle to VtM fans looking for a fun videogame for them, but you can't dilute that without turning off the fans of the Battle Royale genre that would be the larger target audience. Bloodlines bigger struggles are that the BR genre is already very crowded, and Bloodhunt is essentially too late to the party to get traction with what it offers to set it apart.

              While perhaps a bit of of sticky comparison, Bloodhunt had the potential to be the equivalent to EVE Online: a small dedicated fanbase to a slightly higher learning curve game than average for the genre, but with enough uniqueness to draw people in and keep them. Much like VtM was never going to unseat D&D as the biggest RPG around, EVE was never really competition for bigger games like WoW. But EVE is a good game that knows it's niche and how to occupy it such that it hasn't gone anywhere (to the point where in some ways it inspires a form of success WoW doesn't: fans that are happy with the game and don't want to find something else after awhile).

              If Bloodhunt came out earlier, I think it would be in a similar position to EVE: a little quirky, a little different, and thus can stand out in the genre as a game that might not be for everyone, but the people it is for love it. But now BR games have already ground through the audience's desire for experimentation of the formula and they just want great versions of what everyone know works (aka the big names stay big because they're that). If a new game wants to break into the genre in a serious fashion, it needs to be great BR game and offer something groundbreaking to be attractive to players. Bloodhunt is a probably on the line between good and great as a BR, but it's features just aren't big enough to make most people stick around. It does do some cool things, but nothing revolutionary.

              --------------

              Honestly I think they should have focused on the asymmetrical action MMO market rather than BR. There's so much more room for innovation in the asymmetrical space, it's a more accessible genre to causal and new players, and it lets you lean into the VtM setting/lore.

              Like, just off the top of my head, instead of the standard "one superpowered bad guy vs. a group of low powered normal folks" model you see in games like Dead by Daylight, you could have a three way match between vampires, hunters, and humans. In a big twist on the genre, the mortal faction of players operate as both cover for the other two (vampires need to hide among humans to avoid getting outnumbered and mowed down, hunters need to hide among humans to successfully find and ambush vampires first), and you can have mortal that ally with the other factions get perks to help them with their own win-conditions (get cool gear from hunters that helps you survive on the grounds that you'll help fight the undead, or become a ghoul for powers of your own).

              You mix together the social intrigue of Among Us, with the horror action of Dead by Daylight, and the neater combat mechanics that went into Bloodhunt instead and you'd have a pretty solid game that would really be unique in a way that could capture a lot of player.

                  I mostly (but not completely) agree with the main points of CTPhipps here: a lot will depend on Bloodlines 2 and, above that, the upcoming TV shows (lets conflate TV and Streaming for ease of reference).

                  But first it is important to understand the business model Paradox is using by now. They're not investing in that many video-games. Actually, they didn't invested in anything beyond Bloodlines 2.

                  What Paradox does for now is just licensing. You approach them with a project, and they either green-light it or not for using the IP. They get a cut of the profits, but otherwise you're on your own to make the game. Recently they expanded this to make licensing even easier and maybe give some help with your project, but the core idea is the same.

                  That's it. Every game we saw is the personal project of an independent developer or small starting company, and the fact they keep coming is just a result of how many people are willing to give it a try. And while the number of projects isn't small, size matters here, no one approached them yet with a big project but Blood Hunt for now, all those games we got are small fries by people with nothing to lose. The lack of robust projects by now is starting to be an issue.

                  Paradox is a games company by itself, a rising star at that, just not with anything related to WoD and they didn't put their own studios to work on it aside for Bloodlines 2, which we don't know how much they're actually involved. Their main business for now is occupied with their titles of strategy that are already making good money. I can't blame them, but WoD is actually just a side project.

                  For now, the lack of experienced developers with solid projects (not necessarily big) is putting even greater pressure on Bloodlines 2. Unless in their current model they manage to find the rare Indie Gem they're after to change the tides, a lot on the video-game side of their business will depend on Bloodlines 2's performance.

                  On the other side, though, the real big money was always meant to be live action TV. Success in any other field can affect how much they can attract TV projects to begin with, or recover from an eventual blow if their TV debut goes wrong. But a good performance on TV will have a far more palpable effect on everything else. IF they manage to dig gold on TV, then the WoD IP may very well flip to be the most lucrative thing on their hands by now. That's their best bet, and the biggest impact we'll see on the IP's future.

                                                      The “World of Darkness” began with the Vampire: The Masquerade table-top role-playing game in 1991. Soon there were other titles like Werewolf: The Apocalypse, Mage: The Ascension, Orpheus and Hunter: The Reckoning. There have been World of Darkness video games, novels, and more, with the common thread being that they put monsters and outsiders at the center of the stories.

                                                      And now, the World of Darkness is getting the cinematic universe treatment courtesy of Oscar-nominated Arrival screenwriter Eric Heisserer, according to The Hollywood Reporter. He’ll be working with writer Christine Boylan, who’s worked on whos like The Punisher and Castle. The whole thing will be produced by Hivemind, which is behind shows like The Witcher and The Expanse.

                                                      We don’t know exactly what form the World of Darkness cinematic universe will take, but films and TV shows are a good place to start. “The World of Darkness story universe is deliberately and unapologetically inclusive and diverse,” Boylan said in a statement. “It has always made a point of including equal gendered characters, protagonists and antagonists of every race, and representation of all creeds — bringing a female and diverse audience to gaming like nothing prior.

                                                      Its games and fandom are a place where women, POC, and the LGBTQI community feel welcome and we are very proud to bring these stories to life.”
                                                      Heisserer, who’s also the creator and showrunner behind Netflix’s Shadow and Bone series, was also psyched: “The legacy of these stories is way ahead of its time, inviting issues and perspectives other games ignored. This feels like the next step for genre.”

                                                      Finally, Hivemind president and cofounder Jason Brown sold people on Boylan and Heisserer’s Word of Darkness bonafides: “Eric and Christine are among the most extraordinary creators working today, and they’re also gamers who have played in WOD since it began. It’s a rare opportunity when the connection between storyteller and story runs this deep, and that is the alchemy which has led to many of our favorite and most culturally resonant franchises.”

                                                          The amount of corpo-speak in this mixed with the fact that the Shadow and Bones adaptation, while visually well done, was not faithful to the source material in favor of the equivalent of racial diversity, makes me worried that this will be another example of telling an alphabet soup story with fangs, rather than a vampire story that happens to have alphabet soup characters. This is in the same vein as the director of Dog Soldiers wanted to make a soldier movie with werewolves, rather than a werewolf movie that had soldiers in it. For some that is a tricky distinction, but it is an important one.

                                                          That is of course before we touch on the elephant in the room, which is that every gaming group makes the game their own. Their own house rules, their own flavor, and most importantly their own decisions on what themes are important. Which means no one vision for a TV show or movie will make most people happy, let alone all the fans. This is the reason why the D&D movies and tv shows have always been sub-par over the decades.

                                                          Why am I getting this uneasy feeling that Kindred: the Embraced will wind up being a high benchmark? Bearing in mind that series had the vampires day walking if they recently fed, no ghouls ever, Gangrel acted like Brujah, Brujah acted like Ventrue, Nosferatu had Protean & Blood Magic, Assamites were shape shifters, neither the Tremere or Malkavians existed, and the previous prince stepped down because it was time to retire due to vampires going into torpor because of age somehow.
