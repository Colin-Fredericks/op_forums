                                There are two ways to look at it. One is which discipline gets underrated most often in a vacuum, and that's Animalism for the various reasons on which I don't need to elaborate because they've already been stated.

                                The other is which discipline gets underrated in relation to its effective power ceiling; that is to say, players think its power and utility is "here" when it's really "up there" and how great that difference is. That is, by a wide margin, Auspex. Auspex in the right hands is the one discipline to rule them all, and most players seem to think it's "pretty good" to "great" -- usually because they've just never seen it used by someone truly fluent in its use.

                                Animalism would easily beat Auspex out in terms of players' perceptions versus reality, rather than just how often it's underestimated at tables. But, as others pointed out, Animalism's power is effectively capped by how generous the ST is allowing players to use it.

                                    I think Auspex (esp. high dots in Auspex) suffers similarly to Animalism in terms of "player expectations vs. actual ceiling," because it's also a fairly ST dependent Discipline. What your PC manages to learn from emotional impressions on an object, can skim out of a target's mind, or can find via Astral Projection aren't locked in stone as having to have specific plot-relevant positive impacts. How important what you learn is, is up to whatever answers the ST gives you (esp. if your ST is a stickler for the "reveal things cryptically" stuff).

                                    The number of times I've run into players that default to Auspex "spam" where they keep using it until they're satisfied they've gotten everything the ST is willing to part with implies most players to realize how much potential the Discipline has... as do enough STs that players feel like they're not being given easy access to those heights.

                                        Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
                                        I think Auspex (esp. high dots in Auspex) suffers similarly to Animalism in terms of "player expectations vs. actual ceiling," because it's also a fairly ST dependent Discipline. What your PC manages to learn from emotional impressions on an object, can skim out of a target's mind, or can find via Astral Projection aren't locked in stone as having to have specific plot-relevant positive impacts. How important what you learn is, is up to whatever answers the ST gives you (esp. if your ST is a stickler for the "reveal things cryptically" stuff).
                                        I agree to a certain extent, but the difference to me is in how ST's interpret and perceive the disciplines, and what incentives and disincentives ST's face through player character use.

                                        With Auspex, there's a heavy incentive for ST's to be generous with levels 2-4 as it helps prevent derail. It's a quick and easy way for an ST to put their thumb on the scale while making the player feel like they've accomplished something...especially with core book suggestions for Auspex to activate involuntarily at times, and for ST's to roll on players' behalf to keep them from knowing if they've succeeded or by how great a margin.

                                        Ideally the roll is just there to preserve player suspension of disbelief, and you're feeding them the information they need to connect story beats/plot points regardless of it. Sure it's cheaty, but the utility outweighs the cheatiness. Works best with a Malkavian PC in the coterie, but in a pinch any clan with Auspex does the trick.

                                        With Animalism, there's a mild disincentive as social scenes with animal servitors (or solo scenes with possession) tend to exclude other players. Sure, that applies for astral projection too, but generally less so -- bare minimum, an ST can play animals in ways that amuse everyone at the table while still being of narrative benefit.
