                Well, Columbus was italian, who is a language close to latin. Only Linnaeus were swedish, but his surname already had a latinized origin from birth (the "ae" at the end is typically a latinized form), while his first name is Carl, wich is a Germanized version of a latin name and one of the most common names in Europe. In fact, it's the same name as mine in a different form - Carl in swedish; in portuguese, spanish, french and italian, Carlos (my name), Karl in german (my username) and in english it became Charles - so technically I also have the same first name as the prince of England, and Charles V of the HRE and Carlos Martel of France and many many others. The latinized form of which is "Carolus". Linnaeu also adopted a french version after nobilization, "Carl Von Liné", a mix of French (the surname) and german (the "Von" title"), from the time that French was the "common tongue" of Europe.

                Also, "Christopher" is the english form of the guy's name, his original name in italian was Cristoforo Colombo. In latim it was Christophorus Columbus. And "Nicholas" is also only the english form of Copernicus, and his name has a ton of variations for each language, one for english, german, french, spanish and portuguese. The original polish version of which was Mikołaj Kopernik. In the case of Copernicus, it isnt that his name was "latinized", but rather that it was "italianized", his most popular name outside german or polish are variations of the italian variation, given the importance of the italian city states at the time. So, it isnt so much a "latinization" of the names, but rather which language was more important at each time, from italian to french to english, sometimes even to german.

                                            I think it's best to just accept that the official names and many game terms are an artifact of it being an RPG. The writers could either ransack obsolete or foreign words to come up with something that sounds "real" and evocative, or make up nonsense words that would likely sound false to people's ears. The actual terms use in the setting might be different, but don't worry about it. Verisimilitude can only go so far.

                                            The other thing I tell players in my chronicles is that each of the clans have had many names in different times and cultures. Obviously none of the clans were known by their corebook names during the times of Sargon of Akkad, Cyrus the Great, Julius Caesar, and so on. Likewise even now we have different clan names today if you want to go by certain geographical sourcebooks. The current names as we know them from the corebook is just from an "official" standardization of terms used by the Camarilla for the purposes of instructions to the Justicars and their use in Conclaves and such sometime around the 1600s or 1700s. They were probably chosen as some were popular nicknames at the time much like "kooks", "blue bloods", "sewer rats", etc. are used today. That was just to provide clarity so people wouldn't argue that someone wasn't addressing the entire clan, but only a particular lineage or clan members in one particular part of the world. But the terms became popular and are used mainly exclusively today. The variety of older terms are still known, but mostly not used except by the elders in private conversations, when someone from a certain geographical region wants to make a point on how they are different from other members of their clan elsewhere, or perhaps by some Toreador when they really want to be poetic.

                                            I sometimes scribble down alternate names in my notes in case I ever want elders to speak in ways that showcase the difference in age between them and the PCs. Having Critias use a different name for his clan than the Brujah PC does helps gets the point across this a man from a different time despite his ability to fit in the contemporary world.

                                                Not necessarily.

                                                While I'm not sold on changing their names, either, the idea that Latin names would have raised in prominence does make sense.

                                                Most people weren't literate in Latin and couldn't really speak it. But that doesn't mean they had no familiarity with it and couldn't learn a bunch of names, they definitely could and did. Indeed, for most of the Middle Ages most people in Europe would hear Latin spoken at least once a week during Mass, and almost every Roman Catholic in the world kept hearing it at Mass until 1965.

                                                It was not just a language of nobility. It was the language of erudition because it was the Lingua Franca of Europe, in a time when books took so much work to copy that translating was a waste, better to do it in Latin so you can pass on without worrying about the language of the receiver (individual books circled the world a lot back then).

                                                So a Latin name would be extremely useful as even if its meaning in the actual context of the language would be unknown for most, the name itself would be easy to recognize across Europe, and the spelling would be easier to understand even if with some accent.

                                                Also, we don't know how many people really spoke Latin back then, because while they could be illiterate in it, speaking it would still be very useful. People traveled as much or maybe more than they travel today, and many, maybe most, would do a pilgrimage at least once in their lives, which meant a long journey through many countries. Between the personal need to have a common language when asking directions and the mere exposure to foreign travelers using it, it is well within the realm of possibility that Latin was still widely spoken, even if in a broken, patchwork way.
