    I don't think the questions about BG3 or GTA6 are about the specifics of those franchises, but rather how franchises have evolved since Bloodlines 1 came out. GTA is basically the current king of open world gaming and has come a huge way from where it started. The BG series is more to the point as both the resurrection of an older dormant franchise, an adaptation of a TTRPG, and something that didn't come from a major "AAA" studio (they were basically just the Divinity series studio until BG3 happened). It's not "we're expecting BL2 to be as expansive as GTA6, or adapt the fantasy tropes and mechanics of BG3, why is that not happening?" but rather, "Given the success of BG3 in resurrecting an older TTRPG based property and the kind of experiences we're expecting out of GTA6, how is that going to meet the expectations of a sequel to Bloodlines 1?"

    I agree the "journalism" on display in these articles is unfair in it's framing, but the point they seem to be getting at isn't so far off from points 1, 2, and 4 above.

                                A TTRPG player and UE5 programmer I know once said of UE5 that it's a lot like GURPS: if you can do everything you need to do with the core book and maybe one or two supplements? It's actually a really smooth system and runs well. The problems are when you're ten supplements in to run all the different pieces you want to include, because that's when the complexity starts overloading bandwidth. For GURPS, that's mental bandwidth, for video games that's processing power (mostly CPU and GPU).

                                Optimization helps, but it's a lot of cost for the effort. UE5 isn't friendly towards code optimization, but there's a lot of people that are skilled at it if you're willing to pay them so.. that's a thing. There's also the increasing issues that are coming out of the end of the Console Wars. With Sony and Microsoft basically ceasing with console exclusives, most big video games need to run on multiple platforms, which amplifies the problem. Microsoft trying to become the Netflix of video games is another issue because the more people playing on the Game Pass cloud servers, the more giant corporate servers are running the games and optimization doesn't really matter; badly optimized games run great if you're a subscriber, which is another way to squeeze you for money.

                                                Let's be honest that true alternative paths aren't really that common a thing.

                                                For most games "not linear" just means you can wander through an area that's bigger than the main quest needs it to be, so you have unimportant things to do, but the events (even side quests) are still scripted with very little or no variation. Even when you have actual alternate paths, most of the time they'll lead you to the same points sooner or later, and endings rarely take into account many details of your journey.

                                                So while the game may be far more "linear" than we would want, let's not pretend what we want is a lot. Dialogue flavor is the main kind of alternate path anyways. If we keep too focused on this, we may end up being harsher on the linearity than the game actually deserves.
