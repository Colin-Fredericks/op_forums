            Originally posted by Mark Logan View Post
            And as I said, it doesn't seem like something specific to V5, but to the whole WoD. It seems to me that a person or group is responsible for authorizing what may or may not be published, a kind of editorial board, and these people have a very particular vision about what would be acceptable or not.
            Well, yes, that would be Paradox Interactive and their chosen staff to handle the WoD brand. Because that's how companies do things. Ever since the original WW merged with CCP, control over the content has ultimately been in the hands of people outside of the creative process. Every OPP made WoD book has had to be sent to someone else (first CCP, now Paradox) for approval; including content. If Paradox says no to something that OPP wants to do, it doesn't happen, because Paradox owns the WoD.

            What has dismayed me about these new books is that in my view, and from what I can see reading this topic and others on this forum, it is also the opinion of other people, is that the view that the person in charge has about various things in WoD it does not match the reality of the scenario itself or is a distorted view of it.
            More simply? There's a lot of people that disagree with the brand direction Paradox is taking the WoD, for a variety of reasons.

            This issue of Get of Fenris, for example, from what I read in another topic, is a tribe that attracted groups of neo-Nazis who felt represented by the culture and ideas that the tribe represents.
            Because the Get of Fenris literally had a neo-Nazi faction within their ranks. For 1e and 2e, being part of them was not grounds for other Get to kill you on sight for being an evil shit. Neo-Nazi Fenrir were tolerated as radicals among the Tribe until Revised finally said, "no, that's dumb, the Tribe doesn't tolerate them openly," and then in W20 they finally had the Get just off the Swords completely.

            It's not a shallow reading, and the editorial process lead to the Tribe being retconned to get rid of the idea that neo-Nazi Get are part of mainstream Get culture, and moving to the idea that the Get don't tolerate Nazi ideology in their ranks. The Get needed to be modified, or they were, on a deep reading, encouraging pro-Nazi sentiments.

            As far as the Sabbat is concerned, I believe the WW has failed to accept or think of a way to make the sect an acceptable option for players. I sincerely wonder if this question of much of the Sabbat not following humanity is such an "evil" idea and unacceptable to publishers that the book only found the light of day through a work of internal persuasion by Justin Achilli.
            The Sabbat book was announced as an intended V5 product well before Justin was hired.

            Originally posted by Elphilm View Post
            When Mark Rein-Hagen himself says that metaplot is stupid, I feel like I'm justified in that position. This is not an appeal to authority -- you don't have to care about what Rein-Hagen thinks one way or the other. I'm simply pointing out that one of the chief creators of the franchise does not consider metaplot a strength of the game, so it's not far-fetched to suggest that Vampire: The Masquerade is more than just its metaplot (which really only became a thing in the Revised era, as far as I can tell).
            Here's the problem (even though I agree with his assessment of metaplot as a tool for RPG books):

            Mark nearly bankrupted White Wolf, and his removal from his top position in the company coincided with the mid-2e explosion (not Revised) of both metaplot and sales that saved the company.

            White Wolf had a sizeable portion of customers that cared more about "what happens next?" to the metaplot than worrying about more useful gaming material, which added another revenue stream to make VtM and the WoD a major success during the supplement treadmill era.

            That's part of why the metaplot grew so much and by Revised was so prominent in the books (even to the point of starting to diminish its initial positives). That's why you can't really separate VtM and metaplot. We can wax on about how poorly metaplot was implemented (and if the value added was really worth it) but at the time of VtM becoming a brand that people recognized beyond some obscure little RPG, the "living world" the metaplot created was a huge part of the appeal to customers buying the books; even those of us that bought the damned metaplot focused supplements just to know what to exclude before new players came in with ideas from them.

            VtM is more than the metaplot. VtM's metaplot is stupid. But VtM's metaplot is also a significant portion of what made it what it is. It might not be a strength of the game, but it is a strength of the brand. It's extremely hard to escape the idea that most people who want to play VtM, want to do so while completely tossing the metaplot. The metaplot is essential to VtM's brand image.

                                The answer is brand identity.

                                Simple and straightforward, linking rules to a specific universe was never a thing for D&D, but was conversely THE thing with WoD (and many others).

                                It is even more important if you keep in mind that, seriously, as many people as may be waiting for better rules for the game 'cause the rules always sucked, precisely because of that no one was after V5 just for the rules. Selling a book that is pure crunch is hard, and V20 already did it splendidly, so it would be a very hard sell.

                                Would have to offer something simply astounding in terms of rules, an overhaul that was really, really, really big and really good. Something in the line with what they did, but of even greater scope, there must be more than crunch, but a reason to get that particular crunch.

                                V20 did it by offering a veritable encyclopedia of rules that would replace the entirety of your Vampire library to a single book. D&D offers really thorough changes in rules at every new edition. What would V5 offer? It is hard, given the brand identity, to offer something besides metaplot, even if this metaplot could be minimal.

                                Heck, many fans were waiting specifically to see what happened to the setting after Gehenna.
