              Originally posted by Querion View Post
              I'm trying to inform myself as much as possible about the city so as not to reduce it to the stereotyped idea of "outdoor brothel, transsexuals, carnival and Pele's t-shirts" as someone said on the forum and I was wondering if anyone who visited it or who lives can you tell me something more about its atmosphere and maybe about any plots or ideas that you can bring to the game table.

              I ask for this advice with the fear of raising a fuss given the accusations of cultural appropriation, stereotyping that he saw in the forum and commented myself, and that I understand as an Italian (yes, we are all mafia and very Catholic! XD), so have pity.
              My father's family is from Rio and I've been there several times.

              For one thing, people using light clothes most of the time is perfectly normal, and while most people won't be doing it, at any time and any place you may come across someone on swimming clothes. The city has many beaches, for all social classes, and it is really hot in summer, so that's kind of a survival thing (some people still go about on heavy clothes, they're aliens, I'm 100% sure).

              On the other hand, it is maybe as heavily Catholic as Rome itself or more. So nothing as extreme as small towns really manage to be, but still it isn't at all like a brothel. Every year several foreign tourists assume the light clothes and skimpy swimming clothes (they're real and popular, but not the only thing going around) indicate the legends are true, just to end up in jail for indecent behavior or even assault when they try to act like naturists. Some beaches do let a woman go topless nowadays, but that'll never fly about without a lot of shocked looks.

              Transsexuals aren't going around free as birds, either, only if they have a death wish. During Carnival transgender costumes are popular (not the most popular, but certainly very popular), specially for men to dress as women (the opposite is far less common. Misogyny? You can bet), and most aren't actual transgenders. The rest of the years you'll have the usual of any big city, transgender people trying to live in peace and frequently at a low profile, and some more openly transgender working at Red Light districts at night, trying to make a living and survive the combined hate against sex workers and gays. Things like the Gay Parade in Brazil happen in another city, at another state, São Paulo.

              Carnival is a big affair, but it is once a year. We actually have other places in the country that look nothing like Rio de Janeiro and have far more Carnival-related or Carnival-like festivities. For the rest of the year, just no, there's no such a thing.

              No one even remembers Pelé most of the time, but Football is certainly a huge thing. Most people like it, and even those who don't are expected to have a team of choice. Like, not cheering to anyone is actually weirder than just not liking the sport. In fact people love to exhibit the shield and colors of their team, and you'll see from rich girls with a small team shield jewelry pending from their expensive bags and their socialite mothers using it as some other subtle jewelry, to people that have everything adorned in homage to their teams, clothes, car, mug, bedroom, dog, everything. My uncle Claudio's house was almost completely black and white with red Da Gama crosses all over the place (the colors and symbol of his Vasco Da Gama team). While old people may remember with great nostalgia of the players of yore and what they see as a more artistic Football, most people will discuss current players.

              People in Rio talk with a slow accent even for Brazillian standards, and we already have a slow accent in comparison with almost anyone.Their accent have a cadence to it and a way of coming out that I can't quite describe except as being almost as half-singing, although it has no pitch accent to it, just the usual stress accent. For many Brazilians it sounds a lot as a con-man, to be honest, but I really find it a beautiful accent.

              As my parting words for now, I'd like to make the place's climate and geography clearer. Despite being known for the heat, it is actually fairly in the south of the Tropical Region and most neighboring places have a climate more similar to temperate. What happens is that its location works almost like a natural pressure cooker, the city lies between a bay and a range of mountains that traps the hot, humid air inside a giant bubble. As a result, summer temperatures easily raise above 40ºC (105 ºF) and can be as high as 45 (113), sometimes even higher. In winter it is still higher than 20 or 21 (70) most of the time, but for many Cariocas this is more than enough to put on a coat, no joke, some even rattle teeth at this point (I personally find it quite pleasurable and can barely survive there in summer). Some other cities exist in the same general area between the bay and the mountains. The most popular retreat outside the city, though, is Petrópolis, a mountain city that used to be the Imperial Summer Residence, and has a far colder climate (obviously).

              Just some random bits about the city. I can give more and some for other places in Brazil if you want.

                                  Originally posted by Querion View Post
                                  If I can afford, and if you can tell me, also out of interest in obvious preferential paths of many vampire clans, how is the difference between social classes and between favelas and rich residential areas seen by the inhabitants themselves?
                                  To answer this and add to Microcuchon's observation (it is true), I think it's best to quickly explore the history of those shanty towns in Rio, as it is very particular.

                                  During the 19th century the Abolitionist Movement was gaining force (finally) and laws that would slowly eradicate slavery were being passed according to a long-term plan by Emperor Dom Pedro II (an abolitionist himself). As the country's capital and richest city at the time, Rio de Janeiro had a huge urban population of slaves living with their owners.

                                  In 1887, though, Dom Pedro fell ill and went to Europe for treatment, leaving behind the Imperial Princess Isabel de Bragança as regent, someone who had glaring gaps in her education for rulership. When anti-abolitionists almost managed to slow down the progress of the abolition plan, one of the few matters if state that Isabel had an intense interest in, she stepped up and take drastic measures. So much so that in a matter of few days she signed the Lei Áurea (Golden Law), abolishing slavery completely and irreversibly at once, promptly ending every slave-owner relationship.

                                  And so, while the law itself wasn't a bad thing and the risk of many years of fallback on abolition was real, the city literally changed from having a large population of slaves to having a large population of homeless people.

                                  By then Rio was dotted by lush hills that weren't suitable for construction in almost any scale. But that also meant they were the only unclaimed land at a short distance, and the slaves hadn't be exiled or anything, just freed. So up they went, occupying whatever plot of free land they could in the "morros", forming scattered shanty towns in the midst of the city or near it instead of going anywhere far. And since those hills were lush and dotted the city, many with rivulets and rivers coming from them (the city was called Rio de Janeiro both due to one river of same name and the great number of rivers crossing it, although today they're either dry or, more commonly, paved over), more affluent neighborhoods weren't far.

                                  With time the favelas themselves developed, and started to attract the poor regardless due to cheaper housing and living conditions. Some are entire small or even medium-sized cities on their own by now (Rocinha, the biggest one, has 180 thousand inhabitants, and together the favelas have more than a million and a half in Rio). Being cheap, poor neighborhoods they face much the same problems that ethnically identifiable poor neighborhoods face anywhere. The State mostly failed them royally, they're seen as "breeding ground" for crime and anti-social behavior while the wealthier residents of the city ignore that their masses of employees live there.

                                  Employees that act as personal servants are very much a thing in Rio from the Middle Class upwards, with people expecting from the simplest house cleaner far more service than simply cleaning the house, unless they have the money to hire specialized, dedicated servants. This isn't uncommon anywhere in the country, but in Rio the proximity of the favelas gives a particular light to it.

                                  Combining poverty, state negligence and geographical advantage on defense, the organized crime achieved a tight control in favelas to a degree that is completely out of scale. Conflicts between drug dealers are shootings that can be heard several kilometres away and frequently make victims with stray bullets, far enough for some of them being middle class citizens in ground residential areas caught at home. Police operations in favelas are effectively military operation, with the standard vehicle being the "Caveirão" (Big Skull), a bullet-proof armed vehicle weighting above 10 tons. It is not a combat vehicle in comparison with an actual war tank, but it is almost the same in a civilian zone. Reported collateral damage in those operations is big, and unreported is said to be far bigger, so while communities usually don't nurture any love for the criminals that control the hills, the police is openly seen as just as much or even bigger a threat.

                                  Some wealthy residences are built in hills nowadays, but most of those aren't in the same ones as the favelas. Instead they're built in hills whose geography is a challenge even for favela builders, where large sums of money finance the engineering projects to make the large houses possible. But there are large mansions in the favelas too, where the wealthiest drug bosses live and keep their personal arsenals, that frequently include some military-grade weapons and ordnance, national and imported. While state-sponsored social workers are rare in the favelas, research teams and NGOs are a common presence, including international ones, negotiating their stay with the crime bosses and trying to ease the results of state negligence, or simply study the context, the challenges and the means for overcoming them that the locals develop (curiously they attract a number of engineers).

                                              Originally posted by Querion View Post
                                              Mmm, I understand. It is not very dissimilar to certain situations surrounding Italian organized crime, especially in the Naples areas, albeit not with this level of militarization of both crime and police. It's something I can have a close reference to, anyway.
                                              You were, as always, very kind and detailed.
                                              While other common aspects of organized crime and its usual relation with local police still exist in Rio, including corruption of police officials in mundane patrol, the level of militarization and territory control is what really sets Rio de Janeiro apart in this regard.

                                              Of note, an important distinction here in Brazil is that we actually have two completely distinct police forces with their own functions, rules and institutions, called Civilian and Military police. The superficial distinction is that the Civilian police handle Police Stations as the Americans know it, take care of investigations and so on, while Military police will run patrols and engage confrontations. That's actually far more complex, and the system is actually a leftover of the last Military Dictatorship, during which the Military police was created to be State Vigilance for the regime instead of for the law, and this purpose still has deep consequences.

                                              They are a Military organization, indeed (although again, rarely with the level of military-like engagement seen in Rio), but the actual police force meant to act within military facilities is instead called Police-of-the-Army.

                                              As a rule of thumb, I think it is always good, when running a game in another country, to dedicate some time to research how police and the judiciary system work there. Those are two areas where we have a tendency to take things for granted because the purpose is more or less the same everywhere, but those system have actually a lot of purely technical distinctions that are easy to miss, but also easy to understand once you do your homework, and then help for a lot of things to make more sense.
