            As of V5, Masquerade vampires are physically a little bit harder to kill, with very little that causes them aggravated damage (V5 doesn’t distinguish between bashing and lethal) and no chance of instantly killing them without it.

V20DA has some neat changes to make vampires a bit tougher too (e.g., if all your HLs are only full of bashing damage, you only count as Crippled).

            They also are not subject to fear frenzy from small fires, which is a nice change.
            RAW and RAI, you only test in V20 if the small fire poses a threat--e.g., if someone waves a cigarette near your face. Though this is a common assumption people make.

            Their ability to rouse the beast to add to attributes is somewhat more generous, as it applies to *any* attribute, not just physical ones.
            Yes, but you get the bonus for one roll only--not even for the whole turn. In V20 you keep any buffs up to your Generational maximum +1 for a whole scene, and can bump stats up beyond that (max. 10) for three turns after you stop spending blood. (This is costly, yes, but more efficient than Blood Surge in most cases.)

            Yes, in V5 you can spend the blood to gain bonus dice on any Attribute, but as others have noted on other threads, for elders this is a poor substitute for traits over five to begin with. Compare and contrast pre-V5 Helena and V5 Helena for example. One is epic all the time; the other is epic for brief moments when she spends blood.

            Their blush of life is also more generous, lasting the whole night instead of a scene.
            This is a good bonus for V5. Though, the stronger Disciplines and Bearing modifiers in V20 mean Blush of Health isn't as necessary.

            I like the flexibility of Bearings, though. E.g., you might get a bonus to Stealth if you follow the right Path, and that may be more fitting for your character concept than being a wolf in sheep's clothing.

            Discipline-wise, again, the design is significantly different, with V5’s disciplines being collections of similarly-themed powers rather than refinements of a single core power. They’re more versatile and variable but not as potent as the ones in Requiem, at least not upfront.

            Unlike Requiem, V5 limits all traits at 5 dots and doesn’t allow bloodlines to have unique disciplines, though you can see in 2e Requiem is already moving away from the latter. This may make elders appear weaker in V5 if one’s only understanding of elder strength is having disciplines over 5 dots, but in reality they have a wide and unpredictable range of powers, bonus dice to use and resist disciplines, faster healing, more bonus dice to attributes when rousing the beast, and a greater ability to do all those things without increasing hunger (which means they can do more of them at one time and/or keep it up for longer).
            I feel this is possibly misleading. Yes, elders, like neonates, have a more pick and mix platter of Discipline powers, but those powers are frequently weaker and they're still limited to only five powers per Discipline.

            Since XP awards are significantly lower in V5 (despite cheaper XP costs overall), it'll also take elders longer to acquire that suite of powers.

            Many V20 elders can get Disciplines at 6+, which gives them way more versatility in general, since most of these powers are broader than those in V5.

            True, some of these elder level powers were nerfed and are offered as lower level V5 powers, but they generally replace the more versatile V20 powers (e.g., Potence adding to every Strength dice pool instead of a collection of one-off feats of Strength). Having five broad powers is better than having five narrower ones, and if you have elders at 7th Generation or lower, those six, seven, eight or even nine powers begin to massively outweigh the five narrower powers.

            Everything else you describe is also offered by V20 in the form of increased blood pool and increased blood per turn spend.

            In effect it’s not that different from what you see in Requiem’s elders, except with greater unpredictability because of how the dice and hunger system works, so that it’s possible for a Masquerade elder to use disciplines and heal and add to attributes nonstop without ever running out of power during the scene). They’re operating on the same scale as neonates but are generally much better at everything.
            Yes, this is true. But conversely, that same randomisation means said elder could hit Hunger 5 much quicker than a V20 elder runs out of blood points. So it depends if you want more variability in outcomes on this particular element.

            The benefit here is that neonates do always have a chance to luck out, which is good. In V20 and earlier, you need to plan ahead a lot more if you want to take out an elder. You need numbers and every single advantage you can get, and even then, there's a serious risk you'll die.

            But you can win against an elder. We played through the Diablerie adventures for a laugh one year, and it is possible to slay and diablerise an elder (usually while they're sleeping, though). I also remember doing Nights of Prophecy and Baba Yaga being diablerised by the coterie when one of them frenzied (thereby ignoring wound penalties).

            Partially, this is due to the way VTM has always privileged those who attack rather than those who defend, so if you're lucky with your initiative roll, you can be deadly. When initiative became Dex+Wits+d10 it became more swingy than when it was a straight dice pool roll. V5's initiative may be better for avoiding that.

            So, all in all, there are different strengths and weaknesses, but I still think V20 elders eclipse V5 ones. When one side is rolling a maximum of 10-18 dice before blood buffing (for anything up to 4th Generation), and the other is rolling a maximum of 10 (regardless of Generation), it just seems obvious to me that this isn't an even fight (plus the V20 elders might then add on extra dice from the physical Disciplines too).

                Originally posted by AnubisXy View Post
                Sometimes a company decides to stick with something for awhile figuring people will start coming around, especially if it's early on. 4th edition D&D lasted for 6 years (2008-2014), most of which it spend being outsold by Pathfinder (which was basically the old 3rd edition).
                Actually, it was worse than that. 4E attempted a relaunch closer to older editions with Essentials in 2010 and the last product for 4E was released in early 2012 and was a fluff only book (no mechanics at all). The rest of 2012-2014 was spent instead on the D&DNext surveys and playtest documents as they pulled every trick in the book to get older players back by convincing them they were listening to the actual fans this time instead of to some “vision” or “theories.”

                In the context of VtM, I think there is probably some middle ground between V20 and V5 where the vast majority of players (say 80% with about 10% outliers in either direction) would actually be happy with the result. But that would require the developers to bend on their vision because “playable Sabbat” is definitely one of points you’d need to hit that level of support for the overall fanbase.

                Also worth pointing out is that one of the key selling points of V20 is that it was largely “setting agnostic” in terms of presenting the base information and mechanics needed to play without tying itself to a specific point in the metaplot. By contrast, V5’s rules, particularly limiting generation to double digits is really tied to the Beckoning/Gehenna War metaplot period (and the Withering metaplot if you wanted to say the express loss of elder disciplines and 6+ stats is a recent development instead of having always been the case).

                The reason I bring this up is that, as D&D has always proven, its a lot easier to sell a more generic mechanical system that metaplot can be easily tacked onto (i.e. 5e’s system isn’t joined at the hip to the current Forgotten Realms metaplot), than to sell a metaplot specific system.

                Frankly, I think you could probably even mechanically merge the Chronicles of Darkness into that 80% happy fans crowd by just offering different interpretations of shared mechanics (i.e. Blood Potency to Generation) for that setting and basically have VtR as essentially Dragonlance to VtM’s Forgotten Realms. V5 is already halfway there with its switch to the CoD attributes, Blood Potency, Humanity as sole morality meter (which is also redefined relative to its previous VtM meaning) and fixed difficulty numbers.

                Pull back the surge mechanics to the Blood Points shared by V20 and Requiem and change Humanity to something that instead is essentially a “Resist the Beast” stat (where so long as you have personal tenants that oppose the Beast’s influence it doesn’t particularly matter what they are; which enables Sabbat and Dark Ages roads to be easily applied to the core mechanic) and find a level of common ground between the disciplines shared by V20, V5 and Requiem (with version specific disciplines in various splats) and I think it’s something that’s eminently doable.

                A solid shared mechanical core, with mix and match splats for playing various aspects of Masquerade (i.e. here’s how you interpret “Blood Potency” as Generation and the “resist beast” score for a Camarilla game as Humanity and a Sabbat game as Paths), Requiem or a GM’s homebrew urban fantasy setting (ex. take the Sabbat material and create something like the VanHelsing series where a post-apocalyptic world is ruled by inhuman vampires).

                A mix-and-match approach would also work well in expanding out into the other supernatural critters as well. Maybe a GM likes Masquerade, but prefers the Lost Changelings over the Dreaming variety... and a shared mechanical base would allow you to plug VtM + CtL into that GM’s setting easily.

                The degree to which the WoD/CoD has always relied on “fluff” to shape their settings as much or more than mechanics I think would be a major strength to such an approach and taking lessons from how D&D lost and then regained and expanded its share of the fantasy tabletop market and trying to apply those to the World of Darkness seems like a worthwhile endeavor even if the divide between V20, V5 and CoD is not as vast as it was for D&D c. 2012.

                                                That's... not really accurate.

                                                Stripping books is a very old practice in the publishing world. Sellers and distributors rip off the covers of books and send them back to the publisher for a refund (serving as proof the book wasn't sold, and saving on shipping by not sending the whole book back) to clear out inventory without taking more of a loss on unsold books. This was happening to WoD books long before the CofD was decided on. Refund rates tend to be higher when there's a new big thing the distributor wants to push (like a new edition, or a brand new line like the CofD), so stripping is worse around those events.

                                                During the heyday of brick & mortar gaming stores, RPG books tended to be safer from stripping because in gamer culture "old" turns into "collectable" which means small sellers tended to not strip their books for refunds because it was better for them to maintain a back catalog. But the WoD was big enough to get sold in major retailers, which means WoD books were getting stripped all the time, because national chains do not keep old books on shelves that fresh stock could be on instead.

                                                There's nothing nefarious about this. Pushing for stripping was (and in many parts of the publishing world still is) standard practice to help get people to buy new books instead of old books taking up space. It was bad across the industry at the time because when the RPG bubble burst, small stores were desperate and had to do things they normally might not to try and stay afloat (and most didn't anyway).
