    Menele and Helena’s Backstories

    10-05-2025, 06:10 PM
    I am running a V20 game using the old 1st edition material - starting in Gary with Modius and the gang, eventually expanding out into Chicago by Night. And reading CbN, I was struck by something odd.

    Obviously, Menele and Helena are standins for Meneleus and Helen of Troy; what’s odd is, their backstories don’t line up with each other, even though each of their backstories does appear to include a version of the events of the legend.

    Ie, Menele was a Greek king whose wife was kidnapped by another ancient city-state and he went to war to get her back, winding up embraced as a result. And Helena runs off with Paris - er, Prias - to his far off city, sparking a similar war.

    But they are embraced centuries apart, and in Helena’s version of the story, her husband is Minos, not Meneleus. And yet both backstories seem to strongly hint at the Trojan War resulting from each of their origin stories.

    These backstories are both from the same book, so I feel like these contradictions must be intentional, but I am a bit confused. I know there are some folks knowledgeable about the lore here - does anyone have any insight?

                  I think Mark Rein Hagen (?) was being clever here and going with the idea that they have a lot of references to the legendary characters but aren't them. They're either the inspirations for them or they are people who took their present names from the mythological characters. The latter is one I actually prefer. Neither Helena or Menele are the "original" but have adopted the identities due to being so old that they don't recall their original identities.

                  Remember, the writer is aware of what he's doing because they aren't backstories that refer to Troy in the slightest. It was Carthage (which happened millennia later--Troy happened in 1148 BC and Carthage 146 BC) that was the event that destroyed Menele and Helena's relationship.

                  If this was Requiem, it's possible both of them suffer from the fog of the ages.

                  But it's also possible if you want to be literal that Helena and Menele did the whole Troy thing but that was a thousand years before their next breakup.

                              Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post

                              Remember, the writer is aware of what he's doing because they aren't backstories that refer to Troy in the slightest. It was Carthage (which happened millennia later--Troy happened in 1148 BC and Carthage 146 BC) that was the event that destroyed Menele and Helena's relationship.
                              That’s part of what is so weird about it, actually - their backstories both do tie into the events of the Trojan War story, but in both cases, it is their origin of how they became vampires.

                              In Menele’s case, they say he was a Greek king who went to war against an unnamed city in Asia Minor after they kidnapped his wife. Due to the war, he caught the attention of a vampire patron of that unnamed city - Troile (and I feel like the name has got to be an intentional pun, right?) So the events described by Homer seem to have happened in some way - even though his wife and the other city are unnamed.

                              Meanwhile, in Helena’s backstory, she is said to have been forced into an arranged marriage with a king who was also a vampire named Minos. She fell in love with Prias, a prince of an unnamed city state in Asia Minor, and fled with him to his city. Minos went to war to get her back.

                              In both cases, their mortal stories seem very directly tied to the Trojan War, and their corresponding character’s place in it - but they also contradict each other. Helena is embraced centuries earlier than Menele, and in her version the king is a different guy. (Menele’s wife is never named in his backstory.)

                              Their rivalry as vampires doesn’t start until Carthage - but it is very weird that they each basically have a mortal origin tied to the Trojan War, but in ways that contradict each other.
