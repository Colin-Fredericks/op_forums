    By the same highly reductive token as this, the Anarch philosophy boils down to little more than "I do what I want however I want and no one can tell me otherwise" and it's the transformation of the original Anarchs into the Sabbat that allowed them to stop and think about what that actually means as far as transhumanism, as far as something they could exist on with spiritual coherence, as far as not simply fighting to be free, which is the main rationale for why the Anarchs came into being, but what it actually means to have and do something with the freedom of your own existence.

    The Anarchs, from this perspective, became the Sabbat, because the Anarchs offer little to nothing when they don't have something to fight against, because they have little to nothing when someone isn't defining their lives for them, even to shake a fist at, as shown by their eager readiness to turn even on each other.

    The Anarchs came into being, elder manipulations to create them aside, as much to fight for their own survival as anything, being fed into the Inquisition as pawns as they were. The Sabbat later considered the question of "what does being worthy of survival mean?" And the answer they came up with was monstrous and from a human perspective, completely insane, but they certainly put that answer forward.

    Even V5 reflects this in a way. The Anarchs became more spiritual (taking in the Ministry and various cults previous forms of the Movement would have regarded as sickening) because the Anarch way alone holds no meaning or value to sustain an unlife on in the long run, it's just rage and shaking a fist. So in V5, the Anarchs became more like the Sabbat, and the Sabbat went back to being nothing more than Anarchs, and as a result became a hollow play of themselves.

    Now, is that a narrow and super reductive take that ignores a variety of material in order to stress a particular reading? Absolutely.

    But so is what you just posted about the Sabbat. You can't think that anyone who is into the sect and dislikes it being made into a narrow, one note, unplayable version of itself would be happy with being told "they're just a variant of the super cool Anarchs anyway."

                                        Originally posted by MarkK View Post
                                        V5 will continue to do its thing, which amongst other things at this point (outside of the videogames and what have you) is basically be a table top rpg product line shepherded by some people in their late 40s and verging 50s with a very narrow view on how VtM should be played, who want people in their 20s to be under the impression that this frankly very old take on this game is the new sexy perfected form of VtM. When they call anything in the middle of that "legacy gaming" in a way that feels like "go away into that corner", or talk about how that book someone liked only existed because of "the supplement treadmill", the vibe for the gameline is a condescending one at best.
                                        I do think there is something inherently interesting in who is writing the game now, how they view the world, and the ultimate consequence for the nature of the game. Back when Vampire: the Masquerade was first published, the writers at the time where trying to do something different from the game landscape of the era. Their frame of reference was literature and academia, the monsters were metaphorical, and the challenges faced by the players revolved around characters and interpersonal drama. There is a reason why a number of the characters in the first edition of Chicago by Night were derived from characters in The Jungle, and one gets the sense that the author hadn't merely read the book for the purposes of research.

                                        Of course, it didn't always work, but this was something fresh and different, and fascinating to anyone who had started out with mainstream games. Those of us who were young at the time played these games, became invested in the universe, and made the World of Darkness into one of the great sales success it was during the 1990s.

                                        And then we grew up.

                                        In my case, I worked, completed a PhD, and ended up working for a very large commercial organisation. I read actual literary works, and write material for my employer which is more widely consumed than the average V5 supplement. I do write Vampire: the Dark Ages supplements for the Storyteller's Vault in my spare time, as it is a break from the super-serious work I actually do for proper money. I see the flaws of the old White Wolf books more readily than I did when they were first published, but also see the intellectual and creative potential. Which is why I sometimes post on this forum.

                                        Meanwhile, I feel that the people who take the professional lead in the game have taken a very different path. They have been very immersed in the gaming scene for decades; their frame of reference for the world are the supplements of games, and popular culture. They frequently have no sense of how people behave outside of the material they consume, hence the construction of the Camarilla which similtaneously portrays them as incredibly shallow and comically evil. They think of the world in terms of superhero comics, with the notion of continuity focused on namechecking parts of the fictional universe, whilst treating death as a temporary inconvenience to popular characters. They write like they have never read a proper book.

                                        And this is, of course, why we have so many problematic aspects in the modern World of Darkness. They are creating an inherently conservative game, whilst thinking that they are radicals, producing art. They promote dubious, reactionary politics, whilst imagining they are progressives. They have taken what should be a popular intellectual property into the doldrums.

                                        Now, as I have pointed out before, Vampire: the Masquerade should be far more successful than it is at present. It's a game set in the contemporary world, with sexy bloodsuckers, and the potential to capitalise upon modern political concerns and the power of vampires in modern popular culture. That it isn't more of a success suggests that somewhere, something has gone badly wrong.

                                                            I do think there is something inherently interesting in who is writing the game now, how they view the world, and the ultimate consequence for the nature of the game. Back when Vampire: the Masquerade was first published, the writers at the time where trying to do something different from the game landscape of the era. Their frame of reference was literature and academia, the monsters were metaphorical, and the challenges faced by the players revolved around characters and interpersonal drama. There is a reason why a number of the characters in the first edition of Chicago by Night were derived from characters in The Jungle, and one gets the sense that the author hadn't merely read the book for the purposes of research.

                                                            Of course, it didn't always work, but this was something fresh and different, and fascinating to anyone who had started out with mainstream games. Those of us who were young at the time played these games, became invested in the universe, and made the World of Darkness into one of the great sales success it was during the 1990s.

                                                            And then we grew up.

                                                            In my case, I worked, completed a PhD, and ended up working for a very large commercial organisation. I read actual literary works, and write material for my employer which is more widely consumed than the average V5 supplement. I do write Vampire: the Dark Ages supplements for the Storyteller's Vault in my spare time, as it is a break from the super-serious work I actually do for proper money. I see the flaws of the old White Wolf books more readily than I did when they were first published, but also see the intellectual and creative potential. Which is why I sometimes post on this forum.

                                                            Meanwhile, I feel that the people who take the professional lead in the game have taken a very different path. They have been very immersed in the gaming scene for decades; their frame of reference for the world are the supplements of games, and popular culture. They frequently have no sense of how people behave outside of the material they consume, hence the construction of the Camarilla which similtaneously portrays them as incredibly shallow and comically evil. They think of the world in terms of superhero comics, with the notion of continuity focused on namechecking parts of the fictional universe, whilst treating death as a temporary inconvenience to popular characters. They write like they have never read a proper book.

                                                            And this is, of course, why we have so many problematic aspects in the modern World of Darkness. They are creating an inherently conservative game, whilst thinking that they are radicals, producing art. They promote dubious, reactionary politics, whilst imagining they are progressives. They have taken what should be a popular intellectual property into the doldrums.

It's a game set in the contemporary world, with sexy bloodsuckers, and the potential to capitalise upon modern political concerns and the power of vampires in modern popular culture. That it isn't more of a success suggests that somewhere, something has gone badly wrong.
