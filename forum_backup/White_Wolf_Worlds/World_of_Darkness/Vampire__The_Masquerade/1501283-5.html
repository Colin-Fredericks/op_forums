    Originally posted by Bluecho View Post
    Yeah, whatever cultural cache VtM had back in the early 00s has largely been spent over the two decades since the game ended. The 20th Anniversary Edition helped to rekindle its cult following, sure. But if it served to repair some of the broken base, V5 only broken the base in new and exciting ways. Nor did that new edition succeed in really propelling VtM to new popular attention, despite cross-collaboration with the likes of Critical Role. Nor have big attempts to establish it within the gaming space before or after V5 succeeded. Whether it's the MMO that fell to development hell, the Bloodlines sequel that still hasn't materialized and seems no closer to arriving, or the narrative games that seemed to garner more controversy than attention.

    The original Bloodlines seems to be the only lasting legacy among most people who aren't in the tabletop space.

    It's worth observing that within the big controversy over D&D's OGL, and the resulting mass examination of TTRPG alternatives to that system, you don't see many people talking about VtM. Plenty talking about Pathfinder, Call of Cthulhu, and the Old School Renaissance. But scarce little, from what I've observed, about Vampire: The Masquerade.

    I wouldn't say VtM is "dead" (no pun intended). But I wouldn't say its cultural penetration is growing. Not by a long shot.
    Honestly, the first step towards something like regaining some momentum even just in the TTRPG sphere would be to take advantage of the release of the SRD 5.1 under Creative Commons to do a “VtM5e” (as in VtM as expressed using D&D5e mechanics vs. V5).

    The fact is that in the present zeitgeist D&D5e has been the gateway to ttrpgs for an entire generation and there are whole groups out there who aren’t interested in looking at anything that doesn’t use something close to its style of mechanics (a lot has been done on the margins… see the fan produced Star Wars 5e).

    D&D5e itself has so little actual mechanical spine to it that it’d mostly be a matter of figuring out how you want the archetypes to fit into its structure (ex. I’d be inclined towards the “races” being the Clans providing minor buff and clan weakness, then abusing the multiclassing rules such that the “classes” would be the discipline paths (and run say only 5 levels) and you start by default at level 3… D&D backgrounds becomes packages of VtM’s backgrounds… Feats are the equivalent of merits or not used in favor of only ASIs).

    Would it be something anyone here is particularly interested in? Probably not. But only appealing to those already here is just preaching to the choir, it’s not reaching anyone outside the already existing sphere you’d need to again make VtM mainstream.

                Originally posted by Chris24601 View Post
                Honestly, the first step towards something like regaining some momentum even just in the TTRPG sphere would be to take advantage of the release of the SRD 5.1 under Creative Commons to do a “VtM5e” (as in VtM as expressed using D&D5e mechanics vs. V5).
                That doesn't really seem likely to work.

                First, there's a d20 based WoD game out there already made during the 3.X days. While not a flop, Monte Cooke's WoD is more of a novelty item than a serious pull for D&D focused players to look at the WoD. If it didn't work during the first OGL boom, what would make it more appealing now?

                Second, there's a rather large push back against using the OGL that's managed to hit beyond RPG insiders and people that are into the hobby way more than playing D&D because that's what people are playing. The recent events around the OGL has seen a major upswing in people looking for other options. While d20 clones like Pathfinder are going to snag the lion's share of that, it's probably the biggest dent in the public perception that "RPG = D&D" since the height of the WoD's 90s era prominence. We're seeing a lot of community support for moving away from WotC run SRDs and towards things like ORC or just doing your own d20 inspired system (like Onyx20 is going to be starting with Pugmire).

                Third, but closely related to the second point there, is that we've seen a slow but incremental level of success of non-d20 inspired systems getting to wider audiences. One of the biggest drivers of this is that companies looking to license out their IPs to make RPGs are increasing looking at a wider variety of systems. Twenty years ago, everyone wanted an OGL d20 game for their IP... and it mostly lead to a lot of duds. We're seeing a lot more success in RPGs of existing properties partially because companies are being savvier about shopping for systems that are more likely to appeal to their target audience. There's an ever increasing recognition that "non-D&D brand with D&D rules doesn't get you D&D sales" and that sales are better with a system that works of people that want your brand's RPG than trying to force into the d20 mold.

                The big problem for trying to get back to the WoD's former glory, is that space is more crowded now. It used to be D&D, then WoD in a distance second, and then everyone else way behind that in public awareness. There are a lot more companies in the "never going to be WotC, but still big enough a lot of non-hardcore gamers have heard of us," zone. The WoD needs to stand out in that space again, not try to latch on to a licensing scheme everyone else in the same space is fleeing.

                                                    I don't think most setting changes are about drumming up interest. The biggest driver in setting changes when adapting a work is to compensate for the adaptation process. There's things that work in one medium that just don't work in others.

                                                    A simple example from the new The Last of US TV show: they changed how the infection spreads. In the video games, the infection is capable of airborne spread via spores, so in the video games there's a lot of time spent wearing gas masks. The video games are also predominantly 3rd person over the shoulder camera angles, so you don't really see your character in a mask all the time. That's a lot harder on a TV show. Everyone in gas masks means it's harder to tell characters apart, it's harder for actors to emote, it's harder to visually indicate dangerous levels of spores in the air without a video game's HUD. In this specific case they didn't want constant comparisons to The Mandalorian with Pedro Pascal playing a constantly masked character escorting a special child around.

                                                    Obviously in the end, the goal is to get lots of people to watch it, but by making it good as a TV show, not just change to change from the games to be different.

                                                    There are also other reasons to change things when adaptations happen. Multiple Naughty Dog people that worked on The Last of Us games and are working on the new show have expressed how often the TV writers just come up with stuff that's better than the game's version was. Things that if they could go back in time and redo the original games, the show's story beats would be what was always the case sort of stuff. Adaptations are a chance to do those sorts of changes that you basically can't in most mediums (and even if you pull a George Lucas and redo your old movies with changes, it tends to piss people off way more than continuity errors that come from adapting into different mediums).

                                                            On the subject of possibly using different TTRPG engines/licenses, it's been mentioned before that the sort of success Paradox was hoping to have was numbers akin to the Avatar Legends Kickstarter, numbers that rattled the cage and got a whole lot of people realizing that there were alternatives to the d20 license. In that respect, as well as how the Powered by The Apocalypse's tight focus on narrative-driven games is a good fit for World of Darkness own "RPG's-as-art" mantra and would do a good job bringing the games overall into the ways games are played now instead of relying on the 80's/90's models, a launch of a PbtA version of World products that managed to communicate in the branding it's mechanical similarities to Avatar Legends is just as viable a option for resolving the issue as releasing a d20 version of World, if not even better because one of World's selling points is that it is gaming for an audience that doesn't like Dungeons and Dragons, either for it's system, it's narrative idiom, or both.

                                                            The thing of the matter on that, though, is you can hear how hollow and little that actually matters. It's changing the mechanical idiom and structure of the game for the purpose of what's fundamentally a surface level result, like arguing that the reason Cyberpunk 2077 was disappointing on release because it was released for Xbox, Playstation, and PC, and if someone had made it for the Nintendo Switch, it would have been uproariously approved of-not only does it evaluate what amounts to a wrapping paper consideration on the same level as deep and fundamental problems, but it's execution would probably make it an actively worse game.

                                                            The problem is that Paradox's take on World is that it's basiclaly just a bad product all around (with all apologies to fans who do enjoy what's been made), a result of a failure of oversight on a myopic development that has devolved into a desperate attempt to right the ship that's...not working too well, and the only thing that's gonna fix that is taking the time to make a thoroughly good, as-unimpeachable-as-possible product as possible that will get a lot of eyes on it, and that's a problem when you have returns to make.

                                                            If I were to make suggestions on what they can actually do that's close to this vein, it would probably be to first cut away all multimedia plans and focus on just one medium-and if I can be wildly salacious here, I would encourage Paradox to stop thinking about is as tabletop games instead focus purely on the franchise as a video game property, as it's not only what they're good ostensibly good at already and video games are a pretty wide market that will still overlap and interest existing fans, but it also lets them soft reboot and explore different overarching idiom and structures and find a take on the material that fixes their problems and maybe better allows for forward development-and just focus on making one (maybe a few) products really really well. If I were to get a little more wild in suggestion, I might suggest pulling a Marvel Gambit, where instead of trying to fix the mess they made out of their bigger name properties in the franchise, they pick one of the b-or-c listers and focus on making that as good as possible-I know it's technically not World of Darkness, but I'd personally pitch Orpheus (and if we stick to the idea of it being video games, I would also strongly encourage a resistance to temptation to make a game that shares a lot of the trends common today-resist the open world, the crafting, etc. etc. in the interest of making it stand out as much as possible).
