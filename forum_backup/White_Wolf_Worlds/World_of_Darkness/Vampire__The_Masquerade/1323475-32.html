                                                        Elegy

                                                        Beckett presents an “elegy of sorts” at the end of his Jyhad Diary. An elegy is a sad poem, one usually written to praise and express sorrow for someone who is dead. Although a speech at a funeral is a eulogy (pronounced “eugoogly” by many Toreador), someone might later compose an elegy to someone you have loved and lost to the grave. This is an odd term for Becket to use here. His nominally final statement is not a poem, is not sad (he attempts to be hopeful), and is not about the dead (he asserts vampires are alive in their own way).

                                                        I appreciate the aesthetic, and commercial, use of his “elegy.” It ends the work on an empowering, and upbeat note. It gives the text a comforting sign-off for fans who want that kind of thing, and for those who see vampires as anti-heroes and misunderstood. As compared to articulate monsters.

                                                        Its that latter point that means I have misgiving about the “elegy.” I would have preferred something more down beat and resigned to the monstrous nature of vampires.

                                                        Also, he wrote the final passage in the Hagia Sophia. That building over the Baali pit where he was nearly killed and apparently had a demonic chest burster creature implanted into him. So, this elegy was written on his trip to Istanbul, shortly before the Dreams and Nightmares Chapter, when he did not know about the Pit. Or he returned to Istanbul, specifically the site of the Pit with the demonic chest burster creatures, after the Omega Chapter. If it is the latter, then he is the stupidest vampire in existence and only survives because of plot armour.

                                                        I wonder if Beckett is alive in V5.

                                                        The Meta-Plot

                                                        So, there is an old Daffy Duck cartoon titled The Scarlet Pumpernickel. In it, Daffy is pitching a movie to a producer. As producer keeps asking “And then what?” And Daffy keep escalating wildly, including volcanic eruptions and sky rocking price of dumplings.


                                                        Sometimes the meta-plot reminds me of that sequence, with the White Wolf people in the role of Daffy and the fans in the role of the producer.

                                                        The meta-plot was not intended to be a “thing” in VtM first edition. Mysteries were laid into the setting, such as the nature of the larger world, the Sabbat, and from where vampires really came. However, these were largely for the individual storyteller to include and resolve.

                                                        But in VtM success, many fans kept demanding “And then what?” The meta-plot, such as it was, grew out of answering that fan demand. It also did not take any real shape for several years – until new books not only expanded past information and changed established situations.

                                                        VtR took things in a different direction. Its establishment from the beginning was predicated on there being no meta-plot (among other elements).

                                                        My own philosophy about all RPGS is basically a buffet – assemble the meal you want from want you want and ignore the rest.

                                                        And I always assumed most people proceeded in the same way. It strikes me as odd that anyone should be slavishly devoted to some element like a meta-plot in their home games. Field Marshal von Moltke once said something to the effect of “No plan survives contact with the enemy” That is, the reality of a situation will always change advanced plans. I have always assumed the same rule applies to RPG. The PCs are often all over the place even if the GM/Storyteller is following the letter of the text devotedly.

                                                        All this to say that I would attempt to include those elements of the metaplot that interested me, and not worry about the rest of it. Granted, some things that might interest me are not easily tied into a game. For example, running around Eastern Europe looking for the Lost Book of I Changed My Mind (by Saulot) will have little use in a game tied to Chicago.

                                                        I do not worry about it.

                                                        Across the years the classic World of Darkness acquired a lot of meta-plot. The relative importance varied over time. But none of it is mandatory. BJD does a reasonable job of hitting the high lights, not resolving anything (resolving things is for the PC) and keeping the setting interesting. It does provide a nice set of stories and way to include the party.

                                                        To paraphrase Beckett himself, “You who read this need to know that the meta-plot is what you make of it.”

                                                        Pick what suits you for your game, and do not worry about the rest.

                                                            Overall
                                                            I've said it before and I'll say it again that I think this is probably the best book ever written for the World of Darkness.
