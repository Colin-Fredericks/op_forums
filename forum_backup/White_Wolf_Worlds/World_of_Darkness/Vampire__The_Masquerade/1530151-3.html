        I think being a fan means being in love with a fictional setting and elements of that fiction as they have been represented in media. But being a fan should include a critical perspective of said media and a continued reevaluation of said media over time. You like what you like but being a fan should never mean to feel beholden to what one likes. It's always important to examine why you like a thing and your relationship to that thing. Especially once commercial products get involved. There's the thing you like that exists in your mind and there's the product some company is trying to sell you. These should never be treated as the same thing. The thing you like should be determined by your perception of it and not some sales-pitch from a greedy company looking to make a profit.

        Being a fan of something doesn't mean supporting a company that tries to sell you something in every way. That's why threads like this one are important. Somebody fucked up, the fan-community calls them out and a change for the better occurs in reaction to that. This is a good thing.

                                                So...

                                                1) Most bigger companies rely on fan-submissions for such efforts. Spongebob can ask for fan submissions and get enough submissions to post a fan work every 15 minutes for the next few decades. I haven't specifically looked up the Spongebob Twitter account, but Nickelodeon has been doing these things since I was a young kid and it was always fan submissions to get your drawings on TV/etc. Fan-submissions also come with a bunch of fine-print legalese to establish what rights are being waived/exchanged.

                                                2) Most smaller content creators that do this already abide by the general guidelines Knightingale posted in post 34. While not universal this general attitude is basically what keeps the Internet's creative spaces from devolving into everyone pulling their art and being in dozens of different lawsuits. It's a set of general norms most creators agree to in order to meet a level of socially agreed to "don't sue folks" status quo (because again, IP laws, not just in the US, are woefully behind the speed at which the Internet operates).

                                                3) As has been noted, small creators that get big often end up changing their sharing habits because getting big enough creates a number of problems and the easiest solution is to stop doing these sorts of fan appreciation sections (esp, if you're not so big you can do submissions forever).

                                                4) As noted, the situation in question is one where it's not one small creator sharing the works of another small creator as part of a mutually beneficial community. It's a for-profit company using fan art without permission for their own advertising purposes and no considerations to how the creators they feature want to be exposed to a larger audience (though it's actually a very small audience.. so it's not even a great case for getting "exposure" to the wider WoD community when most of the WoD News videos get less than 5K views).

                                                            As some people appear confused:

                                                            - Paradox makes a community spotlight show where they spotlight community work without asking permission.
                                                            - Some members of the community are fine with this, others aren't.
                                                            - Paradox should have sought permission beforehand. My suspicion is that Outstar and Huddy looked through Twitter, Tumblr, Instagram, etc. for this art and collected it for their show without seeking approval from their bosses, because they didn't consider someone who creates fan art would object to their art being spotlit. This was an error.
                                                            - Realising their error, Paradox confirm they will seek permission in future.
                                                            - One vlogger in particular puts the company and Outstar on blast for this error, which has been identified as a trend behaviour by this individual.
                                                            - As part of the "blast" some people mockingly advocate punishment (some implying physical violence, though usually stated with comical exaggeration, YMMV) for the identified wrongdoers - Outstar and Huddy, in this case.
                                                            - People who respond badly to threats of violence, joking or not ("it's just a joke!", "this insult isn't offensive to me, so it shouldn't be to you!", and "it's a harmless term in my country!" aren't legitimate defences in many eyes these days) call out the vlogger for bad behaviour and request these threats stop, citing their hurt feelings.
                                                            - Outstar notes this perpetual antagonism is something they tried to resolve privately with the vlogger on multiple occasions, but without success.
                                                            - The fans have now in part pivoted away from the original issue and are taking stands based on whether they think Outstar and Huddy expressing their hurt feelings and admonishment of the vlogger is appropriate, while others concentrate on the subject of using fan art without permission.

                                                            Sidenote: As mentioned, it's "onus" and "burying the lede."

                                                            It's quite possible for multiple parties to be in the wrong here, and nobody needs to lick boots or burn down a company to make their point. Paradox should have sought permission and will apparently be doing so in future. Fans should not even be jokingly advocating for violence against a community manager or their team, because some people take it seriously, and you don't know the experience of the person you're mocking in this way. Was it a calculated move to distract from the fan art issue to call out this vlogger? I doubt it. I suspect that the stress of being under fire for this error, and from a source that persistently complains about the way you're doing your job, is enough to make you think "This isn't fair, and I'm going to speak out about it."

                                                            People don't always act calmly and resolve things quietly, and sometimes it feels like you can't. People act emotionally and say things they shouldn't. Because it's on social media it's magnified into a greater issue than it would otherwise be. This is a simple situation that's blown up, and I'd suggest each participant in the "feud" returns to their corner and refocuses on something positive and creative instead of trying to score points against their opponent.

                                                            It all makes me very glad I'm no longer the World of Darkness community manager, frankly.
