                They're split between the Anarchs and Sabbat, with just a few in the Camarilla (including the prince of Tokyo). Because the Sabbat has subsumed clan identity into sect identity, the Sabbat loyalists won't have much of a culture of their own. That means traditional Tzimisce clan culture is mostly found among the Anarchs, which is a natural sect for them because of their typical unwillingness to recognize a law beyond themselves.

                And despite the Dracula stereotype, the clan has been around for untold millennia, and the idea that it's been restricted to eastern Europe is beyond absurd. They've existed in Asia and Africa for thousands of years, and across the Americas for hundreds. There is zero possibility of there ever having been a Tzimisce monoculture across all continents on earth.

                The Sabbat was a specifically western European phenomenon that migrated to the Americas in the early colonial period. Like the Camarilla, it was a response to the Inquisition (itself a phenomenon centered in Catholic western Europe), which created the Anarch Revolt (during which many western European Tzimisce cannibalized their elders), and as a result of which there weren't really any Tzimisce methuselahs in the Sabbat to begin with, and it's only in the late modern period that they could be said to have any elders (as the neonates of the medieval period finally aged into that category). And despite the Sabbat's successes early on, the sect has always been an uncontrollable clusterfuck, not to mention anathema to the values of actual Tzimisce elders, which it was formed in direct opposition to. With all that in mind, although the Sabbat does bear the influence of the Tzimisce Anarchs that co-founded it, it can't be said to represent the clan as a whole, being formed out of a defiant rebellion against the clan as such.

                Of course, as much as it has tended to forget it, western/central Europe isn't the whole world. Lots of Tzimisce would have only come to be aware of the Camarilla and Sabbat in the colonial period, in which case they'd have no particular reason to identify with the latter. Even less so if clan identity actually mattered to them.

                        I don't really think that's true. Pretty much all writting on the subject prior to v5 made it clear the Sabbat had entrenched territories in Europe partculary around traditional tzmisce and lasombra territories.
                        The early editions were written by people with a very faint grasp of European geography and history, and who hammered the lore into shape to conform to initial stereotypes (when the Tzimisce were nothing but an antagonist clan based on Dracula and Necroscope). Sure, the Sabbat spread beyond its original boundaries, but its entire origin and aesthetic come out of the Catholic west around the time of the Reformation and ensuing witch-panic. And in those lands few elders would have survive the Anarch Revolt to begin with. It’s got no real connection to the Orthodox/Muslim world they’re usually associated with (the authors were also apparently unaware of the prominence of Islam in the region). And that’s without getting into the problem of restricting the clan to “Eastern Europe,” which doesn’t work to begin with.

                        Those modern Tzimisce obsessed with the authority of age and lineage will find the feudal Camarilla appealing, and those that don’t jibe with it will find that Anarch identity mostly lets them do what they want. The modern Anarch movement isn’t devoid of hierarchy; it tends to be about how much power and influence an individual can hold without institutional structures in place to enforce it from above. And yes, you can get overthrown, but that’s how it was in the dark ages too. But it’s not the dark ages, the clan culture isn’t stuck in the dark ages, and there aren’t that many real elders on the scene anyway. In fact, it’s previous editions in which the clan made no sense, being described as archaic to the point of anachronism, and obsessed with medieval forms of authority, in a sect that was actively antagonistic to all of the above.

                        These two clans fit this edition just fine, if you take them seriously as global phenomena and aren’t bound by stereotypes laid down before the authors properly understood what that meant, and aren’t stuck in the rut of their initial conception as the two antagonist clans invented for the Sabbat.

                                                    The thing with clans is that sires tend to select for certain traits they find appealing. In many ways, that does introduce bias into clans. Most fledglings are also mentored/indoctrinated for a period by their sires. Finally, clan weaknesses (and now Compulsions) reinforce certain kinds of behaviour which make clan an even stronger influence for many vampires in V5 than sect affiliation.

                                                    Sect affiliation, meanwhile, can also, at least partly, be about happenstance. Most Camarilla Kindred, in any edition, are Camarilla by default -- they were Embraced in a Camarilla city by a Camarilla sire, and so that's all they know. Switching sects, unless you're in a city under siege, usually means leaving your city and finding a new one.

                                                    V5 troubles the idea of "sect as structure", meanwhile, by removing most of that structure anyway, or simply making it inaccessible. The Camarilla has retreated into a kind of Old Boys' Club that most PCs have to actively join (rather than it being the default), limiting access to it's structure, and the Sabbat basically has no structure at all now.

                                                    So the only structure most PCs will get to experience is the Anarch one -- which is relatively fluid, because they don't really have structure in the same way the Camarilla does. It's a "make it up as we go along or maybe copy the Camarilla if we run out of ideas" kind of structure. I think the intent was actually to enable to PCs to be more instrumental in making their own city-level political structures, and that can reasonably include a combination of both clan and sect structures.

                                                    So ​​​​​​I don't buy the "clans don't have structure" argument or the idea that sects inherently provide more structure. They just provide different kinds of structure that can interact in different ways. Just look at the Tremere as a clan that's very structured, or the Hecata. Compared to the Sabbat, those clans have way more structure than some sects have in V5.

                                                    ​​​​​Watch actual plays such as LA by Night and you'll see that the way V5 is often played (including by some of the people who created this edition) does involve clans providing some sort of structure -- often a lot of it. Reacting against a structure also requires a structure to react against.

                                                    Yes, clan isn't a singular, unitary thing that overrides all else, but the original clans were one of the attempts to answer the early question "what do vampires do?" when Mark Rein-Hagen and friends were creating 1e. So right from the beginning of the game's publication, they were intended to provide some sort of structure -- including conflict within and between clans, a guide for how to roleplay (including giving you the chance to play against type), the possibility of gaining different kinds of Status, and so on.

                                                    Moreover, clans and sects are two axes of structure and character flavour that interact to create interesting and nuanced roleplaying decisions for players. Leaning into the possibilities of both is a good thing.

                                                    All that being said, I think it's a fair question to ask what the Tzimisce clan structure might be like in V5, and "clans don't have structure" just closes down discussion and opportunities, rather than potentially opening them up. Just because the books haven't focused on clan structure for the Tzimisce doesn't mean we can't consider what one might look like.

                                                    I personally really appreciate shadeprowler's very thorough contributions here. I think that a more Dark Ages version of the clan is probably the best way to go now.

                                                    Hospitality, rules, etiquette and territory would provide a really interesting framework for V5 Tzimisce characters. Conversely, so would that kind of rebelliousness that sparked the Anarch Revolt, as the Tzimisce were instrumental in that. I could see fledglings being raised by a sire who could have taken either route, and that would shape their views (including whether they reject or accept that view).
                                                    ​​​​​
                                                    The "status quo" Tzimisce might double down on their territorialism. For them, pledging allegiance to a sect is a way to keep what power and domain they already have. It's a smart move. They might instruct their childer to behave in a similar fashion and could feasibly maintain an Oradea League-like alliance with other Tzimisce across the region to maintain that status quo. Shared needs (their clan bane) mean alliances make sense.

                                                    The "anarch" Tzimisce (not necessarily capital-A "Anarchs"), might be more loosely structured, and keen to refuse bondage. But they still have a clan flaw and a Compulsion that encourage certain kinds of behaviour, so how do they square that circle?

                                                    In essence, I think they would likely pretend to be rugged individualists, who are masters of their own fates (and domains) and respect their peers' own freedom (and domains), but the effect might look a little like the status quo -- just as less of an "alliance" and more of a "begrudging recognition that you stay there and I'll stay here, and we both keep what's ours".

                                                    An Anarch barony could feasibly become something like a Voividate, too, if a Tzimisce winds up in charge. That could be a cool thing to look at. And then you have the obvious conflict of that: In rebelling against clan, do you end up more like your clan as a result?

                                                    Either group could potentially be very isolationist or very social, depending on the characters involved. What happens, though, when boundary disputes occur? If domain is really important to Tzimisce, then I can see that occurring more frequently than we might initially expect. So perhaps they need a framework to adjudicate that in a way that respects sect rules and the domain of others, without engaging in turf wars that draw the SI. That then suggests other structures that may emerge organically as a result.

                                                    I think more discussion of this idea is definitely a good thing. It can be potentially quite fruitful, regardless of whether we're "doing it wrong" or "missing the point" of V5.
