                            Originally posted by Elphilm View Post

                            Whenever the topic of Dungeons & Dragons comes up on these forums (and in the wider World of Darkness fandom in general), I regularly marvel at how outlandish the characterizations of the game appear to me. I should probably stop responding to all the D&D tangents that crop up, because it has resulted in painful exchanges many times in the past, but...

                            How is this in any way a charitable description of D&D? In my experience, this is a version of the game that only exists in the detractors' heads. "The only systems exist to resolve combat encounters" does not accurately describe the original 1974 Dungeons & Dragons release (where the entirety of the combat rules fit into maybe a couple of pages, and the game is just as interested in castle construction, the establishment and maintenance of baronies, aerial and naval movement, and so on); it does not describe either version of Advanced Dungeons & Dragons (which only expand in a dizzying variety on the social and exploration systems already present in OD&D); and it does not describe any of the Wizards of the Coast editions of the game (each of which has a detailed skill system for interacting with the imaginary world in non-combat capacity).

                            In the 20+ years that I have played or run Dungeons & Dragons, characters have always cared about ideas, people, and things, and the games have always been very nuanced and highly political. Maybe these archetypal "kill monsters and collect loot to kill more monsters and collect more loot" games really do exist. If that's how a group wants to engage with the game, more power to them. I would strongly argue that it says more about the group than the game -- the bunch of 13-year-olds who do the hack-and-slash routine every Thursday night are not going to turn into a mature and sophisticated ensemble of Real Storytelling just because the game changes from D&D to Werewolf.​
                            I think you need to step back here and look at the bigger picture. D&D as a system has a core loop of kill monsters - level up. Through the ages they have done a lot of changes and softening things up, but it's still a system where every character type exists of practically only combat abilities, and even the skill monkies like rogues and bards have a problem with the swinginess of the system. You can ignore this game loop and these abilities (and the way you gain them back in the modern day) but you're essentially ignoring most of what D&D is designed to be.

                            Now, that doesn't mean that you cannot play D&D in a mature and dramatic way that will be better than any high drama modern narrative game, but you'll be doing that by ignoring and bending the core premise and core tools of the system. I say this as someone who loves the settings and ideas in D&D and has been giving it many chances through history (I even did a 40ish session of 5E Eberron campaign because I promised to and I gave up because I fought with the system too much to give the players the story they wanted and I wanted).

                            When we speak of the baseline D&D experience it's still - go to exotic places, kill strange people, become stronger, repeat. Even the modern adventures tend towards that, and recently they've been actually offering ideas of how to bypass "encounters" without combat as a novel thing in the mainline adventures. A shocking revalation and one that most people who moved away from D&D have internalized a long time ago.

                            What doesn't kill you, makes you... stranger.
