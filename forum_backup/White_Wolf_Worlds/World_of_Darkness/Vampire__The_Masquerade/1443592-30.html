                                            Originally posted by Vilenecromancer View Post

                                            Since we're on the topic of DnD. Isn't this the same kind of division that made Pathfinder? Granted, it was hell of a lot more complicated then that but still.
                                            There were a couple different things that made Pathfinder.

                                            The first was the OGL, which let basically anyone publish products under the D&D label. At the time, it was a brilliant business decision. You saw White Wolf actually publishing products for another company's settings and game system. Really good products, too. The 3e Sword & Sorcery Ravenloft books were a more than worthy continuation of the setting from 2e. You saw a huge explosion of third party content during the 3.0-3.5 D&D era that helped keep interest in the game alive.

                                            But the OGL also spawned D&D's biggest competitor when fans didn't like 4e. The new edition butchered too many sacred cows and tried to be too different a game. Way more so, I think, than V5 has departed from V20. I don't remember what the terms of 4e's OGL equivalent were, but they were a lot more restrictive. You didn't really see any third party content. A lot of 4e felt like it was going out of its way to take a dump on the lore of previous editions. Even a WotC April Fool's Day post I remember from the late 2000s felt like it was taking mean-spirited jabs at 3e.

                                            So between those two factors, fan discontent and the OGL, another game actually outsold D&D for the first time in TTRPG history. Fan discontent created the demand for an alternative, and the OGL let Paizo supply it by essentially taking over publication of 3.5 material from WotC. PF was a direct continuation of a game system lots of people wanted to keep playing. "Backwards compatible" was a big selling point of PF back in the day. You could keep using all of your old 3.5/3.0 books, with a little fiddling.

                                            It also didn't hurt that Pathfinder was flat-out better than 3.5 (at least in my view). Production value was higher. Rules books had lots of really cool mechanics. Setting books were top quality. Lot of the guys working for Paizo were top tier talent. Their forums were vibrant and active, with lots of participation from the game developers (which I think company policy made mandatory.) The whole game and the community around it was just better.

                                            Another factor which I think helped Pathfinder sell so well was their emphasis on publishing adventures, rather than rulebooks. By default, D&D isn't a flavor-heavy setting like the WoD. In WoD you can have books on being a ghoul or religious faith among vampires. Eventually you'll start scraping the bottom of the idea barrel, but you can go on for a while. In D&D, the individual settings like Ravenloft, Greyhawk, etc. were essentially sub-brands of a larger and setting-agnostic system. I don't know what sales figures were for them, but the glory days of D&D campaign settings were 2e for me. So that left publishing more rule books as WotC's bread and butter, but past a certain point you suffer from mechanics fatigue and GMs just not wanting to deal with the hassle of learning and using material (some of it poorly balanced) from so many books.

                                            But adventures? Those never get old. Those are the actual bread and butter of any gaming group. Paizo went ahead and did the lion's share of a GM's prep work for them. And if there was a particular adventure you didn't like, well, there were plenty others. You can have rules bloat, but you can't ever have adventure bloat. Even if you never play them, you can steal stuff to use in your own campaigns, or just enjoy reading them and thinking "ooh, I'd like to run this someday."

                                            It's hard to emphasize here just what an excellent business model the AP (adventure path) format was for Paizo. You've seen a few pre-published adventures for the WoD, like Transylvania Chronicles, but nothing on the scale of the Pathfinder APs. The APs were Paizo's vanguard, with setting books and rules books on the side. I know people who continued to buy APs long after they stopped buying rules books.

                                            But Pathfinder's premise was essentially extending the shelf life of a game system people didn't want stop playing in 2008. (3.5 was published in 2003, so it'd only been around for five years before Wizards tried to pull the plug.) And eventually... people did want to stop playing. RPG design moved on with the years and Paizo was stuck with a system rooted in 2000. Their whole business model was being the direct continuation that WotC wasn't, so they couldn't really make any major overhauls. Option bloat became a thing. APs might have been their bread and butter, but rules books still kept coming, and eventually I had my fill. The new mechanics declined in quality. The APs also seemed to decline in quality. Paizo had already done all of the really cool ideas.

                                            I stopped playing PF in 2013 and migrated to the WoD. PF had too many warts and I was burned out.

                                            In 2014, 5e came out. Wizards learned their lesson from 4e. The new edition was more like the third, sacred cows brought back to life, and they reinstated the OGL. People loved it. Lot of PF gamers I know switched to 5e. If I still played D&D/PF, I'd want to play 5e. I like Paizo a lot more as a company. I feel a personal connection with its employees that I don't with Wizards'. I think Paizo's adventures (at their best) are gold. But... at the end of the day, none of that is the actual game system I'm playing with.

                                            In 2018, Paizo bit the bullet and released their own new edition. I've looked it over casually, and it's not my cup of tea. It looks a lot more prolix and fiddly than 5e is. The fandom seems pretty split. I don't know what the sales figures are for 1e vs. 2e books, but most of the PF games I see advertised online are for 1e, so take that anecdote for what it's worth. I can also say that while I'd be willing to play in a PF 1e game, if one came along, I'd be more likely to pass on a 2e game. I already know the rules for 1e.

                                            Paizo doesn't seem to be what they were, either. A lot of their most talented people have left, and had been leaving for years before then. A few of their former people now work for Wizards. It's pretty obvious who dominates the TTRPG industry now. There's ultimately no competing with the D&D brand. For all the top-notch product that Paizo delivered, and for all the amazing people they formerly had on their staff, they were only able to compete because Wizards bungled things so badly with 4e. (Even if you like 4e as a system, there's no denying it was a failure from a business standpoint.) The corporate overlords eventually noticed it was hurting their bottom line and demanded a course correction.


                                            Long digression coming from a long-time D&D/PF fan.

                                            Blood and Bourbon, my New Orleans-based Vampire chronicle.
