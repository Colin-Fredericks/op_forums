              VtM has generally had skimpy social conflict rules, though as noted there was a more detailed than "do an opposed roll" one out there.

              I think the most important thing to keep in mind is what the goal of the social conflict is. That is, what does "winning" mean. Exalted 3e is all about getting people to do something, and it works very well at that, but it's less suited to a court room drama with lawyers trying to juggle being legally convincing to the judge, asking powerful questions to witnesses, and appealing to the jury.

              Most RPG combat has the same core consideration even if we think about it less for physical conflicts. Combat is usually, "Do enough harm to the other side that they stop fighting back." Which is great when that's what you want, but generally they struggle if you try to put in non-harm based goals (either because winning via focusing on harm is still optimal, or exiting combat is more optimal).

              The CofD has amassed a handful of social conflict rules that could be used as inspiration, most of which are focused on different "win" conditions so it can be useful to have those options to pick one that best suits the flow of the game.

                      While the never ending debates over social conflict rules aren't worth retreading too much:

                      "Just do a simple roll off," could be applied to anything. There are games where fights are a single roll (not never both sides roll, just the PC rolls vs. a target based on the threat of the enemy!), because that's how those games are designed. There are games with dozens of pages or rules that are all about fighting with lots and of rolls, because lots of people want more meat to the significance of a fight than a one-and-done roll.

                      The same applies to social conflicts. Sometimes a simple roll off is all people want. Sometimes they want something that makes social traits, powers, and character focus to actually matter more in the system. Painting the point as "causing depression" or "dictating how characters feel," is spurious at best. As noted Exalted 3e, and other things like Social Maneuvering in CofD and Influence actions in Storypath, are about compelling other characters to act. This can be direct by trying to get them to do a specific thing, or indirect by trying to manipulate their impressions of things (though that sort of manipulation can also make direct appeals easier if you're taking your time) so the act differently as their priorities change. The CofD has a Debate system designed for VtR 1e's Requiem for Rome supplement to model Socratic style floor debates that were popular among vampires in the era and setting; but could easily be used in a modern day courtroom legal drama, or a bunch of hunters sitting around the campfire trying to impress with the best story of the one-that-got-away. The core of the Debate system was trying to pick the best mix of your strengths, and what appeals to your audience, to maximize the chance that the audience is most swayed to your side; from swaying a Prince's mind on the best way to handle enemy spies, to getting a judge to throw out a case by clever legal maneuvering, to who got bragging rights for the best story.

                      A more in-depth approach gives players more ways to engage with a social scene by giving them more widgets to play with rather than just tossing their best roll at their opponent/target. The same way an in-depth combat system gives you more to do than just toss your best combat pool at theirs.

                                          Originally posted by Shakanaka View Post

                                          Huh, now this is interesting me. Can you explain this system in more depth? How does it work?
                                          Exalted 3e's social system is built off of a series of single rolls (not an extended action). You basically pick whichever social Attribute and Ability combo that makes sense for most actions (and for the set ones, there's frequently powers you can buy to allow alternate options). As per Exalted, your difficulty is a number of successes you have to beat, and is set by the target's traits.

                                          There are two social DVs: Guile (which protects you from people trying to learn about you), and Resolve (which is used to resist active social influence). Guile can be boosted or penalized by circumstances (your Guile goes up if you can't be fully observed as it's hard to figure out what you're trying to say if people can't hear you, or see your body language, and goes down if you don't know someone is watching). I'll get to Resolve in a sec with Intimacies.

                                          There are a few standard social influence actions:

                                          - Read Intentions is when you try to learn about your target (esp. their Intimacies) by watching how they interact with people, and is resisted with Guile. There is a related Profile Character action in the Investigation system that basically works the same way if you bring knowledge learned that way into the social arena.
                                          - Instill actions are actions designed to add/modify/remove Intimacies from a target. Regardless of your dice, and their Resolve, Instill actions are heavily limited by your ability to play Intimacies off each other ("You can trust me, I'm a cop," is something that is going to work on some people, and bomb with others as an example).
                                          - Persuade actions are the big part of the system and as I noted, they're about getting someone to do something. Like Instill actions, Intimacies are very important here. Besides their impact on Resolve, the game sets standards for how onerous the action you're trying to get them to do based on how strong the Intimacy you're appealing to is; you can't get someone that's just there for a paycheck to lay down their life for you, but a fanatic to a cause is a different matter.
                                          - Threats and Bribes are similar but less complex to Persuade actions as they appeal to baser instincts. Threats are... threats and simply require the target to be reasonably fearful of what you're saying you'll do; and threats creative negative Intimacies against you. Bribes, like threats, need to be something the target actually wants/needs badly enough to do what you're asking of them, and you need to deliver on your promises.

                                          As you can tell, Intimacies are a big deal here. Intimacies are things characters care about. They can be Principles ("Try to be honest and caring towards others") or Ties with a linked emotion (The City's Mayor [Jealousy]). Intimacies come in three levels (Minor, Major, and Defining) which indicate how much sway they have on your character. Batman's Tie of Jim Gordon (Trust) is usually only a Minor because while Batman does trust Gordon, he frequently goes against that trust when he fells more important things are on the line. Batman's "Don't Kill" Principle is a Defining one, not because Batman never actually kills, but because it takes so much to get him to do so purposefully.

                                          Intimacies judge if social influence actions are even possible, but also are the major modifier to Resolve. If you play a target's Intimacies against them, their Resolve is decreased, but if you try to work against someone's Intimacies (esp. if you didn't put the time in to learn them via Read Intentions) their Resolve goes up. Persuade actions also work in a whole additional aspect called Decision Points, where characters can resist a successful Persuade action by invoking an Intimacy that wasn't used in the first roll. This costs WP points to keep from dragging on forever, but it creates a back and forth as the persuader and the target see who can best make use of the target's Intimacies.

                                          ----------------

                                          Though I realize you could have been asking about the VtR Debate mechanics...

                                          Fortunately those are a bit easier to explain. Basically it works more like combat does, but "attacks" are based on your Attribute used. Intelligence is used to doing purely logical and factual approaches, Charisma for convincing people to agree based on your personal persuasiveness, Manipulation for playing the audience's emotions, and Wits to creative word play. Damage is just how many successes you get. So it's basically a race for each side to get more successes faster.

                                          The big twist is mostly that the audience's "Health" track is adjusted by the ST based on approach and has a different one for each side. So a judge in a trial might have less health against Intelligence based arguments than Charisma based arguments. Having a better Charisma pool might not make up for the number of successes you'll need to rack up by taking that track

                                                          Seems like the way to go, yeah.

                                                          Intimacies are better for persuasion than debate, as Heavy Arms said. Though I do love them, because they make social interactions so concrete. E.g.:

                                                          'I want to persuade him to steal that file for me.'
                                                          'Why would he care enough to do that for you?'
                                                          'Well, I know he hates injustice, so I want to leverage the emotions he already has about that to convince him that this is serving justice.'
                                                          'Alright, make a roll...'

                                                          In the above example, you determine the relationship to injustice via Read Intentions, but you could also create an Intimacy towards injustice using instill or inspire, and then exploit it afterwards.

                                                          It's just a nice way to give people milestones. I also use other traits as an equivalent to an Intimacy on the fly. E.g., if an NPC has Melee 5, they would presumably care about Melee quite a bit. So I would allow that to count as an Intimacy worth about half its rating, minimum, for exploiting. So you could use someone's passion for Melee to get them to fight you (or someone you wanted dead) in a duel, for example.
