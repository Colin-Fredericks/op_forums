    Originally posted by Knightingale View Post
    One of the things I hate the most about videogame-critique/journalism is how outlets like IGN or Gamespot popularized the idea that anything less than an 8 out of 10 is an expression of "I hate this game".
    That has a history that goes way back, before even the internet was a thing, or video-games.

    While I also dislike it, it's unfair to put the blame solely on the industry, they're just reacting to how people got used to read those scales.

    People don't like to give bad reviews if they're not really pissed. This changes a little with culture, but never seems to really vanish as a trend, people simply don't feel it's right. That's why in 5-stars reviews, 3 stars are rare, 2 stars are almost unheard of.

    So usually the rationale of most people is to give max or near max ratings, then subtract as they think of aspects they really disliked. Only if pissed they'll do the opposite, either start from half or minimum and make their way from there.

    Most simply have no objective or almost-objective criteria for rating. Then outlets have to work with it, giving ratings in a way people find intuitive and reading them in the way most people mean it.

    Now, could/should we work on that and make for a better general understanding of the ratings? Yes. Are the outlets a big part of this process? Yes. Do they have some fault in the situation? Yes. But more because they perpetuate a previous problem than because they created it.

    Companies that try to make those ratings into actual criteria for policy or benefits, like an NPS rating, that's an actual problem.

    Originally posted by Knightingale View Post
    Who knows how much acrimony there is between Paradox and the Chinese Room at this point? ... And it's all simply because these developers didn't fulfill their unrealistic expectations.
    They're both easily frustrated, but stubborn on giving up. Not particularly out of any hope of success, but just to keep to their self-applied reputation of finishing things (which is also why they finished Bloodlines 2).

    And I wouldn't say their expectations are unrealistic just because they're incompetent to manage such projects. Too frequently what I get is that their expectations are unrealistic, period. Going back to my scale, an 8 is something you can reliably aim for if you have the know-how and resources, but above that isn't something even AAA projects should try on purpose with any regularity. A 9 is something you can try as a big, special project if you're really up to it, but most 9s are happy accidents as far as projects go. A 10 happens, it's a convergence of planets you can only hope to control from pure hubris.

    But those are the things Paradox is doing. Not accomplishing, of course, but aiming for 9 and 10, all the time, no less than 8 if they don't want to put much effort. They think they can outdo bigger companies in things even those companies only get because they make a lot of stuff, so they have more chances to get lucky. Of course, this is a very shallow simplification, but that's basically what Paradox is trying to do, when they don't have yet the resources and experience to even reliably get an 8.

    So, yeah. At the end everything indicates that working with them is a nightmare, and they'll throw you under the bus even if you do a good job, because you simply won't satisfy expectations. And they'll throw the whole project in the trash because they're frustrated, and they don't want to be seen treating a "mediocre" job as worthwhile.

    If you look carefully to the projects they actually abandoned, there are some signs that they frequently were really, really impossible to continue. Sure, at the same time that their frustrated post-release is basically the same as abandoning or worse.

                                            Not really anything too new...

                                            PC Gamer - Bloodlines 2 narrative director tries to get players to lower their expectations

                                            While we will get to choose the clan and gender of our player-character in Vampire: The Masquerade - Bloodlines 2, it's apparently not going to be a full "how many skill points do I want to put into Security" kind of character creation—a revelation that's set a few fans on edge. Narrative director Ian Thomas put in some damage control by explaining that decision to PCGamesN, which I'm sure White Wolf tragics will give calm and reasonable consideration to.

                                            Elaborating on why the player-character in Bloodlines 2 isn't the typical RPG "blank slate", Thomas said, "if you're telling a specific story, I think you need a shape to pour the story into, like a mold. If you completely open up all of the options and throw somebody into a space, how do you make that character the pillar of the story?"

                                            And Bloodlines 2 will be telling a very specific story. Comparing it to Baldur's Gate 3, which does let you go full blank-slate and wander off-piste between plot points, Thomas put the two philosophies into tabletop RPG terms. "For me Baldur's Gate 3 is writing an entire roleplaying setting, whereas what we've done is write a roleplaying module," he said, suggesting Bloodlines 2 will be more about telling a singular one-and-done story than opening up a map and letting you have at it.

                                            The original Bloodlines, while smaller than Baldur's Gate 3, did give you fairly free rein across its open hubs, with plenty of side missions to explore as you saw fit. Bloodlines 2, with its customizable yet defined protagonist and pre-set scenario, seems like it's aiming for something different, and you can see why the developers at The Chinese Room are eager to manage our expectations.

                                            As Thomas says, "It does make it hard when people see [Bloodlines 2] and go 'oh I thought this was an entire setting, I thought this was GTA where we could do anything!' While we're trying to go 'no, it's this slice of time and place.'"

                                            We'll be able to see how that choice pays off for ourselves when Bloodlines 2 releases on October 21.

And if they can't, they should just not say anything. Just release the game. It sucks but it is what it is. But this... Sure, there may be people who don't have a lot of experience with RPGs maybe and since BG3 is their only experience with it but what's the point of going out there and say "Please don't think this is like BG3. It's worse." How is that supposed to help? The Day 1 DLC thing didn't help as well or how even the Chinese Room version had been delayed two times...
