                            That's why marketing is a whole profession yes. One of the reasons people can make whole careers around marketing is because there isn't some simple formula to drumming up interest.

                            While there are common wisdom "rules" in marketing (for example, you always want to put a positive spin on thing like, "we made it better," instead of, "we fixed what was wrong," even if it's the exactly same change being made), the complexities of industry are a lot to navigate. A big thing here, is that conversion rates are going to matter. That is, how many people get interested in something compared to how many people engage further later in time. It's really not hard to make a video that will go viral on the Internet and get millions of views. It is much, much harder to know how to make a video that will also result in people say, buying your product.

                            Changing things just to change them is not considered a good tactic for converting attention to sales. Existing fans (those people that are going to care the most about changes anyway) are going to want changes for reasons they can understand. New/potential fans are easily confused and thus put off by being told about changes as hype if they don't know why that change is supposed to be good.

                            So, back to my examples: The marketing for The Last of US is not, "we changed it because we just thought it you'd all rather see this part set in Kansas City instead of Pittsburgh," but rather, "we changed how Henry and Sam were introduced to make sure these pivotal and iconic characters impact TV audiences like they did fans of the games."

                            Of course, the reason they changed from Pittsburgh to Kansas city is really a mixture of scheduling and securing shooting locations, and a desire to have the main characters move at a bit faster clip than the games because you don't want to make the climax feel rushed so they moved a major plot point farther along the physical path to make it feel like the characters were moving across the country in line with the show's pacing. But none of that drums up attention. The change was going to creating feedback because that's the nature of adaptations,so the marketing team has the job of heading off negative feedback by highlighting the strengths of any changes rather than dwelling on just the fact that things changed, or the minutiae of practical behind-the-scenes issues.

                                        I'm pretty sure we've debated this before, but....

                                        That's equally off the mark as the idea that people change things in adaptations just to excite potential audiences.

                                        Humans are simply not that easy to sum up. If you even wanted to give it an honest shot, it would be something like this: humans like variations on a theme.

                                        We like a familiar core theme because those core themes resonate strongly with previous experiences, but we desire art that adds unique and experimental ideas to that core theme, because hewing too close to a core theme turns it repetitive and dull. It's fairly simple to argue that the true skill of an artist is finding the lines between too close and too far for a core theme and playing withing that amorphous space with creativity and fresh ideas.

                                                That's the problem. The reboot's creators might have been a fairly good (though most critics don't agree it was all that good) movie that didn't work as a Robocop movie because it strayed too far from the core themes. Instead of trying play in that liminal space I pointed early, they flipped around a hugely important part of the original story. In the original, Robocop finds out that he was once human, and struggles to find his humanity (and perhaps soul) that's still hidden under the layers of code used to animate his corpse. In the remake they made it so he thought he was human with some replacement parts, only to find out over the story how much they were overriding his free will underneath that.

                                                Those are two very different core themes to tackle. The original movie is a variation on Frankenstein: the doctor is the real monster, not his confused and erratic creation who, in the end, is the one that truly embraces what it is to be human despite everything that happened. Or in terms more fitting for this forum the original Robocop is a Promethean story, and the remake is a Vampire story (where a human is changed into something else and struggles to maintain their humanity in light how just how different they really are).

                                                If you want to a movie being told you were seeing a new Frankenstein movie and got a new Dracula movie, it could be a great Dracula movie, but it's still going to annoying most people.

                                                There's also a huge tonal differences in the films. The original is much more farcical and exaggerated in its satire. Many people accredit that balancing act to why the movie has had such an enduring appeal despite major cultural shifts (including views of police militarization). Even by the standards of when the original came out, it was oddly campy and comedic in unexpected ways (to long term audience approval). The mixing of dark drama and over the top moments went together well to highlight the targets of the satire involved and in many ways help them stay relevant towards changing cultural contexts.

                                                The reboot is a much more straight drama and the satire feels more forced for it. If you even want to consider it satirical because at a point it's just making value judgements via story rather than really trying to encourage subversive critical thought. By missing so many of the comedic beats (esp. because of the change in character development noted already) it lost of a lot of why people wanted out of a Robocop film.

                                                VTM offers a great smorgasbord of potential themes and a lot of ideas for stories. Unfortunately V5 stripped most of them away to focus on an artificially narrow vision of the product. IE Fledgling anarchs engaging in "street level" play and wallowing in their own misery.
                                                I'm pretty sure most of us agree on this part of things. The conversation has, for a bit now, been talking about how VtM could escape this, not if it's true.

                                                People often fly into a rage when new themes and ideas deny something in previous stories.
                                                There is, however, a big difference between removing things that were explicitly in previous works, compared to establishing something in contrast to fan speculations.

                                                V5 objectively makes things that used to be possible in the rules no longer possible via mechanical changes. You can't master both necromancy and shadow manipulation... because instead of those being two different powers, those are one power now and you have to pick which side to focus on if you want to master one. My old DA Lasombra that decided to opt out of Lasombra political nonsense and join the Road of Bones - including studying Necroomancy from Road followers more naturally talented in those arts - can't work in V5 without house rules.

                                                The idea of an era of a Second Inquisition is a separate matter. The idea that around 2010 secular governments starting taking the existence of vampires as a more serious threat is a big change, and it might cut off some old stories if you suddenly insert that into a preexisting game, but the concept doesn't "deny" what was. It actually plays a lot into what was setup already. How one feels about V5's execution of the topic is a where people really tend to disagree. I really don't like how the V5 setting established a more unified global initiative for a number of reasons, but I can't deny that a big one is that it completely violates my personal feelings about how many of those groups should operate.
