          Integrity is what humans use as a score in Chronicles of Darkness Revised. Like a vampire's Humanity, it reflects how a mortal character is holding it together in a world that tries to drive them crazy or make them do terrible things to survive.

          I like it, and I like what Vampire: Requiem 2nd Edition does with Humanity as well. Both are impacted by what the character is exposed to as well as what the character has actively done, and the resulting combination stat for morality *and* sanity (in the Call of Cthulhu sense of sanity as a game mechanic) just works better for the Humanity score's stated intended purposes in Vampire the Masquerade. Your ability to remember your higher self and interact with society are impacted by the trauma you inflict on yourself, but also by just what living in the World of Darkness does to a person.

                  I think VtR 2e does such a good job because it has a strong design idea of what Humanity is supposed to mean (how well you character holds on to their previous existence as a human rather than a specific morality). The Bane system (you can take on additional traditional vampire folklore weaknesses to immunize yourself against certain Humanity checks) is also a more coherent way to cover the Path issue. Banes are not a get out of jail free card, because they increase your general detachment risks even if they help you with individual risks. You can make a character that vivisects humans to study the lines between life and death in order to try to understand the vampiric condition, without your Humanity dropping into the toilet, but it's a lot more work when you're not holed up in your "laboratory" and have to confront what you've become while trying to interact with people in a more normal way.

                  I think for any of these sorts of mechanics, they work better when question #2 isn't really in the hands of the players. The game designers really need a strong idea of what these things should represent so that's never in question. Players might want to stress something else and implement house rules, but that's easier to do if you know what the base design is doing.

                      Answer to Question 2 in my game:

                      "the values of Humanity, understood as the core principles of respect for life and the adherence to common and natural laws, born during the Humanistic and Enlightened periods... Similar sets of values from non European universalist religions "
                      Also, a rejection of codes of conducts (Paths) that specifically view the vampire as an inhuman monster.
                      So, individuals in the path of Humanity may have certain differences about specific issues (is stealing from a corporation theft? is death penalty for murderers acceptable? is your code defined by yourself, the community or God? ). But everyone should agree on fundamental issues.
                      Now, if the ST wants to emphasize the differences, they can create different subpaths where the top of the hierarchy of sins is slightly different. For example, a socialist humanitarian would have greed as a sin, while a religious one would have not following religious tenet and a conservative pro capitalist theft or vandalism

                                      I think calling it a full on CoC SAN system is going too far.

                                      VtR 2e largely works like Humanity in pre-V5 VtM. The big mechanical differences are:

                                      1) Events beyond the players' control can trigger a check.
                                      2) The level of the check directly relates to the number of dice you roll instead of the roll being based on a trait.
                                      3) There's a more defined set of a modifiers (positive and negative) to the roll.
                                      4) You get a Beat (XP) immediately every time you have to make a check, and you always get a Condition to resolve later for another Beat; high Humanity characters can easily get more XP with frequent checks than they lose buying any lost Humanity dots (though high risk/high reward strategy).
                                      5) You can take extra Banes to permanently remove a breaking point from mattering to your character regardless of Humanity level.
                                      6) Humanity is tied more directly to the severity of the Banes (both the main ones like fire and sunlight, Clan based ones, and opt-in ones), and things like torpor duration.

                                      While asshole STs will always find a way, it's pretty obvious if they're trying to do so in VtR 2e's setup. The more "gotcha" style breaking points tend to be very high Humanity (which mean you might not even have to roll anyway, and if you do you'll have a high dice-pool with low odds of failing so you're unlikely to lose Humanity this way), and really they're just feeding you XP more than they're going to force your Humanity into the ground if they try this sort of stuff. Below Humanity 6, the few remaining things a ST could try to "spring" on you quickly become a limited resource (such as the death of close family members, and it's pretty obvious if your ST keeps making up new family members you never detailed for your character to die), or aren't entirely in the ST's hands either (living extremely long amounts of time instigates a check, but the ST would have to be very rapidly fast forwarding the game by centuries for this to actually come up frequently).

                                      CofD vampires do not have to deal with "holy shit, a monster!" and risk losing Humanity. That's part of the Integrity system that mortals deal with.

                                      So, since nobody's really explained it:

                                      Integrity is the CofD's answer to "are humans on Humanity?" with a resounding, "No." Integrity is a much looser system where breaking points are mostly individual based with some caveats to help keep the tone of the game in-tact (no sociopaths that get to be pure evil and still keep Integrity 10, but a character like Dexter Morgan could work to having a fairly decent Integrity score despite being a serial killer... it would be a challenge though).

                                      Rather than a moral code (though it can certainly include those) Integrity is mostly focused on how well a mortal maintains a sense of self and holds to their idealized version of themselves (there is, after all, a big difference between thinking your a good person and acting according to your standards for a good person) in the face of the Darkness that gives the setting its name. It's important to remember that part of the CofD's setting conceits is that mortals are in same ways aware they're in a supernatural world, but convince themselves that they're not. That's why being exposed to overt supernatural events ala CoC is a universal breaking point for mortals (otherwise they'd be something like a Hunter or worse that consciously accepts that supernatural monsters exist). So you get past different philosophies, cultural norms, and so on as each needing their own "chart" because mortals don't have a chart like that. And while there are good mechanical reasons to try to keep high Integrity and avoid low Integrity, you can't go NPC because of it. With enough work (and probably a lot of help) you can always recover Integrity even if you bottom out.

                                      As such a naive college student and a grizzled cop with twenty years on the streets are not going to roll for the same things (at least outside of major stuff like supernatural manifestations), or with the same modifiers. But that doesn't mean one is "better" than the other. Someone still figuring out who they are is less challenged by things that question more superficial aspects of their sense of self (switching schools sucks, but isn't likely to be a breaking point), but is less inured to the mundane cruelties of the world (having to use force even in self defense can easily be highly traumatic). Someone that defines themselves as their job as a cop is more set in certain things (that badge means something deep and personal to them, and leaving the force is a major blow even if they land a much better paying private security job in the process), but less bothered by things (they've seen the results of violence plenty of times before).

                                                  One nice thing about morality in a supernatural fictional setting is that you don’t need to try and justify multiple belief systems about morality; the GM can just pick one that appeals to them and that is the Truth of that setting.

                                                  If I choose “The Catechism of the Catholic Church is right about everything” then people and vampires are only humane in relation to how well they adhere to those tenants. It doesn’t matter if another religion says X is okay, if the CCC says it’s immoral and a character does X then roll for Degeneration depending on where you are on the Hierarchy of Sins.

                                                  The reason I use the Catholic Church as an example is specifically because Bram Stoker included elements of faithful Protestants being told they had to use Catholic paraphernalia (i.e. crucifixes not crosses, communion wafers, rosaries, etc.) to have any hope of warding off a vampire and the character being told this becoming quite uncomfortable with the thought.

                                                  Basically, in Bram Stoker’s tale it didn’t matter what the characters thought should or shouldn’t work; the rules of the universe just were. I appreciate settings that aren’t afraid to present a “one true morality” even if differs from my own. Having a specific truth instead of the “whatever you want to believe is true” let’s each tale be it’s own spicy dish instead of a continual bland soup.

                                                          It's also possible to make a case that Dracula was specifically weak against the symbols of Catholic faith rather than Stoker claiming that Catholicism is objectively true (at least not in conscious way on the author's behalf). Stoker's fictionalized version of Vlad Tepes was specifically a traitor to the Roman Catholic church, not just a general blasphemous person.

There's nothing interesting to actually play for narrative and dramatic effect if we all know who's right and who's wrong before anything happens.
