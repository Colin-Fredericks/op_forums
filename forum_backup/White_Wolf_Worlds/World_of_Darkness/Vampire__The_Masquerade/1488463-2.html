                                One of my main gripes with some people who criticize Haight (beyond the fact that I love to play the "Devil's Advocate" lol) is that some of the criticism he got from some people I knew, imply that a "recurring villain" is somehow a bad thing for the setting and I'm of the complete opposite idea. Good antagonists are what makes stories good to begin with, if you have a good antagonist you have half (or perhaps more) of what you need to make the story good. When I was a kid, Goratrix was my favorite character from the setting. I mean he's charming, ambitious, genius wizard that invented many things and then betrayed the clan, etc. He was the kind of villain I thought could make many interesting stories, of course of a different scale, as he's not the kind of villain the group is likely to beat in a "fist fight", but then the same could be said about Baba Yaga or even Vecna if we talk about other game lines as well.

                                So, to sum up, when I was a kid I really wanted to have this kind of antagonists created for the setting, but then I rarely had, Goratrix for instance, was never fully explored to make him a great villain where the PCs are going to try to stop him from doing something (and the only story where you can deal with him, i.e. the Transylvania Chronicles, the PCs can't even deal with him properly and are basically witness to the major plot happening), unless the ST creates everything himself, say he creates a story where Goratrix is going to do something nasty and the PCs need to do something, even if it's just to gather allies to deal with him, etc, and that is exactly where I want to get. One of the problems I've always seen in the WoD is that it takes a huge deal of effort for ST, because they need to create many things themselves and many times from scratch, by the way I have mentioned in a thread some time ago that I have a friend who is a ST for some decades and he gave up ST Vampire exactly because he said it's just "too much work", the setting doesn't give us many tools and creating a city and a story, etc, from scratch is a huge deal of work. I mean, I really love this kind of work, because I love writing and creating, but sometimes I wish we had more fully explored antagonists etc. and a some "help to my worldbuilding" and that's why my tendency is to always kind of "welcome" the likes of Haight, so to speak.

                                        I think a major reason why Samuel Haight was disliked is that since the key concepts about him are unique to him, the only way he could have continued to be used as an antagonist is to give him plot armor to prevent the PCs from killing him. If he got killed, an ST just couldn't replace him with yet another Skindancer Ghoul Mage with Thaumaturgy. There's only so many of them that could exist, right? So the sourcebooks he appeared in gave all sorts of bad instructions to the ST that were bound to piss off players to no end.

                                        "Haight has escaped... The storyteller can use Haight as a continuing nemesis in future stories..." - Valkenburg Foundation

                                        "Haight can reappear as the Storyteller likes, but the characters should not have the opportunity to kill him. He will be appearing in future White Wolf products." - Storyteller's Guide to the Sabbat

                                        "Pursuit of Sam Haight will prove futile, under no circumstances should Haight be captured, as he will be showing up again in future supplements." - Book of Chantries

                                        "There is absolutely no chance that the pack could locate him, even if they tried." - Rage Across the Amazon

                                        Yeah, that's fun for the PCs. This is classic White Wolf railroading at its worst. (This was a big problem for LOTS of RPGs around this time.)

                                        The original concept of Sam Haight is perfectly fine. The creator or reviver of the Skindancers. A brand new enemy for the Garou has been created. He could very well be a classic, fun repeating villain in any Werewolf chronicle. Or if he is killed, since the secret is now out there, he could easily be replaced by one of his disciples who in turn might become a classic, fun repeating villain of his own.

                                        Now if you use a faction, as opposed to a specific individual, to initiate the game plot, then there's no problem to keep it going despite whom the PCs might defeat or destroy. In literature, there is no problem with Dracula being the antagonist for the entire book. In RPG games, it's important to have a fall back plan in case the PC Johnathon Harker kills Dracula in Transylvania. The ST needs to be flexible enough to go, "You know what, that's fine. Dracula's destroyed. I'll just send the Brides of Dracula out for revenge to London after Harker returns there. I can preserve most of what I planned anyway using them. They Brides will be the real villains!"

                                                        That's basically about it. At the time Sam was being written there was a real push in the WoD community (at least online) from players about mix-and-matching different splat aspects together. And lots of jokes about playing werewolf-vampires with hedge magic and finding ways to stick cybernetic devices from MtA on them, or how to get access to Changeling powers. etc. Samuel Haight was very much intended to be a meta-commentary from the game writers about the community at the time. And more serious players (who hated that sort of "munchkinism") hated Sam Haight. Players who wanted to powergame looked at him as an inspiration (which just pissed off the serious players even more). And other players just sort of enjoyed the meta-narrative of seeing the writers acknowledge the short of bullshit that some players in the community were trying to do.

                                                        By the time Chaos Factor hit Sam was absolutely intended to be a meta-joke about various gonzo concepts in the WoD fan community as well as about certain players who tried to lean into that too hard. Ultimately Sam was a character where either people hated him and hated the meta-joke he represented, or they hated him while enjoying the meta-joke. At the end I don't think there were too many people who liked him, though in his earliest iteration as the Skin Dancer I think he was pretty cool.
