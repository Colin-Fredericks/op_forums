                                Originally posted by Lysander View Post
                                My next question is why a remake?

                                The terminology around these discussions generally makes them harder to have.

                                This isn't, strictly speaking, a remake. It's not a remake of the 1994 movie, because it's a TV show that's ignoring the changes made in that movie and going back to the 1976 book as it's base source. Calling it an adaption of a book is a better way of approaching it because... that's what it is. It's looking at a 46 year old story, and adapting it to a different medium and updating some stuff for a modern audience. The just also happens to be a popular movie made on the same source material.

                                Let's also remember it's been 28 years since the movie came out. There are people that have been born, graduated university, and gotten their first career oriented job since the movie was released. A whole generation between the last major adaptation of the books and this adaptation of the books is a big stretch to treat as excessive.

                                And of course, why? Because money. We live in a capitalist society (however any of us feel about that and however strongly). AMC is an adaptation juggernaut. Their highly successful adaptations have helped fuel a bunch of highly successful original programming as well. Profiting off of successful adaptations gives them the bank accounts to take risks on a lot of shows that other TV studios might take a pass on, or would get paywalled into premium channels/proprietary streaming services. To paraphrase Robert Downy Jr. to Chris Evans (who is very vocal about how much he dislikes big budget Hollywood studio movies): You play Captain America, you make him the best version of him he can be under the MCU leadership team, and then you have all the money you want to do whatever indie projects you want to do; which more people will watch because the guy that plays Captain America is in them.

                                We don't get TV shows like Mad Men, Breaking Bad/Better Call Saul, and other extremely well received shows from AMC without them also doing things like The Walking Dead.

                                HBO creatives on some of their smaller cool shows frequently joked about getting "that dragon money," because when HBO was flush during the height of GoT's success, HBO green-lit a bunch of smaller stuff they might have passed on in leaner times, and gave them the budgets they needed to really shine.

                                Dislike the system all you want, but it's the system these people have to operate in. They need big franchise money as their core income. That means finding big existing properties to put their spin on. That's why remakes/reboots/reimaginings/new adaptations have always been part of the business. Dragonball, one of the largest animation juggernauts in the world, started as yet another retelling of Journey to the West, which is one of the most retold stories in existence. The majority of Shakespeare's players are not original works, as they're either retellings of historical events, or retellings of previous fiction other people did first. When the US film industry took off, they remade moves all the time.

                                Retelling popular stories is such a default mode of human culture, and cuts across all cultures, that the question shouldn't be "why a remake?" but, "why has it taken this long to get a new version of this?" In pretty much every culture on Earth, once fixed forms of a story (novels, scripts, etc.) are introduced, adaptations to other media and remakes start happening. We have ~500 years of history to attest to this.

                                This isn't to say the new stories don't deserve a chance too, but the reactionary movement against retelling older stories is very new, and mostly brought on by the digital age and much easier access to older versions of the story. The reason people are so burnt out on remakes today is that we live in an age where we actually have easy access to the originals and all the retellings made since, so it feels saturated. Twenty years ago, the difficulty in trying to binge watch every retelling of A Christmas Carol would have been finding copies of all of them, now it's mostly finding the time to watch them all as the movies alone would equate to something like 50 hours.

                                Surely there must be other books and legends about the undead that could have been adapted for films.
                                Again: this is a new adaptation of the novels for TV. They're also hoping to adapt a huge amount of Rice's books that have never actually been adapted at all (there's a lot of books in the Vampire Chronicles and it's witches spin off).

                                And since this is a TV studio making TV, that means they're not taking up space in a movie studio's production budget for doing a new vampires story, or adapting a less known one.

                                I know there is a huge amount of manga books that deal with vampires and even horror writers like Bentley Little did a take on the genre years ago that was interesting and he burrowed from Chinese lore.
                                I believe I've made this point in the recent past already: manga gets adapted to other media all the time. If you want to watch some cool manga inspired shows about vampires, there's a bunch of anime out there for you to consume already, and thanks to the streaming era it's more accessible to non-Japanese audiences than ever.

                                If you think retellings are generally badly received... ask manga/anime fans what they feel about live action adaptations (even ones done in Japan).

                                                Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post
                                                Watchmen was a sequel rather than an adaptation.

                                                I will state that it fundamentally DISAGREES with Alan Moore on superheroes being an extension of white power fantasies, though.

                                                Moore, being a brit, doesn't seem to realize that African Americans have their own tradition of their own folk heroes and vigilantes.

                                                Hell, every country has stuff like that. Many Japanese folktales and in things like Kabuki theatre are stories of folk heroes or members of low ranking samurai families looking to right wrongs or protect the common folk. Many superhero films and comics built upwards out of cowboy movies, which themselves were often inspired by Japanese samurai movies and were themselves inspired by Japanese folktales and theatre. And then you have an equally long history of similar stories about vigilantes who right wrongs with magical or superhuman ability coming from China and India.

                                                Even the earliest comic books like Superman were more about class struggle and fighting against corrupt politicians, land and business owners than anything related to race (though many of the characters in these early comics were write). Now certainly, some superheroes are extensions of white power fantasies or have been adopted into white power fantasies (the Punisher is a good example) but yeah, I don't think Alan Moore was really correct in that assertion that superheroes were something unique to western, white society or being an extension of white power fantasies.
