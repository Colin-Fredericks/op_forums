              It would've likely flopped. Games, especially back in the day, had to release by a certain time finished or not. There's actually a logical reason behind this. The financial advisors and schedulers have to calculate how long developers can work on games without going over budget. It's also important for the advertisements to create maximum hype. Wait too long and interest dies out completely, and it becomes a net loss.

              That said, if the game had as many problems as the articles indicate, then it would have flopped when they were forced to release. That coupled with the waning popularity of MMOs, means it would fail some how based on current data.

              That said, MMOs are regaining popularity in recent years. However, recent games like V Rising have taken the spot of vampure mmorpg. Even if that wasn't a factor, if they began developing it again today, it wouldn't come out in time to maximize hype.

              TLDR, game would have failed regardless if it was released or not. Even if they tried again today, they can't make the game fast enough. Overall, the MMO is gone forever. For now.

                      I think the question and responses suffer a lot from unstated assumptions.

                      Define flop for example. Is an MMO that isn't financially profitable, but has a strong dedicated fanbase that are enough to justify keeping it up and running since there are other associated products with the brand that get increased profits from the increased buzz?

                      It's not like CCP didn't understand the MMO market, the programming demands, or how to make a niche MMO work. EVE as never been a giant in the MMO world, but it's had longevity most MMO could never dream of.

                      There's a huge amount of internal information we just don't have to really know if CCP was setting realistic goals for itself. There's a huge difference between making a movie for $10M that brings in $20M in the box office sales and is counting on some cult secondary market success to shore things up, and a film budgeted for $100M only doing $20M at the box office; there's just no way to recoup that kind of failure to adequately predict the market.

                      Consider LotR Online, ESO, SW:TOR, and FF14 are all doing fairly well, are some of those aren't exactly spring chickens, knowing how to market to existing fans of an existing franchise was clearly not a bad idea of surviving the late 2000s early 2010s slump in MMOs.

                      While I'm not so optimistic to claim it ever had a chance of being one of the top grossing MMOs ever, it reject the idea that there was some inherent level where a realistic level of success was impossible. Obviously CCP did the math and cut their losses, but that was after a considerable development cycle. Had that cycle gotten a functional game out instead of leading to a cancellation, I think it at least had a chance.

                          The main issue with trying to convert a TTRPG to MMO is that pretty much every game group plays it a bit differently at their table. As such, however you design the MMO, it will not line up with large swaths of the player base.

                          I remember some of the original development renders for the V:tM MMO and they were blowing huge amounts of development resources on things like how a long dress would trail properly going up or down stairs. I don't think a single person I have played with over the decades would have put the priority on clothing physics. This clothing focus continued into the V5 art work, and having recently watched reviews of Swansong, I have to say that V:tM is becoming more and more of a dress up dolly simulator during fashion week, than horror game.

                          An MMO based in V:tM would end up being a dozen different games to cover everyone's needs.
                          Bloodhunt for the combat junkies
                          Swansong for the solo role players
                          Bloodlines 1 for the people who are there for the story/replayability
                          ESO for the people who want a lore rich world to ignore when they tea bag each other
                          EVE for those who are into clan and faction related stuff
                          Fortnight for the malkavians
                          Dead by Daylight for the authentic sabbat vampire hunting mortals experience
                          Warframe for the fashion frame end game

                          There is no way to functionally develop an MMO with that diverse a player style and genre disparity.

                          What's worse is that out of all the games I just listed, none of them would have worked for the types of games the various groups I have played with normally took part in.

                          If the developers really wanted to burn resources for an online experience, then they should have sunk the development time into a WoD table top simulator equivalent.

                                  Yeah cosmetics are one of the big generally tolerated microtransaction styles by video gamers. If you don't care, you don't spend money on them, but there's enough people that will drop a little for their favorite skins, and enough people that will drop hundreds on every last option, that a lot of games can go free-to-play and make their money mostly on cosmetics. So.... that's not inherently a wrong-headed priority.

                                  I also don't buy that the MMO needed to be everything to everyone to be successful.

                                  The HtR video games are pretty much antithetical to the HtR TTRPG. A lot of the TTRPG fans of HtR hated the video games on release because the promoted the worst shallow approach to the material prompted by the core books off-brand art. But... once you play them for what they are... they're solid games and the Hunter stuff adds a nice layer of depth and storytelling that most games of its genre don't have.

                                  Bloodlines 1 is immensely popular in the WoD fandom despite differences in playstyle. I've never play VtM in a fashion the resembles BL1... but I love playing the game (well more loved, I don't replay it as much as I used to).

                                  If the game is fun and good, people will play it. If its fun, good, and sells the preexisting brand properly, even more people will play it. People get that different media means different styles of approaching the brand. You don't need to create a video game where people feel like they're playing VtM how they're play VtM in a TTRPG. You need a video game that feels like it gets what VtM is supposed to be about broadly and does something neat with it regarding the play loop. There's a reason (well more than one really) why Redemption is a footnote and Bloodlines is a cult classic (though also a "flop" upon release); and it isn't because Bloodlines somehow managed to find this perfect balance of different TTRPG playstyles to appeal to. Bloodlines feels like a VtM vidego game. Redemption feels like a generic vampire videogame with VtM stuff tacked on. For example, in Bloodlines how you stat your character actually matters to how the story plays out,.as different traits can open up different dialogue options/etc. and make different paths easier or harder to complete. In Redemption how you stat out Christophe basically doesn't matter because the story plays out the same way regardless.
