                        Originally posted by MarkK View Post


                        This review you're quoting verges on feeling like, even in just this one paragraph, a lecture that V5 Sabbat is good because the way the Sabbat were written for some 19+ years of the game (I was going to cut it off at V20, but it's not like V20 changed the Sabbat especially, so, 19+ it is) was creatively bankrupt. If you don't feel like that's telling a bunch of people who had been playing VtM for that amount of time "what you liked about the way this thing in the game was portrayed for the majority of its existence was wrong", I'm unsure of what to say there. It's less a review of why V5 Sabbat is good for any actual merit to do with the book in terms of quality or consistency of its own writing, or even value as a gaming aid, and more that it's good because it got rid of things the reviewer had a grudge against for some time. And that for me is one of the huge problems of 5th edition overall. It feels like vanishingly few people can actually talk about why it is a good edition on its own merits and quality, instead having to default to why they so very much disliked the VtM that came before it and explaining why it was wrong that people liked it. That makes it feel less like a game of its own and more like some fans (and fans who became writers) of vampire getting to feel vindicated on the internet and in LARPs, because the game now agrees with them.

                        When people note that part of their problem with V5 is that it seems basically like saying "almost everything significant after the corebook was a mistake and you didn't like or understand quality VtM gaming if you liked it" and how stunningly insulting that is, reviews like this seem to be replying to that with "well exactly!"

                        And it seems like when people point that out, the default then becomes trying to lecture that in fact in addition to being bad at gaming, it was also morally wrong to have enjoyed VtM for the past 19+ years or so as far as post 1e corebook material.
                        I can speak plenty for why I like V5 on its own qualities. And I do not need to bash the way I played in the last 20 years.

                        I started playing at 12, between 1999 and 2000: Revised was well on its way, but we in Italy of course had some "buffer time" with manuals arriving, so I started playing in a very gonzo mindset.

                        I find things to be loved in every edition of Vampire so far: when I see huge problems and holes, I also try to contextualize them (and usually the answer I find is "it's an homage to the genre by a 20-something american, written without extensive information availability at a time when the Internet was not so informative").

                        What I wanted from a new edition wasn't V20.5, with some minor modifications to rules and setting.

                        I wanted something bold and intriguing, as innovative as VtM was when it first came out. I've been following 5th edition since the playtest materials, and I've always felt new ideas and mechanics to be amazing.

                        Marrying the Storyteller System with game design innovations of the last 30 years and some common sense practices, like "take half", fail forward and similar? Useful to make the game flow smoother and reinforce the primacy of narration over dice.

                        Streamlined combat rules have been a necessity since forever: some V5 choices needed polishing? Perhaps, but they delivered. My first V5 game was a 1-year long campaign with Ancillae characters and a lot of combat, and fights were quick and interesting, not agozining salvoes of dices.

                        The Hunger mechanic is brilliant, it evokes feelings and color scenes, maintaining a consistent tone in the game while making it more interesting: again, I've read extensive rants over its crippling mathematics but I've played a many-dice, lots-of-rolls campaign and it severely impacted (for the better) in just a couple scenes. Again, Messy Criticals may need some polishing, but I just ruled out that if I don't have a ready-made effect, I use them as ST metacurrency to introduce complications later on.

                        Mathematics are instead a strong point of the edition, as in the 5-dots cap / Blood Potency dynamics we now have a more balanced and mathematically consistent framework for building PCs and NPCs alike.

                        "You are what you eat": it's clear that at the beginning they meant something more far-reaching, but the final product, with Humors and Discrasyas, is fascinating and creepy, and, again, really reinforces the themes of the game giving greater player options.

                        Morality rules give you the flexibility to adapt tone and pace of the game to your tastes. I admit that it took a while to give the proper game space to Touchstones, but when I managed to focus on them, in a Tzimisce game focused on abandoning Humanity nonetheless, they were again a huge plot opportunity.

                        And don't let me start talking about Loresheets: is there a more fucking ingenious way to incorporate 30 years of metaplot into a chronicle?!


                        I really like making Thin-Bloods characters, each one of them can be unique, and Thin Blood Alchemy is gory, messy and rich of plot hooks.

                        I absolutely LOVE Disciplines as Power Trees, because in this way every PC and NPC can be absolutely unique, and you can easily port over powers from other editions or even Requiem.

                        Speaking of, I could find of interest even the adoption of Requiem stats: to me, it was the promise of bridging edition wars and easily use a lot of interesting source materials.

                        And change in the metaplot? Believe me or not, I loved the first presentation of source material even MORE than the final product. The mistery of the Beckoning, the idea that Camarilla elders were a powerful but frightened network in hiding, Anarchs finding themselves in government for the first time, Sabbat fighting the fight of their unlives, the Inquisition again a threat...

                        I was and I still am very sad at the amount of vitriol leveraged against the game developers: when it first came out, the people working on it were accused of the most nefarious intentions. I frankly find shameful that a good chunk of the Internet fanbase would not be happy without Martin Ericsson losing his job. Like, it would be the dream of each and everyone of us to develop a new Vampire edition, and scores of people on Internet did everything they could to take it away from "Dracula" because... what, they didn't like his photo in the V20 corebook? Or it was just envy? He was called out daily as a nazi abuser, an allegation that just by looking at his Twitter would seem ridicolous.

                        I do not regret the way I played, the books I bought or my first absurd characters: my first V5 campaign was gonzo action against Tzimisce Methuselas drawing on Dirty Secrets of the Black Hand.

                        But I really think V5 has the best designed tools in the history of the game, and given choice I will never play with another ruleset.

                                    Originally posted by Manfr View Post
                                    I can speak plenty for why I like V5 on its own qualities. And I do not need to bash the way I played in the last 20 years.
                                    So you're a better reviewer than many (and I'm not counting CTPhipps whose review I just disagree, don't find bad, and I know that reviewing professionally is a very different kind of animal).

                                    But let me comment on your points, and I'll actually agree with many of them, yet it won't make me like V5's current path.

                                    I wanted what I think everyone wanted from V5: an overhaul on the rules and a bg change in the setting. And I find extremely offensive the insistence in believing everyone's complains about the changes is because they wanted to play the same game as ever. VtM had bad rules forever. They missed every opportunity to make them even as they made better rules for their other products in the past. Time of Judgement put the setting into a crossroads, where it couldn't go on without dropping some themes to embrace new ones. Those were all things I expected and wanted.

                                    Most of the mechanics delivered, and I mean it. The new dice mechanics are interesting and flow smoother. The combat mechanics are easy and stay on what the game proposes instead of saying they won't be the focus and then diving into a rabbit hole of a simulationist mess. Hunger, Messy Criticals, Blood Potency, 5-dots cap, Humors and Dyscrasias, Convictions and Touchstones, Loresheets, those are all rules that are at least conceptually good and just need polishing, Touchstones more, Loresheets less, I'm not as excited about some as you, but I'm maybe even more excited about others.

                                    Don't get me wrong, I'll definitely try to convince my players to at least give the system a try, just some tweaks here and there. Thin Bloods became better in my opinion too. Their Alchemy is at least a good idea, although it needs some heavy tinkering. The same goes for Disciplines in general, to a lesser extend, I think the concept of the power tree is good, but the execution needs a lot of tinkering and Oblivion is objectively a bad idea. Oblivion, specifically.

                                    Requiem stats were a given, they would have to be too obnoxiously stubborn to not make this change, and wildly creative to find another change.

                                    And most of my thoughts apply to the metaplot in some way. As I said, I did wanted change, I wanted the end of the world to be delivered fully. But then we got the same problems but intensified: good concepts, bad executions, except this time it is a worse execution and, being the plot instead of the rules, polishing is harder. We also got the same stance of ditching things out, but plot development isn't the place to do this. You can simplify the setting by ignoring elements for now instead of actively butchering then.

                                    The Beckoning? Intriguing. But what's happening to those elders? Where are they going and what they're doing there? They went to the Middle East, not to another world, the lack of info isn't disturbing, it just sounds lazy. The Camarilla changing for its elders to survive? Excellent. The Camarilla turning into the GOP? Just what? The Anarchs rise to power in this vacuum is a good development. The Anarchs being regarded as the only sensible faction in the core and getting the treatment they got in their book, not so much. The Second Inquisition? With a proper blown up of the Masquerade as we know it would be a fantastic touch. As the same hunters from before, but nastier because lols and historical buildings in Vienna got "casually" blown up without the no one finding out it was a secret society? That's pretty much intellectually offensive. Sabbat fighting in their sacred war? Good. Becoming dumber and simpler under the justification that every sensible member went to other sects and we don't need the Sabbat anymore? No, thanks.

                                    We got V5 in the last half of the 2010's. This isn't a 20-something American with no proper research tools doing a semi-professional job anymore. Changing was a given, a necessity, but those changes should be well delivered. They should be good ideas with good executions, they didn't need to be awesome either, but good. The setting development ignored a big chunk of the same 30 years of game design innovations that made the rules deliver. Most changes were just focused on the already mentioned subtraction of play styles. The factions got individually dumber, important events went unexplored beyond their intended consequences, and the game is honestly shy from actual major changes, which makes the minor ones seem nonsensical. And they are still horrible at reaching for information and players from other places before writing about those places, making instead shallow references. The Chechnya incident was only the most prominent in the media, but the actual worse example is how they used the Middle East as the focal point of so many things and actually didn't even tried to describe it in any form.

                                    Now you want to blame the backlash on people being envious? Of course it can't have anything to do with context and reality being a complex place. Ericsson didn't loose his job because people freaked n the internet, he loose his job because Paradox freaked out, don't blame company decisions on fans. Yes, the whole debacle was a sorry mess, but unfortunately this is how public opinion works, sorry messes happen and companies need to navigate. In this case, it happened because WoD unfortunately already have a history with neo-nazi players, it isn't the company's fault, but is context.

                                    Please stop simplifying other people's positions for your own convenience. You're in your right to be well served by V5 and like it. You're in your right to defend it with good arguments and you do have them. You're on your right to be angry at what happened to Ericsson and I am too. But reality is this complex, nuanced place where things we like may have problems and people may have different opinions for a good reason, not just because they're envious (this REALLY pissed me off). You have a right to contextualize the game you like to see it under a better light, but not to demand others to do the same, and without contextualizing their opinion when it isn't convenient.

                                                        Here was the biggest problem with Martin: He's a horrible PR face of a company. He's the kind of passionate, enthusiastic, and talented creative that shouldn't be let anywhere near the press, a blog, or a Twitter account while working on stuff. Why? Because he's so wrapped up in how cool he feels something is, that he forgets he's not a fan chatting with fans, but he's a brand representative who's words are going to get scrutinized. He said a lot of dumb things. Most of it was harmless. A lot of it would only take a few seconds of searching to put into context necessary to get what he was trying to say but stumbled around with the words. I'm pretty sure everyone here is guilty of that, but most of us have also likely been in a position where we had to turn off fans chatting on the Internet brain, and put on "polite adult society" brain instead without being the head of a company that's staking a lot on our personal creative vision.

                                                        A lot of people fell into the hole of "Does he keep making these weird comments and then correcting them because he's just bad at this part of the job, or did he mean it they way he first said it and someone at Paradox or within WW had to press him to do the right PR move?" and then every future odd comment gets added to the "maybe he really means this stuff..." pile until they feel like that's the assumption to go with. The Zak S thing cannot be underestimated here in how much that whole mess put a lot of the public impressions of the early WW team in the negatives before they really did anything.

                                                        I'm a pretty critical of V5 and WW/Paradox guy (even if monteparnas basically summed up my biggest problem with V5: lots of good ideas, with lots of poor execution). But I don't think anyone of the people I've sat around in private chats with cringing at the nWW's early missteps were ever envious of Martin. If anything quite the opposite. I remember talking about one of the first recorded public panels the WW team did at a convention and we pitied the man because it was painfully clear he was not ready to face hardcore fans in his new role. There was this whole awesome presentation about the new direction for the WoD in the 21st century, and then very confused looking Martin when the only questions related at all to what he'd just spent all that time talking about was the promise of a more diverse development and writing staff for the games to help avoid the mishaps of the oWW, and then just a slew of long standing WoD fan gripes. "I'm jealous of that guy" was pretty much the exact opposite of what reactions I saw.
