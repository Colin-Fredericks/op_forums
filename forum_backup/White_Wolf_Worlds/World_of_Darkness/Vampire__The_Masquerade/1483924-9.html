        I'm gonna try to help CTPhipps out for a minute here.

        To be fair, some Lasombra did in fact manage to hold onto such power as they clawed out for themselves in places, right from the book. For example, there's a bit from a member of the clan about how the Clan still holds onto their Church influence, apparently in the face of retaining little all else, to the point of looking like they have no other mortal influence coming into new cities. Though it's honestly... not that impressive for the shine they try to put on how important it is that they did when they have to talk about it in the terms of 'it may look like we lost all our influence, but we have this one very specific thing still!'. Even a description of a specifically influential Lasombra is counterbalanced by a gripe that they lost all the ways they used to ply influence more unsubtly when they left the Sabbat, but we'll talk about that in a minute. Because also, from the book, others did not hold onto such things. With, for instance, Talley noting that some Lasombra will be taking the Camarilla's money to work as their enforcers. This is not a thing wealthy people need to do. Otherwise, as far as some Lasombra having had to indeed leave their previous place behind, when the word "migration" gets used, the notion that they pretty all much kicked back in place, chilln out, maxin' relaxin' all cool while shooting some b ball outside of the school, does not bear out. The part where by contrast some of them got in one little fight and their mom got scared and said "you're moving with your Auntie and Uncle in Bel Air", does. Even simply from that the initial clusters of envoys had to go to various Camarilla cities to ask to be let in, let alone stuff about wanting to be entire presences in cities where the clan has not been.

        So again, like everything else, it's a mixed portrayal, or it's at least an effort thereby at a mixed portrayal, further underscored in this case that you are dealing with Lasombra talking about Lasombra, meaning that even while there's a grain of salt qualifier for their statements, that applies to both when they talk about how things are still good, and how bad things have got. Likely again the book trying to go "the truth lies somewhere in the middle."

        The problem people are having is not that it reads like they especially disagree that the book tried to say all this kind of thing. The problem they are having is that they feel the book said all this kind of thing *badly*, in a way that does not, for them, sufficiently paper over the logic holes, plot gaps, and the like, something like this entire sequence would expose, even just from writing trying to say the defection had both positive and negative aspects. That it all just feels like a mess.

        "The book can say what it says, but here is why that thing it said would make no sense/feel entirely inconsistent if you think about it.", or "the book can say this thing, but they did not say it in a convincing way for not actually addressing the issues incident x should cause" is the heart of the note, with various elaborations.

        Ultimately it is worth talking about the whole Doylist vs Watsonian textual approach thing, because this thread is definitely crashing into it. People are saying stuff that boils down to not buying into the underlying narrative craft for feeling the writing choices gloss over whole huge issues that logically should have been problems. Or, to try for half a second to be topical to the thread topic, when they say the actual implications of the punitive game mechanics of the Lasombra's own discipline themselves are an obstacle to what the in setting justifications of the defection want to be, in many ways it isn't satisfying to reply to either of these issues "well, as long as you see that it is justifiable within setting.."

        The problem here is that this is a development meant to serve a meta narrative reason: The Sabbat are not playable in this edition, but the developers want the Lasombra be. So they did a plot to try and salvage that. The choices made for how to write that plot are, certainly for the people posting in this thread, ones that instead of smoothing the meta decision over, raise entirely new gripes of narrative cohesion and consistency with just a bunch of previous plot, or even more basic concepts of logic like "that it didn't screw them up even worse is not believable due to how cause and effect works, even if the writing claims it is". I can say "well, within the setting's internal narrative, this is why they regardless had to do that, and these are the costs they paid", and I did, and you can say that, and you eventually got there somewhat more accurately than before, but saying that doesn't actually solve for those issues.
