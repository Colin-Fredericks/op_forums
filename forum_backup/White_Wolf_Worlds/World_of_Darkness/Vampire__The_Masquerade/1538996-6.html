                            Originally posted by No One of Consequence View Post
                            I think a big part of Black Dog's existence is because someone at WW really, really wanted to republish Human Occupied Landfill, Dirt Merchant Games' "edgy" satire of early 90s rpg tropes. It and its sole sourcebook, BUTTery HOLesomeness, are the only nonWoD releases the brand ever did. And as I said, I don't consider those actual games but snarky cynical efforts at satirizing early 90s rpgs. While "art" is subjective, I'm not sure they qualify in the way that blurb uses the term.

                            The other aspect of its existence probably goes back to the original Brujah clanbook from 1992. One of the templates, the Rapper, used the word "fuck" in its character quote, and this apparently got some pushback from a few retailers. Because Vampire and the other games billed themselves as for "mature readers", I think there was always this desire to try to get into what the American movie industry would consider "hard R" territory and talk frankly about "adult themes" using "adult language" but also having to deal with the reality that RPGs - being primarily sold through comic book stores at the time - were essentially an "all ages" hobby.

                            Plus DC had created their Vertigo imprint just a few years earlier, making comics socially acceptable for alternative hipsters who didn't want to be associated with superhero nerds, and I think WWGS was always chasing that kind of reputation in the RPG sphere.

                            As an aside, around this same time there was a book released called Generation Ecch, a very sarcastic and cynical overview of what was being touted as Gen X cultural tropes and interests (indie films, raves, 70s nostalgia, comics, etc.). Each chapter was opened with a page by alternative comic book maker Evan Dorkin, and the comic book chapter included a panel pointing out that most "mature/adult" comics just ended up being an excuse to swear a lot and draw naked breasts. And if you are familiar with most of the Black Dog label books, you know exactly why I brought that up.

                            The initial wave was four books, one for each line. (God only knows what sort of book they'd have tried for Changeling if that line hasn't just been starting.) And I don't think any of them sold as well as was hoped.

                            I've never fully read the entire Giovoni Chronicles, so I can't really say if they needed to be Black Dog or not. Honestly, I think insisting that the three follow up volumes also carry the label is what even kept the BD brand alive for the next few years.

                            Freak Legion may as well be the poster child for what I mentioned earlier. It is almost entirely juvenile shock value for shock value's sake.

                            Destiny's Price was a good idea, as a guide to inner city street life, the underground club scene, and gutterpunk culture is the sort of thing that would be useful for all of the WoD game lines. Unfortunately, this book is just sort of there. It never really got into the sort of detail about sex, drugs, and violence that would really justify this having the BD label. It feels like this was just going to be a regular book on the release schedule, got picked to be part of this project, and so the art department threw in a few pictures of S&M strippers with bare breasts. (It wasn't really helped that shortly after it was released, films such as Trainspotting and Kids came out, making this instantly feel a little sanitized and dated.)

                            Wraith's Spectres book was probably the one that came closest to deserving the Black Dog brand. Wraith was already a somewhat dark game that rewarded a type of roleplaying based on delving into what made your character tick and what baggage they carried around. With Spectres, you had that compounded by a massive amount of nihilism that I think is best left to players with a certain level of emotional maturity.

                            Montreal by Night always felt like it was using the BD label as a marketing gimmick. It really didn't need it, but "Oooooh, it's a Sabbat city, come look at how eeeeeevil and wicked they are!" Plus some gratuitous human toilets.

                            Clanbook Baali really has no good reason to exist. While half of the book does have some interesting ideas about prehistoric elder gods, it also wastes time with juvenile shock material (including really gross opening fiction that feels like it was written to appeal to the National Association of Marlan Brando Look-Alikes).

                            The Cainite Heresy probably didn't really need the label but got it mainly because of the focus on religious sects and its reexaminations of the Path of Evil Revelations. The fact that it was written by two skilled authors, Ken Hite and R Sean Borgstrom, certainly helped elevate its quality.

                            Finally, Charnel Houses of Europe. I think this is the only book that ever really lived up to the label's potential. It is a serious look at a serious subject, and endeavors to use roleplaying games as a teaching tool to help educate people about the Holocaust. It was a deeply personal project for developer Richard Dansky, and it shows.

                            (Allegedly, there were plans to do a book for Aberrant about mega-social abilities and related powers. Because this would have included cults of personality and people who would use these types of powers in very abusive ways, it was planned to carry the Black Dog label. But all of the Trinity material was moved to the Arthaus label and doing an adults only book just wasn't feasible under that set up.)









                            Apologies for the thread necromancy, but I was thinking about this old post and it got me thinking about Black Dog in general.

                            I'd say Hunter Book: Wayward was a good final note for the Black Dog label, coming out in either 2001 or early 2002, years after the previous Black Dog releases, IIRC. Supposedly the Black Dog label was about to be phased out entirely but was brought out one last time for the Wayward book due to how the book handles subjects like psychopathy, murder, and ordinary humans who snap.

                            I'm genuinely curious what Changeling's Black Dog book was going to be, if any such thing was planned at all, and I know for a fact that Ghouls: Fatal Addiction was initially planned to be a Black Dog release but Rob Hatch made a last-second decision to have it be a general VTM release since it was a flagship for the Year of the Ally promotion and he wanted to get home early that night to watch WCW Monday Nitro.

                            I was also wondering about some books that could've been Black Dog books but weren't.

                            Ghouls Fatal Addiction is the big one, and so was the original Clanbook Tzimisce (to the point it sometimes gets put on lists of the Black Dog releases)

                            I also think the infamous Earthbound book for Demon: The Fallen could've been a contender for the label given how it handled religious subject matter, but by then, Black Dog was a relic being phased out

                                                        This is something of a tangent perhaps but I do want to say a couple words in Demon’s honor, at least in the sense that there was some potential there that could have been nurtured at one point and time.

                                                        Obviously it’s a matter of taste but having Demon deal with cults that feel like they could have been shaken out of the Exorcist III or a Lovecraft story isn’t inherently a bad thing. That pulpy occultism and Satanism still has traction in the vocabulary of horror that WoD has always tapped into and I definitely think something could have been developed to bring out something along those lines.

                                                        Of course there would still be room for a metatextual discussion about the significance and usefulness of the sub-genre.

                                                        I don’t know, I feel like I haven’t fully fleshed this out but I hope that this makes sense:

                                                        There’s a tendency (which I certainly participate in fwiw) in parts of ttrpg spaces to look at the inherited trappings of the genre and put them under the microscope. A classic example is the standard faux-Satanic cult of evil of your run of the mill D&D or Call of Cthulhu scenario. These get criticized, correctly, for portraying a more or less entirely phantasmagorical image which does not resemble what most cults actually look like. So there’s a movement to replace these groups with more realistic alternatives (or do without them entirely).

                                                        However there’s another tendency, an impulse, to maintain the old tradition, and it’s not just held by beer-and-pretzels gamers who don’t particularly want to think about it. There’s another tendency which points out that, even if time has rendered some of these ideas somewhat comic and that they’re often used lazily, they still do have a kernel of artistic potence which has the capacity to capture an audience. This tendency argues that it’s worthwhile to keep these elements, around even if they are basically anachronistic, because under the layers of cheap imitation there’s something solid.

                                                        I think both ends of this spectrum have good points abt the implicit politics, storytelling potency, etc. of these sorts of things. I for one try to carry out a sort of dialectic between the two - maintaining what’s of value from the old pulp material but under the influence of modern times.

                                                        I hope this wasn’t too tangential or meandering but the direction of the conversation brought this to mind for me and I figured I’d share my two cents. Hope y’all are having a good one.

                                                            Cthulhu is, after all:

                                                            * A dead god who will rise again
                                                            * A god who will bring the end of the world
                                                            * A god who is worshiped by all races and social classes
                                                            * A god whose followers want to bring about the end of the world
                                                            * A god who will smite the unbeliever and reward the faithful

                                                            Which is to say that doomsday end of the world cults are not necessarily as unrealistic as we may think.
