                            Most capitalist economists would probably mean that it's better to tax wealth than income, because wealth hoarding locks value out of the economy. Property taxes are one of the few remaining wealth taxes in the US (we used to tax way more types of wealth), and to replace that we tax income and consumption far more than makes any lick of sense.

                            Likewise, vampires don't really care about land itself. Vampires don't buy dirt patches just to increase how much land they have. They care about land the same reason land was the main source of measuring wealth for most of human history: you can't have human civilization without land and putting land to use. Land means agriculture to feed people, housing to home them, and so on. Sufficiently prosperous land can maintain settlements that eventually grow into cities, and that means a high population concentration and increased human nocturnal activity for vampires to use for feeding. Land is the key form of wealth, because all other forms of wealth are meaningless if you can't trade them for stuff that comes from the land. For vampires that's blood.

                                Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
                                Technically, in the US, you pay a property tax for any real estate you own. How rate of taxation is based on the value of the property, and a lot with a house on it is worth a lot more than an empty lot with some weeds growing on it.

                                This is, quite stupidly, the primary method for paying for primary and secondary education in public schools here. Which exists pretty much exclusively to ensure the rich stay rich and the poor stay poor because poor communities own less property, and what property they do own is worth less so is taxed less, and thus their schools get less funding.

                                This has minimal impact on any of the topics at hand though.
                                The funny thing is that there is another level to property tax and real estate value, depending on where the property is. Namely that the tax rate can be locked in at the time of purchase and the sale price. So a house purchased say in the 1940s would pay around $500 a year in property taxes, but if that same property was sold to a new owner the tax rate would be modernized and thus be around $4,500 a year. The loophole being that if you inherit the property from a family member, then you inherit that original tax bracket. All of this depends on local laws of course.

                                As far as the rich staying rich and the poor staying poor, it would be more accurate to say that the system penalizes people who don't set down roots. If your grandparents purchase a house and pay off the mortgage, then your parents inherit it and are able to save money because there isn't a mortgage to pay. By the time the grandchildren inherit the house they might have to do some expensive renovations, but they can easily turn the house into a rental to generate money. All the while benefiting from the grandparents property original tax rate. All of this goes away if the offspring sells the house to finance other things.

                                From a vampire perspective this gets very interesting because the farther back in time you purchase property, the cheaper it is to pay for. With an unlimited lifespan a vampire can use banking and property loopholes in ways mortals can only dream of.

                                    It doesn't really change the "rich stay rich" much, because you need to meet certain levels of financial success to buy a house, pay off a mortgage, and even more so if the area is desirable enough to have multiple generations live there. Poor people can feel like they've set down roots, because they've lived in the same town for generations, but if they never had the money to obtain land-based wealth, none of what you're talking about kicks in.

                                    Also, whether property taxes are grandfathered in like that varies highly by state, esp. compared to how public school funding is determined (which also impacts property values if you want to leverage a grandfathered low property tax to use a house as a source of collateral or income), as does how inheritance taxes get calculated in states/areas with grandfathered property taxes vs. ones that don't. If you live in a state with grandfathered property taxes, but high inheritance taxes that are based on the current value of the house, you can easily get taxed far more for taking your parent's house over than you'll ever save in property taxes.

                                    The problem for vampires trying to take advantage of this, is that they have far easier ways to accumulate wealth that doesn't involve regularly faking their own deaths and being their own descendants (which has gotten much harder), they can achieve all sorts of savings because their budgets don't have to account for nearly as much as living people, and proximity to reliable blood sources is always going to make a bigger difference.

                                                    VtM was mainstream in the past tense of "used to be." It fell out of that in the mid '00s: CCP's WoD MMO fell into development hell, the nWoD/CofD change over struggled with the core fans base and didn't lead to the CofD getting support for mainstream products, and it was a generally bad time for the RPG industry as a whole. As a note, the old TV show came out over 26 years ago, not 16. It was also... not good. Pretty much the only thing it had going for it was the main character, who's actor tragically died the year the show came out. Had that not happened, Fox might have kept the show going in hopes of retooling it to play to its strengths, but unfortunately that never got a chance to happen.

                                                    Where VtM was in 2002 is vastly different from where VtM was in 2022. Two decades is plenty of time for things to fall out of the mainstream and into niche or cult status.

                                                        Yeah, whatever cultural cache VtM had back in the early 00s has largely been spent over the two decades since the game ended. The 20th Anniversary Edition helped to rekindle its cult following, sure. But if it served to repair some of the broken base, V5 only broken the base in new and exciting ways. Nor did that new edition succeed in really propelling VtM to new popular attention, despite cross-collaboration with the likes of Critical Role. Nor have big attempts to establish it within the gaming space before or after V5 succeeded. Whether it's the MMO that fell to development hell, the Bloodlines sequel that still hasn't materialized and seems no closer to arriving, or the narrative games that seemed to garner more controversy than attention.

                                                        The original Bloodlines seems to be the only lasting legacy among most people who aren't in the tabletop space.

                                                        It's worth observing that within the big controversy over D&D's OGL, and the resulting mass examination of TTRPG alternatives to that system, you don't see many people talking about VtM. Plenty talking about Pathfinder, Call of Cthulhu, and the Old School Renaissance. But scarce little, from what I've observed, about Vampire: The Masquerade.

                                                        I wouldn't say VtM is "dead" (no pun intended). But I wouldn't say its cultural penetration is growing. Not by a long shot.
