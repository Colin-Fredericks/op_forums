    I think you may have misunderstood. While I do believe that V5 is a better game than D&D 4th edition, I think there was an important lesson in 4th eds failiure.
    It had a big launch, huge initial success. Corebooks on the bestseller lists. Marketing engine, market leader, conventions, it had the works.

                            Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post
                            This is a very eye opening thread. Apparently people see things very differently than me. I appreciate everyone taking the time to share their thoughts.
                            They do... as has been fairly obvious by the post Likes by various posters for pretty much as long as I’ve been coming here.

                            Purely anecdotal mind you, but the proportions of likes various sentiments generally get align rather well with the local WoD playerbase as well. There’s maybe one person who actually prefers V5 over V20 and, if there is a particular playstyle preference it’s much closer to “Vampions” than “A Monster I am lest a monster I become” or any sort of “Personal Horror” style of play.

                            Basically, most players are interested in playing Blade/Underworld the RPG and trying to get them to consider morality or something like Touchstones would get lip service at best. My current set of players for the most part latched right onto acquiring power and privilege by sucking up to the Camarilla.

                            Attempts to suck them into the nightly hunt led to them finding work arounds including previously mentioned pig’s blood and taking their Resources and opening a blood donation clinic near the university campus with a PR campaign to encourage donations with all but a couple of pints a day (labeled defective in inventory and “disposed of”) going to a local children’s hospital.

                            Basically, they have no interest in RPing that part beyond the bit now played for laughs of the gal playing a hot vampire chick in the game who uses Tinder as an online delivery menu for feeding on casual hookups... “I’m feeling like Italian tonight; swipe right.”

                            V5 spent too much effort trying to cater to one particular playstyle with a very vocal social media presence that it basically shot down the universal urban fantasy toolkit that previous editions were. That’s pretty much the exact same mistake D&D 4E made... it listened to some very loud, but few in actual number voices on their available social media c. 2006-7 who were griping about edge cases and took it as permission to completely gut and rewrite the core of the game when 95% would have preferred just cleaning up those edges.

                            Which actually brings me to...

                            Originally posted by Trollroot View Post
                            It [4E] folded as soon as a competitor arrived.
                            Worth noting is that it wasn’t just ANY competitor, it was a competitor offering a continuation of D&D 3.5e with a few tweaks to things everyone agreed were broken... essentially a 3.75e or “what most fans were hoping 4E was going to be.”

                            That’s what let them steal a bit more than HALF of D&D 4E’s potential market by telling customers “we respect your preferences and wish to cater to them.”

                            Also worth noting on the flip side is that D&D 5e was heavily marketed as a return to form complete with a year long open “playtest” with regular polls asking “does this feel like D&D?” Now, I’m not foolish enough to believe that playtest was anything other than a giant marketing campaign that maybe changed a few things in the margins, but in terms of public perception it at least made people feel they were listening to the fans and the final 5e rules were a LOT closer to a streamlined 3.5e... (and rebranding a bunch of mechanics from 4E that they did keep with new names and saying they were new to 5e) which is why they pretty well pulled back the majority of what they’d lost to their competitor.

                            So, it’s probably NOT an accident that V20 is outperforming V5 on drivethuRPG... if the ability to keep buying 3.5e had been available c. 2008 when 4E launched I suspect a similar trend would have emerged for it as well.

                            Basically, the biggest competitor to V5 is that you can still buy new copies of V20... and because of existing player preferences, V20 is far more likely to be a player’s entry point than V5.

                                                            Originally posted by archderd View Post
                                                            it's kinda funny how this &quot;V20 vs V5&quot;-thread turned into a &quot;let's all talk shit about V5&quot;-thread
                                                            The versus implies a conflict, doesn't it? I don't think it's that surprising, really.

                                                            As always, the complaints on one side seem to be 'I don't have the options I used to have,' and the opposite complaint seems to be '1e marketing always promised this other game, and that's what V5 offers us, so IMO you're wrong.'

                                                            You're never going to get agreement between those two viewpoints. One took the game's initial pitch, its first few books, and its early marketing as the ideal of what the game is supposed to be, and the other took the way the game was popularly played and the way it was developed as the way it was supposed to be.

                                                            In a way, both groups are right. VTM was supposed to be a particular type of game, but it didn't always pull that off well, and quickly outgrew those initial ideas. So in that sense, it *was* (and is again) supposed to be played that way.

                                                            But the game is also the sum of all its parts, and it's also true that the initial intent wasn't well executed or that important to a lot of people. And a game is more than design as intended--it's how it's actually played and how it is laid out in official materials. If everywhere but a few books and some marketing materials it is a kitchen sink setting rather than a narrow experience, then it's fair to infer that that's actually what it is, regardless of what was initially intended.

                                                            What usually makes things go up in flames is when the 'this is the original intent' side implies 'you're guilty of badwrongfun' or when the other side gets too heated about 'you took away my toys'.

                                                            It's not inevitable that it turns to flames, by any means, but managing expectations and setting the right tone in threads that discuss the differences between the editions is very important.
