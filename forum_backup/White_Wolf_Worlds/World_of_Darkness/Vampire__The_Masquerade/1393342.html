    Considerations when building a city and its history?

    06-01-2020, 12:45 AM
    Hey all,

    I'm a new storyteller about to start a chronicle soon and I'm still in the process of thinking about the setting. I'm running V5 and I'm trying to homebrew a version of Seattle that's dynamic and engaging for my players, but I'm struggling with how to write the history of the city and the conflicts going on there somewhat. I've read a lot of the source material -- both sect books that have been released for V5, as well as some of the V20 Camarilla book and a fuckton of wiki articles, forum threads and Reddit posts. None of them adequately explained (in my opinion) how to incorporate/adapt a city's real-world history into the game.

    I suppose a lot of the trouble I'm having is boiling down to me not quite understanding how to write conflict between people who live for centuries. Seattle being a relatively young city almost makes this harder -- how does the Camarilla claim new territory, for example? Can any Camarilla-associated neonate claim praxis over a newly-founded city as long as they aren't contested, or do the Inner Council need to approve? How long do Princes tend to reign? I know that the answer is essentially 'whatever the storyteller needs to be the case for the plot to make sense', but I'd like to be at least somewhat consistent with the rest of the canon.

    I come from a background of running D&D with a homebrew setting where real-world history isn't nearly as much of a concern, and while I find the inclusion of real-world history and issues to be incredibly interesting and engaging, it certainly makes it harder to create a setting and story while remaining sensitive and respectful.

    With all that in mind, how have you gone about writing around history? And would it be appropriate for me to post specifics here for others to critique? Thanks in advance!

      Concerning characters (NPCs), I always try to define this four(five) things:

      - Aims / Objectives
      - Means
      - Likes
      - Dislikes
      - Enemies / obstacles

      Appearance, mannerisms, etc... are the icing on the cake. I come from an academic background (I a high school teacher with some narrative works published) and I really think that characters are the key to a setting. Everybody, including Kindred, has:
      - Something she wants
      - Something he can do to get what he wants
      - Something it loves doing, watching other people do, or just enjoying
      - Something they hate doing, watching other people do, or just... hate
      - Someone or something that is an obstacle to their goals.

      Consider Vasily (Wasyl, Vasilios) a somewhat 500-years old Harbinger of Skulls (Hekata, in V5) in my current Chronicle.

      - He wants to protect his coven and keep on researching death.
      - He has a library, allies and plenty of time.
      - He likes discovering new books on his favourite subjects.
      - He hates racism against Slavic people, as a former Polish monk berated in Russia...
      - One of his most long-lived "friend-enemies" is, now, an assistant to his bishop (A subordinate of an Archbishop, which is the title of Sabbat "Princes")

      - He sees most things within his living days mental frame; if he goes to the cinema, for example (and that can happen, as Cainites can -and get- bored), he considers it a particularly intriguing puppet show. In fact, he is a bit of a movie fan and particularly likes Sean Connery's acting, which he met through the movie "The name of the rose". He mused about having visited that abbey during his long undeath, and was very vexed when two neonates revealed to him that it was a ficional place...

      So, can you see the pattern? The way I began developing the character...?

      Hope that helps; it is 6:50 AM here in Spain and I'm feeling a bit woozy still...

          The Prince is simply the most able and influential local vampire who is able to dominate others and eliminate rivals. It could be through threats and intimidation, or great social skills and alliance building. It is probably a mixture of both.

          Also remember that a Prince is not necessarily all powerful. Some are. Some are weak and dependent on support of the Primogen. Some are inbetween. And appearances can be deceiving. For example, to use Chicago By Night's Prince Lodin, he is seen as being hard ass and very powerful. But in fact, he was rather a "weak" Prince because the Chicago Primogen were incredibly old and powerful. They heavily controlled him (which he resented and was always struggling against).

          The Inner Circle doesn't get involved in that. In fact, Justicars can only remove a Prince if they call a Conclave and can get it to support the motion. And that's just theoretical - it gives the Justicar a pretext to do so. If the Justicar and his Archons and other allies don't have the actual power to do so, it won't happen.

          Princes reign for as long as they can hold power. That may be 1 night or it could be for centuries.

          When it comes to developing a chronicle history. I like to heavily use the city's real history as inspiration. What events in real history had an impact in vampire history? What real world events could be used as cover for vampire intrigue and conflict? How does the city history's inform what kind of NPCs exist in the city. The city's history should "live" through the vampire NPCs that were witnesses to it. A good chronicle history is probably at least 50/50 inspires by real history and the other by ST's ideas for fictional vampires.

                      Originally posted by ShoesandHats View Post
                      I come from a background of running D&D with a homebrew setting where real-world history isn't nearly as much of a concern, and while I find the inclusion of real-world history and issues to be incredibly interesting and engaging, it certainly makes it harder to create a setting and story while remaining sensitive and respectful.
                      From the sound of things you are kind of reinventing the wheel, partially out of habit due to experience with D&D settings. If you want to make this about the city, focus on the city, if you want to focus on the history of characters in that city, focus on the characters. Trying to do both at once can result in an unfocused story because of too many things are trying to be center stage.

                      When using real world history you just pick a handful of incidents or note worthy events and simply say to your self that those are now vampire related, then you work out how that would function given the time period, the clans involved, and related events. From there it is just a straight forward game, saying that there is a vampire crime boss in Seattle is really no different than saying there is a vampire crime boss in Dagger Dale or Hammerfell.

                      Real world history is like a sketch you make on the canvas before painting, it serves to keep your vision of things in focus, rather than going completely free form. Of course if you have specific requirements due to your gaming group as far as tone, genre, and style of game, that further complicates matters.

                      As far as what to do with Seattle itself.... it wasn't always a city. There were native tribes in the region before it was a city, which means plenty of spirits or were-critter storylines can be used for the basis of your story even if the main characters in the story are vampires. Alternatively you can make the storyline less about the city's past and more about what it is now, namely a major transit point on the west coast, illegal smuggling & trafficking operations, travelers from the far east, or just weirding stuff in shipping containers (to quote a certain vampire video game "don't open it").

                      In other words don't try to convey everything about the city in one story, let the players discover each facet slowly. In other words think more along the lines of novellas or short stories, rather than War & Peace when letting the PCs run around the city.

                          I did something similar in setting up my chronicle, only using Detroit for my setting.

                          When looking into the history, I tried to focus on smaller events, or unsolved crimes and bizarre coincidences to round out the supernatural history. I'm one who feels like humanity will still be the driving factor for 90% of a city's historic events, with the Kindred usually trying to benefit or catch up with the advances of humanity. This gave me a workable framework of a few things in the distant past (over 100 years), with an increasing amount as we progress toward modern times.

                          As far as having it "fit" in with canon, I tried to work in things from the more recent updates to the lore, specifically making sire the lore sheets in the books make sense. The Week of Nightmares happened (roughly 20 years ago), and that shook up the power dynamics in the city. The Second Inquisition got big in the 2008-2012 period, which also shook up the power dynamics in some of the larger cities. Some Kindred were able to adapt to the new reality, others have struggled, but that's a time when the past can be more dynamic.

                          The Sabbat leaving the United States en mass and invading the cradle of humanity will also play into the local history. In my case, that left Detroit open for a Camarilla influx, but how open? That was one of the questions I had to resolve through building out the setting.

                          Finally, looking at how conflicts develop over centuries, it's a good idea to remember that most of your NPCs are not going to be all that old. Take a look at the population of the world, and how dramatically it has increased in the last 50-100 years. Your vampire population should probably mirror this, especially if you are worried about verisimilitude. For me, that meant 1/8 of my NPCs are less than 10 years old, 5/8 are less than 60, only 1/4 are more than 100 years old, with only half of those being over 200. 1/16 of your NPCs might be elders - over 300 years old - but that could be a stretch. In a city of 50 vampires, you're only looking at 3 who would even possibly have a grudge going back centuries.

                          When building mine, I put together some factions. I determined the Camarilla started to make a play for the city in the early 2000s, using money and pawns to lay the groundwork to destabilize the local Sabbat. The Second Inquisition was leaked information about the power players that the Cam had been able to discover around 2008-2010, and the mortals did a good bit of the dirty work. That was also the time that the Gehenna Crusade got going, leaving the city in a power vacuum. In general terms, that meant that I got to build the majority of the history only in the last 10 years.

                          I have anarchs, and decided that they had been in the city for a long time, even with the Sabbat being the dominant faction. They were variously used as recruitment opportunities or target practice depending on the circumstances, so the ones left are tough and entrenched. They also tend to live on the perimeter of the city, blending in with the smaller communities that would afford them a little more distance and protection from their enemies. There is one group - a mix of Brujah and Malkavians - who have held a territory downtown the entire time, having come from the rum-runners of the 1930s and being very, very used to violence (that was a bit of the city history I wanted to use).

                          For the Camarilla, that was where I wanted to use a bit of the "ancient grudge" bit to make the city more colorful. My eldest is a former Detroit resident who was kicked out of the city in 1798 as a mortal and embraced elsewhere. She wants Detroit due to her personal history. However, she has a history of antagonism with a Tremere who ended up being named an Archon. He maneuvered to have the Cam support a Banu Haqim defector from the Sabbat - an effort to keep the power "local", prove their support for the new clan within the sect, and deny someone he doesn't like a thing that she dearly wanted.

                          That leaves me with the eldest NPC acting as the loyal opposition to the prince, who is an Ancilla backed by out-of-sight powers of the distant ivory tower. She's going to be maneuvering to get the prince killed or removed, but can't be overly obvious due to his backing from afar. At the same time, there are some tough Anarchs nestled in the local casino operations, and cozy with the mob, who don't particularly feel like giving up what they managed to hold against the Sabbat just because the "nice" vampire faction showed up.

                          My third "elder" is a Lasombra who decided not to go on Crusade and is instead leading his brood to abandon the remaining Sabbat and make a play to join the Camarilla (as in Chicago by Night). Since the local Cam is weak, he feels that they should not get to demand as much from the Lasombra and is working to make deals and secure their place before any "outside" forces (like the archon) can say otherwise.

                          With this as a framework, I filled in specific characters who had ties to my city history. No vampire caused the '68 riots, but one of the Lasombra was a national guardsman who was snapped up as a ghoul and embraced shortly after everything died down.

                          One last bit - it's likely your players will not know the city history nearly as well as you. Make it work for you, and sprinkle in stuff that will add to the story you are telling. Also, make dynamic NPCs who have strong motivations - you can always add things to their past as you learn about the history of the city. You can also change anything you want to make it more "World of Darkness".

                              Unless your players are from Seattle, you don't really need to be super accurate or know everything; Big events might be important, but you can make up little events. The World of Darkness is more urbanized, more poluted, and more populated; More people and more events should exist. Take the Seattle Grunge scene; make up a few new music groups that were big in your version of Seattle, and give them interesting defining events that capture the theme of your city/chronicle (The world famous lead singer of Muddy Mountain had his baby go missing when he was preoccupied with heroin, Little did he know an obsessed fan and Toreador had snatched the child to cultivate what she believed to be a genetic talent)

                              If you lecture too much on real history you may boor your players or look foolish. Alt history forever.

                              Helpful things:
                              -Firearm laws. (a rough outline, not entire documents)
                              -Key places: Street names of the Rack(s)/where elysium is/important stuff.

                              I just think of the characters and their goals/ambitions/things they want to protect/Covet. Leave some areas blank so you can make stuff up later. To me, City conflicts are kinda natural when you've got a few movers and shakers trying to do things and a bunch of other vampires trying to get the best out of it. Of course, since you've chosen V5, you've got a whole host of whacky things to adress: That elder's gone and his territory is up for grabs. The SI is here and someone's using them to get at their enemies. Half the city have been declared anarchs. Some other character messy-critted his attempt to cover up another's messy crit and now it's the player's turn to step in and add to the cycle.

                                  Great advice on general city and NPC building in this thread especially on goals and ambitions.

                                  If you're doing this completely contemporary and not retro '90s, there's not much from the grunge era left here. There's a few buildings and clubs from then, but a lot of them don't resemble their '90s counterparts at all except the huge office buildings, which more are being built everyday. The last decade has seen massive upheaval with Amazon importing a huge amount of high paid workers. This has created a massive disparity between the rich and the poor in the city with huge numbers losing their homes. If you're not making 6 figures a year, you're struggling, and even that's not enough to buy a house in Seattle. A huge amount of the homeless in the state have fled to the city for it's services, further exacerbating the homeless problem. Opiates, meth and crack have seen a resurgence (crack never left some areas), with the widening wealth gap. So a lot of Seattle is either sleek concrete and glass or tent cities and shelters often right on top of each other. Individual neighborhoods still retain some of their personality, but they're being homogenized like the rest of the city and rents are skyrocketing. Some of it's industrial buildings remain in the South, but with land values skyrocketing, they're being replaced with offices, condos and apartments. Off the top of my head The Showbox is about the only grunge era venue still around (completely remodeled inside in the early 2000s). Belltown (in the Northern end of downtown) hosts most of the trendy clubs (Like pop dance stuff, not grunge). It's very much the cyberpunk dystopia that the '80s promised us. I had my living in the future moment around 10 years ago when I saw a bunch of homeless people clustered around a laptop in Pioneer square and felt like I was living Max Headroom.

                                  I'm surprised nobody has mentioned it, but the upcoming Bloodlines 2 is set in Seattle. There's not a ton of info, but they have released some blurbs on a few characters and factions like the Pioneers that seem interesting. You could crib on that if you want.

                                  All that said, it's your city and game, so if you want it to still be flannel, rock and heroine go for it.

                                      With all that in mind, how have you gone about writing around history? And would it be appropriate for me to post specifics here for others to critique? Thanks in advance!
                                      Creating a new city can be overwhelming, especially for a first time storyteller. It's okay. Take a deep breath and realize that you don't need to do this alone. This is a team effort.

                                      Forget for now about the city as a whole. Just focus on what you need to tell the stories of the pcs your players have brought you. Get the players around a table (or, more likely right now, around a video conference).

                                      Unlike D&D, each pc will bring with them a whole crowd of spcs who are part of their backstory. Begin by making a list of these spcs.
                                      1. Include people from the pcs' human backstory. Parents, spouses, children, friends, rivals, co-workers, etc.
                                      2. Include people from the pcs' vampiric existence. This will be mainly their sire, but possibly broodmates, and the sire's sire.
                                      3. Include all Touchstones, obviously. Also, anyone closely tied to the Touchstones, such as the TS's spouse or parent.
                                      4. Include people implied by pcs' backgrounds, such as retainers, herd, and contacts. Don't forget people tied to pcs' influence, resources, etc.
                                      5. Include people tied to the pcs' Ambition, either helping them achieve it or vested in preventing their success.
                                      6. Include people implied by pcs' skill specialties and merits/flaws. A ballet dancer likely knows dancers. Addicts likely have a dealer.
                                      7. Include people involved with pcs' predator type. ex: pet store clerks for a farmer, hospital workers for a bagger

                                      If you figure 5 to 10 spcs associated with each player, an average game with five or so players will have between 25 and 50 (or more!) spcs inherent in the character concepts of your players. This, in itself, can be overwhelming. Don't worry, because again this is a team effort.

                                      Once you have lists of each pcs' spcs, ask your players to help reduce the list by combining characters with a similar role in the story. If two pcs had a cruel boss, this is when you combine those bosses into one person. If one pc has a Touchstone who is a teenage girl, and another has a granddaughter in her teens, combine the young women into one person. Do as much of this as you possibly can; offer your players bonus xp to do so, if you must. This reduces the number of elements in the story. This is important because it makes for more focused stories, and --happily-- reduces your workload.

                                      Okay, now you have a list of around 20 or so spcs, and the pcs. Picture all these people locked in a room for a week. Who would become friends? Allies? Enemies? Who has something one of the others want? Who is admired? Who is reviled? Make note of any of the more unusual combinations... these will become story seeds later.

                                      Think about the cast you have and what themes and motifs arise. Are they fiery rebels or quiet functionaries? Stylish aristocrats, or tough-as-nails street people? Dutiful soldiers, or madcap partiers? Smarmy double-crossers, or earnest do-gooders? Talk about these themes and motifs with your players. Check your instincts against their hopes for the game. If everyone's vision doesn't line up, negotiate until they do.

                                      Once you have the game's themes and motifs, then... and only then... go to the wikipedia page for the city you are running the game in and pick one location, historical figure, or object that in some ways exemplify each theme or motif. This should be a list of fewer than a half-dozen items. Come up with how these elements matter to vampiric society, such as by being an elysium, a place the pcs hang out, a prince, a treasured ritual object, or whatever.

                                      Speaking of vampire society. Draw up a relationship chart including all vampires you have so far. Don't spend more than an hour on this. The less detail it has, the more room to grow it has. Only add to it if there is an obvious, obvious gaping hole, and the new vampire you add on your own is firmly within the themes and motifs of the chronicle... or contrast against them in a fun way.

                                      Now, there are three more spcs you need to create. These are going to be your primary channel for interacting with the pcs, so they may require some time and creativity. Don't spend weeks on them, though. Just make them as you would any pc, set them aside for a full 24 hours, and then make them again for a final draft.
                                      1. You need an spc who can communicate your concerns, as ST, and drop hints to the players, through their pcs. (examples: a Malkavian seer who always technically tells the truth, a Ventrue ancilla who has been assigned to oversee and criticize the fledgling coterie, a retainer who is loyal but rolls their eyes at the pcs' ridiculous antics)
                                      2. You need an spc who is neither clearly an ally nor an enemy. They should have goals which reinforce the themes of the story, but don't automatically put them in conflict with the pcs. They might even be allies to half the coterie, and rivals to the other half.
                                      3. You need a Big Bad. They should exemplify all the identified themes of the chronicle, but in a way which collides with the pcs' plans and goals. Make sure the Big Bad can justify their actions, if only to themselves, as being the best course of action available to them. The best antagonists genuinely believe they are in the right.


                                      There you have it. Before session one, just reread the city's wikipedia page and make notes of any fun local color or cultural details. (examples: Philadelphia is known for psychotic sports fans, Salt Lake City has bizarrely wide streets, Memphis has a pyramid, and San Francisco is made entirely of hills... and by hills I mean slightly inclined cliffs) Pick one or two (at most) of these stereotypes to incorporate into the first scene.

                                      Introduce your pcs to one another, and to the room they are starting in.

                                      Best part of being a VtM storyteller is that, unlike many other ttrpgs, you can then just kind of sit back and let the players take the story where they want it to go. Seriously, I once got a few months of play out of the pcs not being able to agree on where they should hang out.

                                      No wandering monster tables or floorplans of the thieves' guild headquarters required.

                                          I also poured hours upon days upon weeks Upon years researching. Keep in mind sometimes word of mouth legends aren’t on the internet.

                                          I commonly turn subjects of mysterious or grisly deaths into a supernatural one. And I spin a WoD blend into real world events. Riots especially (Presence).

                                          For most of your questions they are answered in the 3rd ed storytellers guide (that’s what I used) vaguely. It’s more guidelines then rules.

                                          For example it gives weak prince with strong Primogen. Or strong prince with weak Primogen. Etc etc.

                                          I also used the city building pdf from mr Gone to quickly fill in the blanks and just do a quick look at what else I need to build the world.

                                          Sometimes being prince is a job. Sometimes it’s a reward. Sometimes it’s surprisingly a punishment.

                                          The inner council and the justicars will assuredly have their fingers in the princes pie. Depending on its strategic political value against the kine or other supernatural entities (if against garou if there are caerns nearby, as an example).

                                          There is no wrong answer how it is set up. Keep in mind players in your setting will shake rattle and roll your dynamic. So don’t get flustered. Expect it to go whichever way.

                                          If your setting isn’t normal or expected, set up a page worth of common vampire history to set the exposition before the game starts. I didn’t and I would have to stop and put things back on track. You’ll save time setting up exposition.

                                          For other supernaturals decide whether you first want to use their own core supplement, use the back of the vtm book rendition, or wing it. You can add more depth to them but this’ll cost you more time.

                                          Each of my various NPCs represent a different mentality and thus have their own values different then the next. Each is tied to one or more side plots.

                                          Reward your players for discovering NPCs backstories. You’ll feel happy or at least get impressions if you did a good job making interesting characters. Then revise as necessary.

                                          You’ll still need to be flexible regardless of how much you plan ahead. Good luck. Pace yourself. Keep asking for pointers to affirm your ideas.

                                              Vampires are old and the past hundred years have changed really fast. Remember that.                                               Multiply the number of homeless people by a factor of 8.
