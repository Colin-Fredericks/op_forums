              Originally posted by Crytash2 View Post
              However, i think every character should be able to "survive" though.
              I don't think this is in the slightest necessary.

              For lore's sake, physical violence (to this degree) is an exception, not a rule. Most of the time, most vampires don't face a lot of risk, otherwise their society would be rather different. Just as in real conflicts, surviving has more to do with not being in the wrong place at the wrong time than being built for survival (more than we already are). Explaining out it shouldn't be harder than "I laid down in a safe spot as the coward I am".

              The blurb also has nothing to do with combat capacity. The sheer amount of dots a starting character is afforded is plenty explanation for having survived, and the actual most probable explanation is, again, dumb luck: you either didn't got to face common accidents of a vampire's life in your most vulnerable period, or you got to survive them for reasons, there are too many possible reasons to say any particular one is more probable.

              Anyway, to the point of the thread, thinking about it now I personally play scholar types more often than anything else. Even when I try to play a brute or social butterfly, I eventually lean toward a more scholarly take to it. That's true even in other RPGs.

                  monteparnas is correct. There are LOADS of ways a character straight out of char-gen could survive. Up to and including "I avoided situations where I was in danger, through caution or happenstance".

                  If you're the kind of Kindred from V1, who subsists primarily from "cow-tipping" (going to the local ranch and feeding on lifestock), your biggest danger is the rancher spotting you or the cattle getting spooked and trampling you. You have absented yourself from the more dangerous aspects of Kindred politics, and even from behavior that might readily attract (or create) vampire hunters.

                  Bringing it back to char-gen, let's take our typical Social Butterfly Toreador starting PC. Since you MUST distribute a minimum of 3 starting dots in Disciplines, you have minimum one (1) that could help you survive. Celerity 1 makes it easier to run away from danger. Presence 1 makes it easier to amass a following of mortals willing to protect you. And Auspex 1 makes it easier to sense when a situation was going pear-shaped, so you can duck out early. Higher dots in any of those Disciplines just improves your chances and opens up new avenues of survival. You don't need to be a badass, if Presence 2 makes people magically afraid of you enough to back off.

                  Attributes, Abilities, and Backgrounds have a more subtle, but still powerful, efficacy at helping a young lick survive their first nights. All without needing to be able to actually fight. (Indeed, one could argue that the best way to survive fights is to avoid them entirely). Stealth, Alertness, Streetwise, Subterfuge, Intimidation, Influence, Contacts, Allies, Mentor, even Resources (just bribe your enemies to leave you alone). If a Kindred can't figure out a way to survive with any of those or more, they may not be cut out for this whole "survival" thing.

                  What we see when a VtM Chronicle starts and a vampire PC enters the story is not them having already proved they can survive. It's the first time their ability to do so has really been tested. You might be playing a badass who survived by beating the crap out of everyone offscreen. Or you might be playing a meek scholar who was really careful and really lucky.

                  (Not even getting into early VtM, where it was more common for official adventures to START the players running newly Embraced PCs.)

                  Magestuck: The Ascension

                      I guess you have a point- When I ST for freshly Embraced characters, I usually run them the way you describe survival can be as simple as caution, dumb luck or leaning on a single dot in a Discipline.

                      But when I play ancillae games (or older neonates), I do think survivability starts to matter more. To me it’s akin to what Alan Rickman once said about acting: “the audience is not interested in seeing you act. They are interested in seeing you listen.” In the same way, I am less interested in characters facade right now and am more interessted in how they survived long enough to matter. That survival story, explicit or implicit, says something real about who they are and how they have interacted with their surroundings.

                      Most of the time, I’ve STed VtM more like a mob game. You don’t get to be an associate in a crime family without a precursor. Some combination of savvy, toughness (and the right allies) is what keeps you alive. If a vampire has reached this stage in Kindred society (even if just a neonate with ambition), I find it strengthens the narrative when the player has at least thought through what allowed them to survive until now.

                      In the end it is more about who is your ST and what kind of tone these ST choses imho. If I know how somebody reacts to violence, how they hunt or how they solve problems It can be used as foreshadowing. It is also very easy for the Player to make goals for such a character, because they already think about their day to day routine.

                      Honestly, the reason I use low survival rates might be that one of my first and favorite clans is the Lasombra Clan. In its Clanbook it says:

                      “Most new Lasombra don’t survive. By making it this far you’ve already beaten the odds, to some degree, but never for a moment think that your existence is now secure.

                      More than half of the kine we choose to Embrace fail to survive their testing period. They despair and commit suicide, or break under the strain, or survive but show that they’re crippled by unworthy personality traits. At least one in a hundred, and sometimes more than that, fail to survive the Embrace itself, either simply dying or emerging so totally mindless and compulsive that they must be destroyed immediately.

                      So of a thousand mortals who seemed worthy after initial investigation, we now have about 450 to 500. Look around you, and notice that where there were ten of you on that first night, now there are only eight. We lose ten to twenty percent of new recruits in the training interval before formal acceptance into the Sabbat. In times of crisis, the percentage can double or even triple, and it’s never much less than ten percent. Too many new vampires retain more humanity than is good for them. That gets us down to perhaps 350 to 450 of the original thousand.

                      In the next five years, experience says, at least half of you will have gone to your respective unmarked graves. You will perish in battle, or offend your superiors and warrant judgment by the Courts of Blood or the bishopric. Some of you will fall in Monomacy. Some of you will destroy yourselves in accidents. One of you may attempt to join the antitribu, and may even survive to do so. If more than seven or less than three of you stand together five years from now, you will be a most unusual pack indeed. Of our initial thousand, as few as 200, or as many as 300, survive.

                      Those of you who make it to five years will continue to face challenges. Of the five or so of you likely to last that long, at least one and perhaps as many as three will perish in the next five years. The pace of this brutal winnowing slows further and further for those of you who endure. Another halving takes place between 10 and 25 years, another between 25 and 50, another between 50 and 100. Your instructors and I face the same challenges. Perhaps one of you will win the right from a Court of Blood to destroy me and enrich your standing in the ladder of generations. Perhaps then in turn one of your current peers will win the right to destroy you. “ (page 45)

                      But this is the Sabbat and that is just the way I tend to approach it in my chronicles. It gives me and my players something concrete to build on and I like how it adds weight to a character backstory. There is no single “right” way to do this and maybe my take leans a little harsher than most. Either way, i hope you can understand why i do what i do. I thank you for your brining this up!

                          I don't have anything against this approach for this kind of game. My point is more the fact that not only do approaches can be more varied, but also that the answers to challenges can be so, too.

                          My personal approach takes into account that most people, including vampires, want first and foremost to survive, and the best way to survive is to avoid danger. Certainly you can't avoid it forever if you want to accomplish things, but it does mean danger isn't the default state of things. Most of all, I like this approach precisely because it let me have a greater variety of characters, even those who wouldn't survive in a more literal cut-throat environment. I also have a pet peeve with the concept of Social Darwinism, so while I like the Lasombra in some aspects, this is not one of them.

                          But that said, the only other thing I want to add is that surviving to Ancilla, even in a hard environment, doesn't necessarily means being ready for combat. You can make yourself useful to others, you can have lackeys that protect you at least long enough for you to flee, you can have allies that make hurting you too costly to be worth the trouble, there are many potential ways to justify you having avoided trouble long enough to flee problems.

                          Certainly an Ancillae game requires a lot more of work to explain how you got to live so long, and for a PC it probably wasn't by being a lowly coward no one cares about (although for an NPC this may be the case). But I don't think you really need more than default vampiric resilience if you have other means to deal with trouble.
