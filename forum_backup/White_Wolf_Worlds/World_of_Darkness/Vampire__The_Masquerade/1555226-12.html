                Cancelling would've been better compared to where we are now. Or, for example, following the advice of that one Chinese Room developer and rename the game, so that you can have better (more accurate) marketing for the game. It's easy to be judgmental now, of course. Hindsight is 20/20, as they say. For me, the thing Paradox management still hasn't sufficiently explained is why they have put this much money towards these projects "outside of their core areas" as they call it. Same with Life By You, the Sims-like life-simulator that they eventually canceled. They were throwing tens of millions of dollars at these projects. I get the part that these failed due to a lack of expertise and understanding of managing games in these genres. But at the same time, where was that confidence coming from to spend this much money on these projects? In both cases, alarm-bells should've been going off way earlier, considering how much money they were spending there. With the type of money they were spending there, these weren't merely "forays into news genres" for Paradox, these turned into high-risk-gambits. And why would you make high-risk-gambits with games in genres that you have little experience in? And it's this type of bad judgment that's looming over Paradox and eroding investor-trust.

                __________________________________________________ ________________

                Here's a link to a transcript of the earnings-call from yesterday (via SeekingAlpha). I will highlight the most relevant part for this thread as Fredrik Wester (CEO) talks about exploring IPs beyond videogames:

                Alexander Bricca
                Chief Financial Officer

                Yes. Fred, this is for you. Many companies are expanding their IPs beyond games into other products and media. How does Paradox think about brand extension and additional revenue streams outside of traditional game development?

                Fredrik Wester
                Founder, Director & CEO

                Yes. I mean we have been experimenting a bit with this. We do merchandising. We do board games. We do licenses to other video games companies for some of our IP, most notably White Wolf IP. But it's a great way to strengthen IP recognition. It hasn't had any significant impact on revenue or profit, but it's still a good way to keep the IP alive and to reach new target audiences. So this is something we're going to continue. I mean there are no -- just -- I think what they're after here is like a TV dealer equivalent. There's nothing like that on the table at the moment.

                But who knows in the future, but I think we need to work more on deepening and strengthening our IP as they are. We work a lot with history in our Grand Strategy games. We work with the fantasy IP in Age of Wonders. We work with Stellaris, for example, that are very strong or Stellaris and Age of Wonders are their own IP where we own everything. When you use history as your background, it's harder to sort of work with the IP, if you like. But also the White Wolf catalog offers a wide range of really interesting ways to expand into new universes of opportunities.
                I think it really just reinforces what has already been said. White Wolf and the book-side of things is not that high on the priority-list. If footnotes could have footnotes, that's probably where Paradox would put all the book-stuff with the WOD-IP in a year-end-report.

                But regarding bad judgment, there's also been some new developments with Bloodlines 2. Namely, they've announced a revision of their release-schedule for the DLC (via PCGamer):

                First up is the Valentine's Update, set to go live on February 11 with "new cosmetics, facial hair, enemy loot, [and] bug fixes." After that is the Loose Cannon expansion, initially expected to be out in Q2 2026 (so, sometime between April 1 and June 30), which is now slated for the maybe-possibly earlier Q1/Q2. Then comes The Flower and the Flame expansion, originally set for Q3 (July 1-September 30) and now targeting the similarly wider window of Q2/Q3.
                Hopefully, this won't impact development too much. But when these DLCs are released and they turn out to be buggy, short, uninspired or anything like that, this will come up as an explanation as to why it might be like that, I'm sure. I get it, Paradox wants this over and done with as soon as possible but do we really need another wave of bad press here if the DLC comes out and it feels like development was rushed?

                                        Originally posted by DarkKing View Post
                                        The only games which seem to do well are CYOA novels. It's easy to see why people say this IP is cursed. At this point, Paradox should probably just cut their losses and sell it to somebody else.
                                        I mean, you skipped the four of the most commercially successful games in the IP's history (Redemption, and the Hunter trilogy), so it's going to look at lot worse. You also counted a game that got cancelled very early in development (Heart of Gaia) but skipped the far more development-intensive-but-still-cancelled MMO.

                                        I think it's also worth putting this into a wider perspective. Adapting existing IPs to video games has always been a pretty iffy thing with way more duds than successes. The most common issue is that adapting is a lot of work, and trying to create a new experience for customers to play is a lot of work... doing both is a nearly impossible amount of work. So most adaptations are just slapping an IP's coat of paint on an existing game and hoping the name recognition is good enough people will play something they've basically already played before but with IP assets on top.

                                        For a long time, that worked fine. But the video game industry has changed a lot and such efforts are increasingly less appealing to audiences. Games like Marvel Rivals have to be excellent games that would do well without the IP; where being Marvel only pushes them over the line into top-tier successful game. Nobody was expecting that big of a game out of Bloodlines 2 (then again, most people weren't expecting such a big game out of Marvel Rivals either), but the last 10 years of video games have solidly shown that IPs from other media don't sell copies if the game isn't good enough to be good without the IP.

                                        Originally posted by Saga View Post
                                        Bloodhunt was (IS) a good game, simple and free.
                                        "Will have been" is an unfortunately clunky tense form in English. Bloodhunt is in the abysmal situation of it's servers being shut down in three months but is still open anyway.

                                        That said, to build on what I was saying above, Bloodhunt didn't really do anything to help stand out in a very crowded sub-market of video games and one that is far more zero-sum than the rest. F2P live service BRs are a dime a dozen and new ones are constantly coming and going. It's not that all the new ones are bad, some are even just as good as the mainstays like Fortnight, but nobody that's playing Fortnight is dropping it to play something just as good. If you can't carve out a niche, or at least do better that someone that's in a specific niche to get people to switch over, it's a style of video game that's unsustainable without a large number of regular players.
