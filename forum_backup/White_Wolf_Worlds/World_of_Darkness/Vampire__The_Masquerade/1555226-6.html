                Originally posted by Knightingale View Post
                I still think this whole notion of "Paradox are only good at a particular niche of videogames" is ridiculous. They have the money. I get it that they started out with a particular niche but they were successful. So they have the money. And so why not hire people who can do the job?!
                So. While I think you're mostly correct, this is an important misconception.

                Making a game, or any other kind of business, isn't just a matter of having money and hiring people who know how to do it.

                It isn't obvious, but companies do a lot of work to get any project done. There are many parts to manage, goods and services to source, a lot of planning and projections to make, there's a lot of resources to allocate, there's marketing, other companies to deal with, like retailers or platforms, there's a lot of things, really.

                And those things are sensitive to what you're doing. Making an RPG or making a Strategy Game, that changes key details on all those moving parts, even if they remain mostly similar, details that can make or break a project. Even your accountant can have a hard time dealing with unfamiliar waters. So "hiring the people who know" means pretty much re-hiring your whole company, not just the devs, but up to your CEO.

                None of this excuses Paradox, as they should know this. A lot of their mistakes come precisely from working every project as if it was the projects they know how to handle. In a sense, they're not off the mark when the CEO says they couldn't gauge sales, this is literally one of many things they have to do and didn't really knew how to, even if this remark was actually just a veiled way to say it was the RPG market that doesn't make money, which is blatantly false. Paradox should have gauged first their know-how and tackled smaller projects to get the handle of things, they should have gotten consultants about each new niche and heard the advice from people who work on them, they should have accepted, in short, that there are things about games they don't know.

                They have a lot of problems, it goes far deeper than "just" not knowing how to make other games. But companies being good at some things and not others is definitely a thing, and in fact well known in the investment world.​

                            So, to keep people abreast of what's going on with Paradox. So, after the announcement from Paradox about Bloodlines 2, plenty of videogame-outlets have written about it. Lots of talks of failure and Bloodlines 2 being called a "flop". Well, but if you were to look at how the Paradox stock is doing and what people in the financial world are saying, it's quite the opposite. They're practically celebrating. The first weird sign was last Thursday when the news broke, one board member massively increased his shares in Paradox (it's not like I'm saying he potentially got tipped off about what was about to happen... insider-trading is a crime after all...). And now, Danske Bank's recommendation shifted from hold to buy when it comes to the Paradox stock. So why do they think that?

                            Danske Bank has upgraded its recommendation for gaming company Paradox Interactive to "buy" (from "hold") and raised its target price to 210 kronor (previously 180), according to a recent analysis.

                            The upgrade comes on the heels of a successful launch for the highly anticipated Europa Universalis 5, with the game trending in line with the bank's quarterly estimates.

                            "Even more important is that the success of this title has likely paved the way for many more years of continued growth for the franchise and its games-as-a-service model," Danske Bank writes.

                            The bank further notes that downloadable content (DLC) has been released for nearly all of Paradox's core games, and that these expansions appear to be performing well.

                            At the same time, the expected write-down of Bloodlines 2 means that the company has finally moved past its "junk period."

                            This allows Paradox to look to the future, which, according to the bank, "consists almost entirely of the genres where Paradox is the market leader: grand strategy and simulation games."

                            In summary, Danske Bank concludes that the company, taking into account all recent successes, "is now in the best position it has been in for a long time."
                            That doesn't mean Paradox can't chart their own course despite what these bank analysts are saying. But I think it will have an impact on Paradox that the money people call all their "experiments" with exploring different genres (other than grand strategy) over the last couple of years a "junk period". And Paradox acquiring the WOD-IP, of course, and trying to publish games with it, would be part of this so-called "junk period". So, the hope from the bank analysts appears to be that Paradox actually let Bloodlines 2 be the last WOD-game they're gonna publish.

                            And of course, they're not entirely wrong. That whole scheme Paradox got going with their grand strategy games and the nigh-endless number of constant DLC-releases certainly rakes in a lot of money. I would have called it the greediest DLC-scheme in videogaming but Bethesda's new thing with "paid mods" is even worse, I think. But still, you can't get around the fact that Paradox has a "games as a service"-model that works.

                            On the other hand, though, this feels like short-term-thinking à la "It works now, so let's just keep doing it and don't try anything else.". They're essentially pigeonholing themselves to be committed to just one thing if they go with the bank-analysts' advice. And I hear you, monteparnas that it's about more than who you hire but Paradox has been at this for years. Clearly, though, whatever they're doing over and whoever's in charge over there - it's not working. So, either hire more people or fire/replace people who are there. And it's a challenge to get into multiple genres but the reward is that Paradox isn't just "the company that does grand strategy games" anymore. So that was an ambitious project but in the end, they simply haven't pulled it off.

                            But they were right to be ambitious. Not just in the sense thatt hey'd be moving up in the world as a videogame company. But they'd be in a safer position, too. You never know when somebody might come along and simply do the same thing as Paradox with their grand-strategy-games - but better.

                            But Paradox aren't entirely out of the woods yet. That 37 million dollar write-down is only part of the story and we're gonna get the whole story at the end of January when Paradox is obliged to release its Q4 2025 earnings report.

                                                For a start, BL 1 and 2 are two different beasts, from two different birthplaces, appearing in two different gaming cultures.

                                                When BL 1 came into being, it was an adaptation of a niche IP (as in it was unknown to the video game audience, it was only preceded by redemption, a game with little clout back then). It also appeared in the 2000, when games were mostly bought physically, online spread of Fandom was a distant dream.

                                                Looking at BL2, the story is entirely different. BL 1 had massed over more than a decade a cult following, in fact I think a lot of people discovered the WOD thanks to it (I’m amongs them).

                                                And let’s be honest, it’s mostly for that factor that paradox bought the wod IP. They were not going to gain money from new editions of the TTRPG. It was always about video games, and BL2 was always resented as the beach head of that attempt.

                                                Add to that the massive change in video game culture. For a start, it has become nearly entirely online, hype and reactions are constantly shared. And the hype for BL2 started as soon s it’s original announcement. So it’s release cannot be compared to a BL1 that release without much fanfare. BL2 was awaited.

                                                So the crash down is doubly damning, especially since, while Troika tried to make a game for some relatively obscure IP, paradox was trying to make the sequel to a classic for an audience that was waiting for a long time.

                                                And not only did they botched it, but they have no excuses. Troika had terrible executive issues, technical problems with the game engine, and ambitions too great for the ressources they had.

                                                Paradox had literally one job. Remake a BL with modernized graphics. And they failed at that.

                                                That and the fact that Cofd was essentially killed to avoid competition with V5.

                                                So we end up with a dead Cofd line, a V5 constantly alternating between the tolerable and insultingly mediocre, a lost of failed or at best passable video games, and a spearhead games that is neither a good sequel to a classic of the 2000s, nor even a good game on is own.

                                                So yeah, the IP is essentially dead. At least during the interegnum between CCP and paradox, OPP brought the flame back to life. Add to that the catastrophe that was W5 and h5, and the future of the ttrpg seems bleak indeed.
