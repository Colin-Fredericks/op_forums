    How fast do you think Vampires should grow?

    09-12-2022, 01:53 PM
    While xp costs might inform our decisions (First edition Vampire offering markedly lower costs for disciplines than latter editions) the rate which we get XP varies considerably and... is largely a convenience for game purposes; It's very likely that the storyteller will have a coterie where not everyone will put the same effort in each night: Character A spends much of his downtime reading on a vast array of subjects and occasionally experiments, Character B Trains her ass off physically with expert instructors. Character C administers his territories and holdings, and character D... bums around. Let's assume D is the worst-case scenario and spends their time uncritically watching the most mind-numbing entertainments and drinks blood out of bags, not even hunting or rekindling human connections.

    Mechanically, they're all equal in most games, and that's probably best for the players.... Well, not entirely. Vampire A could make more gains if he was learning rituals, C has a definite advantage since Backgrounds don't cost XP and downtime is a great time to get them. B, unfortunately, is wasting effort, since they're not getting anything more than D and most given downtime XP rates are really too low for high-intensity training. And that feels a little weird to me. Vampire bodies don't fatigue, they usually have resources and thus access to elite trainers. After hunting, they could easily spend 4+ hours a night getting really good at a lot of things.

    Perhaps the Beast will push Vampire D to do more. Such idle behaviour aggravates the Beast or something.



    And then we get into how vampires gain abilities. They're not Humans. Their minds can learn but I'm not sure their muscles would gain anything no matter how much you bench or squat. And how does one gain intelligence? Don't tell me brain training is real and that it can take you from 1 dot to 5 with enough practice (I mean I don't actually know but from what I can see it just sounds like a great way to sell products, please feel free to share science)

    I'm of the opinion that vampires gain Disciplines from the methods used to hunt, Attributes from hunting people with advantageous qualities, and abilities from... ordinary training, though perhaps the blood of people with that skill help things along. Virtues and willpower can come from character building moments. I suspect vampires will be a little slower than normal humans at acquiring skills, but in turn they don't forget the skills they've learnt, allowing them to accumulate considerable acumen over the centuries without constantly having to practice skill retention.

    I had the idea that maybe instead of around 3xp a session to be spent in any way the player choses, they would have 1xp for disciplines, 1 xp for attributes, and 1 xp for skills. The first two are the natural results of feeding. The latter obviously in practicing skills.

    A similar thought for werewolf: Garou probably actually practice and reinforce skills when they acquire Gifts, since Gifts are often accompanied by a challenge which tests the character. Ergo it's a little strange to sacrifice XP that could be used for skills in order to obtain gifts. Ditto Infernalists.


    However, early nights are really very important. I kinda expect fledglings and early-stage neonates to be training their asses off and getting like.... 30 xp a month or something. Thoughts?

      Vampiric growth is one of those things makes less and less sense the more you think about it. It routinely breaks the setting depending on what you are prioritizing in the simulations.

      Game XP:
      At the end of every game, the PCs get some XP, but this breaks down due to the fact that not everyone has the same session frequency. If we assume a baseline of 5 XP per session to keep the math simple, that means a group that plays once a week and a group that plays daily or just several times a week will have completely different progression speeds. This is just a game mechanic that sits apart from the setting or the fluff.

      Vampires are the same:
      There is no clan or bloodline that has a bonus to XP gains, the closest you get is In-Clan Discipline cost reductions. The True Brujah have a clan weakness issue for paying more XP for increasing specific stats, but all the others don't have any such restriction. This would mean that all of vampire society should be advancing at the same rate. Thus, as fast as the PCs "get good", the antagonists should have an equal number of new tricks. Meaning that being active and older is practically a guaranteed win condition. So the best strategy would be to isolate yourself from all vampire society and keep self training, but that breaks the whole social monster thing that is at the core of VtM. I guess this is the reason why so many anime with RPG elements all have XP Boosters as one of the hero traits that set them apart.

      Gaining Access by Doing:
      As an IRL Martial Arts enthusiast I can easily agree with the sentiment that training gets results, but at the same time the stakes vampires play for are way higher than what most humans deal with. For every vampire that doesn't have Fortitude as a clan discipline, does that mean they have to constantly endure aggravated damage to learn it and then level it? How many vampires end up in the final death trying to gain a dot? Normally you see this potential downside when practicing magic but when you try to think of a training setup for the various disciplines there is a huge risk of collateral damage. Alternatively if you say that you have to learn on the job, rather than in training, that means if the ST decides to run the wrong kind of game, you can not advance. Are you a combat specialist, well the next 8 to 12 play sessions are nothing but politics and social manipulation. Are you a Necromancer, well no one has died in the area who was strong enough to become a ghost.

      XP Costs as Game Design:
      This is one of those things that doesn't get talked about much, but there is remarkably little balance in build costs. Back in Exalted 2nd edition there was a lot of discussion of exactly how many XP it took to create a combat character with very specific charms to ensure damage output and defense, off the top of my head for a Solar combat build it was something around 75 XP post character generation with you spending bonus points to take what you can and use the left over for character extras. Conversely if you wanted the same Solar to be a Crafter, you would have to spend all your bonus points on crafting picks, no room for customization, and then you couldn't functionally do your job as a crafter until 250 XP post character generation. Imagine a fighter no able to hit anything until 250 points into the game.

      VtM doesn't have as an extreme an XP build balance problem, but there are definitely some build types that largely XP sinks. A City Gangrel at 50 XP post character generation is far more functional than a Tremere Thaumaturgy specialist at the same XP level. This is then exacerbated by the fact that certain builds require fewer secondary stats than others. A sniper only needs Dexterity+Firearms with some Stealth dots, while a Business CEO type needs all the social stats and attributes on top of the stats required for his business.

      Games don't Last:
      The sad truth of most RPGs is that groups break up, people get bored, and life happens. As a result, most characters never get within sight of finishing a build, let alone completing the build and beginning to branch out. So, there is a real argument to be had about burning bonus points to get higher powers rather than higher stats. For example, if you put one dot in each discipline, to max out all three it will cost 150 XP post character generation. On the other hand, if you put all three points into one discipline, burn 14 bonus points to max out that discipline, then it will only cost you 120 XP to learn and max out the other two, with the downside that your other stats will be capped at 3 dots. Thus, you get more upfront benefit for the limited time the game runs.


      The Answer:
      There isn't a one size fits all answer. Scale the XP up as the number of sessions per week goes down, keep in mind the differences in build costs to be fully functional and try to create scenarios that balance that so you don't appear to be showing favoritism, then come up with reasons why the other vampires either are not learning at the same rate or are incentivized to not spend their banked XP on abilities to maintain the power gap between them and the PCs.

          I think vampires will act mostly like real people. Very few people "train their asses off" at any point in time. Sure, a very small select group does - usually people in competitive sports, elite special forces soldiers who need to master certain skills to do their job, or certain scientists, engineers, and so on who need to quickly master some discipline as a result of some emergency. But all those people do so in an environment where there is already a large institutional framework to provide them with instructors, accumulated knowledge, equipment, and lots of resources so that those people can make "learning" their full time job for an extended period of time. Vampires actually don't have any of those things. The Tremere come closest, but your local chantry is basically just a special library with a mentor (who may have more important things to do than spend a lot of time on intense instruction of the newbie).

          The time of accelerated learning for vampires has already happened prior to when PCs start their character. The game assumes the average PC has had 5-25 years as a vampire before the first game session happens. That is when they were taught important skills by their sire like how to hunt, preserve the Masquerade, master their initial number of Disciplines, how to handle the Beast, and so on.

          If anything, I feel the normal process of granting XP in typical chronicles results in the PCs increasing their power far too much, too quickly. In a short amount of time, neonates can become very powerful even to many NPCs who are meant to be powerful. I don't mind PCs becoming more powerful than the Prince or a Primogen or even other elders. But I do have an issue with them doing it in the timespan of perhaps 1-2 years in game time. So I absolutely disagree with the idea that STs should be giving out 30 XP a month to the PCs.

          That's why I like to run historical games that can provide the illusion of time to reasonably accommodate all the XP they are getting. I generally go for a 20-50 period before the expected end of the chronicle. After a "story" that might run a few game sessions where PCs might spend a really short of time doing whatever, I'll then have some amount of downtime - maybe weeks or months, or in some special cases years - and start the next game session that much time after we left the last game. Of course, the more time spent in downtime, we as a gaming group will spend a large part of that game session confirming what has happened, what did they do, what did NPCs do, etc. so any of their long term plans can proceed or even come to fruition.

          I tend to view any population in a WoD game to be in one of three groups - the chumps, the lieutenants, and the movers & shakers. Chumps advance slowly on the XP chart. Lieutenants sprint ahead, but not too much. Just enough to distinguish them as the leaders of the chumps. And the movers & shakers gain a lot of XP relatively quickly. It's about a 60%/30%/10% division. I do "level up" NPCs over time, but most will fall behind the pace of the PCs.

          Of course, the PCs are always in the movers & shakers category. Given enough time, the PCs will bypass the chumps and become equals to the lieutenants. By the end of the chronicle, they'll be able to become members of the mover & shakers, but typically at the low end. If the chronicle is in a small city, they might be able to squeeze the existing movers & shakers out. If they are in a large city that has attracted some very old and powerful elders, then there may be Kindred they need to be careful around, but they are still part of the local power structure. I think most games are like that - I just prefer a narrative it took them 50-100 years to do so rather than 3 (even though it's the same amount of game sessions).

          In real life, unique people like Alexander the Great and Napoleon can achieve immense success in a very short period of time. The same can be the same for entrepreneurs in brand new industries - Steve Jobs, Bill Gates, Elon Musk, and so on. But what happens to Napoleon if Frederick the Great, Julius Caesar, and Genghis Khan are still around? What happens to Zuckerberg if Bill Gates, the king of shady business practices, is still actively controlling his empire and has powers to do all sorts of illegal things to ruin the competition? That is the kind of environment vampires deal in. It doesn't mean these incredible individuals still won't move up and build their empires, it'll just be at a much slower pace.

          That's how I see it. But I know I ST my chronicles differently from most people. It's not how I use to run my chronicles, but my current method was born out of some dissatisfaction at the final results. I started changing things because a friend once ran a chronicle that started in the 1970s. He really liked that mid-late seventies feel degenerated decline of the era of disco, punk rock, Studio 54, black outs, famous serial killers, etc. I really liked how the actual passage of time impacted the feel of the game as we moved through the seventies into the early eighties. Our character progress seemed more satisfying as we tied it to actual events (both fictional and historical) in game, instead of it always being "now".

          Now players do want to achieve certain XP milestones. They typically want at least one Disciple to reach level 5. They all want to learn more Disciplines. They want to achieve certain Background goals in game. So players may want to see more XP collectively at certain stages than I am giving out. If so, I might either adjust how much XP they getting per session, and/or award bonus XP during those downtimes. The key is aligning what the players want with what the ST needs, and the chronicle's setting.

                          First off I think that the XP perspective is completely failed from the onset. XP is one of the most contrived abstractions in RPGs ever, despite being a staple and something I'm mostly happy with. No XP system conveys realistic growth for any kind of character, most not even pretend to, and WoD isn't an exception on either. PCs get XP because they're mandated protagonists at the hands of players that need a set rule for expected growth, and that's it.

                          Beyond that there are many facets to this question. I usually don't like to be too strict in defining how mystic processes happen in the game, as long as they don't hinder the game. Growing Attributes, specially physical ones, is certainly a mystic process for a dead body, so who knows? One vampire gains them through consumption, another for some reason does seem to get real benefit from exercising even if by all accounts that shouldn't work. Maybe they're channeling belief, or blood into this? Maybe just having blood in your system twists your body over time and enhance (or damage!) your attributes.

                          Originally posted by Thoth View Post
                          Furthermore Kindred society and unlife in general isn't known for being forgiving or particularly kind to failure.
                          Adding to what Black Fox said, I think this miss something of the big picture of living in a society, even if it is a supernatural one:

                          Mediocrity is the norm.

                          Successful people aren't mediocre most of the time, or at least when it really matters. But success is something that exists in contrast to those who didn't get it. And when we talk society, there's more nuance to it than just failure.

                          Behind the successful entrepreneur there are droves of salary workers. They're not failures. They get what they need to feed their families and lives. Likewise, a Prince is only successful if they have a city to command, with lots of vampires that aren't Princes nor will ever be. Yes, some will succumb at some point, but if people are dying all the time the Prince isn't really successful. Behind their success is a number of vampires with stable lives that can't hold a candle to the movers and shakers of Kindred society, but they manage to fulfill their needs. They may be still incredibly powerful compared to mortals, but among the Kindred they're mediocre. Even if just in context.

                          Unless your version of the WoD is a constant battle royal where only the fittest manage as much as to survive a year, most vampires will do what everyone does, they'll find their comfort zone and stay there. They'll pick the fights they know they'll win and avoid uncertainty, they'll leave trouble for those who want to deal with it, and they'll follow the only really tested and true method of survival against things bigger than them: lay down, don't draw attention and let them eat someone stupider than you. Make yourself useful enough to not be a liability and you're gold, that's more than enough.

                          Those people aren't PCs or important NPCs, of course. But they're the social background of the story, and actually the only real definition of what a "typical" or "average" vampire should be like, not the important characters. Those are special by definition.

                          Originally posted by MyWifeIsScary View Post
                          And how does one gain intelligence? Don't tell me brain training is real and that it can take you from 1 dot to 5 with enough practice (I mean I don't actually know but from what I can see it just sounds like a great way to sell products, please feel free to share science)
                          Since you asked:

                          "Intelligence" is a tricky definition to begin with, and while it is the longest studied mental faculty, the very first thing a Psychologist ever studied in this world, the question if we can get smarter by training still raises a huge variety of answers among specialists.

                          But the actual hard part is to define it, and technical definitions of Intelligence are like every technical definition of something: a language tool for specialists to discuss, that has loads of meaning to them, but has little to do with colloquial usage.

                          All in all, I'd say the best answer to this question without going too much into details is yes, we can improve intelligence, and do so with training. We can improve many things that are aspects of Intelligence or somehow affect it, and we can improve many skill that aren't technically Intelligence, but for practical purposes in our daily lives are just as good.

                          To give just some examples, we can improve memory through training, and that generally improves our performance on most things related to Intelligence even if it isn't intelligence per-se (and guess what, it is considered a part of intelligence by most RPGs, including WoD). We can improve many "tangential" skills like pattern recognition, stimuli discrimination and others that scientists use other words to describe, but for our discussion fall within the game's definition of Intelligence. We can learn new reasoning methods, specially through learning new languages, that increase our cognitive problem-solving tools.

                          Does it matter if any of those things is technically labeled Intelligence in scientific papers for the purposes of increasing a game stat? Overall I think we're well served in this sense, at least as much as we are with increasing any other attribute.

                          Oh, and one important effect of such training is that while it may not really improve "real" intelligence significantly, it does have an effect in fighting off things that reduce our effective intelligence in real life. Most of the time we're not operating at our peak, and we have a tendency for decay of course. "Mental training", even some of the most bogus ones, is good at helping us to lag less behind our own potential and to keep it or even recover through time.

                          Just to clarify, I won't give a source here because the question is actually far bigger in real science because of all those things that aren't real Intelligence for us but are just as good for the game. A research for scientific papers on improving intelligence alone will give a wide range of data running through the gamut of possible answers and really won't help without picking up everything and reading for the details, and then your actual interest may be in some of the many traits that have other labels within academy. Even a sample would take me more time copying and pasting sources than typing this, so I'll really accept the doubt this time if it comes to that.

Using a weapon for intimidation's is in most cases actually better than using the weapon. You've certainly made a colossal mistake if you have to whip something out during a routine feed. Mastering wrestling is a great way to stop things from getting out of control. Once you're firmly in control and the subject is in your arms rather than bleeding to death, you have options.
