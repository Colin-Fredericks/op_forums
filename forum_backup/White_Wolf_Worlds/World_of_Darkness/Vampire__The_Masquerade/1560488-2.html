            Originally posted by DarkKing View Post

            On one hand, vampirism is explicitly supposed to be a CURSE, or a divine PUNISHMENT. So making degeneration of one's Humanity an inevitable process does reinforce that idea.
            Only if you buy the argument rather than question it. Every single word here is up to debate in more than one way.

            Is it a curse? What kind of curse? Punishment for what? From what kind of divinity?

            More importantly, why does inevitable degeneration reinforce this? What's the point of punishment if degeneration is a fate, not a choice? That's completely backwards.

            This logic being backwards is one of the points of 1st edition: the Book of Nod sells a story where ultimately vampirism is a cyclical falacy: you're cursed with degeneration for the sin of becoming degenerate once put on a curse of degeneration.

            What solves the falacy there is that there is no curse of degeneration, really: you're just as free to decide your path as before. The horror is not that your downfall is inevitable, but that it is totally avoidable, yet most will still fall because it is convenient.

            And what if a character don't buy the premises to begin with? All the usual vampire spiel is strictly based on European Roman Catholic premises. Followers of Protestant churches like to say those things are closer to universal ideas, but that's because they don't notice how much they're still locked in Roman Catholic theological priors.

            Many cultures out there don't have many of the premises needed for that reasoning to hold. Take for example that Islam assumes every intelligent entity has the basic free will to join the Faithful, no exceptions. There is no concept in it of a class of beings being inherently corrupt or damned. Many don't have the notion of damnation at all.

            For me the proposal of V5 has merit as a reflex of the horror of a society that build a idealized moral for you, then constrain you to a life where breaking morality becomes a survival need, and finally punishes you for doing the evil you had no choice about. But that's precisely a recognition that this is a setup, you're given a context where you can't win.

            Those are not the premises of traditional Gothic or Punk literature, which are the standards for previous editions. V5 follows "Gothic-Punk" only as aesthetics, which isn't a bad thing, but its literary tradition is Grimdark Satire.

            Valid, but not more valid.

                    One thing I would like a potential V6 to do is reduce the centrality of the Caine myth somewhat, so it's just one of several options. Yes, it's the one most Western and Abrahamic Kindred believe (and it's pretty much an essential belief in the Sabbat), but presenting a few different options would help open the game up.

                    If the Caine myth is always presented as basically true, not only are you chump for thinking the First Vampire was someone else, but it also limits Kindred history to a small period of time from the Middle East to the present day. Every Antediluvian essentially has to come from somewhere between Syria and Iran.

                    But if there's scope for Caine to have been something else, the First City might have been in Ethiopia, or Mohenjo-Daro, or whatever. This also opens us up to moving away from the centrality of Abrahamic faith in Kindred morality.

                    It doesn't have to be a big retcon. Just provide options and refer back to those options later on, instead of saying "it might be these other things" in the CRB and then never really mentioning those other origins again (which is what Revised did).

                        Originally posted by adambeyoncelowe View Post
                        One thing I would like a potential V6 to do is reduce the centrality of the Caine myth somewhat, so it's just one of several options.
                        So much that.

                        But nowadays I notice why they don't, and even other Abrahamic beliefs are sidestepped. Because let's be clear here, the Book of Nod is Christian. It is not Jewish and it is not Muslim, it is unapologetically and unavoidably Christian as it tell everything from interpretations that are in proto forms in post-exilic Israel at best, or are straight up post-Agostinian at worse.

                        The problem, though, is that in all versions the game cycles back to the theme of Damnation and objective Good and Evil. It builds horror over the premise that the taint of sin and evil can condemn your immortal soul by bringing it away from the love of YHWH and its redemption.

                        Most other belief systems don't engage with such priors. Not that they offer other answers, they see the very question as nonsensical.

                        We already have this experiment in the Ashirra and the Revised (and exiled) Banu Haqim: in the Quran every intelligent being has free will and can chose to join the faithful. The notion of a condition that intrinsically condemns you is straight up shut down by the Book itself. The demons aren't defined by what they are, but by their work on straying the Faithful from the Path.

                        Minor, and works as a group within the game. But part of the difficulty of using them as PCs is that they immediately throw half the personal horror through the window. Being a decent person is still hard, but not desperately so, and you have your shot at Paradise like any other person.

                        For most, nonhuman creatures are just that. They are ontologically distinct, which means distinct cosmological roles, but otherwise are there to be themselves. An Hindu vampire will ask their relationship with the classes of gods within Maya. A Japanese one accepts it turned yōkai and moves on. Even the ancient Greek would find the transformation the destiny in itself: the will of the gods caused this, but this has no inherent weight on your later destiny in Erebus if you ever get there. The life you forge out of your new condition is the punishment in itself.

                        Look to the half a dozen religious constructs of the Setites. At least 2 or 3 are actually pretty decent: they give positive drive to the vampire, propose community good, and reconcile this with the predatory nature. You won't be lovable, innocent and harmless, but won't be denied a positive role in society either.

                        Even removing religion: what's the ecological niche of a vampire? Why should a human predator that exists in a complex ecological system with the prey, and a micro-predator by the way, be just consigned to extinction?

                        Do you know how vile something has to be for biologists to conclude the world is undeniably better without you? Most atheists still grew up surrounded by Christians, so they understand and buy the Damnation question easily. But one that doesn't already throws the whole premise to the ground.

                        For us to see alternatives to Nod taken seriously and consistently in VtM, the game has to first grip with what being a vampire means in radically different spiritual frameworks.

                                                            Noddism (which is not just the Path of Caine) is theological even if not a religion by itself. There's a reason why I only used "religious" when directly indexing the Sabbat itself, rather than Noddism lore broadly.

                                                            Also keep in mind that there aren't some sort of objective criteria around what makes something religious or a religion. It's a very blurry line that sometimes gets defined in neater ways for utility's sake. Buddhism is a pretty text book example of something that doesn't really fit what most people think of as a religion on a surface level (including emulating Buddha, not worshiping him), while still filling the vast majority of the same cultural and social roles as a more "standard" religion does. Noddism still recognizes the Abrahamic supreme deity as the world's supreme deity even while centering Caine as it's most important mythological figure (Zoroaster is not the deity of Zoroastrianism for another real world parallel) that serves as more of a prophetic archetype that teaches how to relate to the divine instead of replacing the divine as the target of ritual practice.
