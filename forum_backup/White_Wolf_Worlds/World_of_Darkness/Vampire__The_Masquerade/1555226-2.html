                                There's a giant list of reasons why a certain developer might not get a job: availability, desire, compatibility with Paradox, networking, willingness to commit the level of resources necessary (esp. if the project is beyond normal scope for the studio), not wanting to work under certain conditions (like forking over a 1/3 stake in your company), familiarity with desired design tools, deadline estimates, etc. etc. etc.

                                The big impression given when TCR was announced by Paradox as basically that TCR wanted the project the most. That can mean a lot of things in corpo-speak from, "they were the only people that would actually do it on our terms," to, "they were willing to give us the best terms for what we considered the expected outcomes," to actually, "we really felt like they understood the IP the best," and more.

                                For relatively smaller studios looking to get a gig on a larger franchise there's a lot of selling yourself that the company needs to do. What the winning pitch is, is usually kept between the two companies.

                                            One thing that stands out about how Paradox has handled the book-side of things is how unstable the whole thing has been. Like the situation with Lamplighter's League where Paradox leadership came out and called the game a disappointment and then an anonymous developer on a forum told people that Paradox had slashed the budget of the game because Paradox had lost faith in the project and so 80% of the staff had been let go before the launch of the game. Or one of the games from their "indie"-brand Paradox-Arc called Mechabellum had a successful 1.0 launch last year and got some good buzz from video-game-journalists. Or how Paradox used to work with Obsidian but as soon as their game Tyranny didn't meet expectations, Paradox complained about it and Obsidian left.                                                     I'm not sure that last point really addresses the issue of consistency... sure it's a coherent message from Paradox's WoD team about how W5 is going to be handled, but it's also just affirming that we have no reason to trust in consistency because we never know what will come in from the previous editions, and if it does how much it will have changed in the process.

                                                    A big part of how this fits into the overall pattern with Paradox, is that brands are built on consistency. Brands have value because the labels let you know what you're going to get; even negative things. Calling a product a spiritual successor to a previous work is still a promise of some level of consistency between the products despite not being a direct sequel (though labeling something as a direct sequel/iteration and calling it a spiritual successor is also not great marketing). If the new product isn't consistent enough with the previous product that the consumers feel that "spirit" then it's certainly a problem.
