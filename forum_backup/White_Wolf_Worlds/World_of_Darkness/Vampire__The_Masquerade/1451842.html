    Prehistory theory with Noddist implications

    06-12-2021, 10:05 AM
    An essay by an Archeology postdoc. The core idea of it, is that our ideas of how people lived for most of our species history are based on modern day observations of hunter-gatherer societies. However, because these observations were made in such historically recent times, the bands that were left to observe often inhabited the most barren and resource-poor areas of the world. Additionally, in some cases they turned out to be regressed and post-apocalyptic survivors of larger and more complicated societies that collapsed from disease shock or violence when encountering agricultural and/or modern societies.

    Beyond the !Kung by Manvir Singh

    It argues that humans in resource rich areas in many cases fell into large, organized societies.Sometimes very large indeed. It would put things like Göbekli Tepe into a context. And there is indications going much further back, burials of adolescents with huge amounts of grave-goods they could not possibly have accumulated themselves and indicating far-flung trade networks. Graves 30 000 years old.

    Now, looking at Vampire history, it seems to me, the Antediluvians were originally intended to be from about 8 000 BC, making a good claim to early agriculture and the First City. Then Baba Yaga and later Vasilisa screwed it up, pushing the First city back to 15 000 BC. If this theory holds, there were kine concentrations that could support vampires far back into the Ice Age.

          There’s no real way of telling what civilizations were lost in rapid rise of sea levels that had several spikes; most notably c. 14,000 years ago which corresponds with the dating many cultures have for a “great flood” event) during the end of the last Ice Age. Dogerland and Sundaland were both inhabited by humans until they were flooded into the Baltic and South China Seas respectively. A few thousand years of salt water has washed away all but the occasional stone tool or feature.

          Likewise, the Sahara Desert was a tropical savanna with freshwater bodies bigger than the Great Lakes until just 5000 years ago. There are even non-fossilized whale bones that have been found in thr Sahara speaking to how recently geologically speaking the region had abundant life. Archeological digs have unearthed cave paintings depicting large herds of wildlife in the midst of the desert as well.

          Certainly the most advanced civilizations of their time (probably still Stone Age, but the Aztecs, Inca and Maya were all stone age as they’d never developed bronze or iron and are considered very advanced relative to their neighbors, some copper tools might have been in play too) were located near the very bodies of water that rose and swallowed them and there would have been significant upheavals and technological regressions when the flood spikes innundated them similar to what happened with the Bronze Age Collapse and subsequent Dark Age.

          Heck, we still can’t decypher the Linear-A script of the Minoan civilization and that’s from just 3500 years ago.

          The point being... Aztec-level through Ancient Aliens isn’t an impossible level of technology and civilization for a Vampire game (though I would consider the latter a virtual impossibility in real life).

                  It's an interesting article, and it brings up some good points when talking about Paleolithic anthropology. Thanks for sharing. However, we should remember that without an economic surplus, you can't have any kind of hierarchical societies or economic specialization. And before agriculture and livestock domestication, there's only limited opportunity for that. And remember these societies would have needed to do everything with stone tools.

                  Every land has its own carrying capacity, and hunter-gatherer societies can't overcome that. The two examples given in the article seems to be in areas particularly favorable to hunter-gatherer societies. They are near the ocean and therefore have great access to seafood. They receive lots of rain and therefore quite bountiful. And they don't have long, cold winters - they're temperate or sub-tropical. And we must remember that even if they didn't practice agriculture themselves, they exist in a world that does and therefore were able to have some long distance trade with more advanced societies. And they likely had domesticated dogs to help with the hunt, and possibly some livestock of the few animals domesticated in the Americas. As long as they are able to drive out intruders wanting to share in that bounty, there might be enough surplus to support an elite, some economic specialization, and settlements. But not much.

                  And that's an extremely favorable environment. Move inland and seafood resources diminishes quickly. Go into areas more marginal when it comes to a stone age hunter gathering society (lands that have long cold winters, less rain, worst weather, more dangerous animals), and people need to migrate again so as to not exhaust resources. So the number of areas that could support a settled Paleolithic society is probably quite small. And remember that the Ice Age means a global drought because all that water is trapped in ice. It's easy to forget that Antarctica is a desert because we associate snow with water. It's the same with the Ice Age. More desert. Areas we now think of forests were grasslands because of the lack of rain; grasslands were then deserts; a lot of land is simply uninhabitable because it is buried under ice. The extant biomass is much lower. Carrying capacity is far less than in the Holocene.

                  Comparing a settlement of say a thousand people versus a small band of a dozen or dozens is a big difference when comparing Paleolithic societies. But it does not necessarily mean the large urban communities the game envisions are necessary to support vampires. Compare to the early city states of Mesopotamia and you have communities of tens of thousands of people. Especially if you consider other parts of the World of Darkness like Garou and the Impergium, those smaller Paleolithic communities might not be amenable to vampires to come in.

                  Now if all that is to allow some justification for a creativity to create some cool ideas or mysteries in your chronicle, that's great. Go far it if it adds something cool, interesting, and unique for your chronicles. But I don't think it completely turns everything upside down.

                  In terms of Vampire setting history, what is really important is the date you assign the Antedeluvians from waking up and returning to the post-deluge world. That's when for all practical purposes "vampire history" begins, and new vampires are created. And that date will always be arbitrary. Do you want that to be 8000 BC? 12,000 BC? 20,000 BC? Even earlier?

                  When it comes to the actual Antedeluvial world, you can justify anything because in the World of Darkness if you go back far enough there is no Gauntlet and the spirit and material world are one. The rules of reality would be very different. I am always surprised how many Vampire players seem to assume that the antedeluvial world must correspond to some kind of real history with real archaeology behind it. Even those who take the Biblical myth origins seriously (whether because they like it or complain about it) do the same thing even though there is no reason to think a pre-Deluge world would map well to the post-Deluge world. It's a very strange mix of Biblical literalism and real world science.

                  You can pretty much come up with anything you want to describe that antedeluvial world, but for the vast majority of games it'll be irrelevant. Our real world history probably only occurs after the Deluge happens (whatever and whenever that might be). Whatever archeological fragments of the Antedeluvial world somehow survived on this side of the Gauntlet are probably classified as OOPArts (out of place artifacts), and either dismissed, forgotten, or the preserve of nutcases (some of who in the WoD could very well be right).

                                  The thing is we can’t be certain the Ice Age inhabitants of places like Sundaland or the Sahara Savanna region didn’t have agriculture earlier than we generally attribute it first appearing, but that it was lost as the result of their civilizations collapsing due to innundation (Sundaland) or desertification (Sahara) and just getting lost as populations dwindled.

                                  Take a look at the Greek Dark Age... they literally lost and had to relearn writing; which is why we can’t actually read Linear A. If not for the lucky find of the Rosetta Stone we still wouldn’t know what the Egyptian hieroglyphs actually say and much of their history would be as mythical to us as the story of Atlantis.

                                  Which was the point with our studies of many of the modern hunter-gatherer tribes we’re using as the foundation of how primitive people must have lived; a number of those tribes are survivors of larger more complex societies that suffered their own cataclysmic collapses and regressions.

                                  There’s sections of the Sahara Desert with surface level layers of salt and geography consistent with a massive flood event severe enough to have stripped it down to bedrock. You could have had scores of mud brick villages and towns in that area before the event that would have been obliterated without a trace. We know from scattered artifacts and population genetics that humans lived in the Dogerland and Sundaland regions but 10,000 years of salt water and sediment have all but erased the evidence those places were once inhabited.

                                  Heck, based on the research done by the series like “Life After People” even our civilization with all its advances could disappear with only the barest traces remaining in just a few centuries, much less the hundred fifty thousand years or so humans have been around. We could have taken 100 millennia to become a civilization as advanced as today’s that lasted 5000 years before collapsing and still had another 40 millennia (that’s 2000 generations of human beings) for nature to wipe out the evidence before the earliest writings we’re aware of came into being.

                                  If you treated the time modern human have existed as a day, then the earliest recorded history happened just 40 minutes ago and the Middle Ages was seven minutes ago. What happened a metaphorical eight hours ago or sixteen hours ago is pretty much unknowable.

                                  The point is, in a world with vampires and other supernaturals in it, you could have society and technology be ANYTHING you desire in the distant times before recorded history.

You could say that re-examining the current assumptions on that is the point of the article.

                                      On the hydrological cycle, it is a cycle of evaporation - rain - drainage back to the sea. When the water gets slowed down in glacial form, that does not stop the evaporation step in any way. Which means there is nearly as much moisture in the air. The slowed down drainage step means that the sea levels drop as the water that evaporated out lags on returning through drainage. So instead of humidity dropping, sea levels drop. During the Ice Age, sea levels were as much as 120 meters lower than today.

                                      Also, on Siberia, there is a few points to remember:

                                      -The insolation was pretty much the same as today. The latitudes from the north Mediterranean coast to Denmark got the same amount of heat transfer from the sun as today. And the same "inland climate" effect was going.

                                      -Ice weights down the land, depressing it. So most drainage at the time wanted to drain north. Except there was massive glaciers in the way. So you got lots of melt in the summers, gigantic lakes and a lot of water eventually draining out through the Aral/Caspian to the Bllck Sea. Probably very hydrologically active. Not suited for agriculture, but that wasn't an option with the less stable climate.

                                      Loads of game though. Loads. And fish.

Neanderthals went extinct several times in Europe and re-colonised from the east. Once our ancestors got there, we see an exchange of genes running from Beijing to the Baltic, prior to the coldes period of the Ice Age. Slowly obv.
