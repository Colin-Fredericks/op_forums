    Originally posted by monteparnas View Post

    I don't mind that he did. I mind how he did. And the very point is that none of the possible explanations are explored, it simply doesn't matter.

    "Woke up and broke it with his pinky" obviously isn't literal, but the fact is that it could be and nothing would change. There's no story, no consequence, no exploration, no mystery, and no sense given that it should matter.
    Yeah, I very much agree. I think they wanted to have the Schism and contrived a way to make that happen, but then never really followed up on what Ur-Shulgi did next.

    V20 did some interesting things with the breaking of the curse and the Tremere antitribu attack: BJD presents one possibility where the destruction of House Goratrix and/or the regent was part of the sacrifice necessary to break the Tremere Curse. Now that would've been more interesting.

    Of course, the same book makes it clear that Tremere did the former (perhaps under the influence of Saulot), but Elimelech is heavily implied to have done the latter. One possibility is that Elimelech is an Assamite antitribu, which would mean maybe Galbraith was assassinated to break the Blood Curse. I think he'd make a much more interesting replacement for Ur-Shulgi actually, as a character who's actually got a story and a personality (maybe more than one).

    Then again, I like that Elimelech might also be Malkavian antitribu or Ventrue antitribu. The latter have so few major characters and this gives them a bit of lineage.

    Add to it his poorly made character in a setting already full of poorly made characters. His one of the most boring Evil Lords of Evilness in the setting. Not the most evil, the most boring.

    Ur-Shulgi is basically a single scene with Sam Height style of badass but hollow one-liners followed by a third of the clan becoming more one-note versions of themselves than even the original Assamite write-up.
    Yeah, I think they basically did nothing with that plot. As I've said elsewhere on these forums, an awake Ur-Shulgi disrupts the setting because he's something that needs dealing with immediately.

    I didn't mind that they were setting something up for him. I minded that they didn't follow through.

    The Web of Knives becoming even more stereotypical was a problem. The Schism itself was fine. The defection of the Assamite antitribu, though? That didn't really land for me even when they rowed it back a bit. The Lost Tribe Assamites who became the Black Hand were outcasts and exiles even before the Anarch Revolt, and I can't see someone like Izhim ur-Baal bowing down to Ur-Shulgi.

    Overall, an interesting setup that fed into the Final Nights vibe but did nothing other than giving a reason for Camarilla Assamites to exist. But Ur-Shulgi wasn't needed for that.

    Now, what they did with the Gangrel is outright bizarre. They're like elves, every environment get a new variety.

    I'm thinking about expanding the list just for fun. We need Sky Gangrel! And if we have Gangrel from Mongolia, Gangrel from Britain, why stop there? Let's make Carpatian Gangrel, Desert Gangrel, Yellow River Gangrel, Japanese Gangrel, Okinawa Gangrel, Roman Gangrel, Corsican Gangrel, Polynesian Gangrel...
    Too funny! But now you mention it, I can't unsee it. They totally turned the core Gangrel into wood elves, and that's why they started doing all the elf subspecies as Gangrel variants.

    Ur-Shulgi didn't have to use a Hermetic method, but he had to know what he was countering. He just didn't. What Banu Haqim method to use if you don't know what you're doing even under your own tradition's framework?
    I guess it's possible the Sorcerers worked it out over the previous 500 years but didn't have the power to do it themselves. The curse seems to have worked on basic sympathetic principles: the Eldest signed a deal, binding his clan to him.

    That Eldest was killed and none of the others seemed capable of breaking the contract. Maybe the only way to break it again was to have an Assamite who was at least as old and powerful as the original signatory claim the Black Throne and revoke it. Few were as old and powerful as Ur-Shulgi.

    But the moral of this story is that sweeping changes made by big, dramatic events rarely have the payoff they need to make them really land. You have to really commit to seeing things through, or they fall flat and, in hindsight, end up being worse than if they just hadn't happened in the first place.

                    This anachronism and his rejection of complex politics in favor of ideological purity are his few defining traits. False Slumber would flatten him even more by turning him from almost generic particular trope to a completely generic generic trope.
