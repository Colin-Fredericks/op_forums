    It's not so much a matter of paths I dislike, but rather a problem in the way many were structured.

    To me, the gold standard is the way humanity is done. You have at the bottom the most morality breaking acts and also the most difficult to accomplish, it's not everyday your average humanity based kindred will go on a genocide.

    And the highest the humanity, the more difficult it is to hold. To the point where humanity 10 is about even avoiding having thoughts of harming others.

    Which makes a good model for paths. The lower ratings should enforce the core of the philosophy with the level 1 sins being an utter betrayal of the beliefs, and the higher one is on their path, the more abstract and hard to maintain.


    Now, many paths falls short of that, and it sometimes feels like a much needed rework would have helped.


    Let's take three paths that falls short of this standard, but are in their concepts amongs my favorites.

    The path of power and the inner voice is basically making the average machiavellian elder tendances into a philosophy.

    The level 1 sin is not taking power or advantage when you have the occasion.
    So far so good, that's the core of the path, so it's expected that it would be the level 1.

    But then we have the level 7 sin, which is helping others without gaining an advantage by doing so. And the rationale is that you should always take advantage by your actions.

    Which is.... Exactly level 1?

    This one looks like the writers had trouble finding 10 specific sins for that path

    The we have the path of death and the soul

    So extreme scholarship focused on death. But the priorities of the sins are all messed up

    The level 1 sin is not letting a death happening
    But level 5 is not pursuing enlightenment. By all account, enlightenment is the core oft he path and breaking it should be the level 1 sin

    Same problem with one of my favorite path, at least in concept, metamorphosis.
    ​​​​Now the path makes the capital sin (sorry for the pun) of being borderline unplayable due to it's weird focus on not caring about others.

    To the point that showing compassion (and here we have the vague sin, does it means to have compassion or to show compassionate behavior, the second that may be manipulation?)

    How is that the deal breaker of this path?

    In the same way, why is "postponing feeding when hungry" so high on the list when it's a pretty easy situation to deal with?



    By all logic, the level 1 sin should be the actual level 4 sin of "not modifying yourself when you have the chance.

    ​​​​​​Tldr: many paths needs to have their order of sins reworked
    ​​​​​

            Yeah, I think the whole system of 10 Sins is terrible for many reasons. It was okay-ish when it came out in 1991, it was innovative as concept, and it was easier to manage since all PCs used the same list of Sins, with varying degrees.

            But Paths? The ST gotta know every Sin the PC is over in their particular Path, and remember the Virtue associated, and it becomes a mess.
            The Ethics described in each Path are 4 out of 5 times, the best descriptions and Sins-Virtues of the Path. So they really should've used those as the basis for the gameplay, not an increasing list of 10 Sins, that become awfully cumbersome to remember at all times. Besides, some Sins are awfully described, like Nyremne said, you don't know if it is spirit or in action, if it is to other or yourself; and the order of some is confusing.

            Much better if there were only 5 sins-virtues, working like Convictions to each Path.


            Also. Some Paths seem to be told very differently depending on each author and book, and the memo on some seem to be forgotten when some recurrent characters are written in novels.

            Strange... When coincidence seems too convenient, I prefer to call it fate.

                Part of the problem with the game design of the Paths is that Humanity is an excellent example of how early VtM was innovative but also not refined. The WoD's struggle between being a highly thematic storytelling game, and a verisimilitude enabling reality emulator has never really be resolved. Humanity (and from it the Paths) is, through many revisions still, a hard to handle mix of both concepts that end up butting heads more than reinforcing each other.

                That is basically the origin of Paths: they did start as a "reference" because the Sabbat needed to be monstrous in their rejection of Humanity, but functional antagonists that could operate as plotting scheming vampires even while regularly performing acts that would drop them to Humanity 0 and having no interest in "repenting" to regain Humanity just to lose it again. So the thematic side of Humanity as a doom spiral of moral compromises has to share space with a system that replicates characters with differing moral codes because even among real world humans Humanity is far from a universal fit for how people would rank "sins" so why should vampires have a single locked in objective moral philosophy? As the Sabbat expanded - they were made playable fairly quickly in the scheme of things compared to other antagonists like the Technocracy - the tug of war between "ways to chain the Beast to avoid falling into being a mindless monster" and "ways to represent the diversity of vampire moral philosophy" continued to wage.

                Most of the best fixes to this fundamental issue with the concept of Humanity and Paths, are ones that either side with one part of the intentions over the other, or split them up into multiple places so it's easier for each goal to have a place.

                For example, I fairly decent homebrew I saw once scrapped Humanity and Paths as implemented and swapped in CofD 2e's Intergrity with Path Ethics replacing the character questionnaire in CofD and Path auras staying to handle the verisimilitude aspect. Then they adapted the VtR 1e Atrocity dice to handle the moral doom spiral of being a vampire. Up-keeping a high "Humanity-but-really-Integrity" meant auras (and thus Paths can be very unsettling), and a small bonus when dealing with Atrocity dice, but Atrocity hits everyone the same regardless of what moral codes they try to follow.

                I'm not saying this to promote a specific house-rule, but to illustrate that decoupling some of the important things Humanity/Paths bring to the game into two or three different elements instead can let each shine more effectively than cramming them all in one.

                            I have the privilege of having played with some great players at healthy tables, who took roads and paths seriously and played characters true to them when applicable. I've also played with less-skilled and poorly-intended players, so I see all sides of the argument. Paths/roads in general are a net benefit to the game, when taken seriously and played well; when not, it's entirely valid to not appreciate them as a player or an ST. So that said, the paths and roads I view as problematic aren't necessarily the ones that are difficult to comprehend or play, but rather those with the most leeway for abuse. You know, the "paths of what I was going to do anyway".

                            Atop that list for me are the OG versions of Typhon and Paradox, which were essentially the "Paths of Being an Asshole Player". Other problematic paths can still be played well without wreaking havoc at a table to whatever end, but Typhon and Paradox mandate disruptive gameplay. It absolutely doesn't help those two paths are specific to clans to which problematic players gravitate. Granted later iterations of the paths have been fixed for the most part, but the older versions still stand as the best examples of how paths can and do go wrong.

                            Special note here go to paths like Power and the Inner Voice, and Caine, which either tacitly or explicitly condone diablerie. Diablerie isn't the only potential issue with the paths; it just happens to be the most obvious and illustrative problematic use case. Those paths can still be played well and compelling personal narratives built around them, but they definitely need to be handled with care by players and ST's lest the potential for using those paths as an excuse for playing disruptively come to the forefront.

                            I do agree with others the "one sin per level in ascending order" regime has always had its share of issues. In that, even Humanity is no different -- vandalism being a level 5 sin more or less says it all. Especially when that level 5 sin is neatly crouched between two nebulously-defined and contradicting degrees of manslaughter.

                            For example, is draining a mortal while in hunger frenzy a level 4 or 6 sin? Level 6 specifies starvation (the trigger for hunger frenzy, it's right there in the name), but level 4 specifies frenzy for which hunger frenzy is one form (again, right there in the name). The implication is it's a lower-order sin for a vampire to resist frenzy but go too far feeding from a mortal regardless, than it is to lose control of oneself. Granted that's kind of a straw man example as specific overrides general, and no storyteller of which I'm aware would rule draining a mortal in hunger frenzy a level 4 opposed to 6 sin, but it still serves well to point out the contradictions and ill definition present in even the game's default path.

                                Originally posted by Theodrim View Post
                                Atop that list for me are the OG versions of Typhon and Paradox, which were essentially the "Paths of Being an Asshole Player".
                                Especially as originally written, I think the Setites and Ravnos (and in fact all the non-Camarilla 7 clans) were intended primarily to be antagonists, so Paths that lead themselves to being antagonists aren't a problem for the setting per se. They justified why characters of those clans would be antagonists. But they are a problem when a player wants to play one in a game cooperating with other players. It's like running an RPG set in World War II and everyone makes Allied characters and one player insists on playing a Nazi just because its technically an option in the rulebook.

                                One of the big differences between 1e/2e and Revised is that the earlier depictions tried to present a holistic setting where some potential character choices simply were not ideal from a gaming group perspective, and the other wanted to make everything much more playable. The difference between the Setites of these versions is just irreconcilable. Unless you were playing a Setite who was trying to escape the cult in 1e/2e, the Setites were just going to be assholes. Perfectly fine choice as a setting element, but it did limit their playability in any generic gaming group.

                                Now even those antagonistic choices could still be playable given the right players, coterie concept, and chronicle, but it requires more coordination and maturity than most standard games (which is typically everyone creates their own character, and everyone hopes it all works out together as a group).
