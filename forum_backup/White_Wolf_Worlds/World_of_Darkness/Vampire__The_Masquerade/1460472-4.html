        That might also explain why you didn't quite grok my argument about the American Midwest being a food desert unable to support many vampires despite millions of people.

        Perhaps some pictures will illustrate (and I'll use km instead my more familiar miles since if you're not up on American cities you're probably also not up on our non-metric units of measurement).

        This is Indianapolis, the largest city in Indiana (Metro population 2.1 million; density 947/km^2);

        Note all the green in the background; that's single family suburban homes. At the extreme end its farmland.

        This is the second largest city in Indiana, Fort Wayne (metro area 420k, city density 944/km^2);


        That green? Again, single family homes fading into farmland. The population density less than 20 miles from the city center drops to under 50/km^2 (as in you'd need a territory about 45 km on a side to support one vampire at 1:100k) outside the city. As an example, Adams County, due south of Fort Wayne is just 41/km^2.

        People from the surrounding communities (i.e. all that territory with 40-50/km^2 people in it) drive up to an hour to an hour and a half to come physically shop here (beyond that they're closer to other large urban areas). Even the general population doesn't actually live in the "dense" urban area... the majority live 16-30 km from where they work and shop. There's no way to get any degree of efficient mass transit to work with a population that geographically spread out... thus, automobiles and parking garages everywhere and almost no pedestrians for easy feeding off of.

        And here's a typical "city" in Indiana; Valparaiso (pop. 33,000; density 740/km^2 in the city limits... same less than 50/km^2 when you hit the green).

        The "nightlife" here is over by 9pm, except on Fridays during Highschool Football season.

        And this is the DENSER population areas of the Midwest. Wisconsin has HALF the population density statewide as Indiana does. Cross the Mississippi River into Iowa and the density is half of Wisconsin's.

        Lots and lots of rural small towns where everyone knows everyone equates to a giant food desert filled with ravening Lupines despite tens of millions of people living there.

        * * * *

        Now, for comparison, here's Boston, MA (Metro Pop 4.8 million; density 5400/km^2)


        Note particularly the much greater number of multi-story apartments and centralized urban area that brings a much greater concentration of the population together. That's why vampires are much more likely to flock to large high density urban areas rather than the countryside of the Midwest. Its much easier to hide and be anonymous and for people to not realize when someone goes missing.

                The thing is, that rural vampire could just as easily hit up one of other dozen night clubs in Fort Wayne that the four (at 1:100k vampires) or the homeless shelters or hospitals or perpetual adoration chapels in the one city… whatever areas the bigger vampires aren’t attending and do so far less riskily than feeding by breaking into a rural home.

                Because here’s the other thing about rural America that non-rural Americans don’t appreciate; we’re ARMED and have security systems in our homes. This is a country with more guns than people and most of those guns don’t reside in the big cities. You may have heard the phrase “when seconds count, the police are minutes away”? Try 10-20 minutes in a rural area.

                And at least half the year most people are home before the sun is down or are in their cars driving to church/school events. Everyone knows everyone in the small towns so if someone goes missing, they’re going to notice.

                And have we mentioned the werewolf packs present in the World of Darkness? The ones who prefer the rural areas which are yet another reason why vampires stick to the cities?

                Unless you’re hunting animals for food the rural Midwest is awful and basically a “its this or Final Death” option.                 Of course, you’re not the only vampire with the same idea so that ratio quickly drops further and the only thing keeping the big cities from being overrun is the Prince putting his foot down on numbers (generally requiring force and a few examples be made) and… there you have the grounds where Anarchs spawn.

                                                Originally posted by Chris24601 View Post
                                                Of course not.                                                 How did you come up with the number 60? Even back in 1991, when the original core book discussed how the 1:100,000 ratio is supposed to work, the Chicago metropolitan area had around 7 million people, and could thus safely support around 70 vampires. I'll highlight some passages on page 169 that clearly show that the original authors put careful thought into the matter:

                                                A large city such as San Francisco or Frankfurt normally supports a Vampire population of anywhere from twenty to fifty, depending on circumstances in that city. Larger cities like New York or greater London hold a proportionately larger population and smaller cities much less.

                                                As a rule of thumb, assume that at the maximum there is one Vampire for every 100,000 mortals. Thus, the greater Chicago metropolitan area, a region of 7 million mortals, can support nearly 70 Vampires. Often a Prince will artificially regulate how many Kindred reside within the city, thus ensuring the sanctity of the Masquerade.

                                                The cities could certainly hold more Kindred, but there are legitimate concerns over discovery. The Masquerade is always primary in the minds of the Elders -- better that some Anarchs die than all be extinguished in another Inquisition. Every care is taken to insure that the existence of Vampires is kept secret from the mortal population. Strict controls are kept over the creation of Neonates. Too many Vampires would increase the probability of discovery dramatically. The population is also restricted by the simple fact that few potential Vampires survive their Becoming, many are driven mad by it and put down by their Sire.
                                                Since then, the population numbers have only grown, of course, and nowadays the greater Chicago metropolitan area can easily support around a hundred vampires (population of 9.5 million in 2019). I would also remind people that Chicago was always supposed to be overpopulated by vampires. From the back-cover blurb for Chicago by Night: "No other city so dangerously on the edge of chaos. No other city so desperately overcrowded." Overpopulation is one of the themes of the book, and using Chicago as the yardstick for how every city in the setting is supposed to work actively goes against how the World of Darkness was portrayed in the core book.

                                                            Originally posted by Chris24601 View Post
                                                            See, the issue I have is that, once you count the 5-6 PCs, there's almost no place that can have sufficient numbers for any meaningful politics. When your PCs account for an eighth of the ideal vampire population is a metro area as big as Boston's (4.8 million or 48 ideal vampires) there's just not enough to work with unless you go busting ratios. One coiterie and on coiterie battle to the death would take out 10-15% of the city's whole vampire population; one heavy action adventure could wipe out half the vampires in a city in a night.
                                                            This is an idea which I see quite often, broadly defined as a game needs a population of 100 or 200 Cainites to function, particularly in terms of political structures. This is an idea which gets a great deal of implicit support in Vampire supplements from the revised edition onwards. Populations stop being defined, a notion of indefinite number of vampires in a given domain appears, and scales go out the window. Look at the political structures of the Ventrue laid out in the clan's revised clanbook. You have four distinct ranks of Ventrue in any given city; if you assume that there are five vampires at the top rank, each of whom oversee five vampires of the next rank (and that's about the minimum number required at a rank to make sense of the structure) you would end up with 780 Ventrue in the average city. And that's before you end up with the members of any other clan.

                                                            This is, of course, what you end up with when politics is thought of purely in terms of abstract structures and yet, at the same time, there are far more characters for most of them to have any other role than to be "third Ventrue spear carrier from the left". But how many characters do you really need for a political campaign? When Shakespeare wrote Richard III he included around forty named parts. The Netflix remake of House of Cards contains about twenty primary characters in each of its early series. If you want to really boil it down to bare essentials you can create a political story with five characters; one in charge, two supporters (to create a majority), and two characters in opposition (because you don't want a minority of one). Of course the supporters don't necessarily support, and the opposition doesn't necessary want to overthrow the ruler.

                                                            It doesn't really matter if everything ends in violence, and people die. That is, after all, what happens with Richard III. It can be part of the plot (or in our game terms, the chronicle). You don't want people dying all the time, and most violence shouldn't end in permanent death, having characters killed off can work to a Storyteller's advantage if done in the right manner.

                                                            With all this talk about populations, participants of this forum may wish to try out an exercise. Take an American metropolitan statistical area and then design a set of characters for that MSA at a ratio of 1:100,000 for Camarilla areas, and 1:50,000 for Sabbat areas. Maybe design a few adjacent cities, and think how they might work together, and what the in-game implications of those populations might be. Think about which bits of post-revised game writing work within a universe with these populations, and what doesn't. You aren't necessarily going to stick with this version of the world within your own chronicle, but it is a worthwhile challenge to consider.
