    Do you prefer a directed story or more free form game?

    06-14-2020, 12:54 AM
    I got back into table top gaming a couple or so years ago after a period of not playing since the early 2000s. Since I got back into things, I've been changing my style as a storyteller from tightly controlled story to a more free form style and embracing player freedom. I had a player recently mention they had wanted more storyteller direction in a "What do you like and what do you not like?" conversation about the current game. Earlier games I was getting the impression that players were annoyed with my first modern attempt at running a game for creating too narrow of a story that required battles to play out a certain way and they were just witnesses to and a reluctance to break history. Trying to run a game with the Anabasis as the prelude (which took way longer than I had expected) was proving to be too restrictive. I ran a Dark Ages game that people seemed to like a lot, where there was a story with a decent amount of freedom, but was difficult to write material for a Lhiannan and Christian Salubri Warrior as my main players in 730AD. They seemed to really like it, though. I was trying to create prophecy that would entice them both and I think I succeeded.

    Since the COVID lockdown thing started, I put my dark ages game on hold and decided to do a retro Sabbat V20 game set in 1991 I've been running on the interwebz. This game I've taken a different approach as a storyteller and presented the group with goals in a city with characters and locations, but left where to go and what to do from there up to them. Some of the players seem to love it, others seem confused. I've more just been providing setting and characters with an eventual goal that needs to be reached, but they need to figure out how to accomplish that on their own.

    I think it's the player who's more familiar with table top gaming from D&D that's confused. They kind of expect to be given the quest by a DM with clear steps to get to through it, but this game I've been taking more of an approach like "you want to kill some Tremere in the city you just arrived in, where do you start looking?" sort of style. I feel like in a small city they just lucked out because one of them happened to decide to check the university in town and happened to stumble across the Tremere there. In an earlier town they immediately got into a bar fight with some bikers and learned the lesson that when mortals outnumber you, things can go really wrong. Not wanting to kill 2 of them on the first session, I had one of the other players show up a little ways into the fight that had gone off on his own. By pure chance they decided to capture the bartender to bring back to the motel as food for their pack priest and she was able to inform them the bartender was a ghoul (she's a Tzimisce and had enough Auspex to see that). The priest and another pack member dragged him out to the desert to extract information from him. So I kind of feel like they've only been solving their goals by pure luck in 2 out of 3 of the stories.

    I think I'm going to have their new Bishop tell them to go see an Assamite Antitribu they know and and give them the rundown on stalking elders. I think the players that are liking the extra freedom are more the ones that I had in earlier stories running down a more set path. I'm hoping if I have this more experienced character give them a crash course in crusades and tracking and diablerizing elders that might improve their experience without having to take a firmer hand and spell things out to them. Most of the players aren't that experienced with Vampire and the lore of the world, so that's probably a contributing factor. By now, at least 3/4 of them have played in previous games I've run, so they're getting better. The problem is those were way earlier in time, so they're not as up on the modern factions outside of the video games.

    They seemed to dig the last story that was more directed, where I revealed their Pack Ductus was a Diabolist and tortured them all with the Sabbat Inquisition, then sent them on a Wild Hunt after their Ductus. I've now elevated a player to the the pack Ductus, so he's now in charge. Their pack priest is still an NPC I'm controlling, for now. They've been in TJ, so they've had to talk to their new Bishop and she's set some level of direction and given them some possible quests in a few different cities, but all the ones I've planned are back to more of that more vague "you have a goal, how do you accomplish it" style.

    So from both a player and storyteller's point of view, which type of story do you prefer? A more directed approach or something more free form with lots of wiggle room for the players?

      I've been a Storyteller pretty exclusively for the last 22 years, so I'll stick with talking about that point of view.

      I'm a huge fan of what I call "web sandbox" play. (I am not in any way the only person who uses this technique... in fact, I was just talking with some people in a gaming group on Facebook about it a few days ago. Nor do I imagine that I'm the first one to develop it, although I think a lot of full-time DMs/GMs/STs will eventually come around to it after doing enough worldbuilding.)

      The basic idea is this: the world is richly planned out ahead of time, and every NPC has their own sets of plans and goals. When you sit down to play, you have a handful -- maybe 5 or 6 -- major storylines ready to go. You also do enough prep work with your players so that they have plans and goals as well. The first session or two you run something episodic (like TV shows in the 80s) -- something a little directed and even railroadish. But that's just to get the ball rolling. In the course of that session or two, you drop all the hints, rumors, clues, and so forth for all of your major plotlines. You give the players more to do than they could possibly accomplish all at once, and you let them decide what they are going to take an interest in and what they aren't.

      To that extent, they're just in a sandbox and they can do whatever they want. If they throw you curves and ignore all your storylines, you retreat from the session and draft a new plotline about what it is they've decided to get up to. But the other storylines don't go away. The key to making this work is that if the players DON'T engage with a particular storyline... it keeps going. And ramifications spill over into the other storylines.

      Right about the time the players are completing their first big storyline (which may be broken up by some episodic filler), you draft up two more big storylines and throw those in the mix.

      It's a hell of a lot of homework for the GM. A hell of a lot.

      But it makes for a great game.

                          To have a sandbox style play or freeform play, you need players that are proactive. Proactive players have PCs who have goals, interests that they will follow up on if the GM provides hooks if the setting, and are looking for alternate solutions to a problem than just the one a GM may have prepared.

                          I think WoD games are more in need of that because unlike D&D which has dungeon crawls and a specific place to explore, WoD tend to be more about exploring the world setting in general.

                          However, some players aren't naturally proactive. Other players can be, but may not be familiar enough with the setting or rules to be comfortable about it.

                          I would speak to those players directly who are having trouble navigating the setting. They'll likely tell you why they are having difficulty knowing what to do. Often they are concerned about doing the "wrong" thing, and therefore "fail" or be "punished" or are worried about their characters getting killed. You may want to run some Preludes for them that helps establish parts of the setting through play (if they are unfamiliar with them), or run them through some scenarios where they get to make any decision they want, and see how it goes. For others, you may want to talk with them on their individual character goals, and help them come up with ideas on how they'll want to accomplish it.

                          Make it explicit that there won't be quest givers most of the time, and those that do may be doing so for ulterior motives and shouldn't be trusted. And that it is appropriate at times to ignore or avoid such quests (depending on your style of GMing).

                                  I do ST, but at the moment I am a player.

                                  I prefer to have a directed approach to begin with, than to have a number of suggested paths, than free roaming, than back to a scripted end that ties everything up in a nice ribbon.

                                  If you have a new ST, Co Players, City, Game

                                  1. I have to find a voice for my Character first. - The stuff I write up is good, but it will change in play.
                                  2. I have to develop a feeling for what my options are. - Social interaction, Down time, of screen, Combat, Weird stuff that is not covered by the rules, PVP, etc.
                                  3. I have to see what my ST allows and what he punishes. - Vampire can feel very punishing. - If I am not on the same page as the ST, any social encounter/situation can go south whatever I wanted to portray.
                                  4. See what is fun at the table and fitting to the story.

                                          Originally posted by CajunKhan View Post
                                          Directed. If it's not directed, then players tend to just quickly build empires, even in games where they aren't playing creatures with mind-control powers.

                                          To quote The Gamers: Dorkness Rising: "if I don't keep them focused on the story, they're just going to run around looting, killing, and impregnating my entire world"
                                          I understand that. If the world ONLY reacts to them, then every NPC is passive, and the PCs are the only ones with agency. That allows them to do a lot of things unopposed, or at least always grants them the tactical advantage because they are the only ones to select the time and place of battle. Even in a sandbox style game, the GM needs the antagonists to do something.

                                          In terms of how I run the game, this is what I do as ST.

                                          First, I always have some chronicle tentpole - some big secret or upcoming event for the PCs to uncover or deal with. However, as I believe in PC choice, they can interact with it however they like. This is how I viewed the feud between Menele and Helena in Chicago By Night. I must admit that in the three Chicago based chronicles I played in as a PC, I and my gaming group never once encountered them (or if we did, it went over our heads). We were too busy doing other things (and I think the ST wasn't really clicking with the Methusaleh plots). So I'm biased that it is OK to not interact with the tentpole. It's not like D&D which is a heavy exploration based game, and the decision to not enter the dungeon effectively eliminates the game. I think in WoD, it is OK to avoid the tentpole depending on the nature of the tentpole. (Some tentpoles may not be possible to be avoided - like a Sabbat Crusade invading your city). Sometimes the tentpole is strong (prominent in the setting and unavoidable), sometimes it is hidden and needs to be uncovered (like the feud between Menele and Helena, which is quite active but well hidden), and sometimes it can be weak (the Prince is paranoid and wants to hold onto power, or there is a pack of Archons or even a Justicar in the city observing things). But it's something extra that helps define that specific chronicle.

                                          Second, I do run some preplanned events. This is based on the idea that the PCs are not the only ones doing things, so eventually someone else's plan or action will impact them. This is typical of a GM event or directed plot. I don't hit PCs with this all the time, but it is a regular occurrence. Because I like the passage of time to occur. I typically plan for one such event per "season" in game. Not per session, but every few months in game time.

                                          Third, I run stories based on the PC's Backgrounds and back stories. The people they know - Retainers, Allies, Contacts, others - are doing things of their own, or interact with the PC in their own way. Much of an individual PCs' "story" is based on this, not the ST driven events.

                                          Fourth, I have a sandbox of a setting. And while I don't detail everything out, I at least have a general sense of what is out there. Unlike preplanned events which hit the PC, elements in a Sandbox don't come up in game unless the PCs stumble upon them - either intentionally because they are looking for them (or something like them) or unintentionally. Once it looks like the PCs will be running into one of these, I do more work to create more detail. And even if the PCs never encounter these sandbox elements, sometimes they are the inspiration for a future preplanned event.
