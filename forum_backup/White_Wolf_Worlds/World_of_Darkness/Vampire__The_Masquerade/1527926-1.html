    Originally posted by Theodrim View Post
    It's not a "completely new" discipline, if the player already knows it. This is what the language means: to learn a completely new discipline, is to buy the first dot.
    A Discipline would be completely new to a player up to the point of gaining the first dot in it, even during character creation, but there is further verbiage on that page that makes me inclined to concede the point. I had been basing my position on page 231's mention of Vitae requirements to learn non-clan Disciplines in conjunction with the fact that honing in on a predator type is clearly framed as a learning process despite removing the experience costs, but 244 also provides a clear elaboration on these rules and when it says "to later learn a completely new Discipline", I won't try to pretend that there's a reasonable interpretation of "later" that encompasses character creation.

    What you're missing is when you increase a discipline level, you're not just getting a new power. The powers you already have now function at the new level. The order in which you buy them doesn't matter, save for prerequisites.
    I'm not sure what part of outright referencing scaling benefits from Discipline powers in my earlier response suggests that I'm 'missing' this dynamic, and I even agree that the order in which they're purchased doesn't matter as far as the progression of scaling goes. This lack of difference is precisely the reason why there are, as I mentioned, obvious incentives to get them as early as possible while they're still relatively cheap as far as the experience costs are concerned. The mere fact that a level one power, for example, scales with each dot that you subsequently gain in the Discipline isn't the same as establishing a reasonable basis for cost/value-equivalence between it and a level three power; Dread Gaze is generally going to have a more dramatic impact than Daunt even if the latter is scaled to a +3, and there's no way these trends with higher level Discipline powers don't factor prominently into the rationale for progressively increasing the cost for dots in Disciplines.

    But even if I accepted the questionable premise that generalized scaling of powers per Discipline dot was a meaningful value-equalizer irrespective of the native levels of powers chosen at any point in a Discipline's progression, this premise still neglects to consider powers that don't scale at all. I'll throw out a few examples in case someone wonders whether this is a rare and insignificant outlier, and when I say they don't scale, I mean that they have no Discipline-based dice pools (ones marked with an asterisk have Attribute + Skill dice pools), static or no bonuses, and no other rules which change based on one's number of dots in the Discipline (including modifying other powers that do scale):

    Animal Succulence, Blink*, Bond Famulus*, Cat's Grace, Corrosive Vitae, Oblivion Sight, Rapid Reflexes, Seal the Beast's Maw, Shadow Cloak, Slavish Devotion, Touch of Oblivion, The Binding Fetter, The Eyes of Beasts, Traversal*, Uncanny Grip, Unerring Aim, and every single Protean power in the core book.

    You obviously can't count on scaling to pick up the slack in situations where no chosen powers scale or most don't, but I'd question the ability for it to do so even in more common situations where only two out of five of all chosen powers don't scale. Even a single non-scaling level one power purchased at higher levels like 3-5 (or 4-5 for a level two power) would seriously throw off the balance for scaling-based compensations given the dramatic cost differences. The only way to mitigate this is to deliberately limit powers available at certain levels and/or make them minor or niche enough that the value dips to be closer to lower-level powers, but, again, this creates conflicts with the impact/value expectation from higher level Discipline powers, whether from players or generalized design rationales for their costs.

    I'll take your word for it where the general utility of Melpominee is concerned, but that still doesn't make it a necessary purchase to functionally replicate levels 1-5 to Dementation; at best it's an added perk while chasing after higher bonuses from Daunt, but Specialties, Merits, and Loresheet Advantages, or simply increasing Intimidation itself, are much cheaper ways to boost Intimidation and no amount of Presence after the first dot does anything to change the Difficulty of the secondary effect from Daunt.

    And, with the explicit design goal of eliminating clans as pseudo-classes. What you identify as "key features" of one clan or another simply no longer exist, and that's by design to grant players more freedom within the constraint of a single clan, or to play a given character archetype as one of several different clans. Even blood sorcery was reduced to three discipline sub-trees (counting koldunism now that Blood Sigils is out), and given as a discipline inherent to two clans who can pick and choose from those sub-trees as they see fit.
    Unless I interpret this response as coming from a position that any affinities and challenges in accessing Disciplines or other perks based on clan is some sort of regression into the bad old days of clans as class equivalents, this reads as a non-sequitur - or perhaps even an attempt to resort to false dichotomies rather than engage with what I've actually said, but I'll give you the benefit of the doubt.

    If that is your position, then even setting aside my obvious disagreement, I'd have to have to wonder about what the various ways in which other design decisions - Clan Disciplines, amalgam powers that aren't as easy to access to Chimerstry, clan-based Merits and Loresheets, and arguably Clan Banes/Compulsions - undercut the achievement of this goal throughout the history of this edition say about the designer's commitment to it. After all, these and other clan-specific factors incentivize certain playstyles and introduce significant added challenges/costs to others, much like a 'pseudo-class' would.

    That only holds true if you view dementation exclusively as a vehicle for damaging willpower, and forcing derangements (now compulsions) on targets. Otherwise, that could not possibly be further from the truth: dementation level 1 is about inflaming or suppressing emotions, level 2 literally forces hallucinations on a target, and level 3 grants visions. What I described is the analog to older-edition dementation: presence is there to replicate passion (not just to meet chimerstry requisites), chimerstry and fata morgana are the analog to haunting, and active-use premonition is literally just eyes of chaos.
    This only makes sense as a counter to what I've said if you think that I said the Dementation amalgam covers every single aspect of Dementation, which is a very strange way to read: "Chimerstry and Fata Morgana aren't necessary for Dementation 1-5 at all: the Dementation amalgam power itself already covers the encroaching delusions and loss of control aspects of the Discipline."

    I guess I'll break it down in case there's any lingering confusion: Chimerstry and Fata Morgana aren't necessary for Dementation 1-5 at all, because Dementation as an amalgam already covers particular aspects of the older Dementation Discipline that pertain to breaks from reality as an explanation for its effects (not the only explanation, but certainly one of them). This is not me saying that I think every other power that you mentioned can be ignored if one wishes to replicate the old Discipline's characteristics to any reasonably comprehensive extent. At most, I might suggest that Dementation can also cover the same conceptual ground as Passion insofar as Compulsions represent a range of intensified emotions - by that standard, Daunt's not necessary as a stand-in for Passion, or arguably even sufficient given that the controlled intensification of fear is a far cry from the uncontrolled fluctuations in emotion that Passion represents, but it's a painless addition.

    Again, you're missing the point. Clans and characters in v5, by design, are no longer defined by a single discipline or powers within that discipline.
    This is basically a retread of your earlier confusion over my position and I addressed it above, but I think there's some value in introducing a distinction here: clans and players aren't entirely defined by Disciplines, not that they ever were in prior editions either, but signature supernatural powers and affinities that they have, whether in the form of particular Discipline amagalms, Loresheet perks, or clan-specific Merits, play a notable part in their identity. This remains true in V5 by design as well, and fully opening up Chimerstry to the Ministry with no strings attached undercuts this, leading to my mixed feelings about it.

    You build based on the totality of discipline powers available, to create a single, holistic theme. Choices that are suboptimal or not pragmatic in the white room, can and do have strong uses within that theme -- it just depends what the theme is, and how well individual powers work within that remit.
    I don't doubt that players and NPCs can have standout moments with particular Discipline powers even if they're less powerful or versatile than others, but that's not an effective counter to my position that the limits on Discipline choice in conjunction with substantial differences between powers where general efficacy is concerned strongly incentivize choosing the more broadly useful and effective powers over others, and thus less functional distinction between clans/characters who share these Discipline (which is my criterion for regarding the Ministry's situation as being worse under this Discipline's setup). The only way that it would be is if these moments are so numerous, so substantial, and so applicable to various seemingly niche choices across the board that there is in fact no such incentive or it is diminished to the point that its consequences are insignificant.

    Your Nagaraja example doesn't suffice to establish that, even if it is a neat build with interesting applications.

                        While I understand why people associate Serpentis with Protean, I am partial to interpreting it more as a form of Blood Magic. A friend of mine explained to me once that the fifth rank was clearly based on Egyptian magic, and that got me thinking along those lines. Its higher level powers of corruption clearly have nothing to do with shapechanging. So rather than define it by its shapechanging powers and ignore everything else, I just think it makes better sense to interpret them as also being part of this "magical tradition". Serpentis just seems to have been powers thrown together in order to create Thoth Amon (Thulsa Doom in the 1982 movie).

                        So in my chronicles, I just made Blood Magic one of their in-clan disciplines, gave the name "Path of the Wyrm's Blessing" as the main path (Serpentis), and assigned them several other existing Paths as part of the magical canon they could learn - Path of Corruption from the Players Guide (it works better for Setites than for Tremere, though I make it known to secret Order of the Wyrm within the Tremere) plus all the other Paths called Akhu created for the sourcebook Blood Magic: Secrets of Thaumaturgy. Once the various Akhu paths were added, it cemented the idea to just treat the Serpentis powers as a Blood Magic Path.
