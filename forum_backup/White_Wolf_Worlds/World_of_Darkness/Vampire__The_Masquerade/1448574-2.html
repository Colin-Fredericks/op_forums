    I don't think original VtR touchstones are supposed to be integrated into the story. Rather, the vampire consorts with them in spite of their night to night business, which gives them a human perspective on existence and their condition, instead of doing vampire things and thinking vampire thoughts every waking hour. They remind them that they're more than what their hunger and kindred society demands they be.

    VtM is different from that, because its vampires don't have to address human needs to remain as functional beings; they just have to establish a set of principles and commit to them. A graveyard, where a Hecata probably works anyway, isn't likely to ground them in the way that a person can, but it could help them focus on what they aspire to be.

                As a rough and weird way to think about Touchstones, their akin to Home Improvement's Mr. Wilson in a way-You get into the action and mess of being a vampire, and then you have this person who may not ever be involved directly with the plot, but has an essential perspective to how your struggle, and in particular an element that's been/is now missing in your life*, and the interactions you have with them are primarily to help you reorganize your points of value and perspective on the problems. There's a lot of other narratives that include this sort of dynamic and characters for this person-a end-of-second-act-, beginning-of-third-act perspective bringer who allows you to confront both the conflict and self with the thematic focus.

                Where this breaks down, of course, is also in the interaction-Wilson knew pretty much everything going on in TIm's life, where as things an get lost if a Touchstone is brought too much into a vampires primary life.

                Regardless, touchstones are both there for downbeat scenes (sequels, if we wanna use the technical term) to have your character reorient, and for ramping up tension(any scene risking the touchstone inherently raises the stakes).

                *In the case of Tim, we could summarize it as "a more positive masculinity"-empathy, patience, nuance, humility, etc., in the case of touchstones, it's Humanity (and what that means to both the touchstone and the vampire).

                                        I feel v5 does too much to shift vampires in the direction of analogues for a persecuted, mentally ill minority. (persecuted; The transition of focus towards the SI and thus big goverment as a threat. Mentally ill: Messy criticals) You might like that if you fit the bill or you have a very specific idea of vampires, but for a neurotypical individual with fair social skills, or perhaps someone who liked the idea of vampires being suave political predators, it's a bit frustrating to be shoehorned into this narrow scope of what V5 thinks a vampire should be; Touchstones are just fucking weird. No healthy mind affixes their own convictions to a few individuals, and loses their grasp on their convictions when that person changes or is hurt. How can I play a self-assured political predator when I need my romanticised Bella swans? How can I run a satire of monsters when everyone needs a human to keep them human?

                                        Part of what made Vampire great is that you could play any kind of Vampire. You can play a crazy vampire, a sane vampire, a sexy vampire, a hideous vampire, someone who cares or someone who does not, A saint or a sinner.. In V5 I absolutely have to be maladjusted vampire, a creep, a weirdo, "what the hell am I doing here" kind of vampire; it's mechanically ordained and gilded by fluff: I have to fear messy criticals, I have to fear someone harming the fellow I need to stalk, only the golden rule can save me from this damnation, but how much do we want to rely on that golden rule eh?

                                                I'm curious about something regarding experiences:

                                                Something I was thinking about when coming up with examples of Touchstones in VtM and VtR games I've been in before the Touchstone concept was even introduced lead me to a realization. The vast majority of examples that really clicked were games where there was significant time playing pre-Embrace. Whether just extended preludes, or even really playing mortals for awhile before the PCs got Embraced, humans that they fledgling vampires cared about were abundant, and already well established characters in the game.... because they were NPCs that were already part of the story, ones the PCs had already formed bonds with, and ones the players had already developed interest in keeping around.

                                                The other examples that came to mind were ones players brought to the table and were already planning on having via Backgrounds or whatnot anyway.

                                                I think this hits on a weakness in human touchstones, and somewhat why VtR 2e's approach tends to sit better: When you have to make them, they don't feel organic. They feel tacked on. You're not making a big deal about your college buddy, or your mortal family, or whatever because you wanted them specifically, but because you had to come up with some ideas and you're looking for things to hook them into. In VtR 2e, yeah you have to define one Touchstone, but more (and more is good to have) is something you can just let happen in-game. Whether your character is looking for them, or happens across them, that organic experience makes them more appealing. When V5 basically says, "make 3, that's what you get," I think it can rub people the wrong way because they don't get the real chance to develop why those Touchstones exist by playing their characters.

                                                Do people feel like that might be part of the issue?

                                                            I'd say its something that could be useful for some characters but not others, so it would always be case by case. I always think of Dexter and the Code of Harry when I think of this - how he had certain 'rules' he was conditioned to follow to survive and thrive and remain undetected but also to allow him to indulge his nature. In a sense the Code is meant to keep Dexter functional in human society and 'in touch' with his human side. Now while the Code itself is more a 'thing', Harry the person was a significant influence on Dexter and keeping him 'human' so in a sense would be a touchstone. Likewise to be successfully functional in human society interacting with other people (and having connections) is part of staying 'in touch' with the mortal realm. And this is going to sound 'odd' (and perhaps reinforce that idea that 'mental illness' thing someone suggested) but remember that all humans have to be conditioned into certain behaviors, accepted norms and practices, etc. and 'Normal' tends to be defined in those terms. But humans don't really fit into nice neat boxes and many of us resist being pigeonholed in that way so may resist society's pressures to 'normalize' - we're just talking about learned behaviors and the ability (and method) by which people assimilate them. Hell, growing up *I* had to learn different ways to 'learn' because I don't learn like 'normal' people and I struggle in 'normal' life. Doesn't mean I can't function but it required a different approach to what I suppose you can say is 'being human' and yet I can be as normal as most people. In many ways I can function better (I'm more aware of the process for one thing - most people won't think about it because its part of being 'normal'.)

                                                            But as I said everyone is different and the methods by which they learn and adapt will also differ (What works for one won't work for another) and likewise the 'touchstones' that are effective (if any) would also differ. Heck, maybe touchstones aren't the best approach for some characters and something else should be employed. As long as it serves the same purpose it's all good.
