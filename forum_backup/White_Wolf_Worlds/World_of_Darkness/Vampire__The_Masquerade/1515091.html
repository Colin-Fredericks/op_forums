    Drama or Realism?

    06-15-2023, 06:54 AM
    Because there are some things in VtM where you may go "wait, that's stupid" or "how the fuck does that work??"
    But on the other hand, instability and high emotion can lead to fun stories that are worth the suspension of disbelief. Or simply because you think tears of blood is cool or something.

    Like, my limit is vampire "biology". Their internal organs shrivel up and are useless and they have no body temperature to maintain in order to protect the nonexistent function of their dead organs, but for some reason they sweat. Blood.
    There is a sort of narrative purpose in this (when you're stressed or scared the scent of your blood rouses the beast in the vampires around you, alerting them to your fear and making them view you as prey. But it's too silly for me. They can filter blood well enough to produce ocular fluid and saliva, but their useless glands, which clearly serve no purpose other than to sabotage them, weep blood. M'kay. I don't care if it's a curse, this is silly. Tears are a thing along similar themes and let's not even get into the other body fluids replaced with blood (I mean, r e a l l y?))

    Meanwhile, I don't care how implausible my political drama is, so long as it's entertaining.

    Are there places where your suspension of belief strains or even snaps in VtM as canon depicts it? And what do you do with those points, alter 'em or ignore them?

      I would have to say my biggest pet peeve with the game is the core design of vampire society, it isn't designed to function, but instead it is designed to promote story hooks.

      Every vampire needs to feed, but for some reason the default is always inferred to be hunting, rather than keeping a herd. It doesn't matter if the kept mortals are willing, enslaved, ghouled, or whatever, from a security standpoint it is just too risky to be constantly hunting.

      Theoretically all vampires are descended from Caine, and his civilization. Yet its not like there is a vampire language which every vampire has to learn once turned. There isn't a shared culture to vampire society, the closest you get is the Traditions, but when you consider the implications of those Traditions, the factions of the Camarilla, Sabbat, and Anarchs would never have arisen if they were actually being enforced on cultural level.

      Even the idea of a city being home to multiple clans/bloodlines is counter intuitive because that just creates infighting and power struggles. If each city was controlled by a given clan exclusively then the power structure would be far more functional. This becomes very important when the idea that shifters are constantly circling the cities trying to keep the vampires contained and occasionally doing raids in an attempt to purge the vampires. Whose is going to be a Harpy when you have guard duty to keep an eye out for a shifter raid?

      Perhaps most egregious of all is the vampire who is more than 50 years old and yet still behaves like a teenager or cartoon villain. Anyone who is that stupid won't survive long in the world of darkness setting. If just a couple years of war changes a person into a hardened survivor, how can a vampire who has survived for centuries place any importance on things as useless as fashion or courtly intrigues of who is sleeping with who? To a certain degree this issue was fixed in Kindred of the East but somehow even with new editions of VtM, centuries old vampires are still behaving like the Lost Boys coterie.

      Lastly there is a complete disconnect between the mechanics of the embrace and the affect it would have on the vampire population demographics. If the setting of WoD is as cut throat and lethal as it is made out to be, then when given the choice between embracing a hyper competent mortal or a random mortal who is hot, no one could waste the resources or time to embrace and raise a neonate who didn't already carry their own weight but give a benefit in addition to that.

      When given the choice between a special forces soldier, a top tier scientist, a fortune 500 company owner, or a random highschooler who lacks experience in anything, why would you ever choose the highschooler except for contrived story reasons or for player options?

          Realism. If it isn't magical, it obeys the sciences
          I try to keep things as close to as-written as I can but there's a few times I can't. Of course, reality is often stranger than fiction, so I don't think I lack drama.

          Vampire society is functional-enough. Leaders have a strong tendency towards competence and vampires, despite often being greedy and treacherous, usually have a sense of responsibility to their community.
          Vampire fluids aren't all blood because that's silly. Many Vampires are pale but don't exaggerate it and make them all goths. Blush of life is very common, especially as it's the default in folklore, and because if feeding from sleeping people was the only viable option things would be boring: more Casanovas please.

          Vampires just not burning from sunlight is also from folklore, and such a feature would make the masquerade easier, but I think it's a necessary balance feature so I keep it.

          I make a lot of small but significant changes to disciplines to make the masquerade easier.

          Obfuscate 1 doesn't make you invisible but makes you and your actions less memorable. You can roll your Obfuscate rating against any investigation against you. Eye witnesses to your violent crimes will remember things wrong, an often reported phenomenon when it comes to high stress scenarios.

          Potence allows you to safely knock people out.
          Fortitude protects your clothes when convenient
          Presence actually has a table to dictate how much it effects a person.
          Thaumaturgy concerning technology is more developed. The Tremere even have a few thaumaturgically advanced AIs that'll scour the internet looking for blackmail incriminating materials while posting red herrings.

                                              You only need enough "realism" to help your game table maintain their suspension of disbelief. People don't question the basic assumptions of any setting they play in (in a supernatural horror setting like the World of Darkness, people know that magic and the supernatural are "real"). Because magic isn't real, there is some arbitrariness of what works in the setting. But the sourcebooks generally lay out the mechanics, and players therefore accept that is how it is in this setting or that one, provided it does not lead to outlandish conclusions that break their immersion.

                                              There is also an issue of the age demographic of the genre. Something written for children or adolescents does not need the same level of realism as writing aimed for an adult audience. Problems there only happen when the audience of the age demographic grows up, yet expects the genre to grow up with them instead of remaining where it was. This is a big problems today for the American comic book superhero genre - something originally aimed at 12 year olds - with all the sophistication required to satisfy the expectations of a 12 year old - is now expected to be "real" for an audience that expects something more appropriate for an adult audience. It undermines the setting though since those worlds were originally written for children. It's not that you cannot explore a world where people somehow get super powers and target it to an adult audience. It's just that it will not be the core Marvel and DC settings, just like Sgt Rock or The Howling Commandos will not be the same as The Thin Red Line or All Quiet on the Western Front.

                                              There are some players, GMs, and sourcebook writers who really like to detail things past the usual need of the gaming table. This is generally stuff that is mostly flavor text. When they do, they can cause issues because other players, GMs, writers, etc. have different visions of the same fictional concepts. One is not any more "realistic" than the other. It is just a preference, a taste, a subjective opinion.

                                              Usually by "realism", many people mean that the characters behave "realistically" given the setting. When established characters suddenly stop being portrayed they were before, it gets confusing. When characters make decisions based on the plot needs of the story writer rather than how they've been portrayed, that isn't "realistic".

                                              The other point is when something new gets introduced to a setting that fundamentally alters it in some way, dictating major changes yet the setting and the people in it don't change to reflect that.

                                                  Originally posted by Thoth View Post
                                                  Even the idea of a city being home to multiple clans/bloodlines is counter intuitive because that just creates infighting and power struggles.                                                   Curiously enough, the original setting was one where a city being home to multiple clans was a very recent phenomenon. This detail is largely forgotten these days, for several reasons. Perhaps chief among them is that it directly contradicts the entire Dark Ages game line. The early core books had the following to say about historical vampire populations (emphases mine; note that when the text talks about "hunters," it means vampires as predators, not mortal vampire hunters):

                                                  Originally posted by Vampire: The Masquerade 2nd Edition, page 31
                                                  Only so many hunters can be supported by one herd. In years past only one Kindred lived within each city and each could claim it as her own domain. When cities were small, being a race of loners was an effective survival tactic. However, as cities have grown, so has the population of hunters. Now it is no longer rare for many to live within the same domain.
                                                  Personally, I like this historical background for the modern-nights game more than I like the "vampire society is eternal and static" model that the later editions have adopted. The original game proposed a setting where Kindred culture had gone through a seismic (and destabilizing) shift in recent history, roughly corresponding with the Industrial Revolution in the mortal world. This has vast implications for many core tenets of the game. Does it even make sense to talk about "clans" as meaningful entities before the modern period? Perhaps the idea of vampires identifying with "clans" is as recent as the Camarilla and Sabbat themselves.

                                                  However, like I suggested above, adopting this historical background for the game kind of makes it necessary to... give up on the Dark Ages game line as a whole. The published version of the medieval setting simply falls apart if you accept the premise that all vampires across the globe used to be solitary predators, and each city was the domain of just one vampire (and maybe his or her immediate childer). I'm not sure if it's possible to construct a playable setting out of that premise, but it's interesting to think about.​

What exactly does a Prince do if things like domain, progeny, accounting, and destruction is all in the hands of the sire or eldest of the line? What percentage of childer should be blood bonded to their sire compared to the norm in the modern age? Shouldn't we be seeing more Castle Dracula style havens with a small number of vampires in a more rural setting? Shouldn't the center of vampire "civilization" be the Islamic world with its older and larger cities than the relatively backward Europe? How does this impact the vampire rivalry with the Lupines? How can they avoid detection or attention or dissuade attacks on them?                                                           Part of the problem is that "retcon ravioli" is pervasive in the WoD setting.

                                                          For instance, Rasputin was canonically stated as being a Malkavian, a Follower of Set, a Ventrue, or a Nosferatu. The other game lines also claimed him as being a Shadowlord werewolf, a Cultist of Ecstasy mage, and Puppeteer wraith. So having Cappadocious call a clan meeting to get around 12,000 in attendance and still have not everyone show up is less than iron clad. Don't get me wrong, having unreliable narrators and information distorted by retellings over time make for good story fodder, but it sucks for world building.

                                                          Personally I do run with the section from the 3rd edition/revised Storytellers Handbook
                                                          "How many vampires are there, anyway?

                                                          From a strictly Cainite point of view, probably around 40,000. The Middle Kingdom has roughly 20,000 Kuei-jin, for a grand total of approximately 60,000 – one for every 100,000 people on the planet.

                                                          Of those Kindred, the clans make up the enormous majority of the population, leaving the rare bloodlines, fallen clans and other, less-quantifiable vampires at about 2,000 vampires in total. Cities aren't likely to have more than one or two Harbingers of Skulls or Gargoyles, if they have any at all. In fact, most cities don't have any Kindred other than those of the clans – and that's precisely how the clans like it. It is, after all, better to deal with the devil you know.…​"
                                                          Assuming 13 clans divided into 38,000 vampires, that only works out to less than 3,000 per clan, with the remaining bloodlines being the aforementioned 2,000 vampires. But here is the kicker, that is using relatively modern population ratios.

                                                          In 1800 the world population was estimated to be about 1 Billion mortals, which would mean a vampire population of 10,000 total (cainites & kuei-jin). That would in turn mean that roughly each of the 13 clans only had roughly about 500 members and that covered the ones awake, in torpor, in different planes of existence. Now divide that number down by how many cities were over 100,000 and the number of places with vampires drops to a handful of locations.

                                                          With those sorts of numbers, a single coterie ran a city state or a small kingdom or country with the only conflict coming from a rival coterie or other supernaturals. But once again that is only using 1800s era population calculations, so good luck applying the ratios to the world back in the 1500s or the actual Dark Ages.
