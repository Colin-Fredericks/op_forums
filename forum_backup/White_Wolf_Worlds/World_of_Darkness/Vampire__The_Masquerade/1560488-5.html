                            While I appreciate this is not solely a matter of personal choice, I think you're letting your flavor of neuro-spicy cause friction that you don't need to struggle with.

                            TTRPG books are not written to tell you a story. They're written to facilitate you and your group telling your own stories. Premade settings are a tool for your group to use. The intent of a premade setting, metaplot, and so on is to inspire you to have fun. The creators' intent isn't to communicate an explicit "truth" you have to follow, their intent is to plant seeds that you'll tend to and grow as you see fit. Having a radically different head-canon for your games than what the devs and authors were thinking is not a lack of understanding... it's part of their intention for the setting. They know perfectly well people are going to have different interpretations and they intended to encourage it with enough structure that the game doesn't fall apart as long as a given interpretation does get too far from the base conceits of the game(s).

                            It feels strange to me for you to point to the CofD as it's a far less spelled out setting than the WoD. The CofD might not lean as hard on unreliable narrators, but the authorial voices of the games are still bent heavily towards "here's three to five different possible ideas of why this might be the case, none of them are officially The Reason, and you're free to make up your own." So the setting is just as open to personal takes as the WoD is. At best what the CofD accomplishes is communicate that intent better.

                            To repeat myself a bit, the CofD was created by WoD creators. Yes, that does means they got to make improvements based on their previous experiences, but broadly if you get it when the CofD does something... the odds are you get it when the WoD does it too.

                                            I think it's fair to say what WW said and what it did weren't always the same thing, while it's human nature to spot patterns and try to find answers. Therefore, I think it's perfectly fine to say the WoD often (but not always) wanted things to be ambiguous, while at the same time sticking very closely to one core "mythos" for each game (Noddism in VTM, Gaia in WTA, the Purple Paradigm in MTAs, etc.).

                                            That's why one player can honestly see one mythos as truer than another in practice, while another honestly sees the truth in the ambiguity instead. Both are and were true at different times and under different authors. And that's one of the things I like about the WoD—two people who enjoy it can be enjoying entirely different parts of the game.

                                            With that said, is it perhaps worth bringing it back to the topic of the Sabbat so we don't get too off-track? For instance, I think that subjective dimension is also why the Sabbat is relatively popular both IC and OOC. It also has that element of "people see what they want to see".

                                            Vasantasena likely has no doubt she's fighting for freedom from the elders and sees that as the sect's aim. She was joined by those Malkavians who refused to give up Dementation, seeing it as a useful oracular tool as well as a part of themselves. For those Cainites, it's a check against the abuses of the elders and a commitment to being who or what you truly are.

                                            But a member of the Amici Noctis might see the sect's aim as providing them with power and allowing them to pursue their interests without interference from meddling Ventrue. Many elder Tzimisce see it in the same way. In fact, the elders of both clans only really joined the sect because a) they couldn't bare bending the knee to their ancient rivals and b) because, after the supposed diablerie of their Antediluvians, they knew the anarchs would come after them if they didn't pick the right side in the Anarch Revolt. Gratiani and Lugoj basically committed acts of terrorism, providing perfect propaganda to motivate the anarchs in their clans.

                                            The Black Hand (or Lost Tribe) likely sees the sect as a weapon of vengeance against the Antediluvians. The Knights of the Blood, before they became a pillar of the Ventrue antitribu and the Path of Honourable Accord, saw it as a way to fight the Daeva and wage holy war. They're still fighting the same holy war today, with the Antediluvians as the Daeva and the Camarilla as their agents.

                                            The orthodox Albigensians likely see the sect as the means through which to serve the Demiurge as mankind's jailers. The heretical Cathars likely think they're mankind's liberators through almost identical means. These two groups descend from the Road of Sin, with elements from the individualist, antiheroic Path of the Devil (Via Adversarius), the sadistic Path of Cruelty (Via Crudelitas), and the decadent Path of Pleasure (Via Voluptarius). The Path of Screams (Via Quiritare) obviously gave way to the infernalists who follow the Path of (Evil) Revelations. All of these groups find shelter in the Sabbat as a vehicle for the paths and as a place where their actions won't necessarily be considered grounds for destruction.

                                            The Black Angels and Nihilists have yet another slight variant on dualist/gnostic beliefs that aren't really all that different from the Path of Cathari and the Path of Redemption, and which essentially boil down to them being instruments of Satan (the former) or of divine punishment (the latter), with a bunch of Abyss Mysticism thrown into the mix. The Black Angels' philosophy specifically derives from the Road of the Devil, although the modern Path of Night seems to also borrow from the Road of Heaven and parts of the Road of Sin. The Sabbat gives them ample cover for their actions, especially as they blend in so well with the rest of the Lasombra and members of other paths.

                                            The Shepherds of Caine and the Redeemers are also inheritors of the Road of Heaven, which included the anti-infernalist Order of the Chanticleer (possibly a template for the later Sabbat Inquisition). They seek to understand evil so they can master it and find redemption, and the Sabbat is the only suitable vehicle for that. (There's a common theme of the Sabbat's religious elements being a perfect cover for Gnostic, radical, and even infernalist leanings.)

                                            This means the Sabbat is never without a purpose to someone. And that's probably what helps keep it going. The sect leans so much on the pack mentality that it's designed to keep people riled up. You only need one person in a pack to care about the cause, and they can rile everyone else up. So many of these groups are bound through doctrine (paths, eschatology, Noddism in general), ritual (ritae, the religious practices of paths and the sect as a whole) and blood (Vinculi, rejection of a parent clan, unity as Clanless, banding together for safety) that it causes a chain reaction.

                                            They're also always fighting something (the Camarilla, the Ancients, ideology), so they don't have time to stop and think too hard about all their differences. When they run out of enemies, that tends to be when they turn on each other.
