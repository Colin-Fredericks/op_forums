    Sorcery: a surrogate for the Yearning is possible?

    12-31-2021, 11:49 AM
    According to the Whitewolf fandom wiki:

    In contrast to psychic abilities, sorcery is not an innate ability and has to be learned. As such, it is always a deliberate choice, instead of an accident. Individuals that have taken up the study of sorcery describe themselves driven by something they refer to as the Yearning. This yearning manifests as a hunger for occult secrets and hidden knowledge that every sorcerer shares.


    Here's the question: a person who don't feel the innate hunger for secrets and knowledge but is strongly motivated by:


    - obsession about his own ineluctable mortality coupled by the will to extend his life by any possible means
    - desire for expediency and for a "shortcut" that would put him a step above the normal human beings
    - fear, helpnlessnes and frustration at his condition of common mortal
    - anger and resentment at real or perceived wrongs
    - base greed and lust for power
    - envy toward supernatural creature's might
    - narcissistic love for the self and drive to impose that feeling upon his fellows
    - a bit of almost scientific curiosity

    Could ALL these motivations put together be enough to compensate a lack of innate Yearning?

      Yearning is a narrative thing, not a factual, in-setting thing. And every sorcerer is going to manifest the Yearning before he becomes a sorcerer; but there's some question as to the order of events: do you start with a Yearning, and then demonstrate some of all of the motivations you describe? Or do you start with those motivations and then develop a Yearning? It could go either way.

          This is more a personal interpretation/opinion, but I would also draw a distinction between a "sorcerer" and "someone who has learned some sorcery." For example, I would not be surprised if certain groups (hunters, demon-worshippers, etc.) might teach certain bits of sorcery to their members. In those cases, the sorcery learned will reflect the agenda of the organization or its members, but their knowledge will mostly never expand beyond those specific Paths that serve their immediate goals, because sorcery is just a means, not an end. In contrast, for a true Sorcerer, sorcery is an end unto itself. A true Sorcerer will seek out new Paths, new magical secrets, without requiring any external circumstances to motivate them, actively driven by the hunger for occult secrets, aka the Yearning.

          Since it's hot pop-culture right now, and since I just bought the game for super-cheap on Steam, lol, a good example might be Witchers. They learn a few bits of magic, powerful magic even, but never more than what they can use for their work as monster-hunters. In contrast, Mages dig into the secrets of magic, expanding knowledge in the field through investigation.

          PS The particular motivations are quite relevant, so I'll address the examples you've provided:

          Fear of mortality and danger, greed and lust for power, anger, etc., are good examples of motivators that might push a person to learn some sorcery without being a sorcerer. But they have built-in limits regarding what sorcery would actually interest the individual: a person driven by anger isn't likely to learn healing of protective arts, someone driven by greed probably isn't going to seek out knowledge with intangible benefits. Lust for power is probably the closest to a universal motivation, but even that is going to push you to favour certain Paths over others.

          "Scientific curiosity" kind of borders on distinction-without-a-difference to me; it's still a Yearning, but the person involved is likely to end up an Extraordinary Citizen in the Technocracy, acquiring "techno-sorcery" Paths rather than the more traditional (or Traditional, heh) mystic Paths.

          Laziness and shortcut-seeking is the one example I think I would rule against. If sorcery was that easy and safe, everyone would be learning it. Even with the Technocracy actively trying to suppress it, it would be pretty hard to keep people from passing that knowledge around if any slacker could learn to fly because walking is too much of a bother.

                      The distinction between hedge magic and sorcery is real. In particular, the hedge magic featured in Hunters Hunted 2, Kinfolk, and Ghost Hunters is not the same as the sorcery featured in any of the Sorcerer books. The former three feature dabblers in the arts, who don't have any sort of cohesive theory of magic; just a grab bag of tricks. By contrast, sorcerers are those students of the mystical arts who go above and beyond mere dabbling and do have cohesive frameworks of mystical principles and practices.

                      For the record, I'm mainly referring to X20 products here; though given that the X20 Sorcerer book is a Storyteller's Vault product and thus not official, I'm tending to rely more on the original Sorcerer for the lore. Still, it's worth noting that in all iterations of Sorcerer, you have something to paradigms, practices, and tools, whereas the other examples of hedge magic (HH2, Kinfolk, and Ghost Hunters) do nothing of the sort.

                      Also bear in mind that there isn't a unified hedge magic system: although Paths in each sourcebook share common names, the underlying game systems are often different. And in one case, something that's a Hedge Path in the other books is instead a Psychic Phenomenon.

                              I would note that psychologically, sorcerers are identical to mages. Both are inherently willful and driven (with the arguable exception of some religious devotees like the Celestial Chorus, who actively cultivate humility and thus play against type). The only real difference between a Mage and a sorcerer is that a mage has access to Awakened Magick well a sorcerer has access to sorcerous Paths.

                              Mind you, more can be made of that distinction. M20 states that the Awakening is a realization that the maids has an innate quality that allows him to change the world; and the hedge wizards handbook says that sorcerers are empowered by a connection to an Otherworld — which, in more General terms, boils down to making use of existing power in the world around them rather than using an innate quality of their own. I'm leery of reading too much into that, as there are indications of mages who view their power as being from an external source; and it seems quite reasonable that there could be sorcerers who believe that their power comes from something within themselves, in a manner of speaking; so I tend to view that particular distinction as being more of a tendency than an absolute rule.

                                                  Originally posted by Solomon Draak View Post
                                                  Because sorcery, as is described in the WoD, makes you a god between mortals even at the lowest levels.

                                                  All your considerations are valid from the extremely well informed player perspective, but still don't consider the character perspective.

                                                  Most importantly, you're not understanding the core of the Yearning: it is a way to demonstrate that sorcery is itself a field that requires a lot of talent and effort, and even then with no guarantees.

                                                  You as a player can just buy dots in skills and Resources and say your character is a wealthy master of their craft. You as a person knows that actually achieving that, for the average person, isn't so simple. Sorcery is the exact same thing. You're looking at it as a player, that need only spend points to get dots, but for the character those dots are all the same representative of something you must have a helluva lot talent and effort to get. That's what the Yearning represents.

                                                  It is not your motivation, it is the talent to achieve it. If you're a sorcerer proper, this is the same as saying you're one of the most skilled lawyers or doctors around, the kind of person that can command any price for their service. I'm not talking power, I'm talking ow much talent and effort you need to get there.

                                                  So think through all those considerations by this lens. Becoming a sorcerer isn't a substitute for natural magnetism and experience as an actor and seducer any more than becoming a top model or world-renowned actor is a substitute for talent and learning sorcery. This is not just about your motivation to learn, it is about your effort and talent, as you would put it, as talent itself is a very loose term that don't apply to any of those other fields any more than it applies to sorcery.

                                                  When you talk about substituting the Yearning by some motivation, it is the same thing as substituting the talent to be a star on your field by a motivation. But at the point that someone is so obsessed that they can overcome lack of talent to become successful at something to this degree, isn't this obsession a form of talent on itself? If you have enough of an obsession with your motives to pursue sorcery and have success at it, then this obsession is itself the Yearning. You substituted nothing at all.

                                                  Again, keeping in mind that even knowing that it exists, it works, and how it works is already far, far from a given from an IC perspective, and the people that have this information is already either obsessed by the occult or heavily in contact with it.
