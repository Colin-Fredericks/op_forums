        Don't be afraid to teach via demonstration:
        • Ask them what the character wants to accomplish.
        • Then give them suggestions about which of their Spheres they could use to do so, with some ways that using different Spheres might accomplish the objective in different ways. (Also some info about whether it's vulgar, whether there a witnesses and, if it's vulgar, an idea about how it might be coincidental if done thusly)
        • Followed by a description of the way that a few of their Instruments would allow them to meet their goals. Including whether this is a flat out role or something of a ritual where success are built up over several rolls. Whether they can build the effect (adding successes by subsequent casting rolls) or they are effectively "holding" the effects until they are done with the casting. .
        • THEN start with calculation of Difficulties, deciding what each success is going to mean, etc.

        If you make them a part of the discussion, they are likely to start picking up their part of it and letting you, as ST, know what Spheres they want to use to do X and how it's going to look in game when the character is trying to accomplish that. In many (most) cases, there will always be a give and take between you and the player on setting this up (though it can get pretty fast). That's a part of Mage.

        This isn't just a new player/new ST thing either. Now he's the master-planner of the group.             The stuff about your Euthanatos player, that's awesome! This is the kind of attitude which makes for a very good game.

            Regarding preset stories, I'm not sure I can help you. I tend to make up all the plots I use. I will however say that it should be in some way linked to the characters made by your players.

            The first thing to do is to think up why all these characters are working together or in the same group. You can do this with your players if you like, perhaps they characters already know each other. Alternatively you could do a prelude session where we learn how they came to meet each other, and thus set up all kinds of plothooks.

            Maybe the Verbena Doctor lost his license because the Euthanatos killed someone under his care and got sued for malpractice, perhaps this patient was a respected Hermetic who was known by the OoH guy, and maybe had a day job working with the VA.

            Maybe the reason this guy had to die is because they were a closet infernalist who was trying to summon demons to do stuff.

            This is just an example of how you could tie these players together and give them a few interesting things discuss. Perhaps each one know a small piece about this one person and they all need to rely on one another's knowledge to find out what's really going on.

                I'm glad you handled the problem player of your group. Really, if the player had such a problem with the whole Focus aspect of the game, they weren't going to have fun playing Mage. I find that, with Mage: The Ascension, how you do magick is as important, if not more important, than what you do. It's part of the fun, and makes the players more invested in their characters (as opposed to just seeing them as tools for doing neat magic things unto themselves). And if that player was being so much of a pill, it would only detract from everyone's fun.


                As for what to do plotwise, ask your players what their characters' long term goals are. These goals should be personal to the character, and create fertile ground for stories. As such, just saying they want to increase their Traits is probably not enough; they could conceivably do that without adventuring if they really wanted to. Instead, if they want to increase certain Traits (Spheres, Abilities, Attributes, Backgrounds, etc.), it should be for a purpose beyond just having them.

                So instead of a character wanting to just become a master Martial Artist (which only requires they max that Trait), have them want to become recognized as a master Martial Artist, which may require fighting significant opponents, winning tournaments, or proving their skill to an authority on the subject (like an ancient master). Rather than a character seeking to create a Wonder for its utility alone, have them desire to make something of lasting value to history, or a Wonder that is important to them and their Paradigm (a Chorister who seeks to forge a literal set of the Armor of God, for example). If a character wants loadsofmoney, ask why the character wants it beyond mere financial security (paying for a loved one's medical bills, settling a debt for honor's sake, leaving behind a sizable inheritance for one's children, etc.).

                Or have their goal be wholly character driven, rather than stat driven. Getting revenge on someone. Convincing one's family that your life choices weren't a mistake (double points if the loved one is also an Enemy because of this ideological disagreement). Finding true love. Getting one's community to accept one's Paradigm, making magick easier while there.

                Having the players come up with long term goals will help them get into the heads of their characters, and think of them as more than just game constructs. Get them invested in their characters.

                                Right.
                                Dynamic avatars are rarely consistent I the way they interact with their mage. They could appear as images, sounds, music, vibrations, feelings, urges, voices, commercials on TV...
                                However, they are not random, and will probably manifest in ways appropriate in theme to the mage themselves.
                                Dynamic avatars are agents of change and will constantly push their mage to try and change things up be it ideologically, structurally, or physically, and are almost abstention deficit in their attempts to lead their mage often jumping between several different goals at a time. They get frustrated with mages who try to play it safe and try to stick to the tired and true.

                                Primordial avatars tend to be very ancient avatars and may in fact the remnants or fragments of a great and ancient presence. This could be as an angel, a ancient spirit like Gaia, a mythic figure, or even something more general such as the element of fire, an ancient tree, or simply the Hero with 1,000 faces. These avatars are very consistent in how they appear and are often very personal to the mage themselves.

                                They are cryptic as hell, and have little patience with mages who don't act how they think they should, and are known to resort to extreme measures to get their point across. Some bully their mage, even threatening them with peril, placing them in life threatening situations until he mage finally gets a clue. They are quite formidable and often force their mage to confront the repetitions that takes place throughout history. Showing how past events can hint ate future ones.

                                    The Revised Storyteller's Handbook has an entire chapter devoted to Avatars and Seekings. I'll be summarizing some relevant points, but I recommend checking it out yourself.

                                    Avatars, at their core, are forces that are more or less looking out for their Mage's best interest, but have their own plans and goals in mind. They want the Mage to Ascend, but have their own ideas about how to lead them to it. Some might be indirect, dropping clues here and there. Others might be openly helpful, while others antagonistic. The Avatar is ultimately working for the Mage's own good, but the Mage doesn't necessarily see it that way. Work with the players about what sort of relationship they want with their Avatars, both in how they interact and what the Avatars want to teach the characters.

                                    The Background's rank dictates how heavily the Avatar is involved in the Mage's life, either because of variable power at the Avatar's disposal (a weak Avatar could only do so much), or because of variable investment the Avatar has in the Mage's development (an Avatar could be very powerful, but be very aloof). Regardless, having Avatar 5 means that the characters Avatars are very involved. If they appear in dreams, those dreams will be vivid and frequent. If the Avatar manifests in the flesh, they will make appearances often. These are the sorts of Avatars who show up to talk directly to the Mage, although this is not required.

                                    Dynamic Avatars are devoted to Change, and will encourage the Mage towards dynamic action. They won't necessarily push them towards a consistent course of action from appearance to appearance, but have the Mage running from cause to cause. Their manifestation won't necessarily be consistent either, though it depends on the player's wishes. They could be any number of different forms but with a persistent trait that identifies them (the color yellow, skulls, fire, etc.). Or take the form of a known revolutionary figure, relevant to the Mage's interests. They will try to prevent the character from becoming complacent, even if that means causing trouble for them.

                                    Primordial Avatars, by contrast, are three things: Powerful, Mysterious, and Scary. These beings have been around forever, and embody concepts that are very simple in theory, but complicated in execution. A Primoridal Avatar's goals are usually very specific to the theme it represents, and it usually doesn't have much interest in the Mage aside from getting that theme or message across. Even at lower ranks in the Background, these beings aren't weak, so much as less involved; Primordial Avatars represent primal, potent forces, and even a Mage with Avatar 1 can sense this. A fifth rank Primordial Avatar, therefore, is a force of nature and magick that has a very specific purpose, who has decided to give their Mage as close to their undivided attention as Primordial Avatars ever do. In short, the character's encounters with their Avatar will be Intense. Their Avatar might not be their enemy, but they should still be enigmatic and a little frightening.

                                            As mentioned above the biggest thing Willpower 10 means is that the Mage in question is going to be very difficult to nearly impossible to use mind control on, as well as often having Willpower to spend.

                                            As for Avatar 5 some thoughts, first if they have 5 points there it means they are likely lacking in other spots. While Im not advocating ruthlessly going after background deficiencies I am suggesting not letting them get a free ride one to pay attention to is Node. Having Avatar 5 and being able to spend 5 quintessence a turn is great and all, but is of lesser value when you dont have an easier way of replenishing that. Also I dont see Chantry or Sanctum which means the Mages in question dont have any place where their effects are going to be safely coincidental (barring a few exceptions).

                                            As for what it means regarding the Mage/Avatar relationship, I point to the lines from M20 pg 266
                                            The stronger the Avatar, the greater its influence; one or two dots in Avatar manifest as simple hunches, whereas four- or five dot ones achieve full-blown identities.
                                            A four or five dot Avatar should feel like a separate entity in many ways, often seeming like it has its own desires and goals (even if such things really are taken with the Mage's best interests in mind) and trying to push the Mage toward those desires.

                                            M20 pg 338
                                            Part of the Avatar’s role involves challenge; without challenges, after all, we don’t grow. And so, even the most subtle, agreeable Genius will nag your mage toward new accomplishments and potential failures. A truly strong Avatar (one rated at 4 or 5) can be a terror – a literal demon, perhaps, that drives the mage night and day. Your mage’s Essence determines the sort of challenges it gives you. A Dynamic spirit ignites passions, a Static one demands perfection, a Questing one tugs you to pursue new avenues of experience, and a Primal one presents constant enigmas and puzzles that apparently defy solutions. In many ways, the Avatar is an internal mentor… one that never goes away.
                                            While I'm not saying nag them until they hate having taken the background, do have their Avatars being more vocal than other characters. These potent Avatars will try to push the Mage toward certain actions and deeds.

                                            M20 pg 267
                                            Dynamic - In more subtle forms, this Essence inspires curiosity, impatience, and sudden flashes of brilliance. Appearing in the form of shadows, whispers, and poor impulse control, it goads a mage to treat every day like an adventure. At higher levels, a Dynamic Avatar can nag a person mercilessly, leaving half-finished projects and shattered relationships in the wake of a mage who rarely sits still for long.
                                            Dynamic Avatars want their Mages to be agents of change and to have them take action in the world. Carpe Diem could very easily be a catch phrase of the Dynamic essence. The Dynamic Avatar wants to squeeze the opportunity out of every moment right now. Indolence, sloth, and procrastination are things that will get the Dynamic Avatar up in arms and shouting in the Mage's head.
                                            • Considering the Hermetics background and flaws their Avatar probably comes across like a frat brother or drinking buddy pushing the Mage to enjoy to live each day like its their last. While the Avatar doesnt want to hurt the Mage through their addiction it does feed on the live everyday to the fullest mindset the drugs and gambling represent. Conversely this could mean the Avatar occasionally steers the Mage away from their flaws, especially if it would lead to great danger; after all there are no more moments to seize if the Mage is dead or severely injured but instead of saying no the Avatar is likely to push the Mage to a different action (essentially the Avatar will occasionally be the good friend who takes the keys and makes sure their buddy gets home safe so they can party tomorrow).


                                            Primordial - A Primordial mage loves mystery. Like deep water, she seeps into hidden places and defies easy understanding. Whereas Pattern people are bricks or stone, Primordial folks are riptides and dark pools. The few Technocrats who favor this Essence understand that no science can penetrate the richest mysteries of the universe; they’ll give lip service to technology, but they always keep a few extra cards up their sleeves for the time when the light fails and order becomes a punch line in the cosmic jest.
                                            Primordial Avatars are often some of the most difficult to portray. Generally I view Primordial Avatars appearing various ways, as Lovecraftian entities representing the ancient chaos of the universe, old gods and mythological and religious figures representative of the old ways, or ancestor spirits a direct link to the Mage's past. These Avatars can come across as dark, eerie, sinister, and seductive and influence the Mage to a similar leaning. The Primordial Avatar will push the Mage to remember the old ways and not rely on technology or modern tricks, but rather in their own skills. The Primordial Avatar cautions secrecy and that the Mage should always have a backup plan and offers hidden depths of wisdom and the occasional bit of sagely advice. Essentially Primordial Avatars are Batman without the gadgets - dark, mysterious, with long running plans and gambits that others cant easily uncover but they have the Mage's best interests in mind. Being foolishly open about oneself and relying on technology and new gadgets (not saying dont use them, but not make them the sole basis of their attempt) will have the dark thing expressing its displeasure.
                                            • Considering the characters Past Lives this means the Avatar is likely to appear as a combination of death related entities (Grim Reaper, Ghosts, Hades) or ancestors of the character. While she might not remember what happened to her, an interesting twist could be the Avatar does know but doesnt say (remember secretive) rather taking advantage of the amnesia to push the Mage to achievements that she would have resisted before (the whole she wants order vs Primordial often representing chaos). At the same time the Avatar will influence the style of order as it represents an ancient chaotic state so any order it would support are going to be modeled after the old ways and respect for those who came before.


                                            Enemies is often times a boon to the Storyteller. It gives you a pre-determined foe which can help get plots moving or prod the character to action if they remain staid for too long. The Enemy should be tied to the character and background and at 3 points is going to be superior to the starting Mage; so what did the Mage do to earn the ire of this person or group? An enemy should be more than Technocrats hate the Traditions as Reality Deviants or similar stereotypes; the hatred and loathing from Enemy comes from a personal place, the enemy doesnt hate them because of what group they align with they hate the Mage.
                                            • Death of a partner, loved one, close friend: The Ascension War is a war and its often bloody and the death of someone close can lead to a lifelong hatred. For inspiration "My name is Inigo Montoya, you killed my father, prepare to die." Could work with either character, amnesia makes it easy for all sorts of things to come up and someone into the drug scene especially with money has all sorts of chances to end up purposefully or accidentally getting someone killed (overdose, drug deal gone bad, etc).
                                            • They should have been mine: The Mage in question was being pursued and courted by the enemy and the Mage spurned them (this does not have to be romantic, think Star Wars "He will join us or die.") The enemy takes the logic that if they cant have them no one can to its logical end. Considering the Hermetic's addiction perhaps a Nephandi was subtly providing drugs and maneuvering to place the Mage under their sway, but the Mage caught on (perhaps through that Avatar) that something was amiss and turned the Mage away.
                                            • Betrayal: At one point in happier times the Mage and the enemy were friends or at least close allies. Something happened and they turned on each other and the enemy has taken that sense of betrayal to heart. More likely for the Hermetic as drug use can make people do things they regret later.
                                            • Legacy Hatred: The enemy wanted someone else dead and they had the temerity to die through someone else's hand. Denied their vengeance they decided to go for the next of kin in a sort of macabre inheritance. To their anger this new foe is as recalcitrant to die as their former one and it fuels their hate further. One of the few exceptions to hating the Mage, rather the hatred is displaced from the one that denied them vengeance to their next of kin. Could work well with the girl due to the Past Lives, perhaps the most recent Past Life was the enemy's former foe.

                                                    The Enemies should be worked out with the players, though I can give further suggestions.
                                                    • The Enemy's actions don't necessarily need to be directed at killing the Mage. They could decide they'd rather humiliate or ruin the character, as it could be more satisfying (and safer for the Enemy, if directly attacking the Mage would lead to other problems). Opposition can take many forms, which can keep the PCs on their toes.
                                                    • The Enemy doesn't even need to actually hate the Mage in question. Perhaps their interests are simply opposed, and the Enemy sees their conflict as a brutal necessity. The Enemy might admire the Mage, respect them - even love them. Consider an Enemy who believes they oppose the Mage for their own good. Or the Enemy is messed up in the head, and attacking the Mage is considered an expression of their positive emotions. Maybe the Enemy is a former family member who radically disagrees with the Mage's lifestyle, and is working to push them out of it.
                                                    • The Enemy could, in some way, be a reflection of the Mage. Some aspect of their character - their beliefs, their skill set, their attitude, their background - is reminiscent of the Mage. They could be the Mage if their beliefs or methods were taken to their logical extreme...or, alternatively, that the Mage is the one whose beliefs are corrupted forms of their adversary. It lends a poetic edge to the conflict, as the Mage is forced to look in the mirror darkly (or lightly).
                                                    • The Mage's Enemy is also their Avatar. Consider my above point about an Enemy acting in opposition to the Mage for what they perceive is the Mage's own good. Now consider that the Enemy is right, because they are the Mage's Avatar. Avatars can appear in many guises, and many Mages don't recognize their Avatars for what they are. It's possible that the Mage finds herself opposed by an implacable adversary, who seems to know the Mage intimately, has no recognizable motive besides giving the Mage a hard time, and, worst of all, wields magick without suffering Paradox. (Because, for reasons unknown, Avatars are not affected by Paradox). It's a frightening Enemy to be sure, and one tailor made to galvanize the Mage to action.
