    Originally posted by Saur Ops Specialist View Post
    You're homebrewing. You posted a hombrew setting. A lot of it doesn't exactly line up with the settings as they're written (Supernals aren't Primordials from Exalted and don't have world-bodies, for example). It's not the first time, and won't be the last.
    They way I see it, running a game with true crossover metaphysics and mythos militates that one homebrew. Running a V:tM game with some mage stuff doesn't really require homebrew because you can just use the V:tM explanations for everything (the same goes for running a game based explicitly on one line with cameos by other lines). However, the moment that you decide that you want to have parity between the respective mythos of two lines, you must homebrew.

    That said, once one has undertaken the task of cobbling together a homebrew cWoD cosmology, I think a great deal can be achieved or accomplished by treating the setting material for each respective game as having been written by an unreliable narrator. The Triat of W:tA and the cosmological forces of Stasis, Dynamism, and Entropy that feature in M:tAs, can be the same thing, but simply experienced or characterized differently by those two groups. The phenomena themselves are abstract and out there enough that it doesn't seem like too much of a stretch. If you want to incorporate Vampire, Demon (and I guess Hunter), then you declare that, absolutely above everything in the universe that is even remotely knowable, there is the Abrahamic deity, 'God'. So on and so forth.

    I've been playing these games for about 16 years now (mostly Storytelling though), and I've run nigh the same cosmology for the majority of it -- and I've found that I can give almost equal deference to all the game lines' respective mythos without need to drastically change the character of any given entity (like making Pentex a solely vampiric undertaking or something). Furthermore, unless you're running a super high-level game, a great deal of the harder inconsistencies to smooth over don't actually need to be addressed in the game, as they're of such a cosmological character that the players are unlikely to ever be forced to deal with a problem that requires clear explanations as to what is and isn't the truth.

                            As for the differing theologies, the answer is incredibly simple. No one is entirely right. Every sphere presents their own theories but no one (Demon included, though I usually run them as the closest to right) have all the answers. Each is looking at the world through their own lens. This is why you see so many strange similarities in the mythologies (take a look at the two brothers story from Changeling and how close that is to the Abrahamic Caine and Abel story used in Vampire) with different interpretations. I've got theories as to the overall truth which takes elements from all of them but takes none of them as absolute canon, but I shift these as needed per game and it works well enough. There is absolutely no need to put everyone on the same page, and I think it strips a great deal of flavour out of the world.

                                        Originally posted by Wolfgar View Post
                                        Vampire elders control very little. What they do is influence, which is quite different. No vampire on the planet can just call up the White House, say "Hey, invade Panama for me will ya?" and have it happen. It doesn't work like that. It's like a guy pushing a car. Sure, he has some impact on what direction the car goes, but the weight of the car and the shape of the road are bigger forces. It's still ridiculously impressive compared to most of us - we would be ants trying to the car - but true control is impossible.
                                        What Wolfgar is describing is pretty clearly what the Influence background does. It (including the extra 6-10 levels provided in Elysium: the Elder wars) represents having minions and compromised agents spread throughout areas of interest (in the Elysium levels you're talking about minions spread throughout important systems worldwide). These minions may attempt to influence policy towards any end you want - but they're still putting themselves on the chopping block whenever they do so.

                                        Mechanically speaking, any of these attempts within the Influence levels an Elder has allows them to make a Manipulation+Influence roll against the ST. The impact of successes and the difficulty of the task are ST determined. So, taking 'call up the White House, say "Hey, invade Panama for me will ya?" and have it happen' as the overall aim and methodology, you might have the following set up:

                                        Difficulty 10. 9 if ST is feeling generous (this is about as risky an implementation as is possible)
                                        Botch: Watergate 2 and Elder is in danger (odds are about as good as getting a single success at 10 and a nontrivial outcome for difficulty 9).
                                        Success 1: US relations with Panama become strained.
                                        Success 2: US withdraws from cooperative policies with Panama.
                                        Success 3: US relations with Panama become hostile.
                                        Success 4: US comes to the brink of war with Panama. War is likely but not inevitable.
                                        Success 5: US Invades Panama right this second.

                                        So the Elder might have the minions in place to escalate or diminish an existing conflict (at a risk to themselves) but they pretty much can't 'call up the White House, say "Hey, invade Panama for me will ya?" and have it happen.' ... unless the ST doesn't understand how to set difficulty and successes and/or the static risk-averse Elder is willing to throw away everything on a literal roll of the dice.

                                                        Originally posted by Obyrne View Post
                                                        Masquerade as a setting has its roots in the 13th century where the "Dark Ages Vampire" setting begins to crumble..
                                                        No, the Masquerade as a setting element has its roots as a simple literary technique. When the idea of the Masquerade was first published it had no published in-world origin, and the Dark Ages game line and all of its explanations had not even been conceived yet. The Masquerade as a setting element began as a simple literary technique for enabling powerful supernatural beings to exist in a world that sufficiently resembled our own on the surface. Note that the vampires' Masquerade served the same literary function as the werewolves' Delirium; what differentiated the Masquerade from the Delirium is that the Masquerade began as a generic "secret masters theory", whereas the Delirium operated as a supernatural effect. Some White Wolf authors then later extrapolated upon that generic "secret masters theory" in various permutations, not all of them being entirely compatible.
