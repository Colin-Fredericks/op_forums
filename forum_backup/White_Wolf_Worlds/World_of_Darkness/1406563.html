                                          So, I said that Mages should be at least a little unsettling to most mundane people, and that's certainly true for the Cult of Ecstasy, whose paradigm is built around breaking down barriers and expanding the limits of their perceptions. For the most part, that's about their own barriers, but they also try to push those of society as a whole.

                                          I think one of the major problems with the Acharne is one they share with the other two "rebel" factions, the Hagalaz and the Aghori. Namely, that they appear to have been created entirely around the idea of being against the Code of Ananda, with issues about why they are against it or what they're even trying to accomplish secondary considerations. I think there also seems to have been little thought given to the traditions/subcultures those groups were based one. For example, the Aghori are a real religious tradition in Hinduism. Their beliefs are somewhat complex, but in spite of them being contradictory to orthodox Hinduism in a number of ways, they are often held in great reverence by the rural populations they live near. And they don't really bear more than a cursory resemblance to the real world group. I suspect that what you see in even the original tradition book is based on a misunderstanding of what was described in whatever source Phil Brucato encountered them in. In a similar fashion, the Acharne seem to be derived from a very shallow understanding and misinterpretation of the real world 18th century Hellfire Club and on the French Libertine subculture that largely inspired it. (Trying to tie them into unrelated Greek Stoicism only further muddies things.) I'm not at all into S&M and related subcultures, but I've known people who are, and they were more than happy to answer questions about it, so I have long recognized that the Acharne are conceptually broken.

                                          (TL;DR version of that: The Acharne come across as the creation of someone who things S&M is like what's in 50 Shades of Grey, a book that infuriates actually practitioners of the subculture, because it's actually about an obsessive creep who wants to hurt women who remind him of his mother.)

                                          The other issue is that all of these rebels against the Code of Ananda don't seem to actually reflect what the Code is. It's not a "sunshine and rainbows" thing, and the Cult has never really shied away from some of the rougher and even darker aspects of human nature. The Nine Sacred Passions include Sadness, Envy, Rage, and Hate as well as Joy, Love, and Empathy, and all are considered equally worthy. The Code itself is entirely in the form of proverbs, most of which are up for interpretation. Even as wonky as the three faction are as presented in both editions of the Tradition book, one can make an argument that they are still following the Code, at least as they interpret it. One of the tenets is "Even trees rent by lightning may grow new fruit", and someone - say a member of the Hagalaz - could argue that they are the lightning that is making the way for new fruit by destroying certain aspects of modern society (such as Christian belief and morality, and its influence on the culture). Likewise, the bit about transcending fear. Acharne may claim that they're both overcoming their own fear of demons and of hurting other people, and even teaching others to overcome their own fears of being hurt. (And yes, so a degree, this is a probably self indulgent BS. It really only holds up if the people you are teaching come to you freely and of their own desire.)

                                          (TL;DR: The Code of Ananda is kind of vague and up for interpretation. These groups shouldn't really be rejectors of it but just have their own odd interpretations of it.)

                                              You may or may not find this version more agreeable.


                                              L'Désir Acharné (French for "The Relentless Desire")

                                              Classifed under the Bringers of Joy, the kamamarga (path of ecstasy) devoted to pleasure and joy as the primary way to reach Lankashim. They are believed to be an offshoot of the Fellowship of Pan, born out of the Libertine movement in 18th century France and the British Hellfire Clubs they helped inspire. While the initial members where all men, they were soon joined by a number of women who proved themselves more than a match for their male counterparts. (It's long rumored that Marianna of Balador was a member at some point, but she has never given a straight answer when asked.) Over the centuries, they have evolved with the times, now being heavily involved in modern S&M/B&D subcultures. This includes a commitment to the ideas of consent and limits. Members restrict more extreme activities to themselves and their willing acolytes, only involving outsiders in lesser activities such as simple sex play and efforts to nudge people outside their (usually non-sexual) comfort zones.
                                              Their mystical beliefs are based on a study of contrasts and opposites. Pleasure and Pain are two sides of the same coin, and both can lead to Lankashim. In fact, they two can often overlap, blurring the boundaries between them. They philosophical underpinnings of the master and slave dynamic also form a key pillar to their world view. As they hew closely to the ideals of S&M subculture, this includes the idea that in a consensual relationship, the submissive is the one who actually holds the real power. And while physical pleasure is their almost obsessive pursuit, it is ultimately done in the quest for spiritual growth and enlightenment.
                                              Members will frequently test each other psychologically and emotionally, in consensual contests of will, interrogation sessions, and elaborate mind games. They do this to better understand their own desires and weaknesses, as well as to push past their own limits. A side effect is that this makes them very effective agents in the Ascension War, able to undertake dangerous missions without fear and resist Technocratic or even Nephandic mental assaults.
                                              One of the more controversial practices of the sect is that of demonology. Members summon and bind lesser (and sometimes more powerful) demons as servants. These bound demons are sometimes used as familiars, but most often to engage in mind games and tests of will. They are also used in the act of sacred congress (ie ritual sex), and it's not uncommon for this to involve an S&M dynamic. (This will frequently involve the mage acting as the submissive, proving their ultimate control over the demon.)
                                              Lust is one of the focuses for the sect, but members also cultivate the passions of Ambition and Wonder. In addition, many also delve into Fear (seeking to understand, master and transcend their own) and Empathy (understanding both sides of the master/slave dynamic and subjecting themselves to the same torments they give to others).
                                              While Time is still the key Sphere for all members of the Cult of Ecstasy, members of L'Désir Acharné usually strive to master the magic of Mind, Life and Spirit as well.


                                              (In the 1970s, an extremist faction of L'Désir Acharné founded the LeMarchand Club in Los Angeles. Considered part of the Destroyers of the Flesh kamamarga, members no longer differentiate between the sensations of pleasure and pain. They use magic and summoned spirits to subject themselves to tortures that would be otherwise impossible for mundane humans to survive, all in an effort to achieve the ultimate in ecstatic bliss. Thankfully, they don't involve anyone outside the Club in their activities.)


                                              While I could probably recommend a number of books as inspiration, I'll just stick to movies.
                                              • Quills (2000), a pretty decent bio-pic with Geoffrey Rush as the Marquis de Sade.
                                              • Secretary (2002), one of the better made films examining a master-submissive relationship.
                                              • The Game (1997), for the sort of mind games they do.
                                              • Hellraiser (1987), which is entirely where the idea of the LeMarchand Club comes from.

                                              Also the various episodes of CSI where it was used as their "subculture of the week."
