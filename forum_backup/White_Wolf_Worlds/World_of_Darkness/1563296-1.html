    Sensōji used to be a syncretic temple, but during the Meiji they moved the Shinto part to other shrines and temples nearby.

    But on top of the folk practices being usually mixed, you can also have the same entity fill a different role depending on religion. So Sensōji is not related to the Shichifukujin, but some of them are part of Buddhism and so they'll be there in this capacity.

    This includes Benzaiten. Her Shinto shrines are usually guarding water sources with alleged mystical powers and focus on the magic water, but in Sensōji she's present as a teacher of Sutras and Buddhist prayers.

    Its main deity is Kannon, though, Bodhisattva of Compassion. The temple guards a statue of Kannon that was found in the nearby river and deemed a miracle. The three fishermen who found the statue are now considered Kami, but they are worshipped in another temple nearby.

    Long story short, the Shichifukujin were at first a thought exercise that got too popular. Really too popular.

    They're not originally related in any way. In the 15th century some scholars were tasked with making an allegory to explain what competent statesmen need, and they decided to bunch 7 gods as exemplars of what they called the 7 virtues of state.

    By design the list sought examples from many places and religions, because they were just meant to be didactic. One is Japanese, other three are from India and three from China, with 2 Buddhist, 2 Hindu and 2 Taoist (with an error, the two are actually variations of a same god).

    All the 7 were gods of good luck in some sense though, and so people really liked the idea of them all working as a fortune granting group, the Shichifukujin became increasingly popular and traditions were built around the group with no direct link to their actual theology.

    The "Art of War" is a common European title for Sun Zi's book, it's the same in every European Language. Also, it's a greatly overestimated book, it isn't bad, but quite basic stuff, it can be summed in a 1 page list without much loss, the rest is poetic phrasing and a couple stories to show how Sun Zi was competent.

    On the meaning of art, I recommend studying the history of the concepts of Techné and Art until the Late Middle Ages, and original Greek foundational works on Aesthetics, understand what it was really about before its stupidity magnification process.

    But basically the modern concept of art that's the fundament of the Fine Arts is actually a semantic aberration built in the 17th century Europe as a tool of cultural dominance. It is an artificial distinction between form and function designed to argue for intellectual inferiority of others without need for actual engagement.

    毘沙門天 (Bishamon) — Brujah/Banu Haqim

    Also known as Bishamonten. This "-ten" in the end means something like "heavenly", it's a suffix for divine names that denote a kami that live in certain higher planes.

    - Originally the guardian-king Vaiśravaṇa, tied to Kubera, Hindu god of the north and wealth as much as war.

    - Brother or Husband of Kichijōten.

    - Patron of soldiers and guards.

    - Depiction: standing upright, raised spear, clad in armor and carrying in his left hand the Pagoda of Treasury that holds all wealth of a community, economic or not, he's right foot upon a demonic head.

    - Represent state not as status quo, but institution that guards society and its wealth for the people.

    - Punisher of evil, guardian of the North, the one who enforces. He seeks not dominance, but to care for the faithful.

    - Yūbi's reading: Brujah
    - The clan of righteous violence and rage directed against abuse.
    - They fight oppression, tyranny, corruption of intent. Rebellion is not blind, but to replace the rule of kleptocrats with a proper State.
    - Guard and punishes. As the god they say, "No, this ends now."
    - They too protect the pagoda from pilferers, caring for what's socially valuable.
    - A foot over the head of tyranny, an illegitimate king falling to the will of the people and the anger of the oppressed.

    - Yūbi's reading: Banu Haqim
    - Custodians of the kine against the predations of their Kindred, taking the role of judicial protection.
    - Violence is only legitimate when sanctioned for a proper purpose. To destroy those who abuse their strength is one such purpose.
    - They guard society and a doctrine that keeps it running and safe.
    - Warrior's tension between appetite and discipline, to police or indulge.
    - If the institutions fail, they're the knife to end the tyrant. If they hold true, they're judges that apply law.

    - The mold
    Bishamonten in full red armor, spear raised, one foot on an executive. The point of the spear is the symbol of Anarchism, the Pagoda has the name Haqim etched in Standard Arabic.

            Meijī tried to diminish Buddhism's influence because it is a foreign religion and reduced the legitimacy of the nationalist state they were trying to build.

            But overall that's the history of Shinto: it does not, actually, exists. Not as religion, institution, theology, cosmogony, any of such.

            What Japan actually has is a plethora of local traditions and temples that exist independently, whose beliefs, rituals and myths own nothing to each other, just sometimes develop links out of exchanges and syncretism.

            Shinto isn't a common thread linking all those systems, it is a state policy of promotion for all the indigenous beliefs that accept to legitimate their nationalist agenda. They've been doing that since the 6th century, pretending there's a "true nipponic soul" connecting all the temples and practices as long as they promote that the Japanese state has divine lineage and society in the country should organize according to the political prescriptions of the state.

            And Buddhism has never been a part of this as it is an actual organized religion. Even if it has no central authority, it has centralizing tenets and institutions. When locals create their own syncretic traditions about the role of a temple or of figures of the religion, the monks and the temple do not follow suit. They may and do let people have their beliefs, but internally they are still more related to global Buddhism than local folklore.

            I must admit I wasn't paying much attention if the written kanji were following pronunciation/rōmaji spelling, but basically yes. It's not much of a rule just because Japanese don't have a lot of actual rules on how to written kanji, but it is indeed the case that 天 should be dropped when calling him Bishamon.

            The important part is to know this "havenly" is in a very specific Japanese sense. Traditionally they distinguish the realm where a deity lives in and/or govern, it doesn't indicate divine status or necessarily a hierarchy, but rather where in the "world tree" so to speak a given deity lives.

            Gods of abstract phenomena, celestial bodies and the like are likely to be identified with 天, although using the suffix has other linguistic considerations, while gods of lineages, places, physical phenomena and several others won't. Even something like the myths of a god leading them to move where they live can affect use of the suffix.

            The problem with "demonic" depictions is that it has a lot more cultural assumptions than we are usually told. I personally avoid using the term in many contexts because we are really, really conditioned to think demons are a kind of universal concept when it is nothing of the like even in Europe.

            Buddhism is still at its roots an Indian religion, and there scary deities are common. Take for example that the recurring European/American presentation of Kali as a goddess that revels in wanton bloodlust and destruction is actually an extremely offensive mistake.

            More importantly, though, Buddhism is based in the concept that every entity exists within the cycle of reincarnation and their search for Nirvana, so there's no such a thing as innate evil that can be surmised from taxonomy or appearance. Anyone can choose to follow the Path, and even achieve Enlightenment, without ceasing to be who/what they are in this life.

            恵比寿 (Ebisu) — Lasombra/Ravnos

            As everyone and their dog will remind you if you look for the Shichifukujin, Ebisu is set apart in the group by being the only native god in all senses.

            His influence by himself in Japan is staggering, much larger than any of the others. You'll find references and images of Ebisu everywhere, he is used to name places, companies, products, he's legitimately one of the most important and influential gods in Japan despite relative low theological position.

            If you've seen any Japanese brand with a name that reminds his or Evisu, and/or with a mascot that's a laughing man with a fishing rod or fish, those are references to Ebisu, and include from basketball teams to design clothes, beer, cars and whatever else you can imagine.

            - The only of Japanese-origin in the Seven, god of fishing, commerce and hard, honest work. Ebisugao, his smiling face, never wavers. To live is a wonder.

            - ritually paired with Daikokuten in depictions of the Shichifukujin — the two heads of the ship (I'll talk about the ship later), both patrons of trade. Firstborn of Izanagi and Izanami as Hiruko, the Leech child born without bones or limbs. As the Three Gods of Good Fortune he joins with Daikokuten and Fukurokuju.

            - Patron of fishermen since prehistoric Japan

            - Depicted carrying a fishing rod in one hand and a red sea bream tucked under his left arm, wears a tall Kazaori Eboshi hat. sometimes he's accompanied by jellyfish or fugu and he is said to incarnate as whales, whale-shark, or other marine mega fauna. His depiction has infinite variation as mascot of businesses and their products.

            Obs: because of his relationship to mega fauna fishermen in northern Honshū are adamantly opposed to whaling. For those people hunting whales not only isn't "Japanese culture and pride", but is actually active blasphemy.

            - Born disabled because of an error in his parents’ marriage ritual, cast to the sea, he was raised by Ebisu Saburo of the Ainu after reaching the shores of Hokkaidō. He grew bones and strong muscles, but is still hard of hearing and slow to move.

            - He can't hear the call of the kannazuki, the Month Without Gods when all deities of Japan gather at Izumo. Some say because of this he is the only god available to prayer during this period in the rest of the country. Being crippled also means he takes 7 days to finish his most regular task, cleaning the debris in the ocean, bringing it to the shore.

            - Yūbi's reading: Lasombra
            - Survivalists, they are cast to the dark waters and still live to grow stronger.
            - Even their very reflections reject them, but they stay unabated by it.
            - The fishing rod becomes a leash into the Deep Dark to pull his fare from the waters of the Abyss, as water is their element. In the dark depths they built their castle,
            - If honesty is dubious, their work is still hard, neither taking the easy route or wavering under hardship.
            - With their Bane they are the slow crippled of modernity, without the ears, legs and arms of the century.

            - Yūbi's reading: Ravnos
            - Ebisu has no fixed temple lineage, turning up wherever fishermen are — by nature without roots.
            - Fukunusubi: steal a Daikoku/Ebisu figure for luck at New Year market.
            - As the god is left out of kannazuki in Izumo Taisha, is the clan left out of the reunions of the others.
            - Living with the wandering tribes of nomads as Ebisu lived with Ainu, a people marginalized in their own land.
            - The sea as symbol of dreams and illusions, where Ebisu takes form as animals beyond the reach of a regular disguise or transmutation.

            - The mold
            Ebisu smiling sitting in a chart, he's with a rod, fish and hat. In the pitch black hat lies a pitch black jellyfish that gives it the look of a dark crown. The chart red wheels are Chakra Wheel with the distinct 16 rays depicted in the flag of the Roma.

            While Yūbi doesn't like executives that much, she is trying to approach the one who's in the coterie with an open mind, so she'll use him as model for Ebisu, making for a curious blend of corporate fashion and a smiling fisherman.

            For the Lasombra filling she'll use 黒練りごまkuronerigoma, a slightly sweet paste of black sesame seeds, maybe adding some sugar.

                    The tales of [Lasombra] the Ante and the Clan during his reign also talk a lot about deep 9waters. His castle of shadows is in the water and goes deep, and the Clan explored the lands around and islands within the Mediterranean sea precisely because their main domain was the sea itself, and for along time the Clan was renowned for its seafaring members.

                    While their theologies are diverse and their shrines and temples are distinct from each other, the Shichifukujin inspired the rise of a number of traditions around them.

                    First is the Takarabune, the Lucky Ship. It's said that the Seven get together for a journey to the heavens in the New Year, so during this time it is customary to depict their ship, Takarabune, and the seven within. As gods of fortune and a popular, informal tradition, the Takarabune is a light hearted depiction where good humor is welcome. They can be funny looking, should be all smiling, and having a Takarabune in your house and even your room during the New Year brings good luck.

                    After that comes the Shichifukujin Megumi, a pilgrimage to search them in the following two weeks. The idea is to take those 15 days to visit temples and shrines, or at the very least one statue of each Shichifukujin. Here their not being theologically linked is a feature, as the idea is to visit seven or more different sacred places while looking for them. Completing the pilgrimage with a proper visit to each one is a sure way to increase good luck for the year.

                    寿老人 (Jurōjin) — Tzimisce/Salubri

                    - God of long, wise, good life and of the South Pole Star, where live half of the Immortal Taoists.

                    - Shares much of his portfolio and role as Shichifukujin with Fukurokuju, sometimes said to be his grandson, even sometimes to share his body. He was once a human Tao Immortal of the Song Dynasty.

                    - Patron to the elderly

                    - Depiction: old man with long white beard and a head either bald or with a long and white hair, a small frame less than three shaku high (90cm total), a walking staff from which a scroll dangles. Atop the elongated head sits a kanmuri scholar cap. Usually with deer, crane or tortoise at his side. The other hand may carry a magic fan or a cup of sake.

                    - His scroll carries valuable wisdom or special knowledge. For some is the Wisdom of the World, others is the lifespan of every living being, and other yet it is a sutra. Either of those carries the secret for a long and happy life.

                    - He has the endurance to reach old age and the patience it requires. If you know to persist and wait, time will help you.

                    - Yūbi's reading: Tzimisce
                    - The clan of The Eldest endures in their domains and traditions even beyond transmutation. If Hoshino Yūbi endured 30 years, it was the blessings of Jurōjin.
                    - With their keen perception through Auspex and relationship with land, they carry the wisdom and secrets for a fortunate life.
                    - Recording spiritual philosophy, the spirits of the land and the secrets of the flesh, scholars of the clan are always expanding their paths to their Tao.
                    - Scholars who never stop, the clan keeps changing and living, finding the new experiences that keep the growing pace of the Aged Wisdom in the Scroll.
                    - A clan of land, wisdom, magic and connection to animals, they're with the Deer, Crane and Tortoise.

                    - Yūbi's reading: Salubri
                    - Through patience they endured. It was not a longevity taken by force or shrewdness, but calm patience to choose wisely and wait the time to enjoy themselves.
                    - As healers they grant longevity to others, using their knowledge to bless with what they have.
                    - Seeing and knowing the souls of people, they have the Wisdom, the Sutra and secret to a long, happy life.
                    - With their teachings and calling, it is a clan that also lives, even if in hiding, rather than just surviving. A clan that understand that longevity must be with happiness, not mere continued existence.
                    - Call it what they may, they know to seek the actual Tao enlightenment to cultivate the soul of an Immortal

                    Instead of using a vampire, Yūbi plans on using her mother as a model for a female Jurōjin. At least her idealized memory of her mother.

                    And since she's a Tzimisce herself, the only she knows, the filling for the clan's cakes will just be something she likes: a sweetened cream of Matcha with a touch of vanilla extract and as strong as possible.

                    I revised the fillings for each clan. Some changed completely:

                    Ventrue - kuri kinton, a mix of candied chestnut and yellow sweet potato. It's usually served as a dish in some occasions and colorized for maximum golden shine to attract wealth.

                    Hecata - honeyed umeboshi. It is a fermented pickle made with a Japanese apricot called Ume, usually called Japanese Plum (but it's not actually a plum). It's traditional, healthy, a weird but pleasant taste. Usually it's salty, but it isn't uncommon to wash the salt, add honey and mix in sweets.

                    Toreador - Kabocha paste with spice. This is a pumpkin variety of Japanese origin that's way sweeter than any other gourd, but it's also one of the few traditional Japanese ingredients the US produce is deemed of as high quality as the Japanese for confectionery. Add black pepper and some browned butter, preferably butter from Hokkaidō.

                    Tremere - Beni Imo. It's a very sweet potato from Okinawa that's violently purple. You can't get much more magical, just add some sugar, as it isn't still the sweetest potato in Japan.

                    Brujah - Sakura paste with shisō. The paste can be made with the pickled flower or just extract, and usually has a pastel pink color, but shisō is an Asian mint that can be violently red, turning the Sakura cream into blood while the mint packs a punch of flavor.

                    Banu Haqim - The inspiration for this is Haft Mewa, a Persian sweet made with seven nuts or fruits (actually most people nowadays use any number) and a sweet liquid like rose water or syrup. Here it will be a paste of dates with mashed pistachios, and a syrup mixed with cardamom.

                    Lasombra - Black sesame paste, sweetened with mirin, an extremely sweet rice wine used for cooking. Real Teriyaki sauce is made with it, for example.

                    Ravnos - Doce-de-Leite, a caramelized milk candy, made with kokuto and yukishio, sugar and salt with high mineral content and peculiar taste from Okinawa. Yūbi thinks the archetype for Japanese Ravnos would be from Okinawa, a people similarly dominated by imperialist big empires and later treated as second class and thieves around the lands who exploited them.

                    Tzimisce - As said, Matcha with vanilla. Yūbi likes it, her mother liked it, that's all that matter.

                    Salubri - Yuzu gelly. It's a Japanese citrus variety, sweet but very healthy and good for summer. To add some spice, ginger, hard to be more universal than that. Sesame and ginger are the most widespread ingredients used here.
