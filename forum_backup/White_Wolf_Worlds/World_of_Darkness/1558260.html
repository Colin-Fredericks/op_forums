      Part of the problem is that radiation covers a huge spectrum of matter-energy interactions. The first step in what to do with radiation in a game is decide what kinds of radiation you want to model, and then how much you want to look at genre tropes vs more realistic metrics.               "Nuclear waste" isn't really a thing in reality. The big concern are radioisotopes that form decay chains of ionizing radiation.

              So, first, ionizing radiation is the "big deal" because non-ionizing radiation is effectively harmless outsize of massive quantities. Ionizing radiation is dangerous because it knocks atomic particles out of position, which turns those atoms into ions, and thus makes them unstable and reactive; and in high enough doses is what leads to things like cancer and acute radiation poisoning.

              Decay chains are a problem because they increase the amount of radiation a given radioisotope can emit. Basically, a radioactive atom inevitably decays into a "daughter" atom as it sheds energy in the form of radiation. Most radioactive substances form stable daughter atoms after that initial shedding. The stuff that's really dangerous has a long chain of decaying from one radioactive daughter to another. So the more steps it takes to go from a radioactive parent to a stable multiple-granddaughter atom the more radiation is released; and if ionizing means it can do a lot of harm.

              More than 95% of "waste" from nuclear reactors are about as dangerous as getting an X-ray at the dentist's office. People that work around the waste need to monitor long term exposure, but generally it's safe because the radiation either isn't ionizing or doesn't have a decay chain associated with it. So it's a short small dose that goes away quickly. Above that there's the dangerous-but-not-immediately bad level of waste particles that can cause harm with exposure but can also be medically addressed if acted on quickly. Only after that do you have the tiny, tiny fraction of waste products that are in the really bad category.

              And those products aren't just mixed together when the waste is being processed; in fact quite the opposite. That's why situations like meltdowns are such a big deal: the safety protocols to contain the worst-of-the-worst waste particles get blown to bits and those get mixed in with all the minimal threat materials.

              Particle-Wave duality also makes things complicated to model. We treat radiation as a wave because most radiation acts as a wave, but the most dangerous "rays" are actually particles and are their most serious when they've "calmed" back to a particle behavior. An alpha or beta particle flying at you as a wave doesn't have the force to penetrate your skin or your clothing... that's why they have those emergency showers for people exposed to sudden high doses. But breathing in a lungful of beta particles? That can easily kill you because then they get into your body and sit there, shedding radioactive daughter chains, and so on.

              This doesn't really indicate much for putting soak into things for normal exposures. Obviously there is a physical component to it, but what ionizing radiation is going to do to you isn't something more endurance can change; only help you endure the process of trying to clean it out.

                      Also, one of the reasons I brought up the particle-wave duality issue, is that alpha and beta particles (beta is the majority of nuclear radiation in reactors that builds up over time) - when acting as particles - don't just damage your cells. Once they get into your body they turn your atoms against you by ionizing them into radiation emitters as well through the decay chain. Even your non-living tissue like bones can get alpha (Polonium-210 poisoning works by flooding your system with alpha particles emitted from the polonium decay chain) or beta particles lodged in them and they would keep damaging your internal tissues. Some radioisotopes are very hard to detect (see polonium-210 again) because most radiation detectors focus on gamma rays (which are essentially photons and thus just really short but high intensity light that is easy to detect), so trying to purge them out of a body once they're in can be nearly impossible since you can't isolate where they are.

                              Most of the WoD books imply that serious radiation sickness is Aggravated damage, which takes care of soak, the difficulty in treating/surviving for humans, and the higher tier magics needed for supernaturals to deal with it.

                              I'm not sure I'd let vampires off that easily. Ionizing radiation chains are more serious against living tissue, but they can degrade most materials regardless. Dead cells are still going to get damaged and need to be repaired to full functioning.

                              The problem with how much damage and over what interval is that the dose delivered is what matters and there's no simple way to calculate that. You keep saying "rays" but it's important to remember that radiation is really about particles unless you're next to a high output instant release of energy (like an atomic bomb or a nuclear meltdown). Think of something like pepper spray. A can of pepper spray could do nothing (you spray someone wearing a heavy coat on the back and they can just take off the coat and be fine), to extremely painful (spraying someone in the face or on open wounds) to deadly (spraying someone right in their mouth). You can absolutely do something like you suggest with different stages of intervals and the like, but most people have no idea what kind of exposure leads to what kind of dosage for what type of radioactive source.

                              There's also an accumulation issue. If you're exposed to new radiation faster than you can deal with the old radiation in can compound more drastically than just the increase of damage levels would model well. Effectively this would mead both how much damage and how often you roll can both get worse depending on the type of exposure.

                                      Trinity and Aberrant also deal with it, but just relate it as levels of bashing or lethal damage per turn, which is frankly unsatisfying.

                                      Accumulation in the WoD can only really be related back to "you are already dead" dosages on account of how frustratingly non-linear the ST system is. 1000 rads (1 gray or sievert) would, in that case, clock in at about seven levels of agg, with the caveat that exposure of this sort can still leave the victim alive for almost a day before they are no longer. A very rough equivalence could be something like Dose = (HL^2) * 20.5 rads (divide by 100 for Grays or sieverts), with any alpha radiation or other huge particles that get through counting as double to quadruple the number of rads. If you're calculating the other way, it'd be HL = sqrt (Dose/20.5), again with the dose in equivalent rads. You can potentially simulate the gradual lurching toward death by not applying wound penalties or being incapacitated immediately and instead just metering it out over the next roughly 18 hours.

                                      If you just want a simpler system, you can just rule that you suffer a health level every 100 rads equivalent of accumulation, and that the effects don't hit until (Stamina) hours later. Garou and most Fera get to roll to soak (repel, really) this damage, while vampires don't suffer damage but just keep accumulating rads and have to shed blood points in order to purge some of the longer-lasting particles or activated components of their body, or else they count as a source of radiation in the form of a wandering orphan source, which is probably a huge masquerade breach since it involves being able to move around mostly unimpaired when any human would have dropped dead. Some forms of radiation can count as being like fire to vampires instead, which brings the hazard of damage alongside accumulation of energy and dangerous materials (or activation of whatever the vampire has on or in them).

                                      Anyone else is affected by according to how resilient they are, except wraiths, who are already dead and made out of ectoplasm, which probably doesn't interact with radioactivity since it's the stuff of dead spirits... is what I'd say if WW wasn't arbitrarily weird about this, since ghosts of those slain by nuclear explosions seem to get twisted in some way for it and Yama Kings apparently cannot utilize their tainted energies, except for one who's experimenting with it (I think it was Ravana, who was trying to get India and Pakistan to commit MAD to harvest their dead. Stay classy, 90s WW!). The other Yama King who tried ended up comatose for his troubles (see: Dark Kingdom of Jade Adventures, specifically the one about snagging the relic nuke).

                                              To put some perspective of this, the fastest death attributed to the demon core experiments took 9 days, and the scientist in question got hit with an estimated 1,100 rad dose. The second fastest fatality from those experiments was 25 days after a 300 rad dose.

                                              The demon core dealt with a setup that generated mostly (~90%) neutron radiation, and the second most common product was gamma radiation (~10%). Both are short burst high intensity forms of ionizing radiation; the whole point was to work towards a functional neutron bomb because neutron radiation and gamma radiation disperse far more quickly than alpha or beta radiation. So a target hit with a neutron-primary device can be reclaimed faster.

                                              So it worked much more like "rays." Louis Slotin, the one that died the fastest, was literally the one that cracked the containment sphere and got blasted in the stomach at point-blank, causing wide scale acute radiation poisoning to his gastrointestinal tract and widespread organ failure in the lower half of his torso.

                                              We don't really know what would have happened if his heart or brain would have taken the brunt of the burst; though we can presume he would have died faster simply because of the relative speed of death based on organ function.

                                              So something to consider might be to how vampire bases the amount of damage on surface area of exposure to fire or sunlight. Many of the victims of the demon core experiments survived for years or even decades (to the point where some are considered to have died from other causes) because they only got a glancing exposure.
