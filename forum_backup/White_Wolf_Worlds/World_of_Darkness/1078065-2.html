    Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post
    I'm not a fan of the GNS model, as I feel it implies a certain degree of mutual exclusivity between the features it sorts into each category, which I don't think is inherent to those features. That said, what I like about games that GNS would tend to place on the more Simulationist end of the spectrum is that they tend to place heavy importance on internal consistency in the game rules, which makes it easier for players to reliably evaluate their options and make decisions with full information. This makes these games much more player-friendly than games that fall more on the Narrativist end of the spectrum. Narrativist games tend to appeal more to the same forms of engagement that GMing appeals to, which is all well and good, but the people who want that kind of engagement usually gravitate more towards being GMs than players. This often results in narrativist-style games being very popular with GMs, but their players feeling unsatisfied with the game, without fully understanding why.

    Ultimately, I prefer Simulationist games as a GM because they make it easier for me to create a more enjoyable experience for my players. I prefer Narrativist games as a player because they allow me to engage with the game in a more GM-like way.

    Honestly I think this falls into a big falicy of equating rules Crunch with Simulationism. I can make a game about Vietnam and just use the raw statistics of survival rates roll on a chart to determine based on those statistics if the character dies. The player has no control. But its a very accurate simulation of something, probably more accurate than a heavy crunch high pc options. Conversely I can make the same game more narrative by giving them reliable hard rules for changing the odds.

    I think the bigger break between types of games is some people want to engage in the Meta. They want to tell a story about a character. Some people want to play a Character. Some people want to "Win". SOme people want to engage in the mechanics of the system for the sake of engaging in the system. And there are degrees of how much a person values what and it changes over time.

        Originally posted by An Fhuiseog View Post
        Interesting takes on GNS, I've often found it a rather good basic approximation in that the people I have played with's motivations are:
        Play a balanced &quot;game&quot;, is mechanics navigation and mastery.
        Explore a world/setting
        Tell a story

        People I have met tend to only like two out of the three and their reason for disliking a game often boils down to it weighing toward the third at the expense of one of their motivations. (The other reasons for not liking a game have always been either &quot;edition war&quot; stuff or just finding the games central concept/theme boring)
        It's not a bad approximation for casual purposes, but it kind of breaks down past a certain point. I'm wary of the simulationism/narrativism split because "simulationist" games can and often do have a heavy focus on narrative, especially if they are focused on simulating a particular genre of fiction, and "narrativist" games are often quite capable of simulating believable worlds. Furthermore, game design and balance are important aspects of any game, regardless of focus on narrative or simulation.

        Personally, I prefer to analyze individual mechanics rather than games as a whole, and I prefer to distinguish between mechanics that cast the player in the role of actor (imagining themselves as their character and making decisions as they feel their character would), and mechanics that cast the player in the role of director (imagining themselves as co-creators in the story and making decisions for their character that they feel would improve the story). The former works best when the game mechanics are designed with the purpose of creating an internally consistent world, and the latter works best when the game mechanics empower the player to take control of the narrative, so they generally align with GNS's simulationism and narrativsim respectively. But, the actor/director model presents simulation and narration as properties of the game mechanics, instead of part of a trichotomy with game mechanics, and it puts the focus more distinctly on the type of engagement the mechanics demand of the players.

                    That's certainly the most important part for me. I always had a struggle to internalize the concept of "telling a story about a character" as a gaming style and goal.                     I think this is very common among people who prefer to be players rather than GMs in roleplaying games.

                    I have to be really careful here, because I'm about to say something that can sound like an attack against "narrativist" games. But I sincerely do not mean it as such, and as I said, I prefer "narrativist" games when I am a player. But the thing is, the kind of engagement that narrativist or director-style mechanics demands, arguably, isn't roleplaying.

                    To unpack that a little, I would define roleplaying as imagining yourself as another person and/or in a different situation, and behaving as you imagine you or that person would in that situation. This is the kind of engagement I think most people who want to participate in roleplaying games as players are usually looking for. And simulationist or actor-style games are specifically designed to facilitate this. They aim to present a believable world, and to provide mechanics for executing the effects of the decisions players make while imagining themselves as characters in that world. Conversely, "narrativist" or director-style games often ask players to control their characters from a third-party perspective, making decisions based on what they would like to happen in the story, rather than what they would imagine themselves doing in the scenario taking place. This is a perfectly valid means of engagement, and it's great that games are available that appeal to that style of engagement, but "roleplaying" might not be the most accurate way to describe that form of engagement. I sometimes wonder if the hobby would be better served by drawing a clearer distinction between role-playing games and collaborative storytelling games.
