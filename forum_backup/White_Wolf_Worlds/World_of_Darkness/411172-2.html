        If the concern is "but I wanted to play a (fe)male member of that splat," then I would either not worry about it, or be ready to discuss what the player thinks that would look like. If you have a single-sexed group/splat, then there's likely a reason for it beyond "the writer said so." It's either a cultural/societal thing ("no girls allowed in our club" "Men are unfit to be here") or a law-of-nature restriction ("The Tribble Exalted only come into their power when they first give birth" "The zombie virus only attacks the Y-chromosome"). Ironically, in a world of magic, the latter are the more easily subverted. A society is not likely to break its taboo against accepting the wrong sex, but magic can say, "But somehow, this zombie-girl exists" or "this boy has become Tribble Exalted spontaneously." Then you have to start examining the consequences, socially, of this, as well as what mysterious plot-related circumstances caused it.

        Basically, if their concept DEMANDS they be the wrong sex for the splat, but that they also be the splat, they must have some reason already in mind for why it is. Discuss it with them, see if it meshes with any kind of story you'd like to tell, and decide based on that.

        There are gamers - am am amongst them - who see a restriction like that and think, "rules were made to be broken." Because the "what if...?" exception scenarios can be interesting, if done well. Or atrociously trite, if not.

            It has indeed sucked. But there are things you may not get from the written histories.

            I knew an older teacher who moved from the very Yankee state of Connecticut to backwoods Tennessee to take a job that would lower their loan repayment costs for their education. Each night while he was grading papers the janitor would come in and talk about family matters, frequently mentioning that his oldest boy needed new shoes, what size he needed, etc. This went on for a few weeks. Then one day the superintendent dropped by his classroom and explained that the janitors, at that time "the colored" were not paid enough to get by - therefore when they needed something they would mention it and the teachers would get what was needed for that man's family. Then the super informed him it was his turn to buy some shoes. Is this whole situation of enforced co-dependency demeaningly unacceptable? Certainly. But when observing the bad aspects of past societies it is good to pay attention to the social actions existing which balance things out.

            A friend's father grew up in Camden, NU during the great depression. Up the street lived a Black family who had some land and raised their own food. Occasionally, this generous and Christian family would slaughter several extra chickens. Then the wife would go to a neighboring white families and talk to their wives saying,"My fool of a boy killed too many chickens again, and we can't use them all before they go bad. Could your family help us by taking care of the extra so it won't go to waste?" Today's prejudice is in many ways more honest than yesterday's. It is an open seething contempt from all sides concerned. Yesterday's prejudice was based on an assumption of inferiority. An impoverished starving white family could not socially accept help from an "inferior", but could "help" them by eating the "accidental" surplus before it went bad.

            But it was not limited to mere problems of color. Another professor of mine once told me about his first love. When he proposed to her, his parents and her parents got together and put her in an insane assylum. He was Church of England, and English, and she was Belgian and Catholic. Both families were middle class. Both were unwilling to tolerate the scandal of their children marrying. I still think this should be a movie. (I just remembered that until 1973 homosexuals were routinely incarcerated in assylums and it was 1976 before Missouri Executive Order 44, ordering the legal extermination of Mormons was recinded.)

            I had another teacher in HS who was so used to being charitable, often giving a five or ten to organizations in the median at a stoplight (usually fire dept. or police dept. in our city) seeking donations. One morning on her way in, she rolled down her window and handed a man in the median (who was waiting to finish crossing the street) money. She wouldn't even have realised he wasn't asking for anything if her daughter in the passenger seat hadn't started laughing about it as they drove away. Don't forget that some are overly charitable to "the cause" of the moment.

            So could they be worth points as a flaw? Certainly. However, don't underestimate how being in the socially weak position can be manipulated as an asset. Don't avoid playing to such assets or providing conditions where the flaw is in effect an asset in an unexpected way.
