    To redirect back a bit to the thread's starting topic... one of the ongoing issues that no edition of VtM has ever really solved in a fashion that seems to suit most styles of play is how much the Disciplines represent something "real" in-universe or are abstractions. While some of the WoD games (ex: WtA) are very clear that such powers are in-universe elements that your characters can discuss and think about conceptually, and others are very unclear about it (ex: Mage), VtM has always kinda insisted on some balancing point between Disciplines being something vampires actually know about and can study and teach, and something more abstract.

    Obviously the real driver on this is that VtM drew from inspirations where vampires had specific powers, but they were vaguely defined and frequently never really named (Anne Rice's vampires with their dark gifts also set a precedent for the idea of vampires having unique powers that seemed to inform VtM's proliferation of Disciplines and the way Elder Disciplines were handled). So VtM vampires are supposed to know that there is a vampire way of mind controlling people, that is commonly called Dominate, but despite it existing mechanically in a defined predictable cluster of powers, the games never want you to say, "I use the fourth power in the Discipline of Dominate," in-character.

    This tension has always made addressing some of the issues (if we could even all agree specifically what those are) more difficult than it needs to be otherwise, because there is some level of in-universe knowledge of the Disciplines that means you can't just make major changes to them as you could if they were purely meta-mechanics that weren't predictable enough to be classified and known by the characters.

    Just like how Oblivion in V5 causes issues in the lore because Disciplines are at least partially "real" concepts to vampires still, we saw similar issues with things like the Dominate vs. Dementation Malkavian lore weirdness.

            one of the ongoing issues that no edition of VtM has ever really solved in a fashion that seems to suit most styles of play is how much the Disciplines represent something "real" in-universe or are abstractions.
            My own take was always that they were mostly "real". From their descriptions to their names, and the flourish in the names of the specific powers, it does seem to me that Vampires think of them as distinct and classified sets of powers, and do think of them as having an expected progression, at least until level 5.

            On most Disciplines, the difference between levels isn't either intensity nor general understanding of principles, but the full development of an ability that wasn't previously there, each level doing something other levels just don't. There are exceptions, but this general trend remains even within V5's framework. So while the development of a new level may occur gradually within the fiction, the perception of such powers as distinct developments within the framework of a Discipline seems the most intuitive take.

            Physical Disciplines are an obvious exception, where they probably are seen as a single power that can be improved in a continuum that wouldn't be equated to levels, even if mechanically they have them.

                The problem is this is a very high investment sort of fun.

                If you're a completely new to VtM player, who's just read the core book of whatever edition your group is playing, this approach means nothing. It's a bunch of names - most of which are canon NPCs your ST is never going to use - and have no meaning because your Generation and additional traits like Prestigious Sire or Mentor are going to matter far more than a genealogy will.

                If you're really deep into the lore of the game? Yeah, these sorts of things can be fun. It's also a cool way to help show off the differences in Clan culture by showing which Clans think your lineage is really important, which Clans do it because they have to put up with the first group, and which Clans don't really care about it on various levels.

                Though, that said, I have found WtA to do better at getting people into this than VtM because you don't get the ramp up in power as you go back, and your ancient ancestors are all dead anyway. There's a lot more freedom to come up with custom ancestors to suit your PC and the tone of the group, and since the Garou can actually summon ancestor spirits (well, this is a VtM thread so I'm going to not get into the W5 issues) it matters more than just the more traditionalist and formality valuing Tribes having a social value to reciting their genealogies. Being able to say, "Hey, my great-great-great-great-grandma fought something that sounds awful familiar to this, lets see if the Theurges can get her advice," makes it rewarding to have those details. It makes the sting of not having ancestor-spirits readily accessible to your Tribe a lot more real when it means you have to summon up other people's ancestors and try to butter them up for the indignation of being asked to talk to someone that isn't part of their family.


                Requiem clearly wanted to replicate this to make genealogy more relevant to its vampires. While Requiem's setup does make deep genealogies somewhat shallow in meaning even for very dedicated players, Requiem does have a lot of ways to benefit in-game by detailing at least your sire and grandsire. By getting rid of Generation and one original vampire, Requiem also lets you have genealogical depth vary in at least social importance. You never get stuck on "well, we're now automatically in the heavy hitters of the 4th Gen, and then the mythological dangers of the 3rd and 2nd Gens, and then Caine after that." You can have a game where running into a vampire that's 10 generations back from you doesn't have to mean an epic world shaking badass (it can if you want that, it just doesn't have to be). Requiem's approach clearly can't satisfy everyone - as nothing can - but IME it gives more reason to engage with vampire genealogy that will really come up in game compared to VtM which has lots of interesting canon NPCs you could descend from but most games that'll never really matter.

                                    Originally posted by Shadeprowler View Post
                                    Again, not essential and the game is fine and neat without it - but I always believed it would have been better if we would had that lore
                                    The problem isn't the playstyle, but the need to attach it to lore.

                                    A list of names such as that wouldn't be a small thing to do, to begin with. You have to cover several Generations, for several Clans, splitting Lineages within those clans, spread them across cities, or at least regions. This is a lot of work. And the end result of this work isn't freedom, but restriction, because your list doesn't necessarily fit the needs of the people who'll actually make stories with it, be them writers working on a novel or sourcebook, STs making their cities, or even players making their characters, not to say the extra work those people must put into learning those lists.

                                    It's why Heavy Arms said Requiem's setup works better to do this. On top of 2nd edition rules for lineage sympathy, the fact is that with a system where there's far less need to link your lineage back to a host of important NPCs, it becomes actually easier to make up your own lineage from scratch, dating back as far as you want, tracing it to known NPCs only if it serves you in any way.

                                    While it's true that the game was partially built with the idea of tracking lineages being a very important thing, and that doing so may improve the game experience, making out lists upon lists of such lineages won't really solve the problem, it will make for lineages that don't feel organic and puts pressure without reward for most participants.

                                                That's not actually true.

                                                While Islam came to Africa in the 7th century (not as early as Christianity in Europe, if still early), it's only strong in North Africa and a bit in its East Coast, which is not KotEK's focus point. Eritrea and Ethiopia have a strong and ancient Christian presence that persists today, as do a few enclaves in the north (which was dominated by early Christianity before Islamic conquest), but for most of Africa's history they weren't sizable in comparison with the continent at large. Semitic peoples, mostly Hebrew, did exist elsewhere in Africa possibly since a long time ago, like the Beta Israeli, Lemba and Yibir, but they were completely isolated from the Near East and the Mediterranean world until very recently.

                                                Even considering those parts, most of Africa was still on indigenous religions up to the end of the 19th century, and only started to convert in bulk to Christianity during the 20th century. And they did surpass Islam in the continent in general, so Muslims are not a majority outside their own countries in northern Africa. In the end we have a small Semitic presence consigned to Egypt or cut from the rest, then Christianity arriving in the 1st Century, Islam in the 7th century, but all of those only taking any hold in sub-saharan Africa in the 20th century, fairly recent for vampires.

                                                Add to that the fact that our ides of the African continent as a geographic category is fairly recent, and again isn't the focus of KotEK. North Africa had some contact with the south, sure, but it was far more linked to the Mediterranean for most of history. Egypt and Sudan (Nubia) had more contact with the Near East than with anyone else up until Macedonian arrival integrated them even more with the larger Mediterranean community. Even when smaller, the Sahara was a far more daunting barrier than the sea, so north and sub-saharan Africa are quite different areas even today. Talking about North Africa as more related to Africa at large than the Middle East and Europe doesn't really work.

                                                In this context, Noddism is thoroughly rooted in the Middle East. Only developments pertaining to early Christianity would have any big chance at happening in North Africa and even then that was a world away from sub-saharan Africa. North Africa, especially Egypt, was already plenty covered in other books as far as covering foreign regions goes for White Wolf. KotEK focused on an area that really had no reason to have a strong Noddist presence until very recently, and even then mostly through colonists and Neonates.
