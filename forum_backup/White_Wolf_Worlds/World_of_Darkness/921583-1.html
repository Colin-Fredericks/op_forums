    Originally posted by loomer View Post

    Not really, but most of those cases aren't that common and surprisingly, they often don't pay the millions of dollars claimed in terms of 'real pay' because the costs are often through the roof as well. The lawyer's payment for a long, high profile case might seem high, but it goes to a team of lawyers, paralegals, expert witnesses, lost opportunity costs from missed work, travel costs, rental costs, filing fees, etcetera.

    The best pay doesn't come, typically, from the high profile cases unless the client is filthy rich. It comes from working for the large, high-flying corporate law firms and reaching partnership level. This is also the hardest slog a lawyer faces - it's not uncommon to work 60+ hours a week, as Garkhal says, as a senior associate at those places, and when you're a junior, all bets are off and you better not plan on doing anything but eating, breathing, sleeping, and shitting company business on a salary that's really only adequate for someone working 40 hours a week. It's the trade-off - you work hard and suffer until you hit relatively easy street, but even then, you're probably still working a surprising number of hours for a person of your wealth.

    Most lawyers also don't retire after getting a high profile case. That's the worst career move - if you win a high profile case, or put up a damned good case that loses because it was unwinnable rather than because you're incompetent, you're going to want to keep practicing because clients will flock and doors will open. Stephen Jones, the lawyer who lead McVeigh's defence team, is still in practice today to use one of your examples. On the OJ example, Cochran, Shapiro, Dershowitz, and several others all remained in practice for years or still practice. Law is not a field that encourages young retirement - as you age and grow in experience, demand grows for you, and with it the opportunities to make good money grow. It only slackens if you start to lose or lose your faculties, or if factors unforeseen (e.g. your entire field of specialty is no longer needed (looking at you, marijuana specialists!) because it's no longer a crime.) rise to complicate affairs.

        It depends on a lot of factors.

        Though neither is really viable as a trial lawyer (vampires - too much stuff that needs done during the day - and Garou have the Curse). There's still plenty of kinds of lawyers out there who rarely actually have to deal with people outside of their firm/company.

        Vampires are, in general, going to run into the "hey, this guy doesn't age" issue since lawyers inherently have to deal with other people. A vampire that wants to stay involved in the law would probably be best served by using Ghoul proxies that rely on the vampire's mundane and supernatural skills to advance their cases.

        Garou struggle with a different problem; but a similar conclusion. Garou old enough (Rank and Gifts) to make interacting with humans more viable, and a young law student/fresh lawyer are looking at trying to balance to very time intensive efforts. Assuming a Garou manage to balance their duties with the difficulty of getting a degree in the first place, they're stuck in the same situation as vampires. They'd do their best helping kinfolk lawyers rather than being the face of their firm.

                The kind of rolls would vary a lot; but they don't all have to fall on the same person (hence bigger trials tend to have teams of lawyers on top of the standard support staff). You need Intelligence and Wits for the legal maneuvering that goes into establishing the facts, evidence, spotting technicalities, etc. Politics and Bureaucracy can deal with some of of the outside-of-the-court-room dealings that might need to happen. In a jury trial, you need at least one lawyer that has the social stats to influence the jury; there's plenty of different approaches that could be used like any other social roll.

                Speaking of the support staff, you've got some number of paralegals doing a lot of the grunt work on the law side of things, and any bigger firm has their own private investigator (or more) around in case they want to dig into things.

                As a side note, Pentex can't claim eminent domain. Eminent domain is a power of the state. Pentex would have to get a government official of the appropriate level (varies) to lead the effort (which isn't necessarily hard, but it isn't a hill even very corrupt politicians want to die on if there's any significant public resistance).
