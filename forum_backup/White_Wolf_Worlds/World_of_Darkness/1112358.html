              To actually answer your questions, though:

              1) If the damage taken by a character is of a type they are capable of soaking (mortals, by default, can only naturally soak Bashing damage), the character rolls their Stamina attribute (difficulty 6), with any Armor adding its rating to that pool. Each success on that roll subtracts health levels from the damage taken. If you get punched for five damage but roll three successes on soak, you only take two health levels of damage. For Lethal or Aggravated, mortal characters (which include mages, unless they have a Life 3 effect running that specifically lets them soak the aforementioned damage types) can only soak with their Armor rating, if any. If you get stabbed, and have no Armor, you take all the damage; if you had Armor of a rating of 1, you soak only with that 1 die.


              2) Your player will make two rolls for this: an Arete roll to determine if the magickal Effect happens at all (and how potent it is), and a Dexterity + Martial Arts roll to determine if the character hits anything with it. I said Martial Arts because you specified that the character was using Karate, which would fall under the Martial Arts ability. They might alternatively use Brawl (for an informal fighting skill), or the Do ability (if the character is a member of the Akashayana/Akashic Brotherhood). Either way, the results of these two rolls determine what happens. If the character rolls no successes on Arete, the magic just doesn't happen. If they roll no successes on Dex + Martial Arts, the magic may happen, but they failed to connect with it. If both succeed, you calculate damage based on the Damage/Duration table; 2 health levels per success, with an extra success (and therefore 2 additional health levels) because the Effect involved Forces (damaging Effects that don't include Forces do not get this bonus, nor does this bonus apply if the roll generated no successes naturally).

              This general pattern applies to all attack Effects that need to be aimed as part of the character's Focus. Hand-to-hand attacks require Dex + Brawl/Martial Arts/Do, hand-held weapons require Dex + Melee, thrown weapon attacks require Dex + Athletics, and ranged weapon attacks usually require Dex + either Firearms or Archery or Energy Weapons. An Effect that targets a person directly or attacks an area skips this, and just rolls Arete. Pay attention not only to what the Sphere Effects are, but how the character enacts them through their Focus. An Akashic might throw a fire punch, an Etherite might discharge their flame thrower, a witch might generate a fireball in her hand and throw it, a Hermetic might point a wand and make fire erupt from the ground, and a Euthanatos might lock eyes with the enemy and cause them to spontaneously combust.


              3) Damage from Magick should consult the Damage/Duration table, as specified above. Mundane attacks deal damage according to various ways, specific to how you dealt it. All of the dice pools from the previous paragraph apply here when it comes to determining a hit (difficulty dependent on what attack you use), and afterwards you use a damage dice pool determined by what caused it. Hand-to-hand, Melee, and thrown weapon attacks use Strength as the dice pool, plus any modifiers from the type of weapon. Guns have a fixed damage dice pool, and Energy Weapon attacks are...special cases. Since they're usually Magickal by nature, see the Damage/Duration table.


              4) I don't think there's any mandate as to when a character can do which actions in their turn. If you have the movement or action(s) left, you can probably pull it off. There are no Bonus Actions in this game, though; you have an Action, half your max movement (or a full movement, if you forgo the action), and maybe some free actions like talking or the like. Combat turns in Mage last anywhere from 3 to 10 seconds. You CAN take more actions in a turn, but doing so forces you to divide your dice pools (take the lowest dice pool among the actions you'll do, and divide the dice between all the actions), unless you use Time 3 to make the character move faster.


              5) While you can always keep the Paradox pool a secret if you really want to, there isn't much point in doing so. Whether you're using the 2ed/M20 rules or the Revised rules, the amount of Paradox points obtained is predictable and fixed. So long as you, the Storyteller, have answered two questions - "Is the Effect Vulgar?" and "Are there Sleeper witnesses?" - the players can always calculate how much Paradox they've obtained on their own. You don't roll to determine how many Paradox points a character receives; the value is fixed for the type of Effect used (or botched).


              6) I think it IS unfair to punish players in that way. If players aren't paying attention, shouldn't you talk to them first, before making their characters suffer? Of course, it's your game, and you can do what you want. But this sounds like the sort of behavior that will create a hostile gaming environment.

                              Originally posted by The3obaFett View Post
                              Hey guys! Thanks for the approval. My name is Joe, and I've got a couple years experience as a player in D&D.
                              Hey Joe!
                              Welcome to storytelling mage, it's pretty hardcore - and you've got 8 players. Wow. Hats off to you.

                              I think others have answered your questions above, but I just want to emphasise the core mechanics of wod games:

                              Each player gets a dice pool to do stuff, usually stat+skill. They roll those dice against a certain difficulty, standard being 6+. Each 1 cancels a success. If they do that cancelling and still end up with a 1 left over, they botch. If they get one success after cancelling, it's a marginal success. If they get more than 1, improve the result accordingly.
                              You can (and should) lower or raise the difficulty based on circumstances, and there's a whole load of difficulties listed for specific combat maneuvers in the combat section of the rulebook.
                              Casting spells has a fixed difficulty based on the highest sphere in the rote and whether it's coincidental, vulgar or vulgar with witnesses, and uses arete as dice pool.
                              Trying to do multiple tasks at the same time (two weapon wielding being the classic example) requires the player to split their dice pool between the two tasks.

                              There's other aspects of the system like specialisms which add variation, and resonance and quintessence can modify the difficulty of spells, but that's more advanced.

                              Regarding your massive mob of players, I'd heartily recommend you take a leaf out of the MET (larp) player's handbook, and recruit an assistant storyteller (ast). Work together to keep the players occupied and the story moving forward - you might enjoy creative sessions more with a partner as well :-)
                              That way you can be working with half the group at a time each, or have sub-groups on side plots without losing flow.

                              Edit: actually the way multiple actions work depends on the version of the rulebook you have - so check it thoroughly!

                                              I agree in all points.

                                              Also, The3obaFett , a thing that I cannot stress enough is..if your players are coming from D&D and are used to be the hardcore monster slayer heroes...well. You can play Mage like that, certainly. But a point you need to drive home either way is: Mages are humans. Most Mages were normal, everyday humans with friends, family, a job, and a very normal life until they suddenly awakened.

                                              They got raised by normal parents, went through normal school, had a First Childhood Crush, were possibly bullied (or possibly were a bully..), might or might not be in college debt or be struggling to move up in the world ..all the normal sins, quirks, normalicies and things that come with growing up in a n oh so mundane spot.

                                              Not *every* Mage of course - there are oddballs, gutter rats, members of fringe cultures, children that grew up in Mage-tainted surroundings (or were downright children of Mages..which does *not* automatically make them awaken!), but on the average, a modern Mage had a modern, normal life before.

                                              The reason I am making this very point is, that Mage is heavy on the 'What does it mean to be human?' and 'What would a normal person do if they could suddenly do Magic?' questions. Don't get me wrong - old Mages can be downright alien and inhuman. Mentally and physically. But a big amount of the Mage's life, and possibly to the end, they carry around all the baggae that comes with a normal upbringing.

                                              And this includes having to deal with all that happens in your head when you might be, suddenly, forced to hurt or kill another human being. And being a Mage in the World Of Darkness, that moment comes sooner than later.


                                              An alternative to all that would be to look in the direction of "Dark Ages: Mage", or "Mage: The Sorceror's Crusade", two WoD Mage gamelines that focus on the Dark Ages and the beginning of the Renaissance in the case of Sorceror's Crusade. The latter (Sorceror's Crusade) also uses the Magick system that the modern day Mage edition uses, and has a lot of sourcebooks.
                                              "Dark Ages: Mage" only has two books, but it has a very interesting take on how the Magick system mechanics work between the individual Mage groups - some people love it.


                                              EDIT:
                                              And just to add: What I wrote does not mean that the modern day Mage games can't be epic adventures. Oh, they can be epic. Grand. Weird, dark, utterly violent and/or deciding over the very fate of the game world. They can be as small and personal, or as reality shaking as you can possible imagine...
                                              I just wanted to point out where most Mages, before their awakening, are coming from

                                                  When it comes to crafting a story, I'd recommend the following steps.

                                                  1) Think about the kind of story you want to tell. This can be as grand as searching for 5 lost talismans that have been scattered across the world, to a simple story of finding a lost child's mother. Heck, you can even steal from shows or books you like. Mage can work really well in all kinds of plots. As long as you have an idea of a story you want to tell, you're good.

                                                  2) Ask your players what kind of story they want to play. This can be done individually or as a group. I usually recommend to do this one on one, but with 8 players, that would be quite the undertaking. If they have a basic idea for characters, or already have fleshed out characters, talk to them about this.

                                                  3) See where their contributions can be incorporated into your story, which elements match up, whether certain NPCs can actually be combined into the same person.

                                                  4) Add new stuff or refine old stuff to fill in the gaps.


                                                  As an example, I'll use my Keepers of the Wyck campaign.

                                                  1) Marauder running about town who believes in HP Lovecraft's world, causes breaks in the gauntlet, a lot of different factions want to stop the tears, but no one actually knows what's causing it yet.

                                                  2) I had 6 players, and they each wanted different stories. In no particular order they wanted: A spy/revenge story, a Film Noir, a verbena story with a personal quest to search for King Arthur, a combat focused story, and a story about screwing over the Technocracy with cyber thrills. The sixth player wasn't sure what kind of story they wanted, and were happy just to be a Verbena teacher in this world.

                                                  3) The factions I had in mind for the marauder were a Euthanatos mage, some Void Engineers, the NWO, A group of nephandi (who want to use them) and the marauder's sister, who just wants to find him. Noir detective could be hired by the sister, and have a Euthanatos femme fetale on their tail, and there was plenty of opportunity to screw the technocracy over on their investigations. Combat could be easily done by having a lot of spirits falling through the holes of the gauntlet.

                                                  Meanwhile, I've got two verbena people, and one person who wants a revenge story. Perhaps they want revenge against a verbena person, linking these people up.

                                                  4) I'll need to make a Verbena coven for the Verbena people to join, and have the person the Revenge person's target be one of the high acting members. Also, I can make their spy story work by letting them pretend to be Verbena in order to investigate them on behalf of their own tradition.
                                                  I then made this coven up, the NPCs for it, and called them the Keepers of the Wyck. How do they tie into the Marauder stuff? Quite simply, they are worried about all the spirits that are being flung into the natural world. Not strong enough? I can make one of the nephandi an ex member of the coven.

                                                  And there we go, got some connections started, got a setting underway, all I have to do now is start it off and adjust things depending on what the players do with it.

                                                          This is looking pretty positive.

                                                          If I were in your place, I'd have a sit down with these powergamers of yours and explain to them in plain terms that Mage's magic system is an extremely easy system to break. If they try to powergame it, then they will be able to do so very easily, and as a result destroy the game entirely. I would say to them that this is not the kind of game that can be played like this, and urge them to not think about levelling up or developing a good build, but just thinking about the story they want to tell and the journey of their character's arc. If they then told me that they wouldn't have any fun if they didn't power game it, I'd say that we have too many people anyway, so it might be a better idea for them to sit this one out.

                                                          Personally, I would recommend reading Revised as a starting point. M20 has a lot going for it, but the book is over 700 pages long. Also, I honestly think Revised explains there spheres a heck of a lot better than M20 does, which is light on examples and tends to assume you already understand how the spheres work from previous editions.

                                                          This is an interesting number of characters you've got here. Just so you know, being awakened is a pretty binary thing. Either their avatar is awake and allowing them to do magic, or it isn't.

                                                          Personally I think it's a shame you didn't give them a chance to chose a tradition for themselves as a tradition can really help one figure out their paradigm/focus as well as how they view the universe and magic in general. Having their tradition be thrust upon them by a random choice of object feels a bit like choice taken away from the player. But it's your game, so I can't really say anything. Perhaps your game will be about the struggle between free choice and destiny.
