            Originally posted by Aleph View Post
            And I think that's cool, but also understand why the orthodox might complain.

            About the &quot;Narrative Mechanic&quot;. The very concept of such it's anathema, because any mechanic can be abused by its technicians . WoD was created with the absolute and undisputed power of the Storyteller in mind. You can use to set your players free, to chain them, or to have a middle ground (say, by tailoring Ur-Shulgi to the players. If the players are expected to have Time Stop magic, Of Course that Ur-Shulgi has created a ritual that protects against that. You are protected by the fact that the guy hasn't a &quot;normal&quot; character sheet, until you give him one).
            You're saying the game gives you the right to railroad? Wow, it's not like any other system has that. Except that both M&M and Fate have mechanics that allow for low level railroading, with the idea that you'll give them a Hero Point or Fate Point as compensation (in Fate you can refuse the Fate Point, but that game really empowers the players). Railroading isn't something that began with the cWoD, and it isn't unique to it. cWoD is a simulationist game that just reminds you to consider the story. I lose nothing by converting it.

            Of course that the players haven't solid rules (that they could abuse) to give them &quot;Narrative Powers&quot;. If the Storyteller wants, he can give an Artifact or something to &quot;empower&quot; the players into doing the impossible. The Storyteller has many tools to make any sort of number crunching useless. I think than that's the point (because I've read such reasoning in many of the books. It may be a wrong reasoning, of course.)
            I don't want that, I want a game where the players aren't encouraged to go along with my plot, but where they are empowered to help create the plot. Otherwise you end up with the Haight problem and railroading (choo choo!) instead of narrative play.

            And all of this isn't to weaken the simulationism:

            Helios it's the supreme spirit of the Sun, necessary for it's existence, and the basic book of Werewolf states that destroying Helios should be as difficult as destroying the actual Sun, if not more.

            How many &quot;life points&quot; has the Sun?, how big has to be your stick to destroy it?.
            Several thousand health levels, along with a power that makes it basically intangible and that within radius X things take damage just from being there. How easy is it to destroy the sun when your stick just melts away?

            But then players start adding heat shields to their sticks so they last just long enough to hit Helios. Let's hope they can survive his counterattacks.

            THAT might explain why the ortodox might find more than a little wierd to defeat such a power with the &quot;average&quot; damage that can be caused by a furry, or by a guy with Strength 10 or so. If you preffer to make spirits much weaker than that, it's OK (specially in Demon, that it's a different game, with different asumptions) For Werewolf, however, it's more unorthodox than playing a Salubri with three heads.
            The problem I'm having is the massive amounts of 'wrongbadfun' that was said to me. I have no problems with people using stateless entities, I just wanted to give my take.
