    Your hypothetical Mage 30th edition

    03-04-2024, 02:40 PM
    So I didn't see anything mentioning it in any of the other threads, but Mage is 30 years old now as of August last year, so huzzah! confetti poppers, etc.

    Considering 20th Anniversary edition is itself almost 10 years old now, I started wondering what people thought needed updated in the settings for the past decade?

    Note: This is not a Mage 5th edition thread. Based on other 5th edition books if and when that happens it will be more of a soft reboot. Also lets not bring frustrations over 5th edition here. I more interested in the communities hypothetical 30th anniversary edition so to speak.

    Off the top of my head, one thing I'd change is the Mercurial Elite, and well... just drop it all together. Onyx Path has had nearly ten years to flesh the concept out/differentiate them from Hermetic Technomagic Houses, and the best The Guide to the Traditions could do was basically "Hermitic techno wizards demand the true names of tech spirits, where the Mercurial Elite respectfully brute force guess it." There is plenty that can be done to expand on the Virtual Adepts concept without leaning on mysticism as a crutch, the old vs new guard can easily be re envisioned has a "hardware power vs programming skills" dynamic, and between VR/AR's resurgence in public consciousness, as well as virtual currencies and AI, the name Virtual Adept seems far from dated anymore.

      20th Anniversary editions were, for the most part, compilations of the previous editions, which lead to massive tomes that tried to fit everything into one book. For a 30th Anniversary Edition, I'd prefer something that doesn't try to fit everything in one book: plan to do a series of supplements to flesh out what the core book can't handle, and swim for the core book to be comparable to the size of M2 or MRev.

      That means two things: first, whoever writes the thing needs to be very tight on their writing; you're going to need to pack as much content into as little word count as possible. Second: even with that, you're going to need to be very selective about what you include. You will not be able to cover everything that M20 covers, not in the core book.

      Reorganize the book so that Focus goes front and center in character creation, having it be crafted in Step One, before you even get to the point of choosing which faction (if any) your mage belongs to. In my ideal book, I'd want something like Prism of Focus to be integrated into the Magick system, and have that be on the front end of the Magick system, not tacked into the back like an afterthought. When you get to the Spheres, I'd want some hardcore rule mechanics on the development staff to go through the system with a fine-tooth comb and turn it into something not unlike what the Magick system in Mage: the Awakening is like.

      I would prioritize these sorts of things, to the point that if there's not enough room, I'd rather cut things like the Traditions et al in order to make room for a proper Magick system.

      More thoughts later.

      My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

              I would want, first and foremost, a streamlined and consistent spheres system, with the spheres having similar and easily understandable progressions and purviews. If spheres can't have the same progression then similar spheres should have similar progressions. There should be generalized rules that cover commonly occurring permutations like locking effects and setting conditions, that are easy to understand and give an indication of how to approach more niche situations. It should be clear that the game rules are game rules, not in universe metaphysics. If the magic system is done right, it shouldn't need to take up so much of the book.

              After that, I'd focus on fleshing out the character of the setting. The various places (the Digital Web, High Umbra, etc) should be described as characters in their own right, with a focus on how they feel and why they are important. Breathe life into the world and make it a place the characters want to visit. That also includes the Ascension War itself. It's not a place, but it heavily impacts places and I'd like to see more focus on the fallout and strangeness it unleashes.

              I also think its time to go back to the drawing board on the Traditions. I don't mean throw them out entirely, but many of them have origins that are incompatible with the groups they represent, usually because those origins are based on modern occult groups rather than real historical groups (or believable fictional ones). Remove those odd origins and let the Traditions be more like who they are meant to represent.

                  In the pre-M20 editions, your choice of Tradition largely determined how you're Magick looks, such that if you wanted to play a witch, you did it by making a Verbena. M20 took what had previously been the Theory and Practice sections of the Tradition write-ups and made them stand-alone choices of Paradigms and Practices under the new Focus section; so now, if you want to play a witch, you make a mage with the Practice of Witchcraft. If your mage isn't engaging in Council politics, there's no longer any need for her to be a Verbena.

                  In M30, I'd love to take that further: instead of each Tradition getting a two-page write-up and a position in the book that suggests that you pretty much have to choose a Tradition, I'd give each Paradigm and Practice a half-page spread (again, like Prism of Focus), and I'd reduce the Traditions, Conventions, and Crafts to two paragraph sketches, one of which would be akin to the Focus paragraph for the faction that's found in M20. Put more detailed write-ups for all of them in supplements: an M30 Guide to the Traditions, an M30 Guide to the Technocracy, and an M30 Guide to the Disparates. But you shouldn't need any of them to play the game.

                  My hacks: Trinity Continuum, Scion, Exalted, World of Darkness, Chronicles of Darkness

                                                          I'm mainly thinking that most of the discrepancies between a given Tradition and the practitioners that it's supposedly based on remain, but as “how the Tradition is different”. By contrast, I'd work hard to make sure that the Paradigms and Practices more faithfully reflect the styles of magic that they're supposed to represent. So, taking Faith and the Celestial Chorus as examples, the Practice of Faith should be written up to *ahem* faithfully represent how typical devotees of major world religions such as Christianity would see what Mage calls Willworking; meanwhile, the Celestial Chorus is a Tradition based on a pseudo-Theosophic philosophy that has claimed to speak for all monotheists in the past; though that's held a significant dose of hypocrisy that until recently was tolerated out of the necessity of joining a Tradition in order to benefit from the Council of Magick.

                                                          My own vision of the new Council of Magick would be one that has abandoned the “Nine Seats” model: in the past, when that was in place, mages who only kind of fit into a given Tradition's beliefs often had to bite their tongues in order to have representation on the Council; but without the Nine Seats model, and with a more innovative “open representation” model facilitated by the likes of the Mercurial Elite, the new Council has seen the old Traditions lose a lot of their former influence as individual mages can contribute to council business more directly. It ceases to be “The Council of Nine Mystical Traditions”, and simply becomes “The Council of Magick”.

                                                          Basically, the Traditions are still there; but they're the “old guard”. The new generation of the Council of Magick embrace a much more individualistic arrangement. This does a couple of things: it lets you keep the Traditions in the game, but portray them in a less flattering light than earlier editions did and also make them optional; while at the same time, it enables you to portray the Ascension War more directly as individualists on the Council's side vs. conformists on the Technocracy's side, if you want to. And it does so by letting you downplay the paradox of the supposedly individualistic side operating in groups.

                                                          Alternately, it lets you play up the new conflict between the old-time Traditionists and the new wave of independent mages, with the former struggling to maintain relevancy while the latter struggle to deal with the unintended consequences that come with ditching the structure that the Traditions provide. Can this new vision of a Tradition-free Council work? Or will the experiment fail, with the Traditions eventually reasserting their authority when it is shown that some level of organization and structure is needed?
