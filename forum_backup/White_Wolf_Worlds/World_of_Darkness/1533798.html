                          And note that most of these spells we are discussing are nigh instantaneous. There's no need and no benefit to locking a spell like that as it doesn't need to travel with the subject nor does it stick around long enough to be a drain on the mage's concentration. Locking becomes important when spells have duration.

                          There are offensive spells that could reasonably be locked to a subject, like a slow moving fireball that always moves towards someone, but these are considerably rarer than the instantaneous kind,


                          ----

                          On another note, M20 says you only need locking when the spell doesn't already include the sphere that governs the pattern you are locking to. This is, in my opinion, a poor way of understanding what is going on. You can look at it that way, but I find a better way of saying this is that the locking rules are still being used. That is, a Life 4 spell to make someone bald is actually a Life 4/Life 2 spell but we only state the highest rank of the sphere needed.

                          Ultimately both explanations work, so use whichever one is easier to understand.

                                  Exact same shape as the wall used to be? Or exact same shape as the liquid is now?

                                  It should be that second one. The general idea is that when a spell ends, the effects of the spell end but anything that happened because of those effects persists. A thrown fireball lights things on fire and that fire persists after the fireball spell ends. A magical jetpack might cease working but the person wearing it doesn't go back to their launch point, they just fall. Turn a wall into liquid and it only stays liquid for the duration of the spell, but the shape it takes because it is a liquid persists.

                                  But there's a lot of wiggle room in this so do what most fits the paradigm of the spell and the needs of your table.

                                          We are delving into one of the fuzzy areas of the Mage rules. There are some changes that seem like they should be permanent. If a mage turns a brick into a rock, intuitively it seems it should stay a rock and there's often no dramatic consequence to it staying a rock. On the other hand, in folklore there are examples of permanent transformations and temporary ones. Alchemists sought to turn lead into gold permanently, but faerie gold only lasted a short time.

                                          The short answer is that the duration rules exist and are the default. Spells run out and we have to figure out what that means. The main options I see are:

                                          1) Reality returns to what it was

                                          When the spell wears out reality gets as close as possible to what it was. The liquified wall would return to close to its original shape, the metal would go back to cardboard, and the chain returns to a mace. This doesn't generally match examples from the book, but it is an option.


                                          2) The direct effects stop, any consequences remain

                                          This is the description I gave before. When the spell runs out, it's direct effects end. This gets a little tricky based on what we count as the direct effects. The direct effect of the wall melt was turning it liquid, so it goes back to solid. The direct effect of the cardboard to metal was changing the material, so it goes back to cardboard.

                                          Changing a mace to a chain could be several things. It could be directly changing the form, in which case it will revert, or it could be making it malleable and quickly using that malleability to change its shape, in which case the shape stays. Use whichever one is mechanically and paradigmatically appropriate.


                                          3) Reality returns to the closest normal

                                          In this scenario reality takes the simplest path to normality. The metal chain stays a chain because that is normal. The melted wall becomes a solid wall or a solid pile of bricks, whatever is closest to its melted shape because neither a liquid wall or a melted looking wall is normal.


                                          4) Do it by feel

                                          Some things feel like they should be permanent without issue and some do not. Go with your gut on which is which. My gut says the melting wall is too strange and will eventually go back to solid, while there's nothing abnormal about solid metal so the cardboard to metal and metal chain can stay.


                                          Regardless of which method you choose, you are going to get into situations where you have to make a judgement call. I say, go with 2, 3, or 4 and make adjustments as necessary.
