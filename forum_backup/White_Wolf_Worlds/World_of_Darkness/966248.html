      As you say, everyone is different, and every troupe is different. Heck, in my experience, every session is different, but there ARE some commonalities that I find work well for me.

      First, I approach every game as a player as well as the Storyteller (or DM or what-have-you). It's a thing that makes it far less adversarial – something that the Dungeon Master behind the screen in a D&D game seems to feed – and it gives me a larger stake in the outcome of the story we are telling. Since I've always had small troupes (three or four players maximum, sometimes only two), I'll even go so far as to create a character that I handle as if I were a player, too. You can see that as my first trick. I involve myself in character creation, award experience to my character as well as the others' characters, and generally fit myself into the plot lines such as I can splitting my attention with, you know, running the whole rest of the world. It doesn't always work, but I feel it's worth the effort. That leads directly to my second point of building trust.

      I feel like the behind-the-screen dynamic of a table really hinders feelings of trust between whoever runs the game and the players. Sometimes it's a non-issue (a good old-fashioned dungeon crawl is a great example), but at my table, I WANT players to feel free to tell me what's happening with their gaming experience. Open communication can be seen as a trick, but I think of it as more good manners. That is, I encourage them to say things like, 'We don't get it,' to the point where it's become short-hand at my table for players to say, 'Can we turn on the cheats?' (we're big videogamers, yeah?). I'm open to on-the-fly calls for assistance, and I ask players to do the same when I cannot remember something about one of their characters. That builds trust, but I'll take it even further.

      I want the players to know right away that I WILL cheat on rolls and rules, but I'll only do so to enhance the story. By saying that, I fully expect them to do the same, and I make that clear right from. So, as a second trick, I make it clear that I'll trust them to tell me their rolls without oversight as I expect them to trust me, BUT if I feel anyone is abusing the privilege, I'll revoke it! In practice, it has shaken out to mostly allowing rolls that have little dramatic impact to receive a 'courtesy roll' – 'Yeah mate, the dice say that... Why not?' – to succeed, but when it REALLY matters, we watch the dice closely. Also, botches are handled mostly for their dramatic impact rather than as any 'mechanical' considerations. Figure that's another trick, to let botched rolls 'hang' if they fall at a moment where they won't be exciting or interesting enough. I mean, a botch on a roll to search a warehouse isn't that great, but I allow it to express later in the same scene, usually allowing the player who rolled it decide when it will impact. Needless to say, I award extra experience when this is done well. This has become a lot easier to articulate at the table after the Chronicles of Darkness (i.e. new World of Darkness's second edition) came out, and I feel no compunctions about brazenly stealing from that system!

      Finally, I choose to remain open to more formal positive and negative feedback from everyone involved, but I ask that it be held on until the end of the session. Call this my last trick – though I see as just sensible management of resources – that we go over plot points afterwards to make sure what players did and did not catch in the game. I allow for hard questions (that I may or may not answer) and encourage whinging, as players are wont to do. It's amazingly helpful in ways that surprised me. For example, when I hear from them something that I hadn't expected or planned – but brilliant in a way I hadn't considered – it's like Christmas! For example, them thinking that one bloke's connexion to the Big Conspiracy (Ltd., all rights reserved) was completely different from what's in my notes, their misconceptions become facts after the fact. This is the real trick: taking the players' input as ways to improve the larger story. No doubt, such action demands the trust, cooperation, and open communication that I suggest above. In all, these have made for some very pleasant and memorable gaming for my troupe over the last fifteen years.

              Is this like, an advice thread? Just general shooting-the-shit? Cool.

              I always plan a big, in-depth, sweeping, sprawling, Chronicle, with copious and meticulous notes. And when I'm done with that, I immediately set it on fire. Because it's more satisfying for me to do it myself than watch my players do it.

              Then I take the major plot points and string them together very loosely, and trying to nudge my players in that direction.

              As for tricks/advice, if I give a character a name, I better sure-as-shit write it down with a brief description, because there is an absolute 100% chance that the players are going to want to talk to that character again at some point (and Steve can only be on vacation for so long). Even if that character was just some guy working a coffee cart who they asked for directions in the middle of a chase.

                      Usually the games I seem to get the best reaction from my players with are the "Open World" style games. I make the settings and the factions and the NPCs and hash out what each of their motivations along with short- and long-term plans... then I let the players get into the middle of it. The rest of the setting is continually changing based on their interference or lack of interference day by day over the course of the game... and sometimes that means they miss the action. Players might have went to go intimidate some guy on the east side of the city and then find themselves scratching their heads about that big column of smoke and all those sirens off to the west.

                      That style of game has historically gotten my players REALLY involved, because they're trying to triage events here while pursuing others there. Suddenly there's a very important difference between finishing the battle with 4 HLs of damage or 5, because no one really knows for sure how long the quiet will be before the next shoe drops, or because they know they only have so many days before something else they plan to attend to.

                      That said, that's also a pretty high-stress style for everyone involved. Sometimes you also just want to sit down, take charge of a monster, and go stomp around tokyo for a bit of catharsis. So... I also like to vary it up with some more linear sidequests every now and then for a break (or a flashback story, or a Past Lives story... just something where meticulous notetaking and a calender aren't required).

                          My response to this could go on for several thousand words, so I'll restrict myself to one trick I've been using for my most recent Vampire: The Masquerade game:

                          Have pictures of every NPC if possible. It helps visual learners to keep track of who's who, allows the Storyteller to lay out the pictures so the players can see who's in a scene at a glance, and it can be used for some neat tricks like turning over a picture to reveal an unexpected visitor or a monster appearing from nowhere. Having names under the pictures also helps to keep track of the cast - and it encourages the ST to name and visualise NPCs in advance, rather than coming up with NPCs on the fly and getting stuck for names and descriptions. It helps to have some 'spare' portraits ready in case new NPCs appear, and it can be restrictive when you imagine an NPC a certain way but can't find a suitable image - and it can take a while to sort through a stack of pictures once a game's been running for several months - but overall I think it's added quite a lot, especially in a game like Vampire with a large cast of recurring NPCs.

                              I'm a firm believer in letting the plot shape around the characters. Then, if the players really want something and it fits their characters, give it to them--but always with strings.

                              I usually plan one Big Bad, the main locations and NPCs, and a couple of dramatic twists, then I let the characters stomp all over my setting trying to find all the interesting bits. When characters ignore some clues and follow others, I adapt my story and sometimes shift motivations and narrative threads so they fit together. To keep people on their toes, I send them hunting for clues, surprise them with a fight, introduce a new character, throw in a red herring, etc. You've got to think of narrative beats and be prepared to come up with more obstacles and more tools as the story progresses.

                              The question I always ask myself is: 'Does this create more (or more interesting) conflict or does it just get in the way?' Deviations are encouraged, but I don't want to mislead characters by sending them on pointless distractions all the time. Those distractions either have to be folded back into the main story or the focus of the main story has to adapt.

                              I had a group submit character concepts where several people were members of one clan masquerading as another. I had a choice: either intervene and ask one or more players to change their concept, or roll with it, and make it a plot point. Now those characters have discovered the Prince was a Lasombra and soon will discover another of their allies isn't who he purports to be either. Meanwhile, the hunt by the visiting Archon for Sabbat infiltrators will of course cause them bother, and there's the bigger question: why are so many vampires here pretending to be from other clans? Is someone orchestrating this? Is it part of some inter-sect conflict?

                              I have answers to all these questions, but I reserve the right to change the answers if better ones come along or if the answers prove too obvious. The Big Bad might become an ally. An ally might become the secret puppet master. This Camarilla city may end up in the hands of some other group entirely... Or maybe the Camarilla orchestrated everything to undermine the local power structure and/or shake things up to dislodge some valuable people/items/secrets. Who can say? Right now, not even I can say definitively.

                              I'll also second the idea of suspended botches. They're a great idea.

                                      The most important tip I can give is to avoid single solution puzzles. The easiest and most organic way to achieve this is to simply not write a solution. Just write a story that presents problems, know how all NPC parties involved will behave and let your players hash out a solution of their own. No right answers, no wrong ones - it goes down how it goes down. Your story will develop drama and nuance naturally and you will avoid immersion killing prompting, railroading and adversarial ST/PC relations. It also allows you to write scenarios without neccesarily making them all about the PCs so the world feels bigger and more alive.

                                      I've often set players difficult social/political/polemic issues with no easy or obvious out (if any), played through the sessions and then been asked 'how did we do?" The players were almost invariably surprised to find that there was never a definitive solution or right direction. I, in turn as ST could be impressed with the solutions when they came up with something I didn't anticipate rather than being frustrated my plot fell apart.

                                      And what if players just straight up don't confront the issue? All the better for story development.

                                      Now granted, this method requires a well fleshed out setting and a good ability to think on your feet that increases in proportion with the scope of the story so it isn't for everyone. Nevertheless it yields really genuine, immersive experiences.
