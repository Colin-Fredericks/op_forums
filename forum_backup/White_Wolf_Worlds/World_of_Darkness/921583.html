              It also depends a bit on if you're using the pre-V20 version where Resource ratings are more defined, or the V20 version that's much more vague.

              I wouldn't actually put most doctors over 3 with the V20 version. Becoming a doctor often involves accruing a large amount of debt that results in their high wages not translating into the sorts of assets that Resources 4-5 assumes.

              Though this is also something that applies across professions. How you handle your money matters. I know people with six figure salaries that basically live at Resources 2 because they've horribly mismanaged their finances over the years, and others that make significantly less but would fall under the Resources 3 description by being very smart with their money.

              Age matters as well, simply because even in professions that are considered well paying, younger members of the profession generally get paid less (not just because they're starting, but because there are biases regarding age and promotions/raises*). As well, younger members of the profession are more likely to be in significant debt via education and/or have not had time to start building non-liquid assets.

              Then there's who you work for. Lawyers are a great example beyond what garhkal pointed out. An assistant district attorney, a public defender, and a defense lawyer for a major law firm, are all looking at wildly different pay rates everything else being equal. The DA's office is generally far better funded (and thus better paying) than the PD office, but in both you get stuck with the cases you get, while a private firm can select their cases and demand higher pay.

              -----

              * - As an example, my uncle went back to school and got his DO over an MD, when he was 35. Between opting to go DO, and his age, by the time he was 45, he was making money similar to other doctors in the same age category, not other doctors that had been practicing for less than 10 years.

                  I don't think a lawyer needs a high resources level. It's the field I'm going into, and the pay I can expect for my first couple of years as a criminal defence barrister is barely above the poverty line. Now, that's specific to Australia, but the general pattern holds up in most legal markets, with the first few years for a lot of lawyers being pretty lean when it comes to pay and benefits, or pay compared to hours worked. Even after that, it's only 'good' (let's say 2, 2.5), not 'great' (3+), until you get some good years or make your name for particularly good work. Now add in that the job market for lawyers is shrinking while an enormous number of hungry young lawyers come out of the law schools with significant debts they need to pay off and a willingness to work for less than their established counterparts, the cut throat competition for positions in the high-paying firms, and that a lot of work either doesn't pay or pays a pittance (looking at you, public defense work and pro-bono work!) and you have a recipe for lawyers who's functional wealth is only Resources 1, and who's dreaming of when he can fill in that metaphorical second dot.

                  Lawyers can easily rise from 1 to 5+ over their lifetime, but for a lot of them, they'll never get above 3. It's helpful to keep in mind that in Revised and V20 both, 1 dot encompasses anyone below the financial middle class who isn't functionally destitute (so, most of the working class, but not all), 2-3 cover the middle classes, and 4+ is a rich bugger/capitalist parasite on the working man/hero of the almighty dollar.

                                                  Originally posted by Prince of the Night View Post
                                                  on Lawyer did I seem too cynical about "Trials of the century?"
                                                  Not really, but most of those cases aren't that common and surprisingly, they often don't pay the millions of dollars claimed in terms of 'real pay' because the costs are often through the roof as well. The lawyer's payment for a long, high profile case might seem high, but it goes to a team of lawyers, paralegals, expert witnesses, lost opportunity costs from missed work, travel costs, rental costs, filing fees, etcetera.

                                                  The best pay doesn't come, typically, from the high profile cases unless the client is filthy rich. It comes from working for the large, high-flying corporate law firms and reaching partnership level. This is also the hardest slog a lawyer faces - it's not uncommon to work 60+ hours a week, as Garkhal says, as a senior associate at those places, and when you're a junior, all bets are off and you better not plan on doing anything but eating, breathing, sleeping, and shitting company business on a salary that's really only adequate for someone working 40 hours a week. It's the trade-off - you work hard and suffer until you hit relatively easy street, but even then, you're probably still working a surprising number of hours for a person of your wealth.

                                                  Most lawyers also don't retire after getting a high profile case. That's the worst career move - if you win a high profile case, or put up a damned good case that loses because it was unwinnable rather than because you're incompetent, you're going to want to keep practicing because clients will flock and doors will open. Stephen Jones, the lawyer who lead McVeigh's defence team, is still in practice today to use one of your examples. On the OJ example, Cochran, Shapiro, Dershowitz, and several others all remained in practice for years or still practice. Law is not a field that encourages young retirement - as you age and grow in experience, demand grows for you, and with it the opportunities to make good money grow. It only slackens if you start to lose or lose your faculties, or if factors unforeseen (e.g. your entire field of specialty is no longer needed (looking at you, marijuana specialists!) because it's no longer a crime.) rise to complicate affairs.

Stephen King isn't rich because he put out a few books; he's written 60 books and 200 short stories. J.K. Rowling isn't rich just because she wrote Harry Potter - she was unemployed, divorced, and living on the dole while writing the first book - she's rich because of licensing and merchandising.

                                                          And what some people seem to miss, writing is a job, and it's hard. It's not "you have to be somewhere and punch a clock, manual labor style" hard, but rather "you need to have x amount of pages turned in by y date or we'll find someone else" hard. Sure, you get to stay home in your pajamas while you work, but it's hard to get motivated sometimes, it's hard to stay focused with all the distractions being home provides, it's hard to receive what seems like nothing but negative feedback on the awesome things you wrote, and sometimes you find yourself still working at 4:30 in the morning...

                                                          Sorry, got a little off track there.

                                                          Being an artistic professional requires an amount of self-discipline most supernaturals, quite simply, don't have time for. Going back to King, he writes a minimum of 6 pages a day, and each page can take anywhere from 10 minutes to an hour. That's literally just hours in front of a computer, typing.
