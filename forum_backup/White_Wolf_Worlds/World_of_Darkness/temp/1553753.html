    different event/trigger for the Shattering

    09-11-2025, 03:04 PM
    One thing I don't like in the Changeling setting's history is that the Shattering was caused by the Black Death. I don't associate the Black Death with the rise of reason and science that is supposed to be the real driver behind it. Also, the idea that people lost their sense of wonder or belief in the fae doesn't seem relevant to the 14th century. Instead, I think a later era would be much more appropriate, especially the time period that is covered by Mage: the Sorcerer's Crusade. That's the time period of the Renaissance, the Age of Exploration, and the Scientific Revolution. A 16th and 17th century time period seems much better to me than a 14th century timeline.

    Appropriate triggers might be something like Columbus discovering America, the first circumnavigation of the world, Galileo's discovery of the moons of Jupiter, the rise of modern science with Bacon and Descartes. I personally lean to either the Magellan Expedition or Galileo's discovery as being the best choice for my own chronicle. The Shattering then would begin around this time and "close out" around the Age of Enlightenment and the path to Arcadia seemingly closed forever.

    Has anyone done anything similar in their own chronicles? What event/trigger do you think works best?

      Honestly, I don't think Columbus or Magellan or Galileo are big enough. Newton and Descartes as a starting point maybe, but it's hard to single out a moment that everything changed so dramatically.

      Oddly enough the Black Death does have a repercussion that does contribute in a major way, although not an obvious one. It set up the ground work for the printing press. It killed a lot of the people who lived in cities, among them the scribes and bookkeepers, so there was a shortage of people who could write. It caused a boom time when it was over with all the money people inherited (a bit of a ghoulish aftereffect we usually don't think of). Strangest of all it drove paper prices down because the paper of the time was made from pulped up cloth and people were replacing their clothing due to the aforementioned boom time.

      Now the printing press itself is not inherently banal, but it ended up pushing people in a prosaic direction, literally. One of the great functions of poetry and rhymes is as a mnemonic. It's easier to remember things with rhythm and meter and rhyme. With so much more knowledge being written down and more easily accessible, society moved away from poetic language and towards more factual and prose based speech.

      Now I sincerely doubt the authors were thinking of any of that when they chose the Black Death as the cause of the Shattering, but that change with books makes more sense than a lot of other things. It also has the advantage that the tipping point itself isn't really noticable. It's not when the press was invented, its a few generations later when there were enough books to shift society

          I agree that the Black Death as a cause for the Shattering is kinda weak on a number of levels, not the least the aforementioned lack of any necessary correlation with a contraction in what was popularly imagined as possible.

          The best steel-man case for the Black Death is less that it led to a rise of scientific reasoning or rationality but rather that the combination of death and despair on such a massive scale created a psychic environment so poisonous that the fae were forced to flee to Arcadia or undergo the Changeling Way in response.

          Another factor associated with the Shattering is the spread of Christian hegemony and the gradual suppression of old pagan traditions associated (at least to a Romantic sensibility) with the Fae. Setting aside the actual historiography of such events, the role of the Inquisition in the Romantic literary tradition also proposes that the increasing influence of the Grey-Cloaks also served to weaken the fae (this would at least correspond to irl folklore about Christian religious figures/symbols being used to suppress or ward off the fairy folk). One could also try to connect this with phenomena such as the Northern Crusades to try and suggest that the suppression of the last remaining pagan enclaves in Europe as also playing a role.

          C20 also provides something of a cop-out if none of these options satisfy, with the idea that the Sundering was marked by an eclipse, allowing one to assume that there was some larger astrological significance to the timing of the Shattering if one wants to simply handwave the matter with a ‘the stars were right’ type answer.

                  My problem with most those proposals is that they don't really solve the main problems of the Shattering, or the Resurgence, by the way.

                  1- Defining a single event as the turning point of Glamour for the whole of humanity is kinda ridiculous. People's reactions to any one event are varied, dynamic and build up with other things happening around them. It oversimplifies the Plague (or better, plagues) and the Cold War;

                  2- It claims worldwide influence for something that's actually regional. Most of humanity wasn't in Europe by the time of the Black Death. Likewise, most of humanity wasn't nearly as impressed by the Moon Landing, as the media coverage simply wasn't the same abroad;

                  3- Likewise, it'll by definition emphasize events the author thinks important to an overblown proportion, all the while downplaying many other events around the world that are far more relevant for a lot of people. Research history from the POV of another country or region, like the Chinese, India, Southeast Africa, and you'll find a lot of other events happening there that were far more relevant for a significant amount of people;

                  4- Even defining in which direction Glamour turned is tricky. The same things that stifle creativity in some may increase it in others. The military dictatorship gave Brazil arguably our richest period in music. The Black Death gave rise to new artists and styles, and greatly influenced the rise of more complex, abstract layers to artistic expression. The very explosion of variety and expressions in comics came as a means to work within the Comics Code. Both superstition and reason can be sources of inspiration as much as be tools of conformity.

                  Were I to chose and pick a moment to say it stifled Glamour and caused the Shattering, I would actually put it in the 19th century with the Industrial Revolution, but even that isn't right, not for nothing this period gave us the Second Gothic Wave, the rise of Science Fiction, Mystery, Detective stories and so on.

                  The truth is that the very notion that the Interregnum occurred is ultimately a 90s thing. It's not really about any period of human history, short or large, but about the idea about this period formed in a particular time and context. Reality is, for most people fairies are real. They've been real in the Middle Ages, they've been real in Victorian England, they've been real through the whole 20th century and they're real today. They sometimes take other names, they sometimes take weird explanations, but they're always there.

                  So, I would personally do as Gryffon15 and just place it at an eclipse or something. It has nothing to do with humanity's Glamour, it was just a magic occurrence.

                              Originally posted by Astromancer View Post
                              The Black Death at least effected all of Eurasia West of China
                              Not that much. As a pandemic it reached the Arabic Peninsula, but not much further east. It's reach in Northern Africa was also quite limited, it mostly stuck to the Mediterranean coast, with a few in-land forays in Egypt. Even west of India a good chunk of Asia and most of Africa were spared this pandemic.

                              The problem with the Black Death is that people frequently conflate several distinct epidemics as a single event. The "true" Black Death lasted less than 10 years and swept mostly Europe and the near Middle East. Other outbreaks of the Plague happened before and after, with the Plague of Justinian being already a confirmed case, and archaeological evidence suggests some happening as soon as 3000 BCE.

                              Outbreaks of the Plague happened all over the world, including in China, up until the 20th century. In fact, the Plague still exists, not only does it have wild pockets of bacteria all over the world, there's still an average of 600 human cases every year around the world, 7 of them in the US (luckily in Brazil we didn't have a single human case in the last 20 years, but we have wild pockets we have to keep track of, like everyone else). So it's not only a single event, really.
