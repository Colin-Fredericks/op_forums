    Game preparation?

    05-05-2021, 08:14 PM
    This is a question for the Storytellers here, in particular those with Mage the Ascension experience.

    How much preparation do you do in advance of the game? What does the preparation look like? This is the WoD and Storytelling system -so it will not involve the kind of preparation that does into a D&D session where every room in the house is detailed (along with the monster, furniture, and kind of wallpaper used). But just "winging" it is inviting failure. So, how much preparation do you do in advance of the game?

    What does the preparation look like?

      For the last Mage game I ran prep was the following:
      • Hold session Zero, to discuss with players what they would like to do, and what sort of story we all expect
      • Set up a running timeline for events and how they will progress if the characters don't get involved. Then update and revise the timeline in response to what the characters do.
      • Sketch out key plot places, as locations, NPCs and events - not all these plot places will relate to the timeline or effect it.
      • Work out basic details any other NPCs as needed, especially those coming from characters Backgrounds and back story
      • Work out any other broad details needed. Including where there could be conflict between characters due to their paradigms

      Then I give the players the map and ask what they want to do, where they want to go. Throwing in a range of plot hooks for them to follow up on from the timeline and others.

          In addition to the fine points expressed above, I really like to get a sense of the setting. I try to become very familiar with whatever city the game is taking place in (most recently, NYC and LA), including mystery and mythology. What unusual secrets or individuals from that locale's history make good fodder for the kinds of stories you want to tell? How might you interpret aspects of the locale's history as representative of the conflict between the Traditions, Technocracy, and other interests? What major players are there, supernatural or otherwise? What goals do the PCs' avatars want them to strive for?

          Beyond this, a lot of my stories, especially in the early going, use player backgrounds as jumping off points. I look for an immediate first session hook to bring them right into the action, such as a Technocracy assault on the chantry that drives them into a precarious, "on the streets" hardscrabble life, or the murder of a mutual friend under bizarre circumstances. These scenarios bring the PCs together and give them immediate direction.

          Good luck with your game!

              There are a few things that I look at. It's easy for me to get lost in ideas, though, so the first thing I do is to think of the type of game I'm looking to run. It's an obvious move to many, but one that I often fail to keep in mind while developing the story. If I'm running a game focused on young showbiz-associated Magi fighting a Nephandic circle working behind the scenes in Hollywood, I'm going to want to focus on different aspects of the story than I would for a chronicle revolving around the politics surrounding the choice of a new High Council member, or a group of Orphans fighting to protect what remains of their crumbling neighborhood from greedy Syndicate developers. For the first, aside from obviously researching the entertainment industry and the general culture of the business, I'd also probably either sketch out a brief history of the area (this is where I often get lost in overplanning, since I love creating metaplot), a general sense of the major players and their backstories and characteristics, as well as their relationships to each other (if any). For the second, I'm focusing much more on the backstories, as well as the specific motivations and alliances of each person jockeying for the position, and making a map of same. This will only last until the characters blow it up, but it's important to have as a starting point. For the third, I'll usually focus little or not at all on magical politicking (unless the players bring it up), and work more on the specific threat of the developers while considering the daily challenges of poverty, gang violence, police abuse, and general despair.

              For specific adventures after the first, I spend some time between sessions glancing over my notes and character sheets with an eye towards how the players' actions have had an impact on the story so far, and especially what unexpected consequences (both good and bad) may happen as a result. I then set up said consequences, and have the players deal with the fallout. I also leave a few plot threads out there if they seem to be at loose ends for something to do, and can usually improvise around them if need be.

                  Nicely said, I agree that the between sessions prep is also very important, and in addition to updating the timeline, I take some time to review all my character's "knives" they've given me as a ST, and how to use them to make the next session more engaging for them. In Mage, most of those knives are in the merit/flaw system or the character's background. I have found if I don't take this time, I tend to tell my story, not the character's story, and since starting this, feel I'm more skilled at ST.

                          Perhaps I'm in the minority, but I actually do tend to do detailed write-ups of what is in each room. I don't really like the fuzzy narrative style as much as others.

                          The Timeline of events thing is something important, but also rough if/then's for what antagonists might do given certain events. IE/ if the evil mage is actually injured, then they should stop playing around and break out the big guns. Where the default is that they're looking to scare and revel in their own power, as much as they are trying to kill the players.
