    As has been pointed out, pre-Columbian is going to be a hard row to hoe, especially if you are looking at North America. At best, what exists in European records is recording either, effectively, a post-Apocalyptic or peri-Apocalyptic time. There isn't much to indicate what was going on before Europeans showed up anywhere in North America, except, pehaps if you want to read (wade) through De Vega's book on the Entrada of Hernando de Soto, and even that covers a small section of the continent (Western Florida along the Gulf Coast through Georgia, Alabama & Mississippi and up into Arkansas before it all went south). Maybe a decent chance of getting something sold out of the Southwest and maybe the Northwest coast. But, overall, it's going to take a fair amount of hardcore scholarship for any particular area. Unless you want to make it up pretty much whole cloth. And that's assuming by Pre-Columbian, you mean "right before Columbus". If you are talking about prior to that (since Pre-Columbian technically extends back to Clovis folks running around killing mammoths and further), it's going to get even more difficult. as you are going to be slogging through archaeological monographs.

    Things are slightly different if you are thinking about Central America or Eastern South America, things are bit easier...

    I don't think the major issue with the Technocratic/Mage issue with the conquest of the Americas is the possession and operations of guns. It's how and why disease was the issue. Since that's THE issue. Guns were all well and good, but, they're guns of that era. They are kinda cool, but they are slow and somewhat dangerous. An army of Aztecs could easily have thrown enough bodies at Cortez to remove any advantage received by his guns (and metal armor and horses and metal swords). Cortez was able to do what he did because he moved fast, he was super-duper strange (an "out of context" event), he sure as hell seemed like he fit right into their mythology as a "good guy", the subjects of the Aztecs HATED them and were more than willing to lend their assistance, and, oh yeah, the Aztecs were super-duper sick because the European diseases were moving faster than Cortez, including their leader. (The messengers taking the news about the conquistadors' arrival were transmitting the disease as they raced to the capital). It was pretty much the same with Pizzaro, et al.

    In fact, in some ways that argues FOR the ideas of Mage. A very small group of guys could easily be mages and that era's version of "extraordinary" citizens. After all, if the Void Explorers could manage to keep a whole ship "in paradigm" so that it could even go to places with different types of belief, they could probably keep small mobile groups equivalently supported. Sure the guns worked. Because the gun mages were there and all of the guys with them sure believed in guns. So the funs worked. Then you take into account you are killing off most of the folks with the different belief structure, subverting that same belief structure and imposing a top down governmental group-think, the Pre-Columbian paradigms shatter pretty effectively, pretty quickly.

    A major concern would seem to be what the Hell, the Tradition and non-Tradition mages native to the Americas were doing while all this was going on... I.e. Why did the Order of Reason get away with it seemingly largely unopposed.

        There are several active volcanoes in Syria (Al-Safa erupted in 1850 AD). The Dead Sea is a rift valley, and it is bordered by dozens of dormant volcanoes, some of whom erupted within the last ten thousand years. There are also active volcanoes throughout the Rea Sea region, so the ancient peoples of Canaan were quite familiar with volcanoes. In addition, there is a 40,000 square kilometer volcanic field, Harrat al-Shamah, which contains 800 volcanic cones and possessed a lake of lava as recently as the 19th century.

        Ba'al is a title, similar to El, and it just means 'my lord'. Ba'al was the name given by the Phoenicians while El was the name given by the Hebrews (because of the nature and similarity of two written languages, it is more properly b'l verses 'l, which may be as simple as the difference between 'my lord' and 'lord', meaning that Ba'al and El may be the exactly same entity). The superiority of Ba'al/El over Yahweh would have been quite appropriate, given the historical inferiority of Yahweh to Ba'al/El within the pantheon of Canaan. Initially, Yahweh may have been a hero god, similar to Hercules or Thor, who was elevated to supremacy as his hero cult became more and more popular among the Hebrews.

        There is also evidence that Ba'al/El may have been derived from the Egyptian deity Ptah, the god of craftsman, which would have also been the god of carpenters. That would lead to an interesting idea that Jesus, who was a carpenter, may have been the son of Ba'al/El (Ptah) instead of the son of Yahweh, which would have fit nicely within the thousands of years of Egyptian influence of the region (even during the rule of the Romans). Of course, this is all conjecture because the majority of the texts in the region were burned first by the Christians during the first century AD and then the Muslims during the 7th century AD, so we really do not have much of an idea of how the ancient religions of the region interrelated.

            In any case, Yahweh isn't and never has been associated with volcanoes. Mountains. Yes. Well... more accurately "high places".

            Ba'al is a title. "Lord/King of" But it's also nominative. Ba'al "X" can mean "Lord/King/God of X" but, mythologically, Ba'al is also one guy, properly Ba'al Haddad. Even though there are "different" Ba'al's of different cities, that doesn't mean they aren't also Ba'al Haddad. Meaning, there is a mythological figure with associated myths that was identified as Ba'al, That's the guy who all the Hebrew priests are pissed off that the Israeli's are worshping. As in, that is the name written on the statue or next to the depiction. So... yeah. It is a title, but, that doesn't meant it's not a name or specific personage.

            Ba'al is not El. El, who is not Yahweh. More the reverse. Yahweh was syncretized to the much older El, who is a different figure with his own myths and attributions. (Like adding on all the Marduk stuff as well. Like shedding the wives et al. as henotheism gave way to monolatrism) Old Heliocentric attributions to Ptah don't hold. Sure, there might be some Egyptians looking for a "match" when talking about the gods of the Canaanites, but that doesn't make such an attribution real for most purposes. (It can of course have been real, in that an Egyptian might have left an offering to Ptah in a temple of El and felt that the two were one.... but that's on the Egyptian. It doesn't make El into Ptah.) That's like saying that Taranis is really Mars, just because that's what the Romans called him when they were dedicating offerings to the local deities. Egyptians had hegemony over the region for a limited period of time (as did many many other groups), but much of the salient material predates Egyptian conquest. So that's like saying that the American domination of New York state clearly demonstrates that Hiawatha is, in fact, Thomas Jefferson.

            And neither Ba'al nor El are craftsman. There is a Canaanite god for that who both El and Ba'al have to call on when they want stuff made.... Kothar or Kothar-na-Hawis. He's an all around maker of stuff, from magical godly palaces to all powerful bows to magical clubs for Behemoth and Leviathan and death god smiting.

            None of this requires anything in the way of texts that the Christians might or might not have burned. These from contemporaneous documents of which we have LOTS. Many from the cultures in question and others from their neighbors. In addition to massive excavations of temples and other sites, graffiti, etc. etc. If there's any group of people who are destroying them, it's much more modern, as in ISIS.

                Originally posted by ElizabethSterling View Post
                The big difficulty with Pre-Columbian America as a setting is that before there was time to understand it it had all but been destroyed. We are ferociously ignorant of how life actually worked, especially on the larger scale. It's something that historians are busy revising and learning to this day. A recent study I read suggests that the population of the Americas before Europeans arrived was 70,000,000 with vibrant inter-cultural exchange and more languages and traditions than we can differentiate. Partially due to war and enslavement, though mostly due to diseases like influenze and smallpox, a century later the population of the Americas was less than 4,000,000. Two continents of humans almost totally collapsed. So much was lost that we can never truly get back that you'd have to be very careful and thorough with your research and still acknowledge you were likely to be wrong about 1001 things.
                I remember reading that they estimate around 90% of the Native Americans died from diseases spread through the extensive trade routes that existed between the communities.. By the time Europeans began seriously exploring the central and mid-western parts of North America, for example, the native populations had undergone an extinction-level event a century or two earlier and so many of the habits, customs and civilizations that the explorers encountered weren't ones that had actually existed back in the 1500's, or at least were extremely different form the ones that had existed a century or two prior.

                This in turn means that descriptions of the people at the time don't necessarily give us entirely accurate insight into what life was actually like for the populations of the Americas prior to the arrival of Europeans.

                    Precisely. In addition, European innovations/imports were transmitted well in advance of actual contact. Such as the image of Plains tribes on horseback. There weren't any horses before the Spaniards brought them north of the desert between Aztec territory and the American Southwest. You don't need to learn much to take care of horses, compared, say, to developing the technological base required to make firearms. Those tribes were VERY different than they are typically depicted. They were agriculturalists who had some limited exploitation of the buffalo (actualy American bison, but whatever...), not nomadic hunters whose life centered around buffalo hunting. Etc. And the Pre-Contact lifeways were already generations gone by the time those tribes began meaningful contact with Europeans.

                    This is very much the case in the North American Southeast as well. There was very complex culture there than was barely anything like that of the tribes met by later European expansion. Small cities with structured elites who lived above the populace on raised mounds that harken back more to a cultural continuity with antecedents from several centuries before that extended across vast swathes of the continent. Pretty much nothing like what was later there with Cherokee and their neighbors.

                    Some very recent studies of the genetics of Pre-Contact remains vs. the genes of the remaining Native American populations indicates at least 90% of the genetic variability of the entire population was lost. Though the number of those who died is really almost impossible to determine for several factors (there are problems of chronology, trying to determine the original number, etc.) that is a population bottleneck that is nearly an extinction level event on the biological level.

                                    Originally posted by haldir2012 View Post
                                    The pre-Columbian Americas is where the Mage worldview has trouble. Mage says that consensus shapes reality. In a Euro-centric view, they've explained how that would result in a world largely like the real world at most points in history. In the Americas, it would be a big divergence.

                                    For example - the Order of Reason had to slowly build firearms into the consensus in Europe. Sorcerer's Crusade covers a time when very basic firearms were in the consensus and could be used by anyone, but more reliable/powerful ones were not - so they only show up in that setting as a focus for a High Artisan to work his Enlightened Will with, or as a Wonder/Device imbued with its own ability to warp reality. If that's true, when Europeans landed in America and tried to use their firearms, shouldn't they have been Paradoxical again? Nobody in the Americas, aside from the other guys on the ship, believed in them, so they should have only worked for Enlightened folks who could warp reality.

                                    If the Europeans land and their guns don't work, then no conquering is really going to happen, and we diverge from real-world history too much. You'll have to come up with a reason why technology accepted by Europeans but not by Americans worked in America. For the guns example:
                                    • There weren't a lot of guns on the landing ships - mostly swords and other simpler things. The guns they did have were actually operated by High Artisans or were Devices donated to the expedition by supportive Artisans back home.
                                    • The guns they used were much crappier than in the real world - arquebuses, hand-cannons, etc. Those were barely consensual enough to not explode most of the time, but still allowed them to take over natives via shock and awe.
                                    • They didn't bring guns at all, and conquered native cultures not with technology but by pitting them against each other and spreading disease.
                                    For the spiritual landscape - yes, I think having a generally lower Gauntlet in the Americas and a more common spiritual presence makes sense. Note that the Uktena took it as their job to find all the bad Banes and bind them, so aside from these areas of binding, the spiritual landscape should be in pretty good shape.
                                    Yeah, guns didn't really have anything to do with it. Virulent continent wide pandemics of Measles, Small Pox and Plague turned the Americas into a post apocalyptic wasteland within a generation.

So, are you going to have Plains tribes that hunt bison from horseback? That's iconic Native American, but it wasn't anything like what was actually going on there. There were about as many horses on the Great Plains in Pre-Columbian times as there were Winchester rifles and Model T cars. The American Southeast was pretty much completely different than it was by the time of English contact. The area was dominated by the Southeastern Ceremonial Cult complex related cultures, which were a whole lot more like the much older Mississippian culture that came before them than they were like the Cherokee et al. that succeeded them. Much of what people "know" about Native American culture is actually seeing societies that are effectively Post-Apocalyptic So.... how much authenticity do you care about? It's going to seem off to the perceptions of most to delete the whole "buffalo hunting horse riding Plains Indians" and so on. So, do you chuck reality in favor of preconceived notions (which are often stereotypical to a great degree).

                                                            In addition, you've said that the all the folklore/mythologies (and oh dear, opening that Pandora's jar is definitely a BIG thing) are intact and true, are you going to insert/overlay/shoehorn in the Eurocentric WoD stable of supernaturals? In an "intact" Pre-Columbian Mesoamerica, why are the gods vampires? In a pre-Cherokee North American Southeast, is Ulunsuti a Nosferatu? (If there even should be an Ulunsuti pre-Cherokee...) Why? She may have liked to stick her long fingernail in the livers of sleeping victims to get yummy treats but there's nothing to indicate that she had any problems with sunlight or need for blood. If you want the mythology intact, you are facing a situation very similar to the partial "re-write" done in East Asia (except for werewolves... of course they are the same everywhere. Even though Native American shapechanging myths don't really fit with the legends and myths of Europe, but, then again, the European myths don't really match up very well with the Garou either).

                                                            So... you've got to make choices. If you want the Aztec gods to be renegade vampires and retool what supernaturals you can that are vaguely vampire-like to fill the same roles, you can definitely do so. It's already been done a little bit here and there. (There are game to game issues, as always. If Hutzilopochtli is Shaitan, then it makes it hard for there to be mages in the Aztec priesthood with all of Mage's issues with vampire blood and ghouls and magic, etc.) If you want to keep the mythology intact, you probably have a LOT of work to do and might need to create entire categories of supernatural being that don't exist in the West and probably have to chuck a lot of the "vampires did it". And so on.
