    Immunity

    05-27-2022, 06:35 PM
    So, I was rereading a Mage book yesterday and I stumbled upon que Merit "Immunity" and I was impressed I didn't remember it well, because it's the most broken Merit I've ever seen. With enough points, the character can be completely immune to physical attacks and even damage from any source altogether (besides something absurdly rare if you put a lot of points into it). I mean, it can certainly be cool for an NPC, an antagonist, etc, but it's hard to imagine one ST allowing pcs to have it.
    So, my question is have you ever used this Merit?

      No, I haven't. But I think it could be cool at it's "lower" levels to represent some exotic character type. Like, a certain element immunity as part of being an elemental-related Nephilim.

      In general, I think of it as a very exotic thing. I don't like it a whole lot for pc. I think that at high levels of expenditure it has the same potential of being "broken" as it has of being a trap, depending on how important it's going to be surviving a "conventional" combat.

      Tougths:

      *You're the potential perfect "tank" - but tanks are supposed to be "unstoppable", not just "unbreakable". Since you've put your everything into that Merit, you're not likely to be able to do much else on the short notice. Even the densest of Werewolves would eventually get the memo that "clawing you even harder" it's not going to work, and just grab you and put you under a rock or something :P
      There's a millon ways to deal with enemies that don't involve hitting them with damage dice, and some are concerningly common. For some vampires "Dominate the mortal" it's more of a ready-to-go first tactic than Potence. In Mage I would argue you're often more at risk of being captured and sent to Room 101 (yes, as a Technocrat too) than just killed. In a Traditions game, "rescue character from Technocratic facility" it's as cliched as for Vampire it's "getting a mission from the Prince".

      *It's, maybe, a munchkin's wet dream, since it trivializes such a basic aspect of combat, but it can easily become a nedless burden if the chronicle isn't too geared towards situations that would cause "damage". Since it's nearly useless outside such conventional figths, and it also trivializes them, I think it has the potential to be quite boring. Which may be the harshest judgement that I can have about something on a game. Altrough, as said, haven't tested it so I can't be sure.

      *Immortal 7pts of BoS, while less "absolute", may be a more fun option for those PC with a serious survival issue (like say, a mage that wants to "run with werewolves"). Simply put, you will survive after dying by normal means BUT you're still better off if you don't suffer trough that, thus given incentives to "not dying". Plus, you don't need to "exhaust" your character sheet on it.

      *As said, for an antagonist it's interesting because it could be fun for the players to think and research about solutions to the problem with the versatility of magick.

      *I think Paradox can become whatever it's your weakness for purposes of damage. A lot of it's forms aren't about damage anyway. Hobgoblins and spirits could actually reveal your weakness for the world to see

              In D&D, I had a Greater Barghest as an antagonist in a low level adventure, where his goal involved leaving the party alive. It was a group of four 3rd level characters. He stayed in the woods and organized hunting to ensure his goblin worshipers could eat and multiply. In the multi-tribe alliance, he had established a tradition where goblins approaching old age would organize into attack units and besiege farmers on the edge of town. They would kill livestock with crossbows to provoke the farmers, for who they saved their special arrows. These weren't meant to kill, but create a painful debilitating injury.

              The involuntary screams of those struck provided sound cover. The veteran units, who had started their siege at night, would move silently and reposition themselves. Since they had no plans to survive, they would remain awake for up to forty eight hours, terrorizing the town.

              The town faced a difficult choice; call for the Duke's army to establish a fort there, charging heavy taxes for the expense, or put out a call for adventurers with generous store prices as a reward. The later choice brought the 1st level adventurers to this troubled frontier. The party levelled and made a fortune taking down packs of humanoids, among other adventures, leading up to the encounter with the Barghest at 3rd level.

              He wore them down until it was obvious they would die if the fight continued, whereupon he offered a truce. The party agreed never to attack the members of his tribe again and were allowed to keep all their gear. He took his leave, walked out of view, turned invisible and snuck back into their midst... to listen (that version had Detect Thoughts).

              In a sorry state, the party regrouped to discuss how they were going to get back to town, which other adventures they should pursue that did not violate the truce and, most important to the Invisible Barghest, who they would go to for aid. The discussion revealed most of the important NPCs the players and the town relied on - the healer-priest at the temple, the mage, guard captain, etcetera.

              A few weeks later, the Barghest and his most elite agents carried out a series of assassinations. They took out all of the important spellcasters, official message cryptographer, the stablemaster, killed all of the horses the could and left caltrops all over the main road to discourage travel on foot. They effectively severed communication between the town and the rest of civilization.

              The party had been deliberately left with riches and a lot of inside knowledge about the inner workings of the town, for who they were now the only group with spellcasting and other in-demand skills. The Barghest, using disguise and charms, spread rumors that the party had aided him in his assassination spree, in return for their lives and for the opportunity to be installed in power to serve his evil ends. This split the town between those who believed in the heroes and those who gave into the hysteria.

              The next two sessions - about four hours each - were almost entirely dialogue, with dice being rolled only to determine outcomes of social skill checks. It was more intense than combat and made for some deep character development. All this was possible, because I used a monster who, relative to the PCs in challenge rating, was invincible...

              Currently, in Mage, I have a Technomancer with Time 4, using both Contingency and Time-Stop to facilitate his nefarious plans.

                          As monteparnas says with the right players it can actually open alot of doors. It depends on how you leverage it. The outright invulnerability is interesting if used in a limited way (the 'lower' levels of the merit) but the higher cost ones get boring (and IMHO if you use it for powergaming, you're just gonna invite creative punishments from the Storyteller, which I think motivates the player to collaborate actively in working out the parameters.) As the merit itself noted it doesn't necessarily protect against secondary effects or ancillary effects (destruction of stuff you wear or carry, for example... but this can extend to pain, blood loss, etc. depending on how it works. Pain will still carry the dice penalties, and blood loss would still need to be stabilized even if it won't kill you. Worse, all that blood is evidence left behind at a potential scene that could be used to locate you... or might be used by some supernatural being in some ritualistic manner.) Or there may be indirect ways to trigger it (immunity to bullets or physical injury might work if it touches your body, but not if it transmits blunt trauma through stuff you're wearing, like the in-book example.)

                          In the end it's clear the way the merit is applied is very open ended, as well as in how you balance the cost/benefit dynamic and that is very much up to the players and storyteller to work out, and in that respect it's not different than other similar merits (like 'Immortal' - which is a close analogue to immunity and plays on alot of the Highlander stereotypes again, as does immunity itself.)
