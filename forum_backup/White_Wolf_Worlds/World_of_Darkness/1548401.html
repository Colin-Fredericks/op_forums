    How do the Traditions view opposing Paradigms

    02-11-2025, 12:03 PM
    Something I've wondered, but can't seem to find a definite answer, but how do mages with opposing Paradigms view/rationalize each other's magick. (ex. Etherite and Verbena, or Tradition and Technocracy). Is it that they attempt to rationalize what other mages do under their worldview ("Oh it's not the blood sacrifice that makes it work, it's the application of Ether blah blah blah...") or do the mages understand that belief is what fuels their magic, and therefore no one is entirely 'wrong' in their practice. Or is neither correct and I'm missing something?

          Broadly speaking most mages know that any belief system can empower magic... it's kinda indisputable the second they start running into each other as their magic works despite all of those differing beliefs.

          One of the primary conflicts of the setting is what each group does with that knowledge. The Technocracy is essentially built on the fear of this truth as clearly horrible paradigms such as those held by the Nephandi work, and thus was founded to try to eliminate or at least mitigate this as much as possible to get rid of the "bad" paradigms even if it means sacrificing potentially neutral or "good" paradigms along the way. So the Technocracy ranks paradigms based on compatibility with their organizational goals, and then actively suppresses ones that don't make the cut; inherently acknowledging that those paradigms work on some level, but not accept that they have to be allowed to work. The Traditions, on the other hand, organized in response to being the proverbial babies the pre-Technocracy was trying to toss out with the bathwater (though some of them where pretty bathwater-y as it was), and as such while the proto-Traditions were frequently not such, the Traditions themselves took the universalist approach of paradigm just being the belief system that empower magic. So an Etherite and a Verbena might struggle to get their magic to line up enough to work together directly on an effect, but philosophically they acknowledge that their mutually exclusive approaches to magic aren't inherently better or worse. That said, the Traditions don't hold all paradigms as equal, but it's judged much more in terms of things like the Tolerance Paradox where they can't abide the Technocracy or Nephandi who want to make everything one way.

          The Crafts and Disparates tend to be somewhere in the middle (on a spectrum since ex-Traditions and ex-Conventions both exist in this category with some of the associated philosophic baggage). Most could be summed up as a "first among equals," stance where acknowledge others as having valid paradigms enough that actively opposing other mages on principle alone (besides the Nephandi for the Disparates) isn't really justifiable, but use some form of justification to claim their way of doing things is still better than everyone else's (thus don't side with either the Technocracy's desire for one paradigm to rule them all, or the Traditions' desire to get everyone to play nicely together by giving up on some of their sense of uniqueness).

              A cleric in Dungeons and Dragons uses the favor of the gods to make a fireball. A wizard uses their understanding of arcane theory to make a fireball. A druid channels the power of nature to do that, an alchemist makes a bomb, and a sorcerer uses their inner power. There's no reason mages can't view what they're doing the same way. Just because they're producing similar effects doesn't mean they're actually doing the same thing. Not all mages think belief is that important, and for those that don't "I'm doing etheric science, they're doing blood magic" is a perfectly workable paradigm.

                  Humans are remarkably adept at rationalizing new information to salvage their preexisting beliefs. It's an ability written into our DNA, and it generally works from a survival perspective. (We only pay attention to it when it starts causing problems.)

                  Mages could accept that the post-modern theory that Magick and Reality are affect by Belief unto itself. Some, especially in the Traditions, do so as a matter of practicality or polite conduct with their allies. But that kind of thinking makes a Mage confront that their own belief system might be wrong, or at least only conditionally right. That way lies Cognitive Dissonance, and fuck that noise. It's easier in some ways, rather, to make allowances for Magick outside one's own methods, that still keep their personal beliefs intact. That the other Mage CAN do what they do, but either because it's allowed within the first Mage's Paradigm, or because the first Mage's methods are "better" or "purer".

                  A Mage who believes Magick comes from God or the Spirits can easily say that God/Spirits allow their Arts to work, for reasons known only to them. And despite that the non-believer doesn't give God/Spirits the proper respect. The greater powers decided the other Mage's needs or actions were worth it. (Or, conversely, that a competing entity is acting on their behalf, like a rival spirit or "The Devil").

                  A Mage who believes that the world obeys some kind of laws (whatever those laws may be, from Scientific to Arcane to Fey) understands that anything could potentially exploit them. (THEIR methods are clearly just superior, obviously, as they have a clearer understanding of it).


                  Setting aside Conditioning to train personnel to engage in "Crimestop" against "Wrongthink", the Technocracy may have a sliding scale of excuses given to its agents for why mystics can do what they do. The lower levels (Extraordinary Citizens and low-level Enlightened) are convinced to view Traditionalists and Crafts as outright charlatans engaged in parlor tricks, or else using some misunderstood or cleverly hidden artifice.

                  As an agent's level of security clearance grows, they may be taught that mystics employ very ancient, very unreliable/unstable psychic disciplines. That their Enlightenment "works", but puts the entire weight of channeling Scientific principles on the fragile minds of its throwback practitioners. A practice that inevitably leads to psychosis, delusion, psychosomatic injury, or unintentional manifestations of the superstitionist's cultural fears into the physical world. (Read: Paradox). Ergo, it's clearly preferable for Enlightened personnel to externalize their Procedures through vetted technology that, when it fails, fails reliably and safely (usually by breaking the hypertech tool). Agents should leave Psychic effects to the mystics, and to trained, sanctioned, regulated Union specialists who understand the risks.

                  It's only when the Union rep achieves a high enough threshold of clearance, and when their loyalty is unquestionable (or when they're judged as "needs to know"), will the Union come clean about the nature of Magick. That Belief itself is a necessary component. By this time, of course, the agent will be trusted to understand, fully, WHY the Technocratic Union MUST control the Consensus. Why their vision of Reality is a necessary one, for the good of the Sleepers and the world.


                  Finally, it's worth realizing that, even if a Mage has a belief over why their methods work, that does preclude them accepting the world is a big place. Mysterious and mercurial. That there is more between heaven and earth than is dreamt of in their philosophy. The methods of others doesn't invalidate one's own, much less one's Paradigm. It just means there's more going on behind the scenes. Some may endeavor to fill those gaps, reasoning that other Mages may, if imperfectly, understand something they don't. Alternatively, the Mage may simply shrug, and accept such mysteries as unavoidable or unsolvable.

                  Does the mad scientist NEED to know why the witch's broom can fly? Does the Chorister NEED to know why the mad scientist's raygun works? Or can they trust that they do, and leave well enough alone?

                      Another issue is how broad a mage considers her own Focus to be. There's a difference between "This is how the world works" and "this is how my Magick works". Both provide a framework for how to work your own Effects; but the former also implies that everything needs to be explainable according to your Focus, while the latter leaves room for the mage to admit that she doesn't have all the answers, and that other kinds of Magick reside in the gaps.

                      Early editions of the game lean heavily toward the former, while more recent editions allow for either, depending on the mage.

                                          Originally posted by CTPhipps View Post
                                          I felt like that really made the Technocracy look stupid.

                                          1. It means that they have no ability to actually do science because scientific experimentation would reveal that, yes, they are. So they're just deluded.

                                          2. It means they fundamentally don't understand a central tenant of reality. So they can hardly call themselves Awakened or Enlightened.
                                          Both of these assume that the Traditions are right.

                                          Remember: I said that the Technocracy does realize that what they do changes reality. What they disagree on is that what they do and what the Traditions do are the same thing. Yes, the Traditions also have a way of changing Reality; but it's not the same way. And they can point to at least two pieces of evidence supporting that: first, that Technocrats can't just force something to happen by will alone (what M20 calls "Working Without Focus"); and second, that the Traditions cannot make Talismans as easily as Technocrats can make Devices: the latter have a way of getting around the need to spend personal Quintessence on the effort, nor to invest a dot of Willpower to conclude the process. These might seem like minor nitpicks; but they point to a more profound distinction between Magick and Enlightened Science: the former is Willworking; the latter, not so much.

                                          And finally, there's the fact that Devices have the Coincidental edge, and not just because of Technocratic propaganda: Devices are more likely to be Coincidental because Enlightened Science tends to extrapolate from the Earthly Foundations, while Magick is more likely to just try to override said Foundations.

                                          I'm fine with that distinction, as it gives the Technocracy a way out of the "liars or dupes" box they're stuck in if you do assume that the Traditions are objectively right — as opposed to "we're all doing the same thing" being something that the Traditions believe (and have reason to promote, as it's what holds the Traditions together), but which might not actually be true.

                                          Which gets back to what I said in my first post in this thread: there's a difference between "this is how what I do works" and "this is how the world works". The former doesn't make any grand claims about Life, the Universe, and Everything, and so doesn't have to explain as much: the Technocrat who isn't trying to insist that Science explains literally everything doesn't have to provide Scientific explanations for how a Hermetic High Ritual works; he's free to just say "that's not Science; it's dangerous Superstitionism, and you'll regret it if you get involved with it," without having to say "Magick isn't real".

                                                      Looking back at Ascension, I really feel like the Technocracy as an organization was rather silly, which is kind of unfortunate, as I really liked the aesthetic that they presented. It just feels like they suffered from the sort of concretization that a lot of things in Ascension suffered from, especially as the metaplot continued. On one hand its cool to have the MIB shadow organization with cool tech and weird paranormal research; on the other hand, the Technocracy as written either comes across as deluded or weirdly obsessed with one way of presenting their magic and influencing the Consensus, and I think that their name itself really does a lot of the legwork there. The ideological drive against the Traditions very much feels like a forced "old magic versus soulless science" thing that is both dated and just not very philosophically compelling. And really, one can apply to that reasoning to a lot of things in the setting. Why do the Traditions have to be organizations? Why does your Tradition/Craft/Convention/etc. have to be so important when every Awakened is an enlightened mystic who probably has some inanely complicated and hyper-specific paradigm of their own that could hardly fall within the reach of a splat book? Why does the Order of Hermes has a GD-esque grade system when that kind of thing would probably just be for their hedge mages? I think that Ascension especially suffered from the way in which the splats were essentially forced onto them, which is kind of funny given how mechanically their splats have the least impact (e.g., Focus Sphere, and even then you can just be an Orphan and pick your own). Honestly I think that the Technocracy could have really worked as an organization focused on refining magic into its basic, most unadorned principles in an early British chaos magic sort of way. You could just add on skepticism of the religious narratives of the Traditions (e.g., questioning just how much mystical experiences like the Awakening should be modelled with religious and magical systems that are present in the culture that one has grown up in). Then you could make them have advanced mundane tech just for the cool factor. Throw in a bit of breakaway civilizations or something for external conflict and you're good.

                                                      I do agree with Phipps in that the Awakening should confer some sort of privileged knowledge about the nature of reality in order for it to be significant narratively. As presented, the Technocracy does come across like something out of a comic book, even though they acknowledge the Tradition mages can manipulate reality. Ascension as a whole could really benefit from boiling down the game to its basic premise. If someone wants to play VTM they probably want to play Dracula or Count Orlocke. They can play MTA if they want to be Jesus or Mahavira or something. At that point, having an excessively calcified setting with a commitment to a 1990s interpretation of the relationship between magic and science just gets in the way. When Ascension was first published a lot of its themes might have been largely invisible in society, but now the basic postmodern magic "thing" that Ascension had is relatively common in culture. A lot of STs and players could come up with good ideas for campaigns and the like just from reading various books.

                                                          Verlan has a lot of good points.

                                                          I think the heart of the matter is that every RPG needs a good antagonist that is something that gives the player characters something to fight against but also something that underlines the thematic ideas of the game. V:TM has the War of Ages and War of the Sabbat that provides a question of the corrupting effects of immortality as well as the Humanity issue. Werewolf has the conflict with the Wyrm but also the Garou constantly at each other's throats.

                                                          The issue with the Technocracy vs. the Traditions is that the early Technocracy wants to control all of human advancement and shove it into a narrow box. Maybe even remove anyone Awakening but the way they want. But the latter Technocracy does want to do the Mass Ascension the same way as the Traditions. So it becomes a question of, "why are they fighting at all?"

                                                          To go with the Seers of Thrones example, the conflict is actually pretty thematic of people who want to escape the prison of the Exarch's world versus the people who want to keep everyone locked up. You need the anti-Ascension people in Mage and they just don't exist. The Nephandi exist but they're Oblivion rather than Stasis.

                                                          It makes me wonder if they did a Mage 5 (I still have my doubts), they should probably make a Technocracy/Sons of Ether/VA group that is reformed, a Traditions, a Disparate Alliance-esque group and then a Seers-esque group alongside the Nephandi.
