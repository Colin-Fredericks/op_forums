    Letters from The Shadows: Novels and comics for the World of Darkness

    12-27-2017, 09:37 PM
    I know we always talk movies and music usually.
    but what about books or comics or mangas? which ones would you recommend not only for Splat books but for the general feel of the world of darkness and why?.

    Junji Ito's UZUMAKI: this horror manga tells the story of a cursed town where the figure of Spirals drive people mad. Its a interesting Homage to the Lovecraftian horrors.

    Alan Moore's Providence: Not only a precuel to the interesting Neonomicon and Courtyard, this comic depicts the interconnections of Lovecraft's country and how we can't dettach from the madness within.

    Clive Barker's Books of Blood: My personal favorite so far its the Story titled "Book of Blood." but as a whole, barker is the master of short stories involving sex and violence. deffenetly a must read for those lingering in the dark corners of the earth.

    House of Leaves: Funny enough i haven't finished this book yet, but the bizarre narrative and the way the Houseis depicted within the story makes it a fascinating reading

    John Dies at The End: Returning to the more bizarre, a bit of hunter the reckoning with a bit of post modern magick and you got John Dies at The End.

    The Hound of the Baskervilles: yes i know its not strictly horror, but i like the way Sherlock depicts his fascination with this phenomena. Perhaps normal or perhaps magickal, its a good examination on turning a myth into something more real.

    Philip K Dick is Dead, Alas. Part Satire Part homage, a interesting retelling of the history of PDK

    Anno Dracula: What if the Van Helsing group FAILED? what if Abraham Van Helsing was murdered by dracula? what is Dracula now was the prince of the UK?. and what if a survivor of the Van Helsing group was Jack the Ripper. a sort of Collage of characters from different vampire mythos intertwinned in the hunt for The Silver Knife, the Murderer from WhiteChapel.

    Strangely enough this was recommended by a close friend of mine "The Night land" by William Hope Hodgeson, i dont know much about this book. other than Lovecraft Himself considered it a Quintessencial part of the Horror Narrative.

    The Ilustrated Man by Ray Bradbudy: personally , i always considered that bradbury was not only one of the Fathers of modern Science Fiction (Next to Clark and Asimov to name a few) but the ONLY ONE who actually cared about Humanity. Bradbury was the kind of man who came from a world out of a Norman Rockwell painting and who keept using those values and morals for his histories. Something Wicked this way comes might be a top classic next to Farenheidt and Martian Chronicles. but to me, his most intimate and humanistic work has to be The Ilustrated Man. a series of short stories told as tattoos from this Mysterious Man.

    The Illuminatus! Trilogy: i been trying to read this one for a couple of times and yet its too chaotic and the narrative its so bizarre that it overwhelmes me..and i understand why its so important for games such as Mage or to give a general idea of what the world of darkness should be about from the point of view of conspiracy and madness

    The Crow: Curare. Yes, the original Crow is a master piece...but personally, CURARE its the most Human history of The Crow in the present days that i managed to read so far.
    While The original history of violence and the legacy of Eric Draven was a classic (followed by other stories that managed to become even a bit predictable). I found myself clashing with the latest work's of James O'Barr. Skinning the wolf its not a bad "Nazi Hunter" history but a bit well..predictable. and Pestilence didnt't caught my eye..Then i found Curare, the story of a Little girl turned bough back after she was savagely raped and murdered. The whole idea of the story is that we see it from the point of view of a detective HELPING the girl and its Crow Familiar.

    Good Omens: Ok, i love this book. its on my top tier favorite books of alltime, the history and misshappends of cults. the anti christ, the end of days etc.... neil gaiman and terry pratchett did a wonderful job with this novel. Its the end of the world as we know it but i feel fine

    Guy Davies: The Marquis: Vol de Gull is an ex inquisitor and soldier of the good faith in the middle of a france of Debauchery and Corruption, Seduction and Lust. Fighting these ideals he finds himself a Mask, the Mask of The Marquis from which now he can See the Demons that plague society... i wont say more because Spoilers.


    Preacher by Garth Ennis: One of the most quintescencial comics of all time, Preacher tells us the story of Preacher Jesse Custer whom has been possesed by a being even more powerful than God. Now he must travel america to Find God and try to save him from this being..or know Why at least. its dark humor its only matched by its deep touching moments.

    Batman The Long Halloween: What? a batman story in the world of darkness thread that is Not the killing joke or Arkham Asylum serious house on serious earth?!. Well, The Long halloween its interesting. We are in the middle of Year Two (Second year of bruce wayne as batman) and some myserious serial killer is slowly climbing up and taking down different members of different mafias but ONLY during certain Holidays. Now batman must figure out who is the Holiday Killer, and see how the Old criminals of Gotham are slowly replaced by The Freaks (the joker, the riddler etc..)

    Forum's Official's Joker and Trickster. Pardon my bad english, aint my first language (I Speak Spanish).
    ST: DtF, HtR, WtO, MtA
    Signature Chars: Crowley (hakalu), Joe The Nuwisha (WtA)

              Bram Stoker's Dracula and Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, because it's good to be familiar with the classics. (The werewolf counterpart to these two is apparently Guy Endore's The Werewolf of Paris, which has only come back into print in the past few years, and I've yet to have a chance to read it.)

              CE Murphy's The Walker Papers series, starting with Urban Shaman. It's really good for both Werewolf and Mage, dealing with what it means to be a shaman and mixing Native American/First Nations folklore with that of Ireland. And it's set in Seattle (which I always felt should've been Mage's signature city), so if you want to run a game set there, you might find a few ideas.

              John Steakley's Vampire$. John Carpenter's film really didn't do this book service, as the characters are lot deeper and more damaged than portrayed. It's really good reading for small-h witch-hunter type hunters and not too bad for big-H Imbued Hunters either. It also has one of the better depictions I've seen of how the Presence Discipline can mentally damage it's victims.

              Stephen King's The Dead Zone. If I was going to pick one book of his to recommend to someone, this would be it. I've always considered it a prime example of how psychic abilities should be treated in the World of Darkness, and the book's themes actually tie rather strongly to the Euthanatos in Mage.

              Much of Robert E. Howard's historical fiction, including his Bran Mac Morn stories, which had a major influence on the development of the Black Spiral Dancers in Werewolf. While none of it is exactly "historically accurate", the overall themes and tone of his works - the brutal and savage nature of human existence, the inherent corruption and temporary status of 'civilization', and continuing struggle in the face of certain doom - are cornerstones of Werewolf's worldview.

                          Originally posted by ChristopherZacher View Post
                          SK is one of my favorite authors and I friggin love the Dead Zone book and original movie adaption too.
                          I tend to like his earlier work better, as it was when I was reading Under the Dome that I came to the realization that he appears to take some kind of sadistic glee in brutally killing the sort of people he grew up around. This is somewhat less noticeable in his earlier work, especially his short stories. But I think around the late 80s or early 90s, it started to become a thing, and only got worse after his hit-and-run accident. Still, I would hold up stuff like The Dead Zone, Pet Semitary, Salem's Lot, The Shining, Night Shift, and Firestarter as classics of their period, and highly recommended reading for anyone interested in the World of Darkness (or Chronicles of Darkness) or the dark fantasy/modern horror/strange tales genre in general.

                          And as an exception to the above, I'll plug The Girl Who Loved Tom Gordon as a good book in general and a nice example of how I think the wilderness in the WoD should come across to mundane mortals, especially in Werewolf. Also, Joyland is just a really enjoyable read.

                          And, unrelated, Victor LaValle's The Ballad of Black Tom. If you've ever wondered how and why a normal, sane and rational person might end up joining a cult of the Great Old Ones or what have you, this can give you a pretty good idea.

                                                  Since I was thinking about it in another thread, I'll go ahead and plug Neal Stephenson's work. The man looks like he belongs in the World of Darkness, and has the sort of background a lot of Virtual Adepts or Sons of Ether would line up at the store to purchase.

                                                  Snow Crash is his first "cyberpunk" novel, and had some influence on the Digital Web and other parts of Mage. In addition to things like mnemonics, computer viruses and virtual reality, it also delves into Sumerian mythology and language, and ties all those things together in the sort of odd way that fits Mage incredibly well.

                                                  Cryptonomicon is something I consider almost required reading for the Virtual Adepts, dealing with WW2 code-breaking, modern cryptography, data privacy issues and Athena cults as part of an adventure involving two separate generations.

                                                  While I own a copy of The Baroque Cycle, I've yet to have a chance to read it. It's set in the 17th and 18th centuries, involving Isaac Newton, Louis XIV, Peter the Great and a bunch of other historical figures, dealing with the development of modern science, alchemy and mathematics.
