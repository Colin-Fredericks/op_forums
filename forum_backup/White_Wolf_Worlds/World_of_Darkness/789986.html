    The World of Darkness: A Primer

    12-20-2015, 08:10 PM
    So, with all the news about the (old) World of Darkness being renewed, there seems to be a lot of interest from people who are intrigued by the concepts but might be put off by the seeming density of mythology and metaplot in the setting. Charlaquin in particular mentioned she felt that way, so I thought I'd put together a sort of compendium of the setting for neophytes. Bear in mind, it's been a while since I've immersed myself in the setting, so I may get some things partially or outright wrong. Feel free to let me know and I'll be happy to adjust it. I'll start with Vampire: The Masquerade and attempt to cover all the settings over the course of the holiday season.

    Vampire: The Masquerade

    The first game to bear the "gothic-punk" title. It might be the most metaplot heavy of the games and developed from a game of personal horror to something more in the action-adventure vein.

    Vampires, or kindred, live among us, but they do so in secret, knowing that despite their immense powers, the pure numbers and technology of humans is a danger to them. The prominent de facto group of kindred is known as the Camarilla and retains a neo-feudal power structure where the strongest and oldest rule over the rest. Most cities are under Camarilla control, and their rule is predicated on maintaining a variety of Traditions, the foremost of which is the Masquerade (that kindred are required to keep their existence secret from mortals). The Camarilla is composed of six clans that stick to rough types (though the Camarilla has both the power and arrogance to generally consider ALL kindred under their purview). These include the Ventrue (political vampires), Toreador (pretty vampires), Nosferatu (ugly vampires), Brujah (tough guy vampires), Tremere (wizard vampires), and Malkavian (crazy vampires). The Gangrel (wild vampires) were part of the Camarilla but left throughout the course of the editions to do their own thing. The primary antagonists of the Camarilla are the Sabbat who generally believe in acting like monsters and ruling over humans like cattle and consist of outcast members of the Camarilla clans along with the Lasombra (shadow-wielding coutneparts to the Ventrue) and Tzimisce (evil flesh-twisting vampires who create horrible monsters). The Anarchs are a sort of catch-all term for kindred who fight against the feudalist power structure and have a large presence of Brujah. There's also four other major independent clans: the Assamites (Middle Eastern assassin vampires), Giovanni (mafia vampires), Followers of Set (drug dealing Egyptian cultist vampires), and Ravnos (illusion-wielding gypsy vampires). WW also introduced dozens of other clans or bloodlines that were generally pretty goofy (like the Kiasyd, or faerie-vampires), but they're largely vestigial to the larger plot. If this all sounds a bit silly, that's because it is. Delightfully so.

    The cosmology believed by the kindred is generally Judeo-Christian in nature. They believe they're all descended from the Biblical Cain, who was cursed by God to be the first vampire. His grand children (the Antediluvians), and the fourth generation (Methuselahs), once ruled as Biblical-era demi-god-kings but have now been sleeping for thousands of years. There were considered to be 13 of the Antediluvian, each being the founder of one of the modern clans. They got their name from having survived the Biblical flood, so they're essentially the Lovecraftian Dark Gods of the Masquerade universe. The kindred live in fear of Gehenna, the Apocalypse that will come when the Antediluvians awaken. The general understanding is that simply one of these monsters could rip the world in half without even thinking about it. Others think Gehenna signifies Cain deciding to kill his children as a tribute to God or God himself coming to judge them. Kindred also dream of Golconda, an understanding of enlightenment whereby, through some form of spiritual awakening they can transcend their vampiric nature and become something better. Some think that means living as vampires without the negative consequences of the hunger/aversion to sunlight/etc. Others think it might mean ascending to heaven or being remade mortal. Others have more esoteric understandings of enlightenment that might resemble becoming the perfect monster or something else altogether. "Humanity" was one of the core values on the mechanics side of thing, with the understanding that if you reached Humanity 10, you achieved enlightenment, but WW eventually released other alternate "Paths" that were sometimes troubling.

    The Camarilla was formed in the 15th century and has continued well on to the modern day, with a Prince ruling over each city, usually with a Primogen (council of elders) and a Sheriff (enforcer of the law) at their side. Prior to this, each elder kindred generally ruled over a kingdom with impunity, but the rise of the Anarch movement and the mortal Inquisition forced them into creating a power block that could protect their interests. The Sabbat formed out of these early Anarchs and eventually became twisted.

    And that's generally the background for Vampire in the first couple of editions. Metaplot was introduced over the course of other books but was generally non-essential. The core game was about intra-Camarilla politics, but it was presented against a background of the Sabbat threat and the impending promise of an apocalypse. The third edition (known as "Revised Edition") brought a lot of the apocalyptic threats front and center across the game lines. It suggested that there were ill omens and portents suggesting the end of everything. The default status of new characters in vampire were 13th generation (12 generations removed from Cain). Power levels were more segmented than in Requiem. Your power levels were capped off by your generation, and the only way to go up a generation was to drink dry someone of a higher generation than you in an act known as diablerie (and this was expressly prohibited by the Traditions of the Camarilla), but "thin-bloods" started appearing: vampires with less of the strengths but also less weakness, and were seen as a sign of bad things to come. A red star known as Wormwood that could only be seen by supernatural creatures appeared mysteriously in the sky. Things reached a head in a metaplot way with the Ravnos Antediluvian awakening in a terrible frenzy. This was part of a big cross-game metaplot event known as the Week of Nightmares that eventually led to the end of the line. What resulted was basically a big battle royale among all the various game line splats in India, with the Ravnos mind-controlled into cannibalizing each other basically into extinction and the Ravnos grandpappy tearing through India like Godzilla until he was put down by a magical bomb that ripped open the spirit world (and dropped by the Technocracy. You'll see more about them in the Mage write-up). It was explained away in the mortal news media but basically signified the beginning of the end. Ancient kindred started awakening around the world, the Camarilla and Sabbat went into open warfare with one another and the independent clans were largely forced to pick sides, the facade that most of the supernatural groups kept to keep themselves hidden from mortals generally went to complete shit as ancient things from a time before time began rising up and lashing out, and everything ended with a multiple choice apocalypse end times book for each of the lines.

    And that's Vampire in a nutshell, at least as best as I can recall. Am I totally off base? Please let me know. I'll try to get around to giving Werewolf: The Apocalypse the same treatment shortly.

          I posted this in an older thread asking for book recommendations:

          Originally posted by False Epiphany View Post
          One thing to understand is that Vampire: The Masquerade evolved considerably over its 20-year lifespan. Reading a book published in '91 will be very different from reading one published in '98 or 2010. If you know what to expect from each era, you'll be able to narrow down your choices and pick the books most to your taste.

          1st edition ('91-'92) focuses on the struggle to retain one's Humanity despite being a bloodsucking monster. A sufficiently determined character can achieve Golconda (a state of spiritual enlightenment that equates to becoming a 'good guy' vampire) or even become mortal again. The conflict between newly-turned and elder vampires is also a big thing, and it's assumed that PCs will be Anarchs (young rebels struggling against the established social order that benefits elders at their expense, and very much a metaphor for the times). The larger World of Darkness is a mysterious and wide open place where it feels like anything might be possible.

          2nd edition ('92-'98) considerably broadened the scope of the game by fleshing out the Sabbat, independent clans, larger history of the Kindred, and a million other areas. Rules came out for playing elders, Paths of Enlightenment (alternative moral codes that allow PCs to play utterly inhuman vampires), and the six non-Camarilla clans. The game's focus shifted from retaining Humanity and Anarchs vs. elders to Camarilla vs. Sabbat, globe-spanning conspiracies, crossover with other WoD gamelines, and general over-the-top '90s gonzo action. The edition certainly has its detractors, but I think the changes it made were also necessary ones... 1e's core themes were probably too specific to sustain 20 years' worth of gaming books.

          Revised ('98-'04), like the name implies, aimed to revise the gameline and bring the excesses of 2nd edition under control. Most fans will agree it succeeded there. Characters and concepts are less over the top, and writing quality compared to earlier editions is plain better. It did, however, introduce numerous changes to VtM's established setting (the metaplot), which a lot of fans weren't wild about (for example, several clans/bloodlines were wiped out, Los Angeles was taken over by Kue-jin, various NPCs were killed off, etc). The edition's big focus is on the end of the world approaching, Kindred society collapsing in millennialist panic (again, VtM was very much a game of its time), and the Camarilla-Sabbat war. The world is also a lot bleaker... more NPCs were outright assholes, Golconda was a pipe dream, the Camarilla was painted as being just as rotten as the Sabbat, the Anarchs were basically irrelevant, etc. Plus, everyone's screwed once the Antediluvians wake up. Personally, I find that when you keep in mind Revised is a different game from earlier editions, it's quite enjoyable for what it is. Just like one can appreciate the 1989 and 2005 Batman movies without needing to compare them against each other.

          Finally, V20 ('10-present) takes a metaplot-agnostic approach and tries to incorporate the best elements of the prior editions; ie, 1's elders vs. Anarchs conflict, 2e's breadth of focus, Revised's superior writing and toned-down gonzoness, etc. I think it's the best overall edition, but YMMV.

          Blood and Bourbon, my New Orleans-based Vampire chronicle.

              To make a long story short...

              Revised was released in August 1998, and basically said "everything is going to hell in a handbasket." The Sabbat launched an offensive to capture the East Coast from the Camarilla, the Kuei-jin (Asian vampires) invaded the Anarch Free States in California, and an alarming number of Ravnos Methuselahs were awakening in India (which would presage the Week of Nightmares). The Assamites also threw off the Tremere curse that prevented them from engaging in diablerie, although how they accomplished it was unclear.

              Children of the Night, published in April '99, expanded on many of these plot points and introduced a few new ones. Ur-Shulgi, an unfathomably ancient Assamite Methuselah, was identified as the reason for the clan's broken curse. The Harbingers of the Skulls, strongly implied to be the last remnants of the Cappadocians, returned to earth from their exile in the Underworld to take vengeance on the Giovanni.

              Time of Thin Blood came out in May '99 and introduced the thin-bloods, mongrel vampires barely stronger than mortals who were considered harbingers of Gehenna. The Week of Nightmares also took place, which went as dxanders details: the Ravnos Antediluvian woke up, got crisped by the Technocracy, and wiped out his entire clan.

              In 2000, White Wolf released Transylvania Chronicles IV, possibly one of the most railroady tabletop adventures ever published. Saulot, the long-thought diablerized Salubri Antediluvian, turned out to have retained his consciousness and been co-habiting Tremere's body, from which he played the latter's entire clan for dupes. Tremere, unable to defeat Saulot in their battle of wills, fled his body and took over Goratrix's (a treacherous former pupil of his), whose spirit he shunted into a mirror. He then performed a ritual while inside Goratrix's body to destroy almost every Tremere who'd joined the Sabbat. The PCs got to witness these riveting events occur and do precisely zero to affect their outcome.

              Nights of Prophecy came out shortly later and detailed a significant of metaplot events. The Assamites experienced an internal schism between those clanmates loyal to ur-Shulgi, who called on them to reject Islam in favor of Antediluvian worship, and those clanmates who refused to and defected to the Camarilla. The Kue-jin conquered Los Angeles and basically wiped the Anarch Free State. The Camarilla, after a seemingly endless stream of losses, made a comeback by capturing New York City from the Sabbat--which Lambach Ruthven discovered was the home of the Tzimisce Antediluvian. Baba Yaga, a Nosferatu Methuselah who'd basically taken over Russia, was destroyed by a Nicktuku. Once again, PCs involved in events pretty much just got sit around and watch.

              New York by Night, released just after 9/11, expanded on NoP's plot events in the Big Apple. The city's Princedom was up for grabs and could be claimed by PCs. White Wolf was starting to learn their lesson from Transylvania Chronicles and NoP.

              Cairo by Night came out in February '02. Like New York by Night, it largely set up situations/adventures for PCs to deal with, rather than saying "this and this happened." Most significantly, a Setite Methuselah was searching for a pregnant thin-blood who could potentially be the Last Daughter of Eve, a figure prophesized to lead humanity to salvation during Gehenna.

              San Francisco by Night debuted in June 2002. It had the Kue-jin conquer San Francisco and establish the New Promise Mandarate, finally bringing them into conflict with the Camarilla rather than the Anarchs. Whether the Kuei-jin cemented their gains or were expelled from the city was up to individual troupes.

              August 2002's Mexico City by Night had the Regent of the Sabbat, Melinda Galbraith, assassinated by parties unknown. Again, it was up to individual troupes who the culprit was and how this event would impact the Sword of Caine. Depending on PC actions, the Sabbat might elect a new Regent or descend into civil war at one of the worst times ever.

              2003's Guide to the Black Hand was the last real metaplot book, in that events which took place in future books wouldn't really be referenced in further sequels. GttBH established that Izhim ur-Baal, Djujah, and Elimelech had all disappeared, and that Jalan-Aajav had basically seized control of the Black Hand.

              Later that year, Lair of the Hidden introduced a reclusive coterie of millennia-old Golconda seekers who could be instrumental in surviving or hastening Gehenna. How things turned out with them was totally up to PCs. (LotH was a pretty amazingly-written book, given that it plopped neonate PCs into a scenario with twelve uber-powerful elders and two entities too powerful to even have stats, yet never once railroaded or jerked players around. The twelve elders' fates were completely in their hands.)

              The Red Sign featured and alliance of mortal mages and Kindred luminaries who'd discovered a way to reverse the Embrace and turn vampires back into humans. Once again, the writers had no predetermined outcomes in mind (namely, did the ritual really work or carry horrific side effects?), though it was implied the Biblical ramifications of the Red Sign would lead to...

              Gehenna, the line's last book until V20, which detailed the eponyous end of the world. What particular flavor the apocalypse took was up to STs and their troupes.

              Bloodlines the video game also takes place just before Gehenna in '04, though delays on Troika's end of things resulted in it coming out after the gameline had officially ended.


              Blood and Bourbon, my New Orleans-based Vampire chronicle.
