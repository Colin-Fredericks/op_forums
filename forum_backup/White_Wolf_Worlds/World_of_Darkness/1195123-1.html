        Gothic (not Goth) refers to the Gothic literary genre of the 18th and 19th centuries and it's modern decedents, as well as it's spin-offs (Southern Gothic, German "Shudder Novels", Pulp Horror, Hammer Horror films, etc.) and to certain traits of the late medieval architecture that played such a huge part in it. Said architecture tends to be large and imposing, and is often associated with symbols of authority such as churches and castles. This combination of age, size and power over people gives off a mood of oppression and of the protagonists' inherent weakness within the greater scheme of the world. Examples of such Gothic worlds include Franz Kafka's The Trial, Ray Bradbury's Fahrenheit 451, Ira Levin's The Stepford Wives, Ken Kessey's One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest, and the movies The Godfather (1972), Brazil (1985) and Idiocracy (2006).

        Punk refers not to the music movement of the 1970s but to the attitudes behind it: be yourself and fuck anyone who has a problem with it. It is a theme of being a person and an individual rather than just a number or a cog. You - yes, you - are important and your voice is worth being heard, provided you are willing to speak up and accept the potential consequences that come with not following the crowd. It's not a new phenomenon. Most of the 19th century anti-slavery and women's suffrage activists qualify, along side labor activists, Native American/First Nation leaders fighting against displacement, and leaders of some of the 1848 revolutions in Europe, just to give a few examples.

        Stuff from the past decade or so I'd give as examples of Gothic-Punk include the Harry Potter novels, the Luke Cage series on Netflix, and the movie Frozen. None of them involve subcultures or are grimdark, but all involve the protagonists being haunted by the past events and constrained by present circumstances while still striving against those things in the hope for something better.

        What is tolerance? It is the consequence of humanity. We are all formed of frailty and error; let us pardon reciprocally each other's folly. That is the first law of nature.
        Voltaire, "Tolerance" (1764)

                        What ultimately happened, I think that the writers back in the day had these themes, these feelings and have seen around them the subcultures whose grasped these feelings at the time, but also the world at large and had the same sources which inspired those and also their own experiences with those sources.

                        Then, I think, they went and examined these sources (and the subcultures and social movements too) more closely to see which were the main ideas and emotions and impressions behind them. Then they summarized and extrapolated it beyond the subcultures or the original sources, like literature and architecture and named it gothic-punk. In that, gothic stands for some things and punk for others as we discussed.

                        What is funny, that while it was, I think, a good choice for naming the end result, because it also provided a tie-in to the subcultures and ultimately, the youth of those days, it also ended up making a lot of misconception and even turning away some people after those subcultures have fallen out from the mainstream's fickle favor.

                        Essentially, when goth and punk wasn't chich any more, a lot of people didn't take the effort to understand that gothic-punk is not goth/punk, even if they are connected, by having the same sources.

                                    Originally posted by No One of Consequence View Post
                                    Gothic (not Goth) refers to the Gothic literary genre of the 18th and 19th centuries and it's modern decedents, as well as it's spin-offs (Southern Gothic, German "Shudder Novels", Pulp Horror, Hammer Horror films, etc.) and to certain traits of the late medieval architecture that played such a huge part in it. Said architecture tends to be large and imposing, and is often associated with symbols of authority such as churches and castles. This combination of age, size and power over people gives off a mood of oppression and of the protagonists' inherent weakness within the greater scheme of the world. Examples of such Gothic worlds include Franz Kafka's The Trial, Ray Bradbury's Fahrenheit 451, Ira Levin's The Stepford Wives, Ken Kessey's One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest, and the movies The Godfather (1972), Brazil (1985) and Idiocracy (2006).
                                    I find the Gothic genre also is concerned, thematically, with the ideas of age and decay, both physical and moral. What inspired Gothic literature was the presence of medieval architecture, as writers tried to pen stories that played into the feelings invoked by those old structures. Something I like to call "Desolate Beauty". The earliest Gothic stories were period pieces, set during those medieval years. Concerning themselves with the plight of monarchs and noble houses. And how they fell to degeneration, and decay.

                                    Old school Gothic literature is about once great institutions gone to seed. It's also how that very seeding preludes the fall of greatness and the reclamation of it by nature.

                                    Gothic horror arose from the idea that even the ordered, "proper" structure of modern society is not immune to powers beyond the understanding of mortals. Ghosts, the undead, monsters, even the weather turn against that which once stood proudly. Where even if you think you understand your world, you couldn't be more wrong. And the Gothic Horrors will come and disabuse you of those notions.


                                    These themes are central to Vampire: The Masquerade. Institutions built by ancient figures, working within the structures of "noble" houses. Vampires - who believe themselves ageless and informed - are truly paralyzed by fear of the unknown. The persecution of the Caitiff and the Thin Blood is as much a paranoid reaction against what they could be or what powers they could manifest, as it was against any fear of uprising by the young. The Camarilla is a stagnant ruling elite, and the Sabbat are religious extremists who have lost sight of their original ethos. The Independents - the Giovanni, Assamites, Followers of Set, and even Ravnos - dabble in strange powers and even stranger beliefs. All quake in terror of the savages of the wilderness (Werewolves), shrink away from the mortals who wield strange magic(k) new and old (Mages), and become unnerved when the dead (many made by the vampires themselves) fail to stay quiet (Wraiths).

                                    It doesn't really matter if the vampires wear all black or not. What matters are the themes at play, when it comes to the Gothic.

                                    Magestuck: The Ascension
