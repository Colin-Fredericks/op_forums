    Title question: Why "Wraith" and "Changeling" instead of "Ghost" and "Faerie?"

    11-26-2013, 04:54 PM
    I'm gearing up to start a new Wraith: the Oblivion chronicle. (Yay!) Going through old sourcebooks has brought up an old question that I've never found an answer for.

    In older sourcebooks, references to the game lines in the classic World of Darkness referred to Vampire, Werewolf, Mage, Faerie, and Ghost. That was in times when only Vampire and Werewolf were published, circa 1992 or so. Fast-forward to late 1995, and we have Vampire, Werewolf, Mage, Wraith, and Changeling.

    Why the change in titles?

    I've heard a variety of theories ranging from the armchair-lawyer stuff to the utterly bizarre. While I'm glad for any insights, a citation would be lovely.

    Thanks!

      My guess would be that someone thought Wraith and Changeling sounded cooler than Ghost and Faerie.

                              I'm neutral to the word "wraith" itself, but in my experience, it's not as clearly and widely understood as "ghost." When the product line was named, someone had a good reason for choosing "wraith" instead of "ghost," that's what I'm looking for.

                              Last night I checked out the 1st edition Storyteller's Handbook for Vampire, where they actually describe a generic faerie and ghost in the antagonists section (and yes, they were called faeries and ghosts, not changelings and wraiths). The ghost described there is pretty close to the characters we get in Wraith. The faerie seems a bit different than what we get in Changeling, however. They are described as literal faeries that travel back and forth from their extraplanar realm, not as faerie souls trapped in human bodies. They also mention how faeries often kidnap human children, although they don't outright state that they replace kidnapped children with faeries.

                              It seems that even back in 1992 or so, the designers had some ideas about what those future games would look like... Then those plans changed. That's what I'm getting at. That's what I'm curious about.

                              About the specific titles... When I introduce the game to new people and tell them that it's Wraith: the Oblivion, the reaction is often one of confusion. It's often along the lines of, "What's a wraith? Like the Nazgûl out of Tolkien?" To which I'll reply, "You're playing a ghost. That's just a Scottish word for 'ghost' that the publisher decided on for the title of this game line." As soon as I tell people that they're playing ghosts, they get it. In the same way, you don't have to explain Vampire or Werewolf to new players unfamiliar with the game--everyone knows what a vampire or werewolf is. Yet the reaction might be different if the publisher had decided to name those game lines, say, Vrykolaka or Fear-Faol.

                                      I'm sure the change from Ghost to Wraith was purely a stylistic choice. Wraith sounds more goth than Ghost, and most folks know Wraith is a synonym for Ghost.

                                      As for Changeling's changes, there were five years between when Vampire was first published and when Changeling was first published. That's more than enough time to go from a vague idea of "hey, let's do a faerie game!" to develop and refine ideas (and get heavily inspired by Neil Gaiman's Sandman ) into something quite different from what was in the back of Vampire 1st edition. Not to mention that in those five years White Wolf went from a handful of folks operating out of a garage to a full blown publishing company with quite a few full time staffers. There were a lot more voices pitching ideas when it was time to actually sit down and write Changeling than there were when Vampire was published.

                                      I do remember after Dark Ages: Fae came out, BlackHat Matt said that while he was developing it, he intentionally tried to make the fae in it closer to the old 1st edition Vampire version of faeries. I thought it was a neat idea to have the fae in the World of Darkness change over time in the same way they were written about in the game books.

                                                          "Wraith" meaning "an otherworldly being from beyond death" is, essentially, an invention of D&D. Before that it was no more loaded a word than ghost or specter. If you look at the Middle Earth stuff, wraith gets applied to both your run of the mill dead people spirits still hanging around for some reason and otherworldly beings from beyond death (though they are still just magically powerful dead people, they're not something else). Before Tolkien it is a pretty obscure word that doesn't seem to have any such connotations.

                                                          And mage doesn't mean "a scholar who dabbles in the forces of the universe" it means a person that does magic. Mage and wizard are synonyms. They mean the same thing. MtA was probably called Mage because D&D (again drastically reinterpreting from Tolkien) got to wizard first. I doubt there was any larger choice beyond, "we don't want people to think this is D&D style wizards," beyond wanting people to associate Mage with Ars Magica (where wizard and magus are used pretty much interchangeably).
