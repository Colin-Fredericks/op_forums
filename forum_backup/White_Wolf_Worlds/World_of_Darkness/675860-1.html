    (Glanced at other responses, but just jumping in and not addressing anyone in particular...)

    Like the consensus seems to say, "Wraith" is a tricky one for many players/STs because, as presented, it *really* takes a lot of mental investment in terms of characterization than any other splat. (You can always make up "yet another Toreador", what-have-you, as an on-the-spot character to play, but it's really trickier with Wraith, as you really have to "go deep" to come up with a properly justifiable character who would continue to exist after death.)

    Changeling is sort of on the flip of that, and while not *quite* as hard meta-conceptually, it can be tricky for some players because you ultimately have to understand the dynamics involved. That is, instead of having to "go deep" for reasons to exist a la' Wraith, you have to understand how to be essentially the dynamic "oomph" that makes life/existence worth living (even with the more mopey types, such as the Sluagh, there is the undeniable sense of continuation and "fae-ness" that resonates). It's sort of like understanding what makes a really good stand-up comedian *funny* -- anyone can be "funny", or be quite humorous, but it's *work* and a measure of talent and experience to make a *comedian* (read: Changeling) happen. (That doesn't mean said character has to be "funny", rather I am just drawing a distinction between someone being "funny" -- a typical character from most of the other splats -- and being a "comedian" -- representing the unique flavor and essence that being a Changeling or Fae represents... it's a hard thing for some people to process...).

    Still, though, for either Wraith or Changeling, the key is getting into character and roleplay, roleplay, roleplay. While this "should be" the case for virtually all characters created, it's very much more so the key for these to splats above any of the others. I've noted over the almost 25 years of WoD (and the proto-versions of Changelings and Wraiths play-tested at the early MET Larps before the hardcovers, proper, came out) that it's more so (not always, but usually) the pwrgmrz and the "I essentially always play myself" players who have the most trouble with those two games/splats. Anyone, of course, might have trouble for various reasons -- not "getting" the setting, or understanding that you can tweak/alter various aspects to better fit your own tastes/views -- but generally the key is to roleplay the fuck out of the characters in question and "step-it-up" if you're not accustomed to doing so. That's usually been the "Eureka!"-moment for a number of players I've seen have problems with them over the years but who suddenly "got it" -- they went deep and found the solution was there the entire time....

    I have been around here for waaaayyyy too fucking long...

        Creating characters in Wraith, especially, can be hard. I usually let new players create their Shadows after the first session (they can be played like the P'o in KotE if they need to appear in the first session) and give a bit of leeway in tweaking starting stats once they figure out what works in play. A helpful ST can also sketch out a character concept with a player, then draw up suggested traits, and let the player tweak them as needed. That's worked for me to help players overcome their initial fears about the complexity of character creation. I enjoy creating characters anyway, so it's not too much of a problem.

                I've only recently started reading into classic and planning to run a game there, so my experience with this is nonexistent and solely based on guesswork. That said, I think Wraith would be the hardest. Primarily because all the other games take place in the real world, while Wraith takes place, mostly, in the Underworld. It's one thing for you to run a world that is basically 'our world, but darker,' where there's a sinister agenda behind not-McDonalds, school systems are manipulated by the conspiracies and so forth. It's another for the Underworld, which is not our world, but is a darker reflection of it. It seems like there's a lot more work to be done building the setting and getting players to understand the differences and what the Underworld is like.

                I'm sure this isn't a problem everyone has, but I've also struggled to get my players to embrace the concept of the Dark Ages. They are worried they will not appreciate the differences enough and aren't interested enough in history to really know what's available and what's going on, so they may make a faux pas and try to use concepts that didn't exist yet.
