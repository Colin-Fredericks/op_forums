                                  There are no direct references to Lovecraftiana in the games, but there are various indirect references where the author was clearly inspired by Lovecraft or other mythos authors. As others have mentioned, the most frequent references are in Mage with the Nephandi and Werewolf in regards to the Wyrm with some others in Vampire. Presumably, Changeling might have some references to the Dreamlands stories, but I've read very little Changeling. Here are some things that may be reference to Lovecraft,

                                  1) There seems to be something in Antarctica which some Garou are interested in. Possibly a reference to the Mountains of Madness.
                                  2) Various Necronomicon like books, usually in Mage but Werewolf has them too. Especially the Malleus Nefandorum and the Liber Labyrinthus.
                                  3) The Children of the Baali could be based on certain interpretations.
                                  4) Many of the Black Spiral Dancer Totems seem inspired by the Mythos - Relshab and G'lough seem like common Mythos concepts.
                                  5) Vhujunka seem to be inspired by the Serpent People.
                                  6) There is a direct reference to the Carpenter film The Prince of Darkness (Exhibit 338: the Anti-Christ Serum in Book of the Weaver) which is Lovecraft one removed.
                                  7) Blood Dim Ties has references to a Cthulhu like creature, and the Chluroviah are clearly based on Deep Ones (and Starro the Conqueror of DC comics!)

                                  Those are just the ones on the tip of my head.

                                      Of course, the problem with direct parallels is that cWoD, even WtA, possesses too much optimism. Humans are nothing more than a passing curiosity to the creatures of Lovecraft. They are delusional and are incapable of accurately perceiving Reality without going insane because of a failure in their evolution. While the creatures of Lovecraft may entertain themselves through abusing humans, the majority of them have existed for millions or billions of years before humanity and will exist millions or billions of years after humanity. There is also no grand end in the cosmos of Lovecraft (the Universe existed eternally before humanity and will continue to exist forever after humanity is gone).

There is also no grand end in the cosmos of Lovecraft (the Universe existed eternally before humanity and will continue to exist forever after humanity is gone).
                                          I see this less as a bug, and more like a feature. The World of Darkness, for all its eponymous darkness, is a setting where the Lovecraftian concept of a tiny world in a larger, uncaring universe is directly opposed by and competed with that world's inhabitants, and their own conceptions. The dissonance between themes and outlooks is intentional. With Mage especially, but in all the games, it's a war of world views. And it plays out everywhere, this conflict between small beings and seemingly unstoppable abominations beyond them.

                                          In Vampire, young Kindred fight against the machinations of their elders, including the godlike antedeluvians; trying to impose their own self will upon a world filled with puppet masters, schemers, and domitors. In Werewolf, it's a conflict between the Shapechangers, who represent our little world, and the cosmic forces that have gone mad (or, in the case of the Wyld, were arguably always mad). In Mage, the Awakened fight against each other to define what Reality is, but come together to combat the machinations of their heartless kin, and the blasphemous things from outside the Tellurian that seek to plunder it. In Changeling, those touched by old fae souls - many of them mere children - must contend with an ever-thickening blanket of Banality, that would smother the world and kill the dreams of men. In Wraith, the opponent of every ghost is nothing short of Oblivion itself, followed shortly after by their own personified existential despair, and the lingering doubts about whether there is a true afterlife to look forward to

                                          To walk the difficult path - to push against these seemingly irresistible, transcendent forces - is to affirm one's own importance in Reality, no matter how small. Lovecraft posits an Objective view of the universe as a huge place, filled with monsters and gods and gods that are also monsters. Too huge for any small mind to grasp. The World of Darkness sees that argument, gives it a fair consideration, then throws it back in Lovecraft's face, displaying its own Subjective Reality. In a world where - impossibly - two contradictory truths could both be correct, at the same time and in the same sense, the small things that dwell on this tiny planet can reject Lovecraft's reality, and substitute their own.

                                          A young lick CAN devour a Methusalah. Werewolves CAN stymie the Wyrm. Mages DO tell the Great Ones to get off their property, and HAVE killed a blood god. The point where the competing Paradigms - Lovecraft's and the people of Earth - collide, THAT is where drama comes in.

                                          Magestuck: The Ascension

                                                  Originally posted by Aya Tari View Post
                                                  That being said, WA is probably the closest because the Garou know that they are doomed but fight anyway because they would rather die fighting than die running away.

                                                  Except that WtA, along with all of the CWoD gamelines, run of the assumption that the universe is malefic, not indifferent.

                                                  The Wyrm wants to destroy the earth, the Vampires need humanity to survive, and Mages NEED something to believe in for their magic to work (unless they go Nephandi, but even then they suffer the same Malefic fallacy the Wyrm does.) The World of Darkness is so earth and human centric that every gamelines emphasizes how important humanity is and how precious and special our world is. This isn't Lovecraftian. In a Lovecraftian game setting the earth and every single human and supernatural creature on it regardless of gameline would be ultimately inconsequential. The battles they fight would be ultimately meaningless because they are so insignificant on a cosmic scale that they might as well have never happened at all.

                                                  And for that to work, the universe itself must be indifferent, not Malefic. The word corruption is meaningless (and pointless) in the Mythos, because corruption only exists in a world where evil exists. And the Classic World of Darkness would not be the Classic World of Darkness without that Malefic undercurrent to every aspect of the gamelines.

                                                      Indifference can be malefic. Lovecraft used the word corruption (or its synonyms) in every one of his stories, so he obviously thought that the universe was malefic in its indifference.                                                           Two things first.

                                                          1) The latter point about personal significance & actual significance is spot on in regards to WtA. Well said.

                                                          2) I made a mistake, malefic isn't the right word I'm looking for.

                                                          My point is that the phenomena & creatures of Lovecraft's work don't adhere to conventional ideas of good & evil. Classic World of Darkness very much does even in WtA due to very clear Judeo Christian undertones (Werewolf the "Apocalypse") This idea that there IS cosmic Evil automatically makes a true Lovecraftian story impossible because the Great Old Ones never acted purposefully malevolently. The closest one to ever do so is Nyrlathotep, and even then everything human about the character is a intentional facade to represent humanity's insistence upon trying to understand the incomprehensible.

                                                          It is a matter of point though that in Lovecraft's stories the main source of malevolence comes from human characters, or "sentient human-like entities" like the Elder Things from the Mountains of Madness. Malevolence is more accurately ambitious self-interest at the cost of all else in the Mythos, where people foolishly try to wield powers they couldn't hope to control on order to stave off death or reshape the world to their own desires. And while CWoD does do a pretty fantastic job of this (and is truly the closest it comes to being Lovecraftian) it kneecaps itself by making the "dark forces" truly malevolent entities rather than cosmically indifferent forces that barely register the existence of earth rather than greedily hungering for it.
