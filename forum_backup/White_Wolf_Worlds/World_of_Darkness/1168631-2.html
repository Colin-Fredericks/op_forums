    Originally posted by PMárk View Post
    Which are totally inconsequential from the average buyer's standpoint.
    The amount of resources companies put into these sorts of consideration would indicate otherwise.

    It's not like there are tons of old edition books out there on the shelves with which you can confuse it.
    See, it's actually worse now, because so much shopping is done online (even with lots of RPG publishers desperate to get back into physical stores). People looking for good deals on Amazon or eBay frequently end up asking people that they know are fans (even going to forums about it) to figure out which edition something is because sellers aren't always clear (or might not even know themselves). Just going to the store and buying the most currently available thing isn't even an option for at least 95% of RPGs, and at least around here, a lot of stores that stock RPG books have a clutter of old edition books mixed in with anything that isn't the latest D&D, FFG, or Paizo books. Some even prominently display older books because they're trying to sell them to collectors (esp. in non-gaming primary shops, like comic book stores that also stock gaming stuff).

    The CofD has been the official thing for the old nWoD brand for two years now, and I still see people confused by it despite it being really easy to find information on (like the first thing I get when I Google "chronicles of darkness" is the fan wikia which actually explains how the CofD is a different set of games from the cWoD, and the naming thing).

    So yeah, this stuff does actually matter to consumers, even if it isn't always a direct or conscious thing.

    D&D is a relatively unique position in this stuff because it is still "the RPG" to most people. It's an outlier that really doesn't say how everyone else needs to operate.

    So, I think stating the edition's number on the cover is out of sync with the current habits of the industry and also unnecessary.
    I really don't see the evidence for this. Getting past the limited selection of books you picked out (which are the ones with the most physical store presence because they're tied to companies that do physical distribution by necessity for things like CCGs, minis, etc.), you see extremely little consensus, because it really seems far more situational and company based than you want to accept. The more games you look at, the less there seems to be any direct consistency around this like you seem to be claiming.

    If buyers would want to learn more about the game's history, they could easily do it.
    Sure. But a huge part of the reason to buy V5 is that it is a game with history. That's a giant part of the marketing here. That's why WW funded a documentary about VtM. They want people to know that this isn't some brand new thing trying to push into the crowded RPG world. It's an old thing with a new energy coming back for it's place near the top. Customers need to see that on the cover in a simple and direct way to connect the new book to the marketing..

                                                Of course both previews are bad because WW doesn't seem to get why the alpha/beta test divide is supposed to entail. Alpha playtests are not supposed to be public. Alpha is when you're still hammering out everything and lots of stuff changes. You don't put them out to the public because they rarely represent anything close to the final product, and playtesting is done with preselected small groups; though this is harder to pull off in today's world since leaks are pretty much a given too.

                                                Beta is when you start doing big public playtests, because beta means you're at the point where the majority of the game is already designed and you want extra eyes to refine everything.

                                                            Originally posted by PewPew View Post
                                                            Vampires don't spend blood for dominate, that's some basic metaphysics.
                                                            Eh? This... seems an odd complaint. There isn't some strict metaphysical basis for when Disciplines cost blood or even willpower. The assignments of costs have always seemed to be more on the game side of things to keep things from getting spammed by attaching a limited resource to it. The VtM: Bloodlines video game frequently had you spend blood on things that you don't in the TT rules, because the assumptions of the mechanics are different.

                                                            V5's Hunger mechanics (something I very much dislike) also isn't the same as spending blood in a key way: you can in theory do it as much as you want. From the context of "all Disciplines are an expression of the power of the Beast," and, "any time a vampire calls on the power of the Beast, they risk growing hungry and must feed to sate it or risk losing control," having every Discipline invoke Hunger makes sense. The rules are still bad IMO, but within the framework WW wants to use, this does make sense.

                                                            Fianna fetishes aren't known for their craftsmanship, but their tales, that's the most defining trait of the fianna, alcoholism and metis bashing aside (craftsmanship; Get, Maybe SF and GW).

The Fianna are generally portrayed as one of the most artistic oriented Tribes. It's also a Merit, not a default bonus.
