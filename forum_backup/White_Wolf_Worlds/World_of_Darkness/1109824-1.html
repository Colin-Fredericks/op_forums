            I can't think of a time where I DIDN'T ignore the RAW to make a good story.... Redeem a Widderslainte? Why not?

            It's supposed to take hella-high Spheres to bring back the dead and/or not be possible and I let them go on a mystic treasure hunt and make deals with all sorts of questionable beings, and, when the shopping list was completed and the weird dance was done, the character was resurrected {sorta - it was his soul, but not in his original body, except for that tattoo they regrafted from his baby-momma who had grafted it onto herself, and so on...) without ANYONE having Life 5, Matter 5, Spirit 5 or whatever the requirements are or aren't right now. Which is a better game? The one where the ST says, "You can't do that because the rules say so!" or the one where the ST says, "Well, it's never been done before to the best of your knowledge... You still want to try? Okay, what do you want to do next?"

            After all, the PCs are the protagonists of your game, not the NPCs or the RAW. Like the characters in the movie or novel, they're supposed to be the ones who do the impossible thing no one else can do, unless that's not the game you're playing? I mean, sure, you can play a game where it's all doom and gloom and Kafka-esque fruitless striving. Again, why not? As long as that's the game that people want to play (or the character arc a player is looking to play), it's fine. And picking a Flaw with a high associated cost doesn't necessarily mean you are dealing with someone who wants the gloom and doom. It's important to put these things right out there on the table.

            I guess if the player is taking the Flaw purely for the points and never wants to pay the associated price (whether that's "You are F*#$ED!" or "Man, no one knows a way around this.... Good luck finding it and there is a high chance of failure...") you have a problem, but it's more of a "problem player" problem than anything else.

            And, after all, the irredeemable Widderslainte is not a really a thing in Mage. Senex was able to deus ex machina around it.

                    Aleph's comment really hits the nail on the head for me.

                    I'm definitely not opposed to the idea of player characters attempting, and succeeding, in an impossible quest. But that's why communication is really important. Personally, if I were running a "redeem the Widderslainte" plot, I'd probably try to discourage my players from choosing to play the Widderslainte themselves, for three reasons.

                    The first is the frustration aspect. I'm honestly not sure what I'd require from players to actually succeed in this kind of quest, but there'd be a LOT of potential for failure. The player HAS to be on board with the idea that, more likely than not, their character will have a really painful ending, and that's a hard burden for a lot of players. It can be easier to accept failure when it's an NPC on the line.

                    The second is that if we're talking about a group game as opposed to a one-on-one game, then there's a risk of one character monopolizing the entire story. That can be tricky to balance the focus. If the Widderslainte is an NPC, it's easier to keep the focus on the other players and their emotional reactions to events. The story then is less about whether or not they actually succeed and more about how the quest impacts each of them.

                    The third is that having a player character as a Widderslainte pretty much ropes all of the other characters into this one, very specific story. It's very possible that the other players would have no interest in a redeem the Widderslainte quest. Or their characters, as created, would have no reason to want to undergo a quest like this. If the Widderslainte character is an NPC, then the players have more freedom to decide how they want to deal with the situation.

                    But I think it's really important that a player who takes this flaw understands the World of Darkness doesn't require games to have a happy ending anyway. (There's a reason that Gehenna/Apocalypse/Ascension/Time of Judgment exist after all.) Choosing a flaw like this makes it even less likely. And the other players and storyteller are under no obligation to cater to one player's whims, unless it's a story that they ALL want to play.

                    My original pithy comment was a bit too flippant I suppose, but the fact of the matter is that even if the players are on board with the idea of a cure the Widderslainte plot, we'd be talking realistically about a chronicle long plot that would involve a lot of pain, torment, and endless possibility for failure. Obviously, many players don't want to play a dark and tragic ending. But if that's the case, then they really should not be taking a flaw like this.

                        This is a crazy idea, but I think if someone wanted to play a Widderslainte that really tries to be "good", or as good as possible, and yet respect the destiny of evil that should await the character...It may be neat to have a Degeneration system, like that of Vampire, Demon or even Wraith.

                        Of course you can say that you are a Widderslainte, and act good nevertheless, but if you want to at least do a modicum attempt at respecting the themes of the Fallen, this would be cheating.

                        A system of Degeneration can create the detachement from your character that you need in order to keep a will to be good, but still understand that you're becoming less and less good. It provides a measurement that takes a bit of the weight of interpretation out of your shoulders, and marks a line you can't cross if you don't want to be full "unplayable" Nephandi.
                        Vampire plays very well with this, as vampires that try to keep their Humanity at higher levels soon discover that this it's easier said than done (as vampire world it's very violent). Humanity score works as a way to keep tabs on your degeneration in spite of the fact that you don't actively "try" to fall. You could even make it so that "humanity" cannot be recovered once lost (like Abominations) if you want the struggle to be especially harrowing.

                        The Shadow in this case would be the Fallen Soul/Inverted Avatar, that pulls you into Nephandism. It would be like having a Nephandy trying to corrupt her, but this time the Nephandy it's her own soul that knows everything about her and can't be kept away. The Morality could be build arround ideals of Ascencion, thinking about how these can be twisted into Descencion.There will be a turning point in wich your character would become unapologetically evil, and turn into an NPC...Of course this isn't a desirable outcome, and thus searching for a way to heal your "condition" would be very desirable.

                        What can the Inverted Avatar do to contribute to Descencion? Well, I'm not a fan of giving the Inverted Avatar more power than it should have...that being said there are several options dependant on the Avatar. that are well within the rules
                        Of course a lvl 5 Manifest Avatar it's going to be much more dangerous than a lvl 1 Circunspect one. I would like my Widderslainte PC to be good sport and take a potent avatar, but shouldn't be mandatory unless it's painfully obvious that the player wants to cheat the curse.
                        At the very least, I would say that even the weakests Avatars can make sure that their mages cannot ever succeed in a Search unless they loose a point of "humanity", after all, it's the Avatar the one who judges (and yours it's an asshole). This, by itself, presents a strong moral chalenge that's not unlike what a character like Ghost Rider has to put trough-You want more power?, fine: are you ready to bargain with the Devil?.
                        A really active evil Avatar may offer teaching (an Avatar can be your Mentor for Arete and Spheres), Past Lives, and more access to the Dream in exchange for a few bad acts.
                        And all of this would be on top of the 7 lvl Flaw bonuses and penalties tempting you to the dark side. And that's before Banes, devils, and actual Nephandi take interest on you...

One Chorister can have a completely different Focus than another in all three parts (and in each of the sub-parts of the Instruments) and everything about those parts can be described in a very different way (say.... a jazz soloist with a set of very Secular Humanist style Universalist underpinings to a completely visceral faith vs. a tone deaf Orthodox rebi with a vast knowledge of Judaic law and the Kabbalah with incisive logical understanding worthy of Sherlock Holmes). It's not a common Practice that all Choristers use, at least in part, because there is a list of options that is explicitly wide open. It's that missing something that was meant with one of the common (though, admittedly, especially in hide sight, imprecise) meanings of "paradigm" before M20.
