                Originally posted by Dataweaver View Post

                Where I'm coming from with this is that in many cases, Coincidental Magick is to Mage as Dramatic Editing is to the Trinity Continuum or Twists of Fate are to Scion. In each of these cases, the reality that you're dealing with is more cinematic than realistic. You can see this in M20, where the Technocracy's counterpart to mythic threads are the hypernarratives: basically, if it's a “movie reality” thing, them you get -1 Difficulty on your working for tapping into a hypernarrative. Coincidental Effects that actually represent the kinds of coincidences that make up such a big part of cinematic reality are part and parcel to this.

                And you keep reading “might have been” as “wasn't, until the mage changed things”. That's specifically not what I'm talking about. The Storyteller can't be expected to pre-establish every detail about the scene and the surrounding environment; so a lot of details (such as whether or not there are any delivery trucks nearby) aren't known by anyone, because the Storyteller didn't nail them down. And that's where the convenient delivery truck resides: in those details that haven't been specified. That's why I brought up HAB vs. HOO: the latter assumes that every detail has been nailed down; the former doesn't.
                I can't agree with this. For several reasons.

                This is a power I already give my players in every RPG I run. The world can't be fully defined to us and so we can fill in unestablished yet reasonable things about the world (subject to veto). To give this ability over to Coincidental Magic I have to take away narrative control from the players and I see no reason to do that.

                The ambiguity and gaps that you are exploiting are general elements of shared storytelling, not properties of the world of Mage. Just because we as the players don't know what is in the blank spots doesn't mean they are undefined in universe. The truck driver knows whether he's there or not, as does the hypothetical observer.

                I realize there's not much mechanical difference between the players deciding there is a nearby truck and the spell deciding there is a nearby truck, but from a gaming standpoint the difference is vast. Mage, to me, is not a game about the characters realizing they are in a story and magic is not the process of them exploiting the limitations of the storytellers.

                As for coincidental magic, milage may vary, but at least in PBD I consider it to be purely a function of what the observer will accept. I realize there are examples of coincidental magic where the mage just lets the spell loose to figure out how to resolve coincidentally, but 1) I've always seen those as RBD examples, and 2) I don't think that works under many paradigms.

                                        The thing is, serendipitous developments shouldn't be restricted to Entropy; any Sphere ought to be able to arrange for them, as Coincidencal Magick. Granted, this would involve taking a closer look at just what Entropy does and doesn't cover, since “happenstance” isn't uniquely its thing. And once you've clarified exactly what Entropy is about, you might want to rethink whether or not it still makes sense as something that the Syndicate focuses on.

                                        In keeping with the idea that there's overlap between the various Spheres, a lot of what Entropy does is stuff that I wouldn't have a problem with Time or Correspondence/Data doing, too. Heck, even the “cause things to break down” kinds of Effects are the sort of thing that Prime/Primal Utility seems to be able to pull off, in that removing value from something has an effect on it that's not unlike decay.

                                        As I see it, Entropy as a Sphere may not have anything unique to it, with its raison d'etre being more about convenience (i.e., getting this specific combination of Effects for the cost of raising one Sphere instead of raising two or three other Spheres that do a lot of things you aren't interested in as well as the handful of things you do want) than it is about providing access to powers that you can't get any other way. The Syndicate in particular probably has more interest in Data and Time as ways of manipulating the odds and predicting/shaping the future than with the rather esoteric blend that Entropy represents. If I had to rank the top three Spheres for the Syndicate to focus on, they would be Primal Utility, Data, and Time, in that order.

                                        Of course, part of this is that I generally don't view Correspondence (and especially not Data) as being primarily about spatial relationships. To me, Corr/Data is primarily about the formal sciences such as mathematics, with the spatial elements being practical manifestations of geometry. Where it overlaps with Entropy is that another area that Correspondence ought to cover would be system theory: the ability to treat things like ecosystems, weather systems, or networks (both digital and personal) as “things” in their own right. Accessing Primal Ventures should be a Primal Utility/Data conjunctional Effect, not purely (or even primarily) Primal Utility: Data to treat an organization as a thing, and Primal Utility to extract Value from it.

                                        Another area where I see considerable overlap between Data and Entropy is with Chaos Theory and Statistics: complex systems with many moving parts where a minor change to initial conditions can snowball into vastly different outcomes. Data shouldn't be as good at such such things as Entropy is; it should require more dots and/or possibly a conjunctional use of Time. But it should be doable.

They shouldn't need Entropy, Data, or Time to use those tools, in the exact way an Akashic shouldn't need Prime, or a Taftani shouldn't need Spirit, to simply throw a fireball provided they have Forces 2 or 3 to begin with. Do those spheres correlate to the practice, would they be immeasurably helpful to a mage with a corresponding paradigm, or is a mage with that paradigm highly likely to raise their dots in those spheres? absolutely, in all cases.                                                 No, indeed the same effect should be the same spheres in all paradigms. The key, then, is if it is the same effect. Since you mention fireball spells, here's an example I removed from a previous post:

                                                The standard fireball (without a preexisting source) is Forces 3 Prime 2 and it doesn't stop being that just because of variations of paradigm or theme. A psychic who uses pyrokinesis to hurl fire from his hands isn't doing anything different from a wizard who shoots it from his wand. Mental powers may be the paradigm but no Mind effects are taking place. This remains true even if the paradigm changes the motif or the character of the spell. A shaman who channels the power of a spirit into a single fireball could also use the same spheres and would not have to add Spirit to the mix. She could probably cast an extremely similar effect with Spirit, of course, but if she chooses the Forces, Prime route the spell would not additionally require Spirit, even if the fireball has eyes and a glimmer of personality. Same for a mage who has an angel appear, throw a fireball, and disappear just as quickly.

                                                On the other hand a Mage who initiates a fireball by having a huge dragon strafe by needs more than Forces and Prime. It is a different effect. The dragon is far more than an incidental element of the fireball itself and bringing it into the scene requires something extra. That remains true even if the Dragon is conveniently already around the corner.

                                                That's how I approach bringing the truck in. It is too big an effect on the scene to count as incidental for a small item teleport so it needs to be accounted for. Making it (and the corresponding rerouting of transportation networks) part of the foci is one way of doing that. Barring that the spell needs to include a way of bringing in the truck (and presumably driver).

                                                    That said, that “something extra” shouldn't require you to determine ahead of time whether or not there's a truck nearby to decide whether you're simply locating something that might possibly already be there or you're creating something out of nothing. That is, you shouldn't have to get the Storyteller's approval ahead of time to summon a delivery truck if there's a reasonable chance that there's already a delivery truck: you should be able to just assume that there is one nearby, unless and until the Storyteller overrules you.

                                                    And yes, some of the terminology I'm using there is fuzzy. That's to be expected in a game where units of time are measured in scenes and game sessions. Reality Zones don't have robustly established borders beyond “they're the stage upon which the current scene plays out”. And so on. Leave the seconds, minutes, hours, yards, miles, and percent chances to games like GURPS; Mage operates on a much more freeform basis.

                                                    But ultimately, there's a big difference between “use a few dots of Correspondence to contact a local delivery truck” and “use Prime 2 plus mastery of Matter, Life, Mind, and possibly other Spheres to create a delivery truck and its driver out of thin air”. It's worth noting that Syndics wouldn't even attempt the latter, and not just because of the extreme Sphere requirements.

                                                    And that, in turn, brings us back to the question of whether those Sphere requirements should be so extreme. As Theodrim has pointed out, and which I also indicated above, Mage doesn't operate on a simulationist model; it works more on a narrative basis. If a character shows up who is only going to be incidental to the scene — what cinema calls an “extra”, you shouldn't have to go with mastery of multiple Spheres to summon him just because he might turn out to be more important than that. And the mage who summons a delivery truck as a piece of mobile scenery with the intent of stealing it and thus making it far more important than he purportedly intended it to be is acting in bad faith — something that is a “gaming contract” issue rather than a “game systems” issue. Mages shouldn't be unduly curtailed in what their magical can do just because a jerk might choose to abuse the group's trust.
