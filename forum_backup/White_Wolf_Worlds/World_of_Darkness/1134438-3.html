        First off, I think you might be a bit off on the representation of a twisted cosmos, which can't really be defeated just by defeating a few, or even a myriad of, jotun. This is actually one of the big things about Werewolf as a whole - tackling strategic problems rather than perpetually concentrating on tactical ones, thus making a greater difference in the war at the cost of personal recognition within the culture. You'll be fighting less often, but accomplishing more; trying to get into the warrior's glorious afterlife is a goal that you'll have to set aside if you want to actually make a difference.

        Secondly, the points where the Get of Fenris diverge seem like they would be pretty big. Wotan in Werewolf is an evil necromancer, and possibly also a vampire. The Aesir and Vanir are disdained as enemies, of whom only a few gain recognition as powerful or notable in a fight, which is where the respect of the Fenrir ends. Also, they might be ghouls. Fenris will eventually slay Wotan, and this is presented as a good thing. Also, the cause of eclipses? It doesn't seem to have anything to do with Fenris, who is also not a child of Loki (the Revised Tribebook made a salty statement of humans confusing "image conjured by Loki" with "created/born by Loki"). Also, if we're going by the antagonist nature of the Aesir and Vanir here, they seem to be on the side of the thursar, or at least indifferent to the evil they wreak in the world. Also, the Fenrir killed a bunch of Asatru when they purged the Swords of Heimdall from their ranks.

        Werewolf doesn't really have a setting that exists to validate human beliefs. That;s where it gets most of its placement in the horror genre; everything you knew as a human prior to your change is at least sorely mistaken in some fashion, and likely to be flipped on its head. The wolf is the hero; the flock has eaten the mountain and forests and must be stopped. The dragon is keeping the living history of the world; those who come to slay her deserve the death by fire that they get when they intrude on her wallow. The serpent made an error in trusting someone, once, and now must secret herself away from the world of humans and strike at its most outstanding evils. If the tiger disregards its nature and tries to be human, it will bring ruin down on all tigers. And, finally, you root for the shark, because you know that Peter Benchley regretted what happened after the novel hit.
