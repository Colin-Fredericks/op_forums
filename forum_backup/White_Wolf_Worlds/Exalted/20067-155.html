                                Merchant Prince sounds like a heroic actor -- one who is out and going and doing. And that's exactly what it is.

                                Factors are the investors. Their role is less glamorous but due to the nature of the Guild as a financial institution, both more fundamental and more powerful.

                                You're right that the Merchant Prince sounds like the guy who should be in charge, but just swapping the terms wouldn't work, because factors aren't out and going and doing the way the term "Merchant Prince" sounds like it should be. Likewise, "Factor" doesn't much evoke "Active head of caravans and other high-reward trading activities." So to "fix" this "problem," you'd actually need to redesign the entire organization of the guild so that instead of it being run by the moneymen, it'd have to be run by the head actors. And that, uh, would certainly be a thing you could do; it'd even sort of be in-genre. But I like that the Guild is sort of deliberately grounded in its structure that way -- the real people in charge are the ones growing fat and happy off the work of others, rather than the ones doing the work. Because that does seem to be how actual capitalism works.

                                                    Such a large trading system required a financial ecosystem to fund and insure each voyage. Perhaps the wealthiest citizens of Rome and Athens could have launched voyages themselves. But the wealthy derided merchants and business ventures; like the upper-class characters in Downton Abbey, they saw such work as beneath their station.

                                                    Instead wealthy Romans and Athenians preferred to prosper from trade indirectly by fronting and insuring traders, who tended to be foreign and less wealthy. In any case, the fact that lenders provided lots of bottomry loans shows they understood that it was less risky and more profitable to act like financial intermediaries than business operators.

                                                    ...

                                                    It’s tempting to view early traders as bold men who crossed treacherous seas to fill their holds in faraway lands. But captains rarely owned their own ships, and even merchants and captains who had the money preferred to finance their vessel, the voyage, and the insurance on each from maritime lenders who spread the risk. The picture historians have sketched out of ship captains is less Han Solo and more middle management.
