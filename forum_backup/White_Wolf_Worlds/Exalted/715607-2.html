    Battle groups are the new mass combat system. We roll a bunch of fighters up into a glom; they only do Withering damage, but it rolls over into health track damage if you Crash (yikes!). Meanwhile, any Withering damage they take goes straight to their health track, but, (1) it’s a big track, and (2) I hope they run out before you do. Battle groups can also effectively grapple you – at which point they’re making Withering attacks at Defense 0 that roll over into your death. That’s… ouch.

    (Here’s a guy running through a burning hunting lodge, or something, carrying a kid. It’s not bad, but why is it here?)

    Battle groups get nastier when they’re being commanded by a leader – the leader’s extra successes on a command roll translate into extra dice for the unit. (If I kill a legion’s commander, can it immediately pop out another dude as the new commander? Does “defending yourself” count as an action for which the unit gets extra dice – i.e., does its Defense go up?) It appears that splitting one battle group into several chunks will produce something that can murder the original – more attacks, more net health levels – and so, naturally, we get a sidebar sternly scolding us not to do that. Er, then shouldn’t combining two groups produce a net gain of capability, rather than a net loss?

    We also get a section on generals trying to out-general each other, which basically comes down to, “Make opposed Int+War rolls, high roller chooses one of these bonuses.”

    Okay, social influence time. This also means we start getting definitions for some of those earlier terms – your Resolve is resistance to persuasion, and your Guile is your Ability to keep your feelings to yourself. Your Intimacies can boost your Resolve – the stronger the Intimacy, the more it boosts (or penalizes) your Resolve. The numbers for boosting and penalizing aren’t equal, though, which is a little unfortunate.

    So, what can you do socially?
    • You can instill Intimacies, adding or removing them from someone else – but if you want to buff an existing Intimacy, you have to have another Intimacy to support the effort. I like that – it encourages you to build supporting networks of belief.
    • You can persuade them to take an action – but only if you’ve built or discovered appropriate Intimacies first! A Minor Intimacy will get someone to inconvenience themselves; they’ll give up their job for a Major; they’ll face almost certain death for a Defining. Again, this is a really nice context-sensitive system.
    • You can bargain or threaten them into taking some action, offering valuables or the threat of harm in lieu of an Intimacy.
    • You can inspire them to be filled with an emotion, though you can’t control what they do with that emotion.
    • You can read intentions to suss out someone’s motivations or Intimacies.
    I really like that, in general – it feels like a broadly useful spectrum of actions that learns a lot from 2e’s missteps. It’s also complex enough that it’s probably hiding some missteps of its own, but it looks good.

    Appearance still grants bonuses to social rolls, though it’s scaled way back – you only gain it on offense, and based off the target’s Resolve rather than their Appearance. That’s nice, but you can also use Appearance as the base of a social roll, now – is there a concern here of double-dipping, or is it just expected that most rolls won’t be able to use Appearance?

    You can still spend Willpower to shake off most social influence, which is good. Attempts to persuade you have a totally separate system – and honestly, this is the part I like least; it feels like a lot of complexity in a very small interaction. To shake off persuasion, you need a second Intimacy – not the one you used to defend, and not the one they used to attack. There’s some weird interactions here; you might want to keep one of your best Intimacies back for use in “shaking off,” because the one you use for that has to be as strong as the one they’re attacking with.

    We also get rules for re-persuading someone – broadly, it’s harder to convince them after they’ve been convinced once. This was a big hole in 2e, but I’m not sure it’s better now; as others have noted, there’s some incentive here to persuade everyone in the party to do the-thing-they-were-going-to-do-anyway, because that makes them much more resistant to later persuasion. Which is a bit silly, but it works!

    Is that a Firefly shout-out? It looks like a Firefly shout-out. *approves*

    There follow the Million Specific Systems, which I’m not going to try to summarize in detail here. We get some nice rules for Investigation, which clarify what the Storyteller should be telling a successful investigator – this is useful advice. We also get a pretty terrible picture of Volfer at a bar. What’s happening here, anyway? Fata-Kun is clearly sitting; where are Volfer’s legs? What’s going on with the way the wall bends behind them? Who thought it was a good idea to draw a head with the topknot as the only visible feature? … Is that Peleps Deled with a moustache? Why is Fata-Kun drawn with fat black lines when Volfer and the rest aren’t? What's wrong with your faaaaace?

    Leadership! All right! Leadership rules from the guys who gave us the CRM system, who finally solved the “Bureaucracy doesn’t do anything” problem. Let’s play god-kings, with some actual rules for the “king” part of that. Let’s do this. The leadership rules are…

    … uh…

    Okay, the Leadership system in its entirety is: Does the player have the stuff he needs to do a task? Then he can do it! But maybe something bad will happen, like a war or some assassins or something. And… that’s it. That’s how we’re supporting playing god-kings: “Make something up.”

    …

    Did I mention these rules take four pages?

    Anyway. There’s a sidebar on social penalties, which feels about ten pages out of place, and some middling art, and then we switch topics. Feats of Strength now have prerequisite Strength as well as minimum successes – wonder what’s the motivation there? We get environmental penalties ranging from “it’s too hot” (1B/hour) to “Adorjan hates you” (4A/round). Falling damage is… pretty darn fatal; if you fall two range bands, you’re looking at 7+ levels of damage. The rules advise you to let characters stunt to avoid falls – well, yeah, given that they’re otherwise going to explode, I bet so. (How far is a Medium range fall, anyway?)

    Poison! So this should be interesting; 2e poisons were stupidly broken, tending to make every blow a potentially fatal strike. Here… interesting. Most combat-time poisons only do Initiative damage until you Crash, at which point they begin killing you. So what happens if someone poisons my drink with Arrow Frog Venom – that is, if I start suffering from it outside of combat? Poisons still inflict penalties on all die pools, though, which feels pretty crippling – but if you have to land a Decisive to inflict them, maybe that’s not such a big deal. At a glance, Curare looks like the way to go – it’s going to stick them with a -2 penalty that’s likely to last the rest of the fight.

    (But really, why can I shrug off rattlesnake venom but take lethal from arsenic? Some of the choices here don’t make immediate sense to me.)

    (The matching of relevant tables to their rules is not really ideal here; most things are ending up at least a page after the rules discussing them.)

                    This next section is pretty sick – er, I mean, it’s the list of diseases. We get a pretty good list of basic human illnesses to release as sweeping plagues. (Aside: I ran a 2e plague game, which was ultimately just infuriating for my players - “You are helpless when faced with these ravages” is a strong thematic statement, but didn’t fit with anything else the game was pitching to them. We’ll see what it looks like this time.) We also get a cool supernatural illness, the puppeteer plague, which brings your skeleton to unlife inside you and has it claw its way out. That’s terrifying!

                    The resultant undead is called, um, a bonesider. Really? That’s what you’re going with?

                    Leprosy is described as minimally contagious. It’s difficulty 1 to resist contracting, i.e., “on par with removing someone’s appendix safely.” Again, these difficulty scales seem kind of whack.

                    “Treating wounds” follows the rules listed back on p. 174, except that it omits the (+1 for treating yourself) described there.

                    Treating poison requires an hour, apparently. Hope you were poisoned with hemlock or arsenic, because the others are going to be done by that. Wait, except, “on the battlefield,” you can treat poison in one action(?) for +2 difficulty. Okay, but again – what happens if I drink rattlesnake venom?

                    So here’s an interesting thing; Lore now allows you to simply declare facts about the world, as long as you have a relevant specialty and the ST doesn’t reject them – “Oh, this side of the mountain will get all the rain” or “I know an herb we can burn to turn the flame blue,” say. We get basically no guidelines for setting difficulties for the roll to do this, though, other than a statement that the ST should make them up – well, yes. Or more explicitly: the rules say to base the difficulty on “How thrilling is it” if a character knows the thing. What does that mean? Are thrilling things easier to know, or harder? I would have assumed that, y’know, obscurity was the more relevant feature there…

                    I don’t know if I object to this system in general, but it feels like this weird, touchy-feely narrative thing has been bolted onto what is, in general, a very gamist-simulationist system. Almost everything you do has its difficulties set based on what the world is; we don’t make the cliff harder to climb because it would be thrilling if someone fell off. The design logic here may be perfectly sound on its own, but it feels orthogonal to the rest of the game’s direction.

                    Ah, here’s a partial resolution to the complaint: we break Lore into diff 1-4, 5-7, or 8-10 based on… okay, no, I’m still not actually sure where to peg anything, from this description.

                    Let’s change the subject. The next section is… Crafting.

                    Oh dear.

                    So, Crafting has been a controversial topic on the internet for months now. SLS affectionately called it a “cookie clicker” game; I’d apply the same term, but probably with a less positive attitude. In broad terms, crafting comes in four levels of project: Basic (fletch one arrow), Major (make a mundane sword or boat), Superior (build the Titanic, or an artifact), and Legendary (build the Five Metal Shrike.) Each level generates colored experience points – silver for Basic, gold for Major, white for Superior – which have to be cashed in to perform the next level up of project. Also, you only get color-xp if the project does something: earns you money, makes a new friend, furthers an Intimacy, etc. Some levels of crafting also require slots, which determine the number of projects of that level you can work on at the same time. (It appears to be the case that, if I have thirty Major slots, I can finish thirty Major projects in the time it takes someone else to do one, which seems… a little weird? And yes, a mortal with sufficient color-xp can do that.) Superior and Legendary projects only allow a fixed number of rolls to complete; hope you brought an Excellency, dude, because even Artifact 2 requires you to be able to average 9 successes per roll (in addition to stockpiling ~60 gxp). Artifact 5 requires you to average, uh, 21 successes a roll. Also, you’re going to need Craft (Artifacts) for sure, Craft (Some relevant mundane thing), or Craft (Geomancy) if you want a manse. (For those of you keeping score at home, it’s 19 XP to raise one Craft Ability to 5, assuming you favor Craft.) For fancy old-timey projects, you’ll also want Craft (First Age) and sorcery, to use its entirely separate make-stuff system that isn’t detailed here.

                    (There’s a picture here of a guy gesturing and doing… something?... with lightning and a couple of giant blue men. I can’t make heads or tails of this picture – relative distance, scale, and even foreground-background ordering don’t make any sense to me. The top half looks like the two men are standing right in front of Gesturing Guy, except one of their hands is behind the house in the background. But then there’s a tiny little man at their feet, so I guess they’re really far off, actually? I don’t know.)

                    Just to be clear, I strongly dislike basically all of this, but two things in particular: (1) I hate that Major crafts still require prerequisites, i.e., a swordsmith can’t make swords unless he works in more basic projects periodically to fuel up. (Because…?), and (2) it irritates me that all of this complexity is in service to something that isn’t a game. Like, the actual process of crafting affords no chance to be clever, or to make interesting decisions; you can try to optimize your color-xp rewards (“Intimacy: I love making stuff” and a good relationship with a shopkeeper cover two of the three), but the actual making-process is just a sequence of grinding rolls with no choices. You don’t get to weigh choices, or consider alternatives, or ponder the tradeoffs of different materials – you just keep rolling them bones as long as you can afford to pay up.

                    Make it simple, or make it something I can strategize about. Combat does the latter; Investigation, say, does the former. Craft does neither.

                    </rant>

                    There follows a kind of weird interlude on boats. Boats actually give you separate die bonuses for different parts of a ship – like, you might get +1 to sailing from the rowers, +2 from the sails, +1 from the design, etc., if you’re trying to get somewhere in a hurry. Two ships in a fight both roll to collect Momentum (not Initiative? Why the new term?) which is spent to perform various maneuvers against the enemy vessel, like boarding or ramming them. This is not a bad system, but it’s strange that it’s so divorced from the land-based maneuvering system – it seems like these should be two cases of the same system, not two entirely different rules sets.

                    Also peculiar: one possible strategem rolls +Speed, while the others roll +Maneuver. But every single listed ship has a higher Speed than a Maneuver – given that this is a contested roll, is it intended that the guy jockeying for position is always disadvantaged vs. the guy just going for all-out speed? It would be nice to have that explicit, if so.

                    And with that – and an entirely blank page – we’re done.

                    The Good: Combat and social influence both look much improved from their 2e versions; you can tell that the folks who wrote this had played 2e and had felt some of the failure-states of that system. Most of the miscellaneous rules – environments, disease, etc. – look pretty solid, as well. Battle groups look much easier to handle than War Pants ever were.
                    The Bad: The utter lack of a large-scale management system. The odd multiplication of systems in a couple of places – naval vs. land warfare being the obvious one, but there are a lot of edge-case systems multiplying here (“how to hold a knife to someone’s neck,” for instance). Individually, these may be fine, but this chapter is ~60 very dense pages.
                    The Ugly: Craft looks like a frustrating mess. Having played with it now, it also plays like a frustrating mess.

                    Up next: “Chapter 6: Charms.” Heeeeeeere we go.

                        Volferfic. Actually really hilarious Volfer fic, in which he uses a strategically-labeled loincloth (“Contents personally inspected by the Scarlet Empress”) to bait a bar full of Realm soldiers into a fight. It’s a pretty great picture, as well. Alas, same mortal-interaction problems as the previous bits: one mortal gets a half-sentence out, and then he dies. The only other speaking parts are Volfer and a Dragon-Blooded lady.

                        We get a little side call-out that no one would ever try to label Charms as if they were discrete powers. Sure, because who ever heard of that happening in wuxia? It’d just be ridiculous!

                        “Magic, techniques, powers – Solar Charms are all of these, and none of them.” Well, I believe the first half of that. This entire section – which goes on for most of a page, insisting repeatedly that, no, this isn’t magic, we’re just saying that it is – is a little bit ridiculous; if it’s simplest for us-the-readers to think of this as doing magic, why isn’t it simplest for the folks in the world to think of it that way, as well? (And if there’s some easier way for them to think about it, why don’t we talk about it that way, too?)

                        None of this seems to matter, except that it’s going to throw fuel on a thousand internet fights, but it seriously stretches on for more than a page.

                        Next heading: “Charm Style” and OH MY GOSH IT’S STILL GOING. “The Charms allow the player to describe and depict a Solar with greater and greater levels of power and skill, but it is not the Charms which allow the Solar to have such power and skill.” Just… just stop, introduction. I want to read about my awesome Solar magic, not be berated for thinking about it as awesome Solar magic.

                        (Oh, but Martial Arts Charms are distinct techniques, and everyone thinks of them that way. Naturally.)

                        Hm. All Charms have to be activated pre-roll – including soak Charms, per Holden. Er, but in what order do we declare? Simultaneously? Attack-first? Do we counter-bid, where each of us declares until we both pass?

                        Sorcery counts against the dice cap now – interesting.

                        Hm. Okay, we have nothing like a steps-of-resolution process here, nor do we have the old Charm-conflict-resolution table. So how’s that going to be handled? There’s a general, “Well, this happens, unless the Charm says otherwise” – guess we’ll see what goes down.

                        Hm, so you can’t spend Initiative below 0 to pay for Charms. That’s a good rule – but is it a general one? Does it apply to, say, Disengage?

                        Per this description, Supplemental Charms enhance a rolled action, but only one of the matching Ability. Reflexive Charms create actions or enhance unrolled actions. Cool, that’s a clean break between types.

                        Bridge Charms have alternate prereqs. Are they an Integrity-only thing? This description keeps mentioning Integrity as a weird exception to the normal rules. The idea of alternate prereqs makes sense, though – hearkens back to some of the Dawn Solution stuff. Mute Charms don’t add to anima – that’s a nice touch. Psyche looks like the new UMI keyword. Combo-OK is gone, and we here say thankya.

                        Excellencies have kind of a weird break point – they’re both Supplemental (when used to enhance die rolls) and Reflexive (when used to enhance static values). That’s awkward, though it does reflect the division above. (Is the Reflexive/Supplemental break still meaningful? It used to matter to combos, but that’s all gone. What’s lost by making everything Reflexive, specifying that Charms can only enhance their own Ability? We already have a category of “Supplemental, but breaking the normal restriction.”) On the plus side, free Excellencies in anything Caste, Favored, or with at least one Charm!

                        Okay, so here’s how I’m going to do this: I’ll break Abilities down one-at-a-time, but heck if I’m going to try to mention every single Charm. I’ll call out stuff that stands out as I go, though.

                        Archery

                        Number of Charms: 32
                        By Ability requirement: 0/1/3/4/24
                        By Essence requirement: 7/7/9/4/3/2

                        Right off the bat, a concern: Sight Without Eyes is Reflexive, 1m, removes penalties. It also says “She may make an Archery attack” – if it wasn’t wildly out of line with the cost, I’d parse that as this creating an attack, since that’s one of the two major jobs of a Reflexive. Surely “If she makes an attack…” or similar would be better. (At E5, the Solar can see through cover, which does… what?)

                        Several of these powers (i.e., There Is No Wind) are Reflexive and yet enhance rolled actions (i.e., shooting people). Doesn’t that break the fundamental definition from a few pages back?

                        Yeesh, Accuracy Without Distance is still a perfect attack. Ouch.

                        Heh, Immaculate Golden Bow can grow Evocations now. Nice.

                        Rain of Feathered Death doesn’t appear to work as in its example; each attack does “Current Initiative – last successes” damage. So, I have 10i; I roll 10i for 2 successes on the first hit. On the second hit, I only roll 8i, for two more successes. But there’s no indication that my “current Initiative” drops – so on the third, I roll 8i again. Except, no, that’s not what the example shows at all. Surely this should be “current Initiative – sum of successes on previous hits” or similar.

                        Searing Sunfire Interdiction says you can act first on the next round. And if two Charms both declare this…?

                        Good gosh, at E6, Searing Sunfire Interdiction allows you to just indefinitely cancel the turns of anyone you can hit – everyone you can hit.

                        Hm. Heavens Crash Down says it can be used to Clash – that is, it grants an extra Clash attack? Again, there’s ambiguity as to what is enhancing an attack, and what is providing an attack – this and Sight Without Eyes are both Reflexive, so that’s no help. The repurchase says it “removes the Perilous keyword, allowing the Solar to spend herself into negatives” – but that’s not a feature of the Perilous Keyword; that’s a default property of all Charms. Seems like Perilous serves no function here.

                        Kudos to Holden for reorganizing the Charms by Essence rating – it’s a much better intro. The themes of Archery seem to be making a lot of big, Decisive attacks, ignoring the need to Aim, and punishing anyone who gets close/tries to stop you from leaving/has the temerity to crash near you. There’s also incentive for you to stay close to take advantage of several of those. I like it!

                        There are a lot of multiattack Charms here – upwards of five, depending how you want to count – though most of them require you to make a Decisive.

                        Yes: Revolving Bow Discipline – keep shooting the guy until either you miss or he crashes. (Then zap him for Crashing, with the right other Charms.)
                        No: Probably Sight Without Eyes, which lets you shoot in the dark – again, I almost never see STs use situational penalties, which means this almost never does anything.
                        Just Plain Weird: Relative to previous editions? Probably Heart-Eating Incineration, which costs levels of anima to fire.

                            Athletics

                            Number of Charms: 30
                            By Ability requirement: 1/2/7/4/16
                            By Essence requirement: 11/7/7/3/2

                            Nonstandard dice tricks: reroll 5s and 6s until they disappear, 1i for every 10 rolled, reroll all non-successes, Excellency cost permanently reduced.

                            Monkey Leap is a little less useful than it used to be – it lets you cover the same ground as before, but with a jump rather than a normal move. Er… actually, how far can you jump, normally? How hard is it to do this feat without a Charm?

                            Soaring Crane Leap lets you survive a fall without dying. Which- hm. Which is actually really useful, because falls are lethal, except that the rules on falls said to give players every opportunity to not fall. One wonders whether falls are just too lethal – whether no one will realistically use them, and so Charms to mitigate them are unneeded. Why force this Charm to be repeatedly reactivated? Is it overpowered for it to let you survive falls in one usage?

                            We get a sidebar on Aiming, which feels about five pages late. It’s also a lot of caveats for which Charms don’t need to have an Aim action – this feels like prime territory for a keyword.

                            Foe-Vaulting Method is a once-a-scene Charm requiring a successful roll; if it works, it gives your enemy -2 Defense vs. one attack. That’s not nothing, but it’s pricey. But… okay, hang on. This is a Reflexive Charm, so it creates an action. So I pop it, succeed, and make a Decisive attack, resetting to base Initiative. (1) Do I still get a normal attack this turn, or was that it? (2) If I can find some way to reset to a “base” of 6+ Initiative, can I immediately pop it again in the same round? (The Dawn Anima will do that at E6, but that seems like a high cost.)

                            Lightning Speed is the first in what I suspect will be a long list of Charms that do nothing but fiddle with dice; this one adds basically +.1275 successes/die.

                            Increasing Strength Exercise gives +(Essence) damage to all Decisive attacks, which gets mean fast with, say, Archery’s mega-Decisives flurries. At E3, that’s plausibly +18i at no added cost every turn.

                            Ten-Ox Meditation does not feel like a great Charm. Okay, the process is this: you make a (Strength + Athletics) roll that you normally couldn’t succeed at. For each three successes you get beyond what you actually need, you can momentarily boost your Strength by one; if you boost it far enough, you succeed. That feels pretty edge-case-y; if you’re low strength, you’re already going to have an awful hard time to rolling three over what you need. The justifying example already feels pretty implausible – that’s eighteen successes on twenty dice; even with a reroll, that’s not likely, and since you’re activating the Charm before you find out whether you’re going to roll well...

                            Thunderbolt Attack Prana doubles all rolled damage on an attack, which sounds like the start of a pretty nasty fight-opening combo. It’s also nice for swatting guys out of the sky – fun.

                            Spider-Foot Style has a duration measured in multiple turns, which is a little sad – I’d hoped we were done with that.

                            Unbound Eagle Approach adds “glide more” to Soaring Crane Leap. Just not feeling this one, guys – 16 XP to float sideways-ish?

                            Leaping Tiger Attack is great for closing the distance with a kiting archer – it’s basically an extra band of movement. If you’re not already high Init enough to use it, though, you’re stuck – if you can’t catch the archer, you can’t build up, either.

                            Bleh. Athletics-only motes – thanks, game, but I’m quite content with the resources I already have to track.

                            Thunder’s Might presumably has to be activated in advance, like any other Charm, but I can see being confused by that – it’s a reroll effect, and that’s not the natural inclination.

                            So, Nine Aeon’s Thew lets you meet any Strength prerequisite, which makes Ten-Ox Meditation completely redundant. Better yet, it’s not even an upgrade – it’s in a totally different branch of the Athletics tree.

                            Themes of Athletics seem to “go anywhere” (treetops, water, the walls, the sky), “lift anything,” and “close with anyone.” A lot of very solid-looking Charms, and then a handful that just look like padding. The Rush game is strong here, though – closing from four range bands or more is entirely possible.

                            Yes: Thunderclap Rush Attack. Cool, powerful, multiple uses.
                            No: Ten-Ox Meditation. Redundant, unreliable, underpowered.
                            Just Plain Weird: The speed with which Athletics effects want to turn themselves back off again. I’d guess it’s to prevent sniping from mid-river and/or the ceiling, but it’s well below the rate of mote replacement if you really are untouchable.

                                    Awareness

                                    Number of Charms: 22
                                    By Ability requirement: 0/1/7/5/9
                                    By Essence requirement: 11/5/4/2/0

                                    Nonstandard dice tricks: Reroll 6s until they disappear; 1m for each 9 and 2m for each 10; revert (Essence) of the enemy’s double-successes to single successes; free full Excellency; reroll 10s until they disappear; 10s add to a pool of bonus successes for future use; reroll Essence nonsuccesses, and reroll another die for each success that turns up; enemy’s 1s become your 10s and 2s become your 9s; reroll 1s until they disappear.

                                    This is the first Ability with no Essence 5 Charms, which seems odd given how front-and-center Supernal is. Given fully half the Charms are E1, actually, this (at first glance) seems like one of the worst choices for a Supernal. The number of Ability 5 Charms is still persistently high, though – it really seems like you’re going to want to go “all in” or “not at all” in an Ability.

                                    Surprise Anticipation Method can retroactively make Charms cheaper, which… no, please and thank you. Its second effect is to allow the subconscious use of Charms, which feels a bit like an air-breathing mermaid – has anything previously said you can’t activate Charms while asleep?

                                    The Keen X and Y Charms are kind of odd. You can really feel the attempt to make the non-sight senses pay off – “you can smell things you have Intimacies for” and “you can taste the feelings that went into a meal” aren’t bad, but they’re a little bit niche; “I can smell how many people are in the room” is getting a little silly, though.

                                    Studied Ear Espial is 1m for +3 dice on an attempt to spot stealthy characters while moving. So, er, 8XP for a 2m discount on your Excellency in a very specific circumstance?

                                    Sidebar: “Space-Saving Concession, Developer 5, Essence 3” – there’s an implication there somewhere, I feel like – paraphrasing, “Every Awareness Charm with X and Y, except these two, can be extended to a duration of one turn.” This… really feels like it should be a keyword.

                                    Awakening Eye generates a bunch of extra successes that go into a pool for later use. I have really liked the first two trees; I am really hating Awareness so far. Every other Charm seems to introduce some new resource that needs to be tracked: a library of smells, a pool of extra successes, a batch of Awareness-only motes, a sidebar with effects not mentioned in the actual Charms…

                                    I mean, look at just the unique mechanical complexity! That’s just how the die rolls get twisted, before we get into the new extra resources.

                                    Living Pulse Perception is one success when someone is hiding in an enclosed room with you – again, two motes off on your Excellency in an extremely narrow circumstance. This is just kind of thin soup – there’s almost nothing in here where you’d go “WOW,” despite this being the smallest tree so far.

                                    Is it just me, or does Unsurpassed Sight Discipline have no actual rules text until you get to the Special Activation Rules?

                                    Blink again does not specify whether it needs to be activated in advance of the roll.

                                    “The Exalt can visualize an object she is taking, in sections no larger than a horse’s head.” You need a Charm for that? In a splat that can reflexively generate light for 1m?

                                    In contrast with the above, Dedicated Unerring Ear is really cool: you can hear your Intimacy-people speak your name and talk to you, no matter where they are. That’s very cool – give me more Awareness stuff like that! And Eye of the Unconquered Sun is still crazy-good – instant disintegration of almost every concealing effect, at the cost of being a giant glowy “IIIII SEEEEEE YOOOOOOOU” figure.

                                    Themes of Awareness are… man, I don’t even know. Noticing stuff good, I guess, and then a weird hodge-podge of knowing things about people and not ever getting snuck up on. Dip Sensory Acuity Prana and Keen Sight Technique and get out.

                                    Yes: Dedicated Unerring Ear is a broad new capability that would otherwise be entirely impossible. Awesome.
                                    No: Any Charm that gives a handful of still-counts-as-a-Charm bonus dice, but at a verrrrrry slight discount, in This One Exact Circumstance That May Never Come Up.
                                    Just Plain Weird: Any Charm dealing with taste and smell; maybe taste and smell just shouldn’t be dedicated trees, I dunno. The absence of any Essence 5 effects.
