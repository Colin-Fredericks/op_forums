                                    I agree with many of the things others already wrote.

                                    I can understand "race as a shorthand", "race as a trope codifier", allowing everyone to be on the same page regarding what to expect from a character and their player.

                                    I'm still a little... unsure. Let me try to explain.

                                    The concept of race in fantasy was mainly born with Tolkien, and since he's so goddamn universal (you have to read it! It's incredible! Such mastery, such excellence, real literature etc) of course race has spread to all kinds of fantasy and even to science fiction and other creative writing/art/media/games... and so on.

                                    The problem of that concept is that Tolkien conflated race, culture, and ability. Hobbits are small and dexterous and have strong ties with the earth, and are thus naturally stealthy, and never study magic. Men are a race (I understand his need to avoid the term "human", but I don't completely agree. Menschen would work, in German, but Modern English doesn't work like that). Elves are a race. Dwarves are a race. Orcs are a race. Some, like Aragorn, or the Uruk-Hai, have mixed ancestry. Unlike in real life, where race is a social construct, in Middle Earth race is an objective fact, an aspect of nature. Moreover, it dictates your culture, and your aptitudes. One could make the case that yes, they are races, since they can theoretically (and sometimes very practically) have offspring with one another. The reality is that Tolkien wrote in a very nasty historical period rife with misconceptions and terrible pseudoscience about race. And was inevitably (?) influenced by them. He wanted the elves and dwarves of Germanic tradition to interact with humans and his own hobbits, and the way he found to describe the differences between these creatures was to categorize them as different races. I'm quite sure he didn't really understood his mistake. In any case, the concept of race found in Tolkien is awfully flawed.

                                    But we worship him, so we create games meant to evoke his work. First of all Dungeons and Dragons. And since D&D has enjoyed such an unprecedented success in fantasy and rpgs, the term race is still being used everywhere. D&D itself, now in its 5th edition and with its popularity at stellar levels, still employs that term - race. Again, not to refer to its RL, social construct meaning, but to use it exactly as Tolkien did back in the day - an unscientific, cliché-ridden mischung of race (?), culture, and ability. Each "race" has certain physical features, and certain cultural traits, and certain aptitudes, quantified as modifiers to the base attributes and small laundry lists of special abilities. It doesn't matter if they derive from physiology, culture, education, habits - it's all the same. Race, as shorthand for... well, the essence of the character.

                                    I do understand it can be useful in a game, but it's still a contentious (and just plain wrong) definition. It breeds stereotypes. It teaches/reinforces gamers of all ages that different races have different abilities, and even different moralities. Orcs are evil. Of course orcs are also emphatically not white, savage, brutal, violent, and conventionally unattractive. While elves are most often white, conventionally attractive, cultured, magical, superior, good. But not the drow, they're evil. In fact they're black. But still conventionally attractive, unlike, it seems, real Black people - drow are black-skinned but with thin and delicate faces and bodies, slightly muscular but not musclebound, never even slightly chubby... I could go on but this is turning bitter, so let's not.

                                    Giving a fixed alignment to a fantasy race presents a world in which an entire culture/race/species can be monolithically good or evil, inherently. As what we play, watch, read, has a profound effect on our assumptions, that... is not a good thing. Not to add that imperialistic Europeans colonizing other continents often strived to portray the natives as not just other, not even just subhuman, but inherently evil (as great writer and racist Rudyard Kipling so famously wrote in "The White Man's Burden": half-devil and half-child).

                                    This creates worlds structured along racial lines... not unlike Earth, and I don't need to specify how well that turned out for us. It's probably not intentional (although I know of many racist or sexist writers and designers, so... ?), but to constantly depict dark-skinned races as inherently evil only reinforces a narrative of white supremacy.

                                    But of course there's Drizzt do'Urden. Who, despite being a dark elf, has a conscience, and a sense of honor, and treats his friends well. Completely contrary to all expectations. Because by now you expect all drow to be rotten pieces of shit. He's an interesting character for a certain value of "interesting" - rogueish, nonconformist, sensitive, intelligent, eloquent, misunderstood, and above all, 1) a total badass, and 2) unerringly good. Drizzt is sometimes subjected to racism. But being racist against an Always Evil race of demon-worshipping slavers and murderers isn't exactly prejudice - even spells can reveal their evil, because it's objective reality. The one time you try a detect alignment on Drizzt (if you don't immediately charge him, because yo, dark elf), you'll think there's something wrong with the magic in this neck of the wood, or he's wearing an amulet hindering your efforts, or any of another dozen justified reactions. But no. Drizzt is a credit to his race - he rises above the many inherent flaws of the drow. How charming.

                                    It's not that Bob Salvatore didn't notice. He does, and tries to find ways to undermine the racist narrative of the D&D worlds. He's just struggling with difficult odds. Some People of Color identify with a protagonist such as Drizzt and find that he helps them deal with the prejudice and abuse they suffer in meatspace. Others resent that drow in general and Drizzt in particular are always subjected to racism (no matter if justified by the warped narrative of these worlds), because it simply hits too close to home. Me, I think if elves naturally came in all skin colors and only some factions - political, criminal, religious or any other kind - engaged in demon-worshipping, enslavement or other traditionally villainous activities, not because of their skin color but because of their individual backgrounds, needs and propensities, then the worlds in question wouldn't be so jarring - they would be more similar to ours, in that even though people can have the stupidest preconceptions against a race, a gender, a sexuality, an age bracket, and so on, they are entirely unfounded. Things are never as simple as that race is evil (thank the gods).

                                    So... non-human races. What I'm advocating is: sure, why just humans? Creation is a world fashioned by beings we can scarcely imagine - who knows what wondrous alien life forms they've created? And there are beastfolks, who like standard humans come in a riot of different appearances and cultures - follow that example. There are Dragon Kings, who anatomically and psychologically are alien, but not so far removed from us as to not be understood, and through time have developed different societies in different places - some are sleeping down the ages in stasis chambers, some live with the gods in Yu-Shan. Maybe add more cultures, maybe don't make them all followers of the UCS, just to avoid generalizations, but the concept is good. There are the Air Folk, who were created with wings, but have different cultures and different races just like humans do, only their morphology allows them to fly, so they will by necessity differ from humans - but they'll still be, at heart, humans (or not?), and capable of all our fabled diversity (which is always the pitch when telling you about what makes humans special and different from other races... because they apparently have all been cloned from the same dna and brainwashed into the same homogenizing culture). Deconstruct the racist structures of traditional fantasy and bumpy-forehead-alien science fiction, embrace the new work being carried onward by new generations of writers and designers. Embrace the diversity.
