    So, Isator, you asked me to go back and look at the difficulty descriptions in the book, and say what I think about them.

    Originally posted by Core Book p.185
    Tasks which runof-
    the-mill individuals in Creation would consider challenging
    (such as picking a lock or removing a patient’s
    appendix without killing him) are ordinary fare for heroes.
    Such tasks are appropriate for difficulty 1.

    Performing challenging tasks under significant duress
    (such as picking a lock or removing an appendix in the
    dead of night, without sufficient light, in the midst of a
    howling storm) is appropriate for difficulty 2.

    Tasks which might daunt even heroes, by contrast, are
    appropriate for difficulty 3. Examples might include
    plucking a gem from a nest of writhing serpents without
    being bitten, or breaking a man-eating horse born in the
    depths of the Wyld so it accepts the hero as its rider.

    Performing such tasks under significant duress (such as
    plucking the aforementioned gem while the temple collapses
    around the intrepid thief, or breaking the maneating
    horse in the midst of a raging forest fire) are appropriate
    for difficulty 4.

    Near-impossible feats, even by heroic standards, are appropriate
    for difficulty 5. Examples might include reading
    a letter in pitch blackness by feeling the texture of ink on
    the paper, leaping over the rail of a sorcerer’s flying chariot
    to land safely in a hay cart hundreds of feet below, or
    running for three consecutive days and nights without
    succumbing to exhaustion.
    Other than differentiating between 1 and 2, and differentiating between 3 and 4, which I didn't go into (as I'm writing posts, not rules), I'd say... yeah, that's basically what I was saying.

    Things a regular person finds challenging, but someone skilled finds ordinary (but not so ordinary as to mean you don't need to roll), is difficulty 1. Picking a lock is a good example. I've done it like once, it's pretty hard and took a while.

    Tasks which are challenging for very skilled individuals are difficulty 3. Both the examples look good.

    Tasks which are very difficult for very skilled individuals are difficulty 5. Again, seems good.
    I'll admit, it's a little more difficult-sounding than my gut feeling would go for; but maybe that's because I'm so used to games where PCs have 10 dice in a ton of stuff, and can excellency up past 10 dice in 90% of tasks, at which point these incredible feats become so humdrum as to become boring. Which feels wrong. But now I'm running a game where most players only have excellencies on less than half their rolls, and sometimes are only rolling 3 or 4 dice, it'll probably feel more impressive for people to succeed on difficulty 5 rolls, so they can and should be that epic.
    (I should say though, in practice, I virtually never assign things difficulty 5 unless the rules specifically call for it, so in reality I'm not really doing it any differently from the book.)

    I'd say the only problem I have with this section is, as I said in my first post, the one about taking out an appendix, which with pre-modern medicine I can't see famous and skilled doctors finding ordinary; the first appendectomy wasn't performed until the 1700s. No-one was doing them in the bronze age or even the Renaissance.

                Originally posted by Chausse View Post
                I guess I was really nitpicky about the granularity of the system in my post, thanks for your patience. I still don't really like the idea of a hard-cap so low for non-confrontational rolls, because it creates strange system behaviors and strange expectations for what a good roll should be (like if in a contested roll A roll 12 successes and B rolls 15 successes, since they are anyway bound by the human limit, shouldn't they be in a tie because they can't achieve more than what is humanly possible ?) but that's my problem.
                I wouldn't necessarily consider it a hard cap exactly, more like a pretty firm cap. You might push past it for certain things in rare circumstances. I just caution allowing too good of a roll to emulate other charms too well. Dragonblooded are my favourite splat in this game, but Solars really need to keep that feeling of just being straightforward, powerful superheroes. A Dragonblood using medicine, for example, can get 11 dice from stats, 6 from an excellency, which if maxed adds a bonus non-charm success, and a lot of their low essence charms let you double 9s, which on average adds 1 extra success for every 10 dice rolled.

                So, with that, a level 2 stunt and a willpower you're looking at about an average roll of around 15, if they're essence 5 and use Most Beneficent Seed of the Five Dragons, it's more like 25. If you're looking to match that to the difficulty scale when 1 was remove an appendix, and 2 is remove an appendix in the dead of night without adequate light in the midst of a howling storm, then 15 is probably looking along the lines of performing an hour's worth of treatment in the span of a couple of seconds, or maybe cure blindness, deafness, or allow for the regrowth of lost tissue. In the game though, those are Essence 2-3 Solar charm effects, things that even Solars can't do at chargen unless medicine is their one special thing. Many of those effects even cost more motes and willpower than the DB spent getting those successes.

                If Dragonblooded are able to do that from their chargen it just takes away a bit from how special Solars are. Which is okay, because DBs are special in their own ways, but we still have to draw the line somewhere, and putting it around where the greatest mortal heroes of Creation top out is a pretty decent place. That way every kind of exalt has the relative same starting baseline, and the developers can focus on making each one unique in their own special ways, with their own unique charms.

                For example the Essence 1 Dragonblooded charm that lets them convert levels of lethal to bashing over the course of six seconds. Solars don't get a charm that converts health levels so quickly until Essence 3, and it costs twice as much, and is only usable once per scene, and can't convert nearly as many health levels at once, the only benefit, which is quite good, is to heal bashing or lethal completely. The reason for this is because Dragonblooded medicine is not really like Solar medicine, in spite of it being way worse overall, it has a lot more elements of magic and manipulation. Solars are the greatest doctors in the universe, but Dragonblooded just dump a bunch of life essence into your veins, which closes all your open wounds in mere moments.

                So what do Solars get that's so cool that Dragonblooded don't? And I keep using Solars and DBs because they're polar opposite ends of the power scale, and I know them both better than Lunars right now. Well Solars can perform surgery without tools, using their incredible skill, they can even cure the blind, the deaf, crippled and even missing limbs. That's something Dragonblooded just can't do, believe it or not, in spite of their 25 success medicine rolls, and it's important to keep that distinction.



                For contested rolls you might even consider it a kind of granularity adder. At that point you're not really determining an upper limit, just who is slightly better. Like two exalts climbing a rock wall, if one gets 15 successes and the other only 5, the winner probably doesn't speed by the loser Roadrunner style, unless he's using Hare Racing Method, but he does solidly win that contest.

                If she IS using Hare Racing Method, or Godspeed Steps, or One Extra Step, or god help you all three combined, then yes, the Solar flashes to the top of the rock wall so quickly that only the supernaturally enhanced vision of other spiritually powerful beings would likely to even see that she moved at all, rather than just teleported.
