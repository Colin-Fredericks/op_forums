    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey View Post

    Well, it's maybe not quite THAT bad, but sometimes looking at the various antagonists makes it feel like there's a disconnect between what the writers of those antagonists expect the players to be able to do, and what they can do.

    There's just a bunch of enemies in the books, and supplements even, that have like, 6-7 dice to their attacks. [snip] They're absolutely zero threat to the fighters, which is okay, but they're almost no threat at all to the people who aren't either. It only takes about 10% of your starting dots to get melee 3, pick up a sword, maybe a specialty and with the free Solar excellency you're just nearly immune to those attacks for less than your per-round mote regen.
    While I do agree with you that the creatures in the book, presented as antagonists, should be a challenge to at least some Solars, I think you're kind of exaggerating the problem here. First, I think the lower dice pools you're talking about for the antagonists are the exception, rather than the rule - going through the animals in the Antagonists chapter, there are 25, of which 17 (68%) have dice pools of 8 or more. If you discount the animals that are obviously intended to be there as color or as stats for a familiar or mount or something (which I'd include the cat, horse, and raiton in), the percentage climbs even higher. And most of the weaker animals can get an immediate boost if you put them in a battlegroup - you only need 2 hellboars on the battlefield to immediately give them +1 to their dice pool, and let them attack twice a round, after all.

    Second, I think you're kind of overestimating the effects of a small dip into combat for the PCs, too. Someone with Dex 3 and Melee 3, with a specialty in defense and a medium weapon, has a parry of 5, which can be boosted by 2 for 4 motes. A parry of 7 is good, for sure, but there's lots of antagonists who can roll better than that. You might need a 14-die pool to have a 50% chance of overcoming it, but the pool system in Exalted is pretty swingy, what with double-10s and such. I don't know the stats offhand, but it wouldn't shock me to learn that something like a 10-die pool had only a little less than 50% chance of beating Parry 7. And penalties to defense are pretty common and generally easy to apply. Overwhelming is the obvious one, of course, but there's plenty of others for taking various actions (both flurrying and taking any miscellaneous action both reduce Defense by 1, for example), and I feel it's reasonable to apply defense penalties for things like fighting on rough terrain or in poor conditions like pouring rain or heavy fog. All of those will bring that non-specialist's Parry back into the range where they're pretty easy to hit even for the lower-pool creatures.


    All that said, I do definitely agree with you that it should be possible to challenge a Circle of Solars with non-Exalted antagonists, and that this is desirable. Battling great beasts, for instance, has a lot of narrative resonance in the sources that Exalted draws on - half of Herakles' labors, for example, involved him dealing with things that were basically big animals, with few obvious supernatural powers beyond size. And the pulp tradition has plenty of such fights being portrayed as impressive feats too - Conan fighting a great ape barehanded, for instance. And 2e really swung the needle far too far in the other direction, making the vast majority of mortal opponents absolutely trivial, and essentially endorsing the "you must be this magical to ride" attitude. Personally, one of the reasons I liked 3e to begin with was the promise that fighting mortal antagonists would be meaningful.

    So, with that in mind, here's some tricks I've found to be useful when designing fights between a Solar circle and non-Exalted/powerful spirit opponents:
    • Don't be afraid to add dice! The QCs in the book represent typical examples, but it's perfectly reasonable, especially with the animals and other QCs where there's only one example, to have tougher creatures. I think adding up to 4 dice to attack, combat movement, and other relevant skills like stealth, is justifiable by assuming this particular specimen is just a bit more skilled, smarter, or just plain meaner. Another 4-6 dice can be justified by outright magic like mutations or other supernatural buffs.
    • Also don't be afraid to spend willpower! Spending a point gives an automatic success on a roll or boosts a static value by 1, both of which can make a big difference.
    • When fighting a whole Circle, use multiple opponents, and by multiple opponents, I mean multiple targets. The action economy means that if there's only a single target on the battlefield, even if that is a battlegroup representing 10 actual opponents, that the Circle will tend to overwhelm it with attacks, and it won't get to do anything interesting before going down. My rule of thumb is that for every combat-focused character in the Circle there should be one serious opponent, and one more opponent for the rest of the Circle, ideally one capable of being a danger to all of them, like a battlegroup or something capable of area-effect attacks. For example, my own group currently has a Dawn Caste who's heavily Melee-focused, a Night with excellent Archery, and an Alchemical who's currently got a charm loadout that makes him a grapple-monster, while the Twilight and Eclipse aren't really combat-heavy at all. Against them, I'd usually throw 4 distinct targets.
    • Use battlegroups heavily. If you've got two characters who are basically identical on the battlefield, they should always be in a battlegroup. It saves you time and energy as an ST, and the bonuses battlegroups get are nice.
    • To combine the above point and the first one, a good combat challenge is often a battlegroup of "typical" creatures from the Antagonists chapter, and one or two bigger, tougher specimens, the "alphas" of the group. A sounder of three average hellboars, for instance, lead by a bigger, meaner old sow with +4 to her attack, can be a serious threat even to someone who's dipped into a combat ability.
    • Also be willing to apply penalties fairly liberally. Lots of Solar charms let you ignore penalties, but that's only helpful if those penalties are imposed in the first place. To avoid people feeling like they've "wasted" charms, the ST has got to be using them in the first place.

    There are a few more things I'd suggest to make mortal antagonists a bit more of a serious threat, but they veer into house rules territory, so I'll discuss them over in the thread for that.
