        Originally posted by Aquillion View Post
        Both the Fair Folk and the Underworld (which was the origin of the Great Contagion) are outside of fate's purview. Creation survived that because of luck or, if you prefer, because that's just the way things happened, like in the real world. But it wasn't preserved by any sort of fate. That was literally the entire reason that "outside of fate" was created as a category. And there's important reasons it was set up that way...
        I desire to fill a cup with water. I cannot move the faucet. I can move the cup. Woe to me for I cannot move the faucet, for it is beyond my reach, unlike the cup, which I can affect. If only I could do something with the cup, that is not beyond my reach, to ensure the faucet is positioned over it to fill it with water. But woe, it is not possible for me, who can move the cup, to ensure the faucet fills it, because I cannot move the faucet. If only there were some way that I, who can leverage motion upon the cup, could ensure the faucet I cannot move would fill it with water. But this is an unsolvable conundrum. Alas and alack!

        Also, ordinary raksha and demons below the Third Circle, and ghosts without comparable strength become entangled in fate when they're within its' boundaries, so unless the Great Contagion was secretly a Third Circle Demon-equivalent spirit, and the Balorian Crusade was composed entirely of Unshaped, they become predictable by the RAW we know.

        If my giving the cup incentive to move towards underneath the faucet denies it protagonism then so too do storytellers by setting up conditions which heroic characters respond to, hopefully in a manner they expect - sure, maybe the PC will join in beating the slave rather than stop the slaver, but I think characters tend to be a little more predictable than that.

                    Before I would address anything said specifically again, I want to briefly bring up a point that I forgot about when it came up earlier: the sense that the idea of things being Outside Fate (at least as regards being an obstacle to the foresight of Sidereals, or the viability of their long term plans) has shown itself a bit to not be the best thing in practice. Partially because of the degree to which it seems to undermine the credibility of the Sidereals in the eyes of many readers, partially because its actual mechanical implementation always seemed kind of spotty (neither previous Edition gave it anything to do with actual prophetic Charms), and partially because predicting some horrible monster from the Wyld or Hell seems like precisely the kind of thing that Sidereals should be doing.

                    When I said that it's okay for Sidereal prophecy to be fallible, that was partially a sense of their being latitude for circumstances or choices to shift things away from what a Sidereal predicted, and partially based on the weird and circuitous ways that those Charms were written. One might combine that with a sense that the Sidereals themselves can be fallible in the form of not always asking the right questions (the return of the Solars catching them off guard can really be as simple as nobody ever asking if that was going to come up in the next few years), and an idea that some of their prophecy has always been a bit of a cheat in the form of them predicting something that they themselves have Fated to happen (with at least some fault in that being something they definitely can't do to demons or behemoths from Zen-Mu).

                    Otherwise I feel as though a straightforward idea of some of the major parts of the setting being simply invisible to an important part of the Sidereals' toolset kind of sells them short.

                    This all arose from a sense that at least one way in which the First Age could deal with a gigantic fae crusade led by a Raksha Incarna was authorities of the Realm being warned by a prophecy from Heaven.

                    Mind, even if they were facing something at full strength, I would assume that many parts of the First Age were vastly better suited to dealing with it than the tail end of the Shogunate. Good Lord, what else are those royal warstriders that you've been churning out daiklaves to get the white points to create even good for, if not working in concert to cancel the apocalypse?

                                            Originally posted by Aquillion View Post
                                            Kinda. I think that the underlying presentation of the Fair Folk invasion and the Great Contagion as the Sidereals' great failure is unavoidable, though. There's more to the Usurpation than the Great Prophecy and the Usurpation itself - there's a core philosophy to the Bronze Faction that IMHO the books ought to go into more detail on.

                                            They believe that the ideal state of the world is essentially the best and the brightest Sidereals ruling the world from behind the scenes, using other Exalted as puppet kings. Whether the Great Prophecy encompassed those exact details isn't really relevant, because the core of Kejak Chejop's philosophy is essentially that only he (and the Sidereals) can be trusted to do what is necessary and that they were therefore justified in seizing control.

                                            In other words, rather than viewing the madness of the Solars, the Great Prophecy and the Usurpation as the big philosophical center of the Bronze Faction, I feel it's better to view them as the precipitating event that catalyzed a larger, more all-encompassing philosophy of Sidereal leadership.
                                            Not to be a response to the argument in this post in full (which way too much being argued than I have time to get into and also I don't really want to distract from the broader and more interesting arguments currently dominating this thread!), but generally just on this point, I would qualify all of the above that by about mid-1e it was established that there are a number of constraints on why the Sidereals might chose to rule in secret, when they would feel that there is no alternative but to take on much of the role in running the world:

                                            1) The breaking of the Mask: Depicted in 1e as an accidental effect of overshooting the preparations for the Usurpation.
                                            2) The canon is that Solars openly ceased to disregard their advice, telling them to "butt out" effectively, and the Lunars of the First Age were in close alliance with the Solars, leaving few options for partnership. The first mover here, against a dialogue, is the Solar Exalted and clearly the Solar Exalted. (To the degree the canon is clear, it's clear that the Sidereals fit within the system as advisors and the Solars disdained the relationship, not that Sidereals were first movers in developing an ideology that they should rule). That's gotta constrain the degree to which the Sidereals had to operate a closed shop.
                                            3) Their integration into the First Age world government and Heaven, where they were expected to be unobtrusive, of the Heavens, and absent from human civilization for the most part, and that seems to be the role that the Five Maidens wanted them to have; virtually akin to the role that the gods held (Yu Shan governed largely for and by the gods, Creation for the mortals), and maintained for the same reasons that division was maintained (albeit that the Sidereals are limnal figures). Indeed if this were not the case, and they left a high density of traces within the world, it would be hard to sustain.

                                            Of course, there are members among them that do believe they should be in charge with no voice for anyone else, for purely ideological reasons, but I think no more frequent than among the other Celestials (and perhaps less so if you compare to the Solars of the First Age zenith).

                                            Not that I guess you couldn't go back to the 1e core and go "But all the above are just set dressing on their true preference!". I think that would be to discard a more interesting articulated set of circumstances that led them to where they are, though.

                                            As a coda, just because I don't think it would be right to wholly ignore the overall thesis of your post, I generally (and no surprise) disagree. But it's interesting that systematic Sidereal dysfunction, and this being because they chose to exclude other contributors is arguable, even if I think it's mostly wrong, and I would not necessarily want to totally squash that.

                                                    I find Exalted: The Sidereals isn't too bad at telling you in general terms, who they are and what they do, why they matter to human history, and the active, choice based actions they take to hold human cultures together. It does not over-emphasize, to my mind, that they just maintain the fabric of reality in a holding pattern or do random tasks they don't understand for ends they don't know.

                                                    The problems we might have as a fan community in talking about this are probably due to;

                                                    a) We never had Caste books for the Sidereals, so actually fleshing out with individual cases and specific examples, is a bit thin on the ground. Adventures and campaigns also weren't written for Sidereal perspectives, so it's lacking a bit there as well. The only real material about what they do is in the two Sidereal books, and MoEP:Sidereals is widely thought to be a fairly poor book.

                                                    b) There was a fashion about in the fan community during 2e to think it was better ("edgier" I guess you could say) to have the Sidereals be indifferent to human culture and human welfare and simply be concerned with maintaining reality and existence as a whole. As well as to amp up the elements of "Sidereals have random missions they don't understand to generate butterflies moving Creation onto the right course" and of course, "Creation has only experienced decline at all scales during the Age of Sorrows" (rather than emphasize the Age of Sorrows as a time of rebuilding and new things as well).

                                                    I could speculate about why this happened, but ultimately probably mostly as everyone was caught up in the trend and there weren't any authoritative, respected, charismatic voices to actually go back, look at what was written, be willing to argue for that and push back on what was popular, sadly. And we just haven't had much publishing on Sidereals since then, so the ideas have sort of stuck around and not been much displaced even if people have been writing posts against these sort of ideas since at least the beginning of 3e.
