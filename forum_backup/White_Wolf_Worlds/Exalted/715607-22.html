    Invulnerable Skin of Bronze is daylong no-commitment bonus soak and Hardness. Score! I can’t tell whether this prevents you from wearing normal armor or not – it’s a natural soak bonus, which says no, but it also says you’re “considered wearing medium armor.” Can you put on armor if you’re already wearing armor? Actually, wait, is there any rule that says you can’t stack multiple sources of armor? P. 194 is silent on this issue. … Anyway, the armor also gives you a handful of basically-free Charms, which is pretty great. As a control spell, the armor adds (+Essence) bonus soak, whether or not you’re in armor, at the cost of having kind of bronze-y looking skin. Woe is me, my permanent tan now also turns aside arrows.
    Vance has said in another thread that yes, it stacks with armor.

    It's SKIN of Bronze, and adds to your natural soak. But if you practice, say, Ebon Shadow Style, which says "May not be practiced in armor", or in Snake Style which can only be practiced in no- or light-armor, then you count as having Medium Armor. So no, your Ebon Shadow Ninja can't stack Bronze Skin on themselves and become a stealthy juggernaut.

                                11-06-2015, 08:55 PM
                                Moving right along: The Celestial Circle of Sorcery!
                                1. Cantata of Empty Voices is an immediate jump from anything we saw at the Terrestrial level. It takes a while to charge up – which is going to make it slow to pull off in combat – but at the end of that process, you’ve created a mile-radius zone full of the dead. Yeesh. If you do want to go for combat applications, it’s no slower to fire than, say, Death of Obsidian Butterflies. Hm – the control effect indicates that you normally lose control of the spell during Crash. Is that a standard rule for shape sorcery? I missed it, if so – you lose the 1wp bonus, but Ctrl-F doesn’t turn up anything else. Ah, the spell’s perilous – okay, that took a second read.
                                2. Demon of the Second Circle: second verse, same as the first, a little bit louder and a little bit AGH HELP IT’S EATING MY HEAD.
                                3. Impenetrable Veil of Night mutes all lights within a mile, as everything is enveloped in inky darkness. This can panic armies, and at a minimum, it makes stealth pretty easy. As a control spell, this is potent, but with major tradeoffs: bonuses to stealth and movement in the dark are countered by matching penalties in the light, and your voice is permanently distorted.
                                4. Incomparable Body Arsenal grants instant access to any weapon you want, as you turn into a weird techno-golem made of blades – I picture something a little like the Shrike from the Hyperion books. Your new weapon-body also doubles as heavy artifact armor; if this is a control spell, you also get Evocations! Getting distorted is bad news, though – you suffer as though from amputation until you recover.
                                5. Ivory Orchid Pavilion grows you an instant mansion. This costs only 1wp – which means you can plausibly cast it at the end of every day at no resource outlay, giving the party portable food and shelter. Very fun!
                                6. MAGMA KRAKEN summons a GIANT OCTOPUS made of SWEET LAVA TENTACLES to DESTROY EVERYTHING. It does not actually permanently force the sorcerer to speak in all caps (instead, it makes fire grow tentacles around you), but heck, why wouldn’t you? If anyone objects: KRAKEN TIME. The kraken can try to attack multiple people at once, but really shouldn’t, since it divides its successes among all of them; on the other hand, if you can assign someone 0 successes, there’s no risk, I guess. On balance, this looks like a much better “wreck stuff” Charm than it is a direct combat effect – the combat pools aren’t bad, but they aren’t great, either. There’s a basic problem with this spell, though: there’s no actual way to pull Initiative out of it, and yet there’s no way to kill it without making a bunch of big Initiative-consuming Gambits. A sorcerer who (quite sensibly) stands back and lets his kraken do the talking is going to find it nigh-immortal (or else subject to the gamemaster completely fudging the scene’s resolution).
                                7. Shadows of the Ancient Past lets you peer into visions of local history, as long as you don’t go too far back (~500 years) or too recent (~1 year). Those… actually seem like really sensible caveats. If you have this as a control spell, you can even save the visions for later replay. Wait, why do I hear “Come and Get Your Love” playing?
                                8. Travel Without Distance is a personal teleporter, out to some dozens of miles. As a control spell, you can take people with you. This is one of my favorite control effects, though I really like Shadow of the Ancient Past’s effect, too – it doesn’t just make the spell slightly better, it fundamentally changes how you’re going to use it.
                                We get a sidebar about summoned demons and sorcerous workings. Okay, game, but what’s a sorcerous working?

                                The illustration for MAGMA KRAKEN is everything you would want a picture of an undying lava-monster to be.

                                And at least, we reach the Solar Circle:
                                1. Benediction of Archgenesis lets you make an enormous region fertile. You can’t stack activations too nearby – I guess to stop That One Guy who wants to Archgenesis the entire south? There are also some interesting hooks about it potentially depleting the soil. This is fun, but I wish there was a system that things like “massive amounts of food, suddenly” could actually hook into. As a control effect, you can cast the patches closer together; you also get that “plants spring up under your feet” thing, so good luck with the secret identity. And – and then we literally switch font sizes mid spell, what the heck.
                                2. Death Ray is… descriptive, I guess, if a little out-of-genre as a name; the description says “your conjoined hands [turn] into a channel for violent energy,” but I suppose calling it “Ki-Ko-Ho” might have caused a few problems. The spell says it adds your Willpower and extra successes to raw damage – okay, but what’s the base for the damage? It looks like your Initiative, but earlier spells (i.e., Flight of the Brilliant Raptor) were explicit about that. Hitting anyone with the spell is likely to knock them out of range of it, unfortunately – but the spell gradually extends its range, until you’re zapping everyone in a line out to six bands away. That’s stupid far; this is a spell that can nuke castles from literally three times as far as they can fire back. As a control spell, you also get motes for killing people with it, just in case you had any concerns about it shutting down early.
                                3. Demon of the Third Circle is as the name suggests; there’s nothing to actually do with this spell, unless the book actually includes a Third Circle demon. This is probably the poster child for “PCs solve a problem in a fashion worse than the actual problem.”
                                4. Rain of Doom melts an entire city. As a control spell, it becomes mobile; also, you’re now bad at any social influence that doesn’t involve threatening to melt someone’s city. (Really.) Welp, that’s a thing.
                                SORCEROUS WORKINGS! Well, it’s nice to know that these actually do exist…

                                Sorcerous workings are maybe the coolest thing so far, and definitely my favorite new subsystem. If you’re a sorcerer, you can try to permanently alter the world as a sort of one-off thing that’s not really worth a spell of its own – turning your hair into snakes, say, or making Howl’s Moving Castle (which is all-but namedropped as the example Working). Doing so requires three steps. First, figure out your Ambition, i.e., roughly how powerful the thing you want to do is (and so how many successes you’ll need on an extended roll). At the low end, an Ambition 1 Terrestrial Working can do some major earthmoving; at the upper end, an Ambition 3 Solar Working is, well, the Salinan Working: universe-wide permanent reality edits.

                                Second, figure out how much Finesse you want – that is, how much fine control over the results. The more control you give up, the easier each individual roll is, but, uh, how much wiggle room are you willing to give your new snake-hair?

                                (There are rules here for botching a Working. Game, I’m going to roll every single die I can scrape together, here, plus a Willpower – I’m not going to botch.)

                                Finally, you look at your Means – the tools to help you accomplish your goal. See, you can’t just declare a Working: you have to actually do stuff in the world to make it happen, which means other people have a chance to do stuff to stop it from happening. Cue instant plot generation to stop the Deathknight for ripping a hole between Yu-Shan and the Mouth of the Void, or whatever. You get a base of 5 rolls to make your Sorcerous Working (representing 5 weeks of effort) before you have to give up on it, but with appropriate Means, you can add more rolls before the deadline. Anything that logically would help, does help: if you have relevant Abilities, or Charms, or spells, or powerful allies, or extra time, or exotic materials… well, all of that just made your time limit easier. This is gorgeous: it’s a broad, flexible set of guidelines that still provides mechanical support for doing exactly the sort of things that make sense to do, while generating storylines for either the sorcerer or his opponents. This is a great system; I only wish I saw this design applied elsewhere in the game.

                                There are some nice follow-up notes: desperate/crazy sorcerers can try to perform almost-uncontrolled workings beyond their normal limit (hi, plots!), and anyone near an ongoing working can feel its presence and general effect (hi again!).

                                Why would anyone not be a sorcerer? This is a wonderful sentence to type.

                                The section on Howl’s Moving – I mean, Ivory Eyes’s Mobile Fortress starts three sentences in a row with the word “However.” Guys.

                                (Are there a lot of font changes through this chapter – like, the text suddenly getting smaller for a few lines – or is it just me? Also, really starting to hate those blood-spatters that overlap the bottom line of text in this chapter.)

                                If there’s one problem I have here, it’s not actually in the Working rules themselves – it’s in the probabilities they assume. Say you want a Finesse 5 Solar Ambition 3 Working – that’s 75 successes, losing the first 4 successes off each roll. Even as a Solar with a maxed-out pool of 21 dice + stunt + Willpower, I’m only averaging ~12.5 successes a roll, netting 8 – I need to extend my Means to 10 to have better than 50/50 odds of success. That’s a lot of plotting I’m going to need to do.

                                … Unless I activate Fate-Shifting Solar Arete on each of those rolls. Which I can, upping my average results to >21 successes per roll. Now even the base Means 5 is enough for a likely success.

                                The above note is not original to me, but I think it’s an important observation: FSSA breaks the game’s expected probability curves everywhere those curves exist. It is the best Craft, Sorcery, or Wyld-Shaping aid in the game.

                                We close with a section on Thaumaturgy, which is… eh. Okay, so in 2e, Thaumaturgy was a sort of magical science practiced by shamans the world over. It’s kind of weird, one-off miracles now, for those weird, one-off miracles that can’t be covered by sorcerous merits or Wyld mutations or Working side-effects or divine parentage or minor Exigence or you get the idea. But these powers are so, so trivial, and they’re so absurdly rare on top of that. Like, there’s a “Start a campfire” power; it hasn’t been seen in ten years. Well, then, who cares? What am I meant to do with that? It’s rare enough to not inform the setting, and it’s not like the Second Coming of Campfire Guy is going to shake up the houses of power. Another power lets you spend 1wp to break one loaf of bread into two. Would you take that power, if it was offered to your character for free? Would you use it, if you did? (Yes, obvious Jesus allusions in this power – but they didn’t call it the Feeding of the Five.) Exorcism, at least, is mildly useful – but we’re now two pages into something that feels like a general Occult power, and I’m still not at all sure what Thaumaturgy-as-a-thing is adding to my game.

                                So the final verdict on this chapter is mixed. I really like the shorter Charm cascades and tight theming of martial arts, though the power levels there seem to wobble somewhat, and they suffer from the lack of clarity in earlier chapters. The sorcery section needs a good beating with the edit stick, and thaumaturgy seems so far to be a waste of page count (to me!) – but the sorcery section is beautiful and enticing, and Workings are a system to be proud of.

                                                    Chapter Eight: Antagonists

                                                    We get a fiction from Abyssal Wario, i.e., the Physician – huh, is this the first returning signature character? I guess not – we had Seven Devil earlier. Anyway. It’s a pretty good piece – not a lot happening, but it gives a good sense of flavor and has some fun black humor with the banality of evil: “Who would have known that practicing black magic would involve so much cobbler’s twine?” The illustration is competent, but uninspiring. Unfortunately, this chapter continues the trend from those previous: no mortals with speaking parts here. (I don’t believe the last chapter had any, either, though it had some off-camera who were theoretically important.) The fiction is selling me a world where mortals are setting, not actors – that’s going to be a hard impression to shake.

                                                    Actual chapter text! We have a new quick characters system, which is one of the much-ballyhooed features of the new game. It boils down to assigning Essence, Willpower, and some basic die pools – no stats, just guidelines for how big to make different pools. For something like a mortal opponent, this is probably plenty; heck, it may be overkill. The actual magical powers involved seem to still be largely a matter of “make something appropriate up,” supplemented by some specific examples – which, that doesn’t feel all that much better than just having full sheets. Still, for some folks this will probably be a big help; it’s not really my thing, but it seems to do its job about as well as is possible in a system based around thousands of independent rules exceptions.

                                                    We open with some mortal antagonists. There’s an odd thing here where some antagonists get to be specific examples (the medium infantry example is a lady named Rain Plum), where others are just generic summaries – it’s a little funny when the text abruptly switches from one to the other. (This is one of those places where a sidebar feels like it would be appropriate, honestly.)

                                                    The mortal stats don’t impress that much – I mean, mortals, but still. The elite soldier has a Withering pool of 11, which will probably actually hit fairly often, but most of the weaker examples are throwing around seven dice – that’s not even meaningful opposition for the (brand new!) players I have. I get the sense that these examples are trying to do double-duty as worldbuilding, but still – four pages of perfectly vanilla mortal antagonists?

                                                    Ahh! Okay, I was not expecting the illustration of the bright yellow snake-lady. I’m not sure what it is that bugs me about this piece – it’s not just her face, but the merchant guy looks off, too. The next page makes up for this by giving us the fogshark; I am pretty sure my players still don’t know what Sidereals are, but they all know Fogshark. Apparently the fogshark carries an “Interregnum sword” – ah, this must be one of the places where the text is hidden behind the illustration.

                                                    We see again one of the sample spoiler characters: Kina, the hungry ghost with the misfortune of still being alive. I respect the goal here, but we’re more than a paragraph into this odd exception before we know what the base case looks like – this is a rough introduction if you’re new to the world.

                                                    There are a lot of ghosts in this section. With a lot of unique powers, which is nice – I feel like I could hack together a spirit-enemy (or just an NPC) from what I’m seeing.

                                                    Black Breath of the Abyss is one of the first Eclipse-tagged powers I’ve seen, and it’s mean: everyone living out to medium range is struck blind. Solar animas dispel the effect, which is great if you’re a Solar, but for anyone else this is pretty crippling.

                                                    The Nephwrack is pretty sweet – this is a cool boss for a party at about the E1-2 range, depending on their composition. He’s got a number of really nasty unique powers, but my feel is that he’s going to have a hard time crossing “the Excellency line” – that is, unless he’s supplemented by a large number of minions, he’s just going to have trouble actually connecting with an Excellency-equipped party.

                                                    We get some basic rules on spirits – good! Divine immortality is discussed, as well as a few standard spirit Charms – that’s a good space-saver. Measure the Wind is mentioned; it detects Exalts (the first power so far to do so) but fails automatically against magical disguises (good).

                                                    Hm. The dogs of unbroken earth are gods now, not elementals. Wonder why. New Eclipse Charm: sense when intruders enter an area claimed by a dog. Hmkay – that’s a bit less impressive.

                                                    The field god gets us another Eclipse Charm: grow a bunch of wheat instantly, for 10m1wp. Excuse me, two powers: another lets you spend 30m1wp to harvest a field in a day. Okay, we’re not trending in the direction I’d hope, here.

                                                    The storm mother gets us an Eclipse power to generate a storm, which is still kind of ho-hum – but it also lets us call down lightning. I’m not sure how great that power is in general, but anything that lets a player generate environmental effects on command is worth noticing, at least.

                                                    Here we go: Ahlat. Ahlat is a big god; from what I understand, he’s probably the toughest nut to crack in the book. And justifiably so: he can trigger a second Join Battle roll at any time, which means he’s going to act first; he can drop up to 10 dice on his already-quite-accurate attacks; he can throw 24 dice of accuracy with double-9s on a Decisive that leaves the target bleeding to death (or just straight-out dead). On defense, he can give himself infinite soak and halve Decisive damage, spending Initiative to raise his Parry and cancel further damage, and he heals whenever he recovers from Crash. It’s the offense that’s the real problem here: Ahlat will build up to 20+ Initiative, if he wants to, and most of that is going to dump into someone. There aren’t a lot of tricks to survive a load of that magnitude; Adamant Skin Technique and Heavenly Guardian Defense probably will, but it’s not a long list. If you can actually weather that first big hit, he’s very vulnerable to magical flurries: no Onslaught negation or flurrybreaking unless the GM feels like letting Whispering Warrior Souls give him some appropriate martial art. (Pity the party where he suddenly acquires Steel Devil.)

                                                    (Actually, this is a point where Accuracy for quick characters shows a problem. No one cares if the medium infantry gets a couple more dice on his Decisive, but when Ahlat gets an extra 3 dice beyond a Solar’s maximum, it’s kind of a big deal – that’s a huge jump in the probability of the attack actually landing. Really, Ahlat seems like exactly the kind of character that the opening of the chapter said shouldn’t be a QC.)

                                                    We move into elementals. The garda bird gives us the Eclipse Charm Immolating Pyre, which… seems pretty legit, actually: enemy-only bonfire damage out to medium range. That’s pretty nice! There’s some good plot-hooks here; the greenmaw, for instance, might as well have “Hit me with sticks to get treasure” written on it, and the tidemare serves a clear purpose as “living submarine.” (The tidemare’s Eclipse Charms, which lets a Solar make pretty lightshows or move an extra range band underwater, seem a little… iffy.)

                                                    We also get Fakharu, Censor of the West – that’s another familiar face, and another big fight. Fakharu isn’t going to throw the raw damage or defense of Ahlat, but he’s built well for facing a large group simultaneously: he has a couple of multi-target attacks, reduces incoming damage, and has a poison breath that… I don’t quite understand the rules for. First, the poison’s duration is kind of absurd; ten rounds means that basically no one is going to resist it. Second, the poison says that Initiative lost becomes a penalty to resist the poison, but at the point you’d actually lose any Initiative, you’ve already made your resistance check – what? Fakharu’s always been a good character to have around: he’s sympathetic and somewhat approachable, but he’s also part of the problem of heavenly corruption. Getting him on your side is one of the big neon arrows labeled “Start CLEAN UP HEAVEN project here,” and his Intimacies in 3e look calibrated to make that hard, but not impossible.

                                                    Man, this demon picture is… not great. Why is the blood ape two stories tall and mostly bald? Why is the neomah, of all things, kind of hideous? I miss 2e’s weird, alien androgynous illustration – that was a believable demon-courtesan.

                                                    The array of demons here is high-utility, for the most part: agatae for transportation (and driving home just how alien demons are – they don’t understand your confusion any more than you understand their weird, arbitrary reactions), erymanthoi for footsoldiers, neomah for pleasure or children, Alveua for crafting, Mara for seduction and training, Octavian for face-beating, Sigereth for ill-considered wagers. Neomah give us a Charm that grants a targeted +2 Appearance; that’s pretty nice, for some concepts.

                                                    I like that we get a strong sense of the default for each First Circle, as well as some variations – the offhand note that some neomah are enthralled by dance or poison suggests some fun unique characters.

                                                    Erymanthoi have one of the broadest Measure the Winds I’ve seen so far – “taste someone’s blood” is easy enough to arrange that I can picture a sort of Wyld Hunt TSA backed by Erymanthus tasters. (Not as a general thing, of course, but if they know there’s a Solar in the village somewhere…) I’m glad that MtW fails against magical concealment; it’s a little odd that random First Circles can say “Yup, that’s a Solar,” when even, say, All-Encompassing Sorcerer’s Sight can’t.

                                                    We get Alveua’s usual catchphrase, which name-drops both the Ebon Dragon and Erembour, neither of which we otherwise know anything about (though Ebby seems to be in the background of all the pages of this chapter).

                                                    Octavian gives us an illustration, which is beefy and intimidating. None of the other Second Circles get pictures, which is kind of a shame – there are descriptions, but I miss Alveua’s “tiny lady with a ridonkulously huge hammer.” Octavian is also, yep, a big pile of NOPE – poison fists, double Decisive damage, Withering attacks that multiply their damage by extra successes, and so on. His defense is similarly piled up, sporting Parry enhancers, reflexive Clashes, Disarming counterattacks, and (while not Crashed) the power to cap how much damage he actually takes from an attack. Late in 2e, I had a player test his build by soloing a beefed-up Octavian, and it was a narrow thing; I’m curious to see how a rematch would go now. Binding him actually doesn’t look that hard – he can’t in general enhance unrolled values, so his Resolve caps at 6. As with Ahlat, Octavian does not have any kind of Onslaught negator or flurrybreaker; I suspect a circle with, say, Revolving Bow Discipline or a bunch of magical flurries are going to give him a bad time.

                                                    Oddly, the demons are the first ones we’ve seen with Principle of Motion, which makes them both more dangerous and more mobile than their base stats would indicate. (What does happen if you flurry a Rush, anyway?) It’s odd that the gods and elementals don’t have that – a deliberate design choice, or just happenstance? It’s also worth noting at this point that there’s almost nothing like a general spirit Excellency – the die adders we see are (1) very limited and situational, like Fakharu’s “add (current Willpower) dice” or Ahlat’s “Add (Initiative) dice,” and (2) all seem to have a minimum Essence requirement of 4.

                                                    The demon spread is probably the best so far – which is good, since they’re going to put in regular appearances in any sorcerer-heavy game. This section is enormous, which I have sort of mixed feelings about, and a GM expecting “quick characters” may be a little surprised when some of their choices take up nearly three pages. Still, they effectively convey a sense of individuality – that these aren’t just Ancient Red Dragon #37, but they’re particular beings with their own drives. This is a solid (if somewhat bloated-feeling) section.
