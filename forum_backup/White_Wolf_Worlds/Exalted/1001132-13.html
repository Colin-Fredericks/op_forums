If either of each traveled too far into the other's territory, they would get destroyed in short order. as the territory of the opposing faction plays to that factions strengths. Of course, the Hill-Tribes have the edge, since the Hundred Kingdoms are hilly as shit.

This would allow the Hill-Tribes to encircle and destroy the Hundred Kingdoms forces piecemeal.

In game terms, the Hill-Tribes would have higher stats, but lower Drill and effective morale (Willpower)), holding the forts would be an entirely different story. Forget actually standing in the field: an Oathsworn cavalry charge will scatter them in an instant.

    Something like 90+% of a Hill-Tribe warparty is light skirmish infantry (archers, slingers, javelinmen, attacking from ambush, cover, high ground, or preferably all three at the same time), and while they can fight in melee, they "prefer" ( its complicated. While the average Hill-Tribe warrior youth is more than eager to mix it up and gain some glory, and possibly/probably pretty good at it, it isn't their strong suit; they fight in loose order at best, and lack the discipline and morale for prolonged melee outside of ambush. ) not to. Only the Chosen warriors of the Chief are going to be in wargear that even approaches heavy, and only they will have the discipline to actually see orders through.

    Basically, Hill-Tribes are "warriors", and Hundred Kingdoms are "soldiers" (http://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.ph...ldierVsWarrior). In a drawn-out melee or charge, chances are the Hundred Kingdoms would come out on top, while the Hill-Tribes have the edge in ambushes and skirmishes.

    Finally, Hill-Tribe warbands have no effective logistics to speak of: each warrior is responsible for their own food, drink and armament, and you can only really carry so much. While each warrior is going to carry a few days worth of food on them, they are largely dependent on seizing the enemies stores for "long-term" attacks. Since most raids are undertaken in order to steal needed goods, they can't really "afford" to stay in the field for that long, on top of getting slowed down with the booty.

    On the other hand, the Hundred Kingdoms have, by comparison, effective logistics (although it pales in the face of the Shogunate or even the Realm [both Rome stand-ins in my games]). While each peasant Man-at-Arms and Oathsworn retainer is responsible for bringing their own wargear, chances are overwhelmingly likely that at the next castle there will be enough food for the army. The Hundred Kingdoms armies are basically tied to their wagon-trains, and the Hill-Tribes would cut them to ribbons with hit-and-run attacks.

        The Hundred Kingdoms

        A teeming morass of hundreds of petty kingdoms situated to the north of Vaneha, across the Yellow River, the Hundred Kingdoms region is largely viewed as a cultural backwater (especially by Vaneha), where brutish warriors and robber barons extort the fruits of the land from their peasant’s brow. While the above description is ….largely correct, if only in the technical sense of the term, one must dive deeper into the culture, social mores, and social strata of the region to fully understand why it is so.

        -History

        The Hundred Kingdoms were formed in the smoldering aftermath of Sword-Prince Kendorei’s stalled invasion. Left behind by their warleader, the clan-leaders and subalterns started to carve out sections of the newly-conquered Red, Black and Blue river valleys, keeping their subjugated Hill-Tribe quasi-slaves in line with the traditional Vanehani Attendant forces and proto-Oathsworn retainers.

        Initially, the Hundred Kingdoms were ruled over by a Vanehani-appointed military dictator, called the Shogun, a term (appropriately enough) stolen from The Shogunate. After time, and as the power of the local landowning Lords waxed and the Shogun waned, the position of Shogun became less and less important, until in RY 351, the Shogun Tamura Emishi was sent back to Vaneha in various pieces for failing to bow before the Lord Han. Thus ended overt Vanehani overlordship of the Hundred Kingdoms.

        Almost as soon as the Vanehani yoke was overthrown, the various Lords and realm in the Hundred Kingdoms began squabbling between themselves for the overlordship, or for more localized power. To the outside world, this internecine infighting has continued for over 400 years, with no end in sight. Yet, if an outsider (usually Vaneha, who is still a mite sore about getting kicked out) attempts to intervene, the Hundred Kingdoms put aside their differences (at least in public), band together and take out the invaders. As soon as the Hundred Kingdoms are safe, the realms start infighting again in short order.

        Over the years, many realms, clans and rulers have fallen, and the Hundred Kingdoms region is littered with ruined castles, dilapidated manors and other detritus of the ages. One can find a poor family with little to their name living in a collapsing manor-house in the backwoods, claiming descent from Kendorei himself!

        -Phenotypes and physical characteristics:

        The peoples of the Hundred Kingdoms are largely descendent from two ethnic groups; the Vanehani and the Hill-Tribes. Almost overwhelmingly, the nobility is descended from the Vanehani, while the common-folk tend to be a mix between the Vanehani invaders (especially the lower ranking members of the clans) and the Hill-Tribes, with Hill-Tribe characteristics being more common the farther north and higher in elevation one travels.

        The nobility tend to be of lighter skin tone; yellow tan, pale brown and peach, (although most nobles tend to be rather pale anyways, as sun-tanned skin is emblematic of the working class), with straight, dark hair and eyes.

        The common-folk range from the pale skin tones of the nobility, to the dark huskiness of the Hill-Tribes, and anywhere in between (although, yet again, commoners tend to be darker in general, as they spend more time outside), with hair tending towards dark brown, although red can be common in Hill-Tribe influenced areas.

        -Climate

        Overall, the Hundred Kingdoms region can be thought of as being relatively warm, without the searing heat of the Southern deserts, chaparral or veldt, and without the bitter cold of the Northern taiga, tundra or seacoast.

        The Hundred Kingdoms, like most of the East, is amazingly fertile by the standards of most of Creation, capable of fielding three harvests a year, year after year. The limitation that lessens agricultural production in favor of the Blessed Isle isn’t fertility, but weather.

        During the spring and fall (or, Seasons of Wood and Water), the Hundred Kingdoms are inundated with heavy rains, often jokingly referred to as “Dragon’s Piss”. During the summer (Season of Fire), the weather is relatively mild (aka about 70-80 degrees Fahrenheit), but humidity soars through the roof. Finally, during winter (Season of Air), snows sweep from the north, often burying the river valleys under several feet of snow.

        This often-extreme weather is a result of the unique geography of the Black, Red and Blue river valleys, and the mountains the Hill-Tribes live in. While the mountains are high from the Hundred Kingdoms-side, they are little more than high hills from the direction of the Thunder Plains (aka the grassland steppe several thousand miles across, to the north), and these heights serve as little more than speedbumps to the storms that sweep across the Thunder Plains. They cause the storms to rise into the atmosphere, then slow down and condense, dumping the accumulated precipitation onto the Hundred Kingdoms.

        In the summer, the mountains that cause the inclement weather serve to trap the humidity, making life miserable for all involved.

        In the low river-bottoms, trees are largely deciduous: beech and hickory, maple and elm, oak and ash. In the heights, conifers (pine, cedar, fir) start to become more common, along with birch and aspen, but do not replace the other species. Bamboo is everpresent, growing entire forests in the river bottoms and groves in the uplands.

        -Settlements:

        For the Hundred Kingdoms, “Nine out of Ten” is a pretty good rule of thumb.

        That statement means that, on average, 9 out of 10 people live in the rural countryside, as opposed to in the urban castle-towns, as well as approximately 9 out of 10 people being commoners.

        By-and-large, most people in the Hundred Kingdoms are going to live within either 30 miles of the Black, Red or Blue Rivers, or within 5 miles of a tributary of the same. Most settlements are built on the banks of some body of water, be it even a small stream.

        This is due largely to the fact that most land in the Hundred Kingdoms is wrinkled worse than an elephant’s backside, with most arable (or, really, easily arable) land being located on the banks of the major rivers. Some 70-odd percent of the population lives in these river-bottoms, and the woodlands, hills and mountains in-between the major river valleys are comparatively empty. Most of the river-bottoms are comparatively overcrowded, with little hope for younger sons and daughters to inherit farmland, leading to an exodus to the castle-towns (more on that later).

        Generally, most settlements in the Hundred Kingdoms will meet one of these gradations:
        • Town: A town is a larger settlement, with a permanently-staffed Immaculate Temple and a designated market-square. Walls and bridges/dikes, as well as dockyards, are also important qualifiers. Most towns, including castle-towns, are located on the banks of the Red, Black and Blue rivers.
        • Village: Smaller than a town, a village nonetheless has an Immaculate Shrine, although it might not be permanently staffed.
        • Hamlet: Smaller still, hamlets are basically little more than wide places in the road, or clustered farmhouses in the countryside, and every inhabitant of a hamlet is possibly/probably related to everyone else.
        Generally, hamlets are clustered around villages, which is where the farmers bring their goods for trade, and villages are clustered around towns.

        Regardless of the size of the settlement, most settlements are built around a core plaza/square, where social gatherings and market-fairs (in the larger settlements) take place, and around which craftsmen (if the settlement has permanent craftsmen) and “common buildings”, like tithe-barnes for the local Lord and Immaculate temples, are located.

        Around the core plaza are located the private dwellings of the inhabitants, which tend to be square structures, built of wattle-and-daub, fitted drystone, or bamboo, with roofs of thatch, bamboo/wood shingles, or slate or ceramic tiles. In the lowest of river-bottoms, houses and other buildings might be up on stilts to prevent flooding and dampness. In market-towns and castle-towns, the streets and buildings will be built to an ordered plan (usually oriented to defense), but in smaller rural villages, buildings tend to be built wherever the owners felt like, leading to cramped alleys between buildings.

        Farther out from the center are the agricultural fields and livestock pastures, as well as farm-related outbuildings (barns, sheepfolds, sheds, stables, etc), surrounded by the forests and hills.

        -Social Organization:

        The Hundred Kingdoms can be broadly divided into two social classes: the nobility and the commoners.

        The commoner-class is made up of peasants and serfs, with slaves beneath them. The main division between peasants and serfs is the ownership of land, with peasants owning land and serfs renting it from a landlord. Peasants tend to be wealthier, larger and stronger, better educated, and more “respected” by the nobility, while serfs are the opposite.

        Peasants, as expected, make up the bulk of the Hundred Kingdoms “bureaucracy”, limited as it is, serving their noble landlords as foremen, followers and armed soldiery (the Men-at-Arms are overwhelmingly drawn from the peasant class), while serfs are left to work the land (as it all they can be trusted with, in the minds of their lords). Peasants, as landowners, pay their landlord taxes on the land they own, while serfs instead pay rent on top of working on their landlords private lands; the amount of work depends on the lord and the realm.

        Nobles, on the other hand, run the gamut from the Great Lords of the major realms, to the Oathsworn landlord you pay your taxes to. The “lower” one is on the nobility scale, the more common it is you will interact with the common-folk. A Great Lord, or a member of their clan, might go their entire lives (although that is difficult) without interacting at all with a commoner, while their Oathsworn retainer, granted land for their service, would probably interact with their tenants every day.

        As a result of the overcrowding of the farmlands, many younger sons and daughters of peasants (and even serfs, although serfs leaving their lords land without permission is illegal) pack up and head to the larger towns for work. This has led to the spontaneous creation of the “middle class” of townsmen; skilled craftsmen, merchants and guildsmen, not dependent on working the land for survival, and quite capable of hypothetically making money hand over fist. Most nobles find this middle class quite confusing; as they are technically legally serfs or peasants at best, they are nonetheless quite wealthy, with skilled merchants and craftsmen rivalling the Great Lords in wealth and influence. Most townsmen belong to some sort of guild (not the Guild); an organization of skilled workers who group up for protection and other benefits, and many Guilds have power, wealth and influence that far surpass their liege-lord. After all, the sword can only reach so far, whereas money is good wherever you go.

        As a result, many towns are ruled over by their landlords in name only, with the Guilds taking on most aspects of day-to-day running, and several Guilds have petitioned their liege-lords for “charters”, or the right to administer, build and maintain not only towns, but harbor-facilities, walls, gates, bridges, etc. Not many Lords have granted charters as of yet, but the efficiency of the guild-run towns compared to the noble-run ones cannot be denied. After all, safe towns are productive towns…

        -Hundred Kingdoms clans:

        All facets of Hundred Kingdom society, from the highest of nobles to the lowest of commoners, is determined by one’s clan, or extended family. The clan is an individual’s lifeline, social network, past and present and future. Actions are undertaken in order to bring honor to the clan, and to avoid bringing dishonor.

        Among nobles, the clan is represented by the “family name” (aka Shiba, Matsu, Harutane, etc), and noble clans can be quite large, with many dozens of members, cadet-branches and subfamilies. For example, the Shiba clan is divided into the Teriake Shibas (aka Lord Iysens family), the Iyana Shibas, the Wuyi Shibas and the Shino Shibas. Most noble clans will have a “senior” branch/family, the relatives of the head of the clan, and the one who makes the decisions for the clan.

        Among commoners, clans tend to be smaller, a couple dozen people at the most, and they tend to be much closer together, both in relations and in territory. For example, you might have several families in a clan live in the same village, with the entire clan spread across the villages in the valley, or in isolated hamlets, everyone in a village could be a member of the same clan.

        Regardless of if a clan is noble or commoner, the clan is by-and-large the social lifeline of the individual in the Hundred Kingdoms. Clanmembers stand by each other, assist each other in running businesses and the like, arrange marriages, look after children and invalid relatives, aka anything they can do to help each other. Filial piety is everything to a clan; leaving clanmates by the wayside is the ultimate dishonor, and clan ancestors are commonly worshipped. Clanmates are commonly called upon to represent them in court, to back one up in a fight, or anything else you need help with.

        If someone in the Hundred Kingdoms lacks a clan to back them up, that person is in trouble.

        -Food and Clothing:

        Most clothing throughout the Hundred Kingdoms is going to be broadly similar, even between social classes, differing mainly in textiles used, the fineness (threadcount, with higher threadcounts being finer and nicer cloth), and the length/ornamentation of layers of fabric.

        Most everyone wears undergarments around the groin to avoid rashes, with commoners wearing cotton and hemp, and nobles/the wealthy wearing linen or even silk. Over these, a pair of loose-fitting trousers and a open-breasted shirt are worn, again of cotton or canvas and linen or silk.

        The shirt, instead of being pulled over the head or buttoned up the front, is entirely open, with lapel-flaps being folded over each other and the entire garment bound shut with a belt or a sash.
        In colder or wetter weather, a jacket of quilted wool, canvas or linen is worn, fastened in much the same way as the undershirt. Conical hats, made of straw, are also worn in all weathers and seasons, to keep off the sun, the rain, and snow, as well as “coats” made of the same.

        For show, a loose robe or mantle of finer cloth is worn over everything else, itself made of linen, velvet or even silk.

        As for footwear, a kind of cotton, canvas, wool or linen sock-thing is worn, except when one is asleep or working in the fields. In wet or cold weather, wooden sandals are added.

        Most clothing is layered, with underlayers being longer than the one above it. The higher the social class, or the less physical work one is expecting to do, the longer the skirts, sleeves and other layers of clothing will be. A peasant farmers shirt might fall to the mid-thigh, while a nobles shirt would go all the way to the ankles, with sleeves draped down to the waist.

        Difference in wealth and social class is instead inferred through material and ornamentation. Commoners usually wear rougher, harder-wearing fabrics (although a well-off peasant could certainly afford some comfortable cotton), with nobles wearing linen, velvet, and silk. Dyes and colors are not restricted, any everyone will generally wear clothing decorated in some fashion, but generally nobles (aka “old money”) are going to wear articles of surprisingly simple cut and coloration, usually single colors and muted prints (for example, Lord Iysens robe is a severe dark blue and white, but it is made of exceptionally-fine silk). The idea is that the nobility doesn’t “need” to show off their clothing in order to show their power.

        Instead, ostentatious clothing is the indicator of the town-dwelling middle class (aka “new money”), with bold, contrasting colors, flashy patterns and prints, and extravagant materials the norm for all those that can afford it.

        (basically, look up Japanese clothing)

        As for food, it largely depends on where one lives in the Hundred Kingdoms. The most southerly realms are about the northern limits of where rice will grow reliably, meaning most people subside off other grains, like wheat, corn, oats, etc, in addition to other vegetables. Vegetables include things like various legumes, cabbage, radishes and turnips, onions and leeks, potatoes, soybeans etc.

        Orchards are relatively common, mainly owned by landlords; cherry,apples, plums (always a favorite!), peaches and pears are all cultivated. The fruit is eaten fresh, preserved, cooked in soups and other dishes, made in tarts, pies and other deserts, and made into ciders and wines. The blooms are often related in poetry and other arts to the concept of mortality as a whole (especially the noble warrior, a concept/symbol inherited from Vaneha), and every spring it seems like every valley is filled with the slowly-drifting petals.

        As for meat: most everyone, commoners and nobles alike, eats fish, either fresh or smoked, and pork is by far the most common terrestrial animal consumed, valued for its fat and smooth taste. Pigs are intelligent enough to allow loose into the forest, put on weight nicely and produce large litters, all pluses to the farmers who must look after them. Other meats include chicken, goat/sheep, and wild game like deer, boar, pheasant, rabbit and, for the brave, tiger.

        The majority of the diet is made of three parts: bread, broth and brews. Bread aka grains is not usually baked in an oven, but usually made into dough, then into things like noodles or steamed dumplings. Vegetables and meat are put into soups, stews and stuffed into dumplings. Brews, or alcohol and tea, are consumed by everyone, although the only the nobility really drinks tea on a regular basis, as it has to largely be imported.

        Spices are mainly imported, with only salt, soy sauce, mustard, peppers of various heats, honey and vinegar being “native”.

        -Religion:

        Overwhelmingly most people in the Hundred Kingdoms are going to be some degree of Immaculate-fearing, with an Immaculate Temple or shrine available in some fashion throughout the entire region. There are plenty of travelling monks to attend to the needs to the faithful, and the dates of festival-days are known by practically everyone.

        Spread across the Hundred Kingdoms are a multitude of monasteries and abbey’s, usually fortified, and filled with monks devoting their lives to service (and, in times of war, desperate refugees). In many of these monasteries, martial training accompanies the spiritual, and there are as many folk-stories about righteous warrior-monks travelling the lands and righting wrongs as there are about Oathsworn. These warrior-monks usually train with the spear, the glaive or the bow, as the sword remains the symbol of the nobility.

        This does not mean, however, that other deities or spirits are not honored. Most travelers will make offering to spirits and gods of the roads and of the rivers before setting out, and ancestor-spirits are outright worshipped, in spite of the Immaculate Faith looking down on the practice. Even a peasants house will have a small ancestor-shrine, usually in a corner or in a cubbyhole, with candles/lantern, offering bowl and other votive objects on fully display. In noble manors, the ancestor-shrine can be an entire room in and of itself, with carved stone tablets, small bell or gong, and devotional objects.

        -Relations with outside parties:

        The only two groups the Hundred Kingdoms interact with to any large scale are the Hill-Tribes and the Vanehani, both of which are negative.
        The relationship with the Hill-Tribes has already been described, but the interactions with the Vanehani is weird. Like, the Hundred Kingdoms eagerly absorb Vanehani culture, yet otherwise want nothing to do with them. This is mainly due to the efforts of the Vanehani to subdue/conquer the Hundred Kingdoms in the past.
