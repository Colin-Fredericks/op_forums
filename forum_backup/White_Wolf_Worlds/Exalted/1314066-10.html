        Originally posted by The Wizard of Oz View Post
        Of course, Dragonblood tend towards an aristocratic model where powerful families rule far-flung regions, because of their blood potency.
        Yes, I think the Realm is constrained to this quite unusual model of a long term stability of relatively cohesive blocs of competing aristocrats combined with a strong centre, because of the degree to which the central power is constrained to replacing aristocrats with loyal appointees (the Empress is not like the Unconquered Sun in this regard, where he can turn out personally loyal appointees to facilitate his authority rather than bother with independent family power bases). So this unusual model of trying to balance power between blocs of aristocratic families emerges, rather than just trying to smoosh them.

        (Which is great because it leans into much of what the Grabowski found both interesting and worth of criticism about Chinese government over time, as well as the sort of Dune/Rokugan-esque model of powerful houses and clans, which is appealingly gameable and fairly rich for intrigue).

        The Empress is also building this in the consciousness of how the Shogunate worked, with a *lot* more dependence on a central authority.

        On history, I was going to note earlier, but thought off topic, a lot of the Realm's ideology is constrained or enabled by the degree to which they're putting together an idealised version of previous Realm society (Shogunate and more hazily, Old Realm) which really were world empires. That's something where Realm imperial does owe more to China than nautical imperial powers like Rome, Britain, Spain that expanded over the seas; the ideology has a bit more of "The empire long divided, must unite", and restoration of the legitimate power, and protecting the fringe against the outer barbarians (Fair Folk, unruly spirits and so on). More so than Rome's "We must conquer these expansive Mediterranean and Central European states/tribe like Carthage and the Gauls before the conquer us", or Spain/Britain's conquering of Americas/Asia in order to capture more tax revenues in order to remain in theory more secured as a nation against French demographic power / "Barbary" / the "Turk" etc, without much thought of deriving legitimacy from an earlier predecessor that spanned those regions. (Realm collaborators probably argue that they're helping to reassemble the old empire, rather than that they're subjugating their nation).

        Originally posted by Gigaton-Falcon-Emu View Post
        You can't even make the argument that pure freedom is a good thing in Exalts, since unlike we mortals, they can fabricate doomsday weapons and Workings that turn continents to metaphors without the requisite infrastructure and intermediaries that prevents someone with access to control of atomic weapons from drawing a happy face on the moon with them.
        A go at an argument to the contrary: Some possible upsides to Exalted individual freedom though;

        - More individualized Exalted actors may be less likely to lead to high stakes wars than situations where all Exalted are under large blocs. That might reduce the frequency of seriously apocalyptic events, and at least distribute risk.
        - More individualized Exalted actors won't struggle against authorities for their freedom
        - More individualized Exalted actors are more free to follow passions and interests, and that could have significant upsides. Free Exalted might get you a terrible Working you might not expect, but also a highly positive one. We don't have an equal but opposed (creative rather than destructive) nuclear device example, but the Exalted do.

        The big problems with nuclear proliferation in our world seems like the long term damage to the biosphere and the chances that a lone actor rogue state can tip the world into a full blown apocalypse, due to organisation of huge quantities of those weapons into armed camps and the possibility that they can precipitate a nuclear winter.

        If we had a world where small states could nuke each other, but with no consequences beyond that (no huge organisation of the world into two armed camps, no nuclear winter or biosphere collapse), then nuclear proliferation would be a very different question.

                                                    Okay, had some other things to attend to, so now (after a fairly straightforward picture of officers engaged in high society that lends itself a bit of flash with a distinct regal colour scheme) the question of support staff, which I think would be useful for rounding out the cast and considerations of a chronicle concerned with campaigning.

                                                    I particularly like being given a description of what the field staff of a legion’s general consists of, as it helps to provide the picture of what high level command duties are in between actual battles, and generally characterising what running an army on the march would be like. The layout of how an army communicates in battle seems not only useful for characterisation, but it complements the advice about running combat in play to have an idea that a focused battle group could be sending and receiving messages to the background as part of shaping the story of how a larger battle is going. Engineers are straightforward, while the idea of a uniform distribution of sorcerers adds to the sterling reputation of the legions in terms of their added destructive power and any such magic as might aid communications and mobility. I like how the first duty of attached Immaculate monks is tending to the spiritual needs of the troops, even if interacting with local gods and rooting out heresy are the more overtly dramatic ones; I find the idea of the cults that might develop in legions to both flesh them out, and provide inspirations for how some of them might develop having gone rogue. The baggage train gives a good breakdown on the makeup and significance of the supplies and maintenance personnel that would follow alongside a legion (and apparently it is still going with the idea that the slaves who manage such are treated better than the skirmishers, if only in the context of good treatment as a general strategy to prevent disruption of the baggage), and I think the high rank of the quartermaster would make for some interesting personality scenes for command staff. It’s a slight pity that camp followers are discouraged, since historically such people lend an interesting quality to marching armies being followed by what are virtually mobile cities (often of less than reputable people), but it makes sense for the quality ascribed to the legions, and I would say there’s still interesting potential to reap out of the arrival of a sanctioned merchant and their wares and services; maybe makes it more of a special treat for the soldiers.

                                                    Okay, sidebar on Artifacts. It would seem that, in keeping with their more personal approach, they didn’t go with an idea that legions or the empire as a whole would maintain arsenals of the regular ones to assign as needed, so it falls down to what one can get from the private stocks of one’s House (even if being in the legions means that such a grant is nigh permanent) and special gifts from highly placed superiors. I think the last line of that paragraph gives the concept its strongest story and character potential, both in the idea that a legion’s officers might acquire a patchwork of weapons acquired from its various battles (or sacking cities or sacred places), hire services as needed, and especially of officers occasionally making their own weapons. I think the last lends itself to both characters wielding daiklaves or similar that are very strongly matched to them and iconic of some of their harshest challenges or greatest achievements, or acquiring a weapon with such a backstory. The part about First Age weapons seems to mostly come down to a plot hook, but a very nifty one.

                                                    Auxiliaries probably constitute the most intriguing development of both the Realm’s military doctrine and makeup and their relationships with the satrapies. Somewhere I had an impression that the Realm’s source for cavalry was being modified, but it would appear that it remains the province of auxiliary troops (with the curious addition of the Marukani of all people, despite their traditional depiction as allies of Lookshy; it makes me wonder if that society will place greater emphasis on the self-determination of given homesteads and communities, and thus more flexibility in who they serve with). The main difference that I see here from previous depictions is the manner in which it is used to add places to the setting and give distinct origins a more unique character when fighting alongside the legions, as well as the premise that waging war against a power that might be on an equal footing to a force commanded by several dozen Dragon Blooded leads them to swelling their numbers as large as possible. I would suppose that outright conscription is the most severely intrusive practice that the Realm generally employs in satrapies, and could even see such a thing as significantly transforming a nation’s attitude towards them (especially given how they’re employed in battle).

                                                    I’ve wondered about the precise meaning behind the use of the term “janissaries” since it was introduced attached to Medo, so I can see sense in the premise that it’s modified a bit to the context of the Realm’s imperialism by way of serving as a tribute of professional soldiers (which could possibly also be a source of superior archers to Realm skirmishers in theatres where they might be seen as necessary). I like this possibility that a martial satrapy might end up as one with significant cultural influences not as a direct result of Realm colonialism, but of deployments of their people in distant lands.

                                                    I vaguely recall the martial orders being described in Fangs at the Gate’s section on the Caul, but don’t remember them being directly described as they are here. Obviously they have a lot in common with Christian military orders, especially the association with what is virtually a crusader state and capacity to reorganise as charitable organisations (not to mention the centralised sanction, with the Scarlet Empress in place of a direct religious authority), while I’d say that the general Immaculate aesthetic and the idea of forming in times of significant external danger or internal civil disorder synthesizes in a lot of the Japanese sohei. It’s an interesting idea for activities that might be undertaken by far flung monks, and could conceivably be a more visible presence of the Realm’s influence than even the satrap. The example groups all have something to recommend them: the Wardens strike an image distinct from how one might typically regard a martial order or arm of the Realm with specialisation on guerrilla tactics, the Sword-Saints are an interesting premise for an element of the war for the Caul whose arrogance and infighting is probably doing more harm than good for their cause (another reference to the Crusades), but the Dragonguard are the major standout. They’ve got a nice sense of transition over time, of feeling and recovering from a major disaster, they stand apart from most Immaculate branches by proselytizing through direct conquest, they effectively adapt the core premise to present something that can rule as a sovereign power while still technically following their mandate, and they’re a good example of the kinds of consequences that can result from Exalted intervention that takes on a life of its own.

                                                    Since the presence of the Dynasts in the Imperial Legions has been downplayed, I find it makes sense for mercenaries to constitute a significant way that they would historically assert themselves militarily in the Threshold. I like that for providing an avenue in backstory for Dynasts to be intertwined in the kinds of intriguing disaster that can have often developed from dependence on mercenaries, such as the history of warfare between Italian city-states, and the manner in which mercenary contracts and rapine perpetuated the Thirty Years War; I could see it as the background to particular sources of outrage directed at the Realm, and involved in the downfall of a House or two. That they’re being drawn to the Blessed Isle itself bodes especially poorly for the prospect of civil war, adding to the conflict between House the possibility of large companies that, where not unreliable and untrustworthy, find themselves free to roam the Isle aligned to nobody. It seems like a particularly effective means for other Exalted to get to the heartlands of the Realm unnoticed, hiding amidst a group that a Dynast or other official has obligingly provided with a plausible excuse for arriving, and thereafter disappearing within the more diverse and turbulent environment that those people have created. I’m amused by the image of a Dragon Blood who comes to the horrified realisation that they provided Anathema with access to the Realm’s shores, and gives pursuit to try and correct the mistake before it’s realised by their peers.

                                                    The first word on the typical strategies and tactics is the kind of thing that I’ve supposed in the past; that while not unsophisticated, the Realm’s military thinking is inclined towards the conservative, although it reads to me here as incentivized a bit by where one can place accountability (although I would have to imagine that a certain status quo to their dominance and the kind of power they bring to bear limits a perceived need to modify over time). The idea of an emphasis on speed and manoeuvrability is also something I’ve been developing over reading this chapter, in combination with the familiar format of the legions and scope of the world they’re against. In essence, the Realm strikes me as a power in which focusing their forces around a core of Exalted commanders lends itself to many situations in which their armies are outnumbered by the enemy; the impression I get of their response to that is a preference for using high speed campaigning around enemy territories to disrupt supply lines, population centres and strongholds, leaving an enemy army that will be tired and weak that can either be engaged while poorly deployed to defeat in detail or else lure against a defensively arranged formation that waits for an opportunity to surge against a weak spot in enemy lines with a wedge of well-equipped, highly disciplined troops tipped with a concentration of Exalted power, rather than trying to match a lot of enemies in lines against lines. I would find that to be consistent with the text provided for the Thousand Correct Actions that was provided in Second Edition (which I’ve always presumed was drawing on notes compiled by the game’s original developers).

                                                    While we’re not given a direct outline of what constitutes innovative tactics for some generals to employ, I’d say that we’ve been given a strong enough picture of the makeup of a legion to be able to infer some possibilities. I would imagine things such as tactics that place a much stronger emphasis on archers or cavalry are among them, perhaps trying to integrate a style particular to a given janissary corp into the wider tactics of the legion. Still, the idea that the success of such things can end with them becoming incorporated into the military canon makes me thing that they might more typically take the form of using the traditional legion composition and deploying it in novel and experimental ways that carry noteworthy risks; I’m thinking of something such as Hannibal’s unorthodox formation at Cannae, and a battle plan dependent on having a weak centre deliberately give ground without breaking to be able to draw superior Roman numbers into a lethal encirclement. And some might plain depend on the application of powerful or unusual magics, or manipulations of the environment; I can see the odd circumstance in which a Realm officer finds that the way to deal with the enemy is to trigger an avalanche down upon them (Dragon Blooded can certainly possess some very powerful and destructive magic in their own right, but I feel as though Realm tactical doctrine would discourage treating them as the primary cog in a battle plan because their distribution is not uniform and access to them can be disrupted). In any case I can suppose such tactics being drawn into the library for consideration, while still not given the greatest emphasis or first choice when regarded as unnecessary. Like, you’re going to remember when a legion defeated an enemy force with a heavy concentration of archers, but that’s something to fall back on when circumstances seem to demand it, not something to build an army towards.

                                                    Still, a lot of that may be more academic, or a consideration for circumstances in which veteran outcastes come to the fore, with the idea that less well-drilled and experienced Dragon Blooded are being given positions of command in the new House legions. On that note, I like the number of possible axes given for the origins of particular favoured tactics among the legions, even going beyond their own organisation and the military Houses; Mnemons might have been a minority among legionary commanders, but it makes sense that they would bring the outlook and expertise of their backgrounds to a preference for fortification. I particularly like the idea given of a tactical tradition passed along a single family line deriving from the applications of a personal heirloom Artifact.

                                                    The basic outline that the sidebar on intelligence gives for how legions gather strategic information is fine, and the continued significance given to the All-Seeing Eye in the form of general data on the composition and objectives of enemy forces to provide to commanders that is now being withdrawn is a nice emphasis on their new sense of power and purpose. But the biggest thing that I take from that paragraph is the focus on how House Sesus stands out in the current environment. What Fire Has Wrought outlined Cathak as the greatest military power owing to its number of legions and pre-existing strong military tradition, Tepet’s outlook has been improved by pointing to foreign militaries of high quality that could be willing to ally with them, and now we have a light shone on Sesus as the one that have the most superior intelligence gathering capabilities at a time where that component of other forces has been compromised. None of these things quite render the military Houses as the only contenders, but it does draw attention to how their having a stronger and deeper background in warfare makes them major complications and obstacles. It all effectively sets a stage for the Realm teetering on the brink of a kind of conflict that they’ve never before known.

                                                    And I’ll finish off for now with the descriptions of the specific and somewhat idiosyncratic forces. First is the Vermillion Legion, which within the overall picture currently given for the Realm’s military I can only assume is a group that at some point in its history displayed a particular willingness to recruit from social quarters of low repute, found itself treated as an appropriate dumping ground for Dynasts of poor quality, and somewhere along the line decided to lean into that image in things like the willingness to employ more unorthodox or unsavoury tactics (where it wasn’t forced upon it by the kinds of assignments it was given and recruits or officers it found a hard time ranging beyond). It’s probably the pulpiest element of the Realm, and its features are so iconic that I find it to have made it across Editions almost entirely intact (the main loss seems to be for a strong emphasis on Dynasts being posted to the legion with the specific intent of them being killed); while I think it has just enough to be woven into the general milieu of the Realm, I would think those qualities ought to be embraced for the sake of a force that has a very grimy texture to it and an endearing sense of proud iconoclasm in a society that is so concerned with conformity. Mind, it’s ironic to consider how, out of all the legions, this might have been the one in which the highest proportion of officers were actually Dynasts throughout its history; although I can imagine that more than a few outcastes who graduated from Pasiap’s Stair with honour and began their careers in more savoury positions ended up here due to machinations, undue experimentation or bad luck. Such a thing would probably have been a strong basis for the legion’s core emphasis on merit, competence and internal camaraderie, even if it’s probably accumulated a lot of Dynasts who internalised and perpetuated such.

                                                    Now, the Legion of Silence of the Scarlet Empress. The first thing that I notice is that there’s no reference to them being eunuchs whatsoever. I had had a feeling that element would be removed; there had been criticisms at times that, magic or no, such a thing actually lends itself to severely diminished physical prowess. In retrospect, it also made them a bit gender uniform in a manner that felt somewhat out of place. In any case, I’d say the contribution it made to them was questionable. The second thing that I see is how they’re explicitly made immensely strong if not outright superhuman with the use of sorcery; I remember a period in which some develop thoughts were to attribute their prowess to the highest quality of training, but I would ultimately think that doesn’t quite make them stand out enough. The current approach to Charms precludes their enhancements from being attributed to a specially developed branch of those, but the current approach to sorcery rather obviates the need. I’ve always liked how they would be assigned duties of escorting Dynasts on important missions at the direction of the Empress, since I’ve never really like the premise of the very highest quality soldiers being confined to bodyguard duties for a monarch who exists at the heart of fortifications that would seem to give them little to do on a regular basis (looking at you, Adeptus Custodes), although with the current approach to Lunars one might comfortably attribute the Legion with making a major contribution to the Scarlet Empress still being alive, in the face of impressive opposition no less. I find this overall reference to the Legion of Silence to lend them a bit more agency, with the focus on them as continuing at their posts, answerable to nobody, to lend an effectively sinister air, and the emphasis on their inscrutable internal communications carrying the suggestion that they’re up to something. I even find the description given of their upbringing (and a little bit of how their commanders received orders directly from the Empress) to humanise them a bit; that one is both in just giving some focus to the image of them as children and the circumstances that make them what they are, and creating a background of interactions with other people. Their little sample write-up looks reasonably impressive, although I appreciate the added notion that they’re generally mysterious and Storytellers have a freedom to imbue them with initial capabilities as needed or desired; it gives me an impression that this write-up is the image that most would get seeing them at rest, with the unnerving potential or more beneath. Of the Merits, I like how the Imperial Devotion one limits its availability by way of the elegant solution of a very particular background from which such an extreme could arise.

                                                    Last is a sidebar on militias, rounding out both the military and administrative make-up of the Blessed Isle by taking the form of minor forces recruited under prefects and governors to provide the most basic municipal protection duties. I would find the majority of this description to be useful as finishing off the picture of what Realm life is like, providing identity to who might turn up at some problem situations, and a bit of background for some people associated with the legions. The most forward facing quality of them is how they figure into the prospect of civil war, finally addressing the idea of extending beyond the familiar scope of Realm legions and into the image of great masses of peasant conscripts set on the march to plunder neighbouring prefectures or provide home defence that might be ineffectual against even House paramilitaries, let alone true legions. It’s the foundation for a very brutal image of the human cost that such warfare might have (or at least the assets, should one’s priorities rest on that side).

                                                            Maseiken, I'll also try to avoid the OT aspect as much as poss, while hopefully responding to your overarching point rather than necessarily everything you've raised (may lose some nuances). I'm not so much responding to the question of whether many smaller wars are "better" than a few larger ones , but whether largely distributed, unaffiliated risks and lots of benefits to individual Exalted having personal freedom would precipitate the rise of political orders to try and control all of the Exalted in the manner of the systems that I believed Gigaton seemed to be talking about. It's a question of the process of political evolution towards a particular kind of government question, mainly.

                                                            So on that I'm generally of the opinion that individual Exalted being able to do these things would probably give rise to local responses (finding local counters), rather than commitment to a big overarching system (which would be more likely to arise from alliances between Exalted growing really big, if at all). Yurgen Kaneko probably inspires a local response in the North, which then collapses or inspires another response, etc. rather than catalyzes a world dominant system to try and manage all the Exalted (in the manner that the Scarlet Empress seems to aspire to manage all the Dragonblooded).

                                                            Re; nuclear proliferation, I agree in a world absent NATO and the USSR where there was deeply fragmented proliferation among many actors and then much use probably would have been apocalyptic, not so much under dispute; I'm disputing whether a real means of restraint of nuclear proliferation would even arise in such a world with many actors, rather than two big ones concerned with a big conflict in which each had huge stockpiles and clearly led to MAD. Political evolution question again.

                                                            Also noting that such an outcome wouldn't necessarily hold in Exalted as there are a lot of high quality gods and Exalted working able to fix lands that have been struck by devastating magic etc. Though this probably would not stop civilization being pushed to limits a number of times.
