        Possibly there's less of an incentive for the Realm,* but it is an effective measure to ensure the Delzahn (who still have many members far from Chiaroscoro living their traditional life as horse nomads and raiders) raid non-Realm territories rather than Realm territories. (The Delzahn of course get the benefit of not being raided or invaded by the Realm. They know their Northern border is safe.)

        Medo is a lot less lucrative for the Realm, as Medo doesn't have much wealth, but they have evolved this janissary system where Medo supplies them with skilled auxiliary cavalry (which benefits Medoan tribes, as their warriors return battle-hardened and cosmopolitan).
        Originally of course, Medo did try and invade Realm territories, but the Realm defeated them and now these barbarian horsemen are under loose Realm control; not enough to give up their religion, but enough to supply troops and raid the Scavenger Lands rather than, say, the lands of House Ferem, which are relatively close to Medo. They can go raid Sijan and Port Calin instead, that'll teach the so-called Shogun of Port Calin to refuse the Realm's offers of protection.

        Similarly, Linowan doesn't provide huge benefits to the Realm, probably less than Medo. They don't have much to pay in tribute, except perhaps slaves and certain woods (but they're a society of fishermen, farmers and warriors, not loggers). But through its House Pelleps satrap, the Realm is able to wield influence over them, directing their aggression towards Halta and not Realm-aligned lands (like Medo, to the south). And all the Realm has to do is defend them against Anathema.
        (Which, recently, turned out to be way harder than House Tepet expected. Unless the canon's been changed so the Bull didn't invade Linowan, which is quite possible. I think Vance may have addressed it, but I forget.)


        Essentially, my overall idea is thinking of China's relations with steppes tribes; some areas you subjugate to make money, like the Tarim basin, but a lot of that land is poor. There's not much money to be made. But relations with them are still very important, because it's easier to send them some (possibly fake) princesses and help them against some other barbarians than have to constantly fight off invasions by a horde of horsemen.
        You may need to occasionally invade them, crush their armies, enslave thousands, etc, (as the Realm fought the Touman Prince), but that doesn't mean your relations need to always be hostile. Build cultural and trade links, and direct the horde to invade somewhere else.

        Various other Empires have done the same thing, establishing a buffer of allied "barbarian warriors" via political, religious, and trade links, encouraging them to concentrate on raiding your enemies instead of you. European powers in America with allied American federations (Britain's support of Native federations, arming them to raid the USA rather than Canada, was one of the reasons for the War of 1812), Russia's use of Cossacks and Tartars, the Eastern Roman Empire and Persia's use of Arab tribes against each other*, etc.

        *This one is notable because of the religious dimension. The Romans used Monophysite Arab tribes, the Persians used Nestorian and Jewish tribes. I can well see the Realm using a semi-Immaculate barbarian horde led by a savage Dragonblood outcaste as a buffer against, say, Lunar-worshipping tribes of beastmen.

        Often, the Empire is powerful enough to just invade, kill, and enslave, but that's expensive and leaves a void for another lot of barbarians to migrate into.


        Saying this, I imagine this is the sort of debate Realm politicians might have. A nomadic horde is encroaching on strategically important territory. Do we send some forces to scare them off? Negotiate to bring them under our control, as happened with the Delzahn? Forcibly convert them to the Immaculate Faith? Or invade, crush them, kill the old, crippled and infants, and enslave the rest? Different houses and different Dragonblood will have different ideas. And as you mentioned, personal motives (like the general who has to decide has massive debts and desperately needs to make money via slaving) may push decisions to certain outcomes.

            On the number of Dragonblood in a Legion;

            If I remember correctly, at its height there were 80 Legions? And now it's down to about 30, a lot of which are under House control?

            So, if it was 80 a Legion, that'd be 6400 Dragonblood in the Legions at their height (let's say 50 years ago, long before the Battle of Futile Blood and the loss of most of the Caul) and 2400 now.

            If it's 11 per Legion, that'd be 880 at its height and about 300 now.

            The first number seems too high to me, and the second too low. So 11 as a minimum and maybe 30-40 as a normal number seems reasonable. So there's maybe 1000 Dragonblood currently serving in the legions, which is far below its height when about a quarter of Dragonblood were serving in the Legions at any one time.

            (But I might have remembered the number of Legions wrong, or the number of Dragonblood in the Realm. I think it's over 10000, but less than 15k? Anyone remember? The devs did say at some point.)

            Of course, it's not just purely a matter of overall demographics, but also this; if an Imperial Legion arrives to invade a place the PCs live, how many Dragonblood do you want? I think 11 is too low, but I think 80 is too high; they'd just become a featureless mass of generic officers. I think maybe 30 would be a very scary threat for PCs, but with enough to have a variety of interesting characters with a wide range of abilities.

            And I also think 30 is, frankly, enough that a Lunar is not going to want to attack them head on. Even 5 Lunars (which is a hell of a lot of Lunars) are going to want to be careful. 11 is not enough for a pack of Lunars to find very dangerous (it's quite dangerous for one, but I think the Dragonblood won't be in enough places at once to be a huge issue; essentially, they could maybe do minor sabotage to the Legion without being in huge, huge danger, though it depends on the Lunar), 80 is utterly overwhelming.

            So I think 20-40 probably leads to the best play experience.

                        Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                        You say "not a good strategy", but what's your value judgement on good here? One that achieves their goals? One that preserves their integrity? One that makes a priority out of not radicalising the local population such that, ten years later, they widely support an extremely severe theocracy?
                        Just the inflexibility of what seemed to be "always use punitive expeditions as the tactic" (the impression I got, wrong or right) is what seems like a poor strategy for me, from a practical point of view, and a less interesting one, in terms of the Realm encompassing fewer of the tactics and strategies used by the empires from which it draws inspiration.

                        But I think I may have had a more irritable and more distracted reaction to that point about whether the Realm pays off "cooked barbarians" to deal with "raw barbarians" than was necessary. And just as much I may have too much emphasized use of this strategy as common in my post you were quoting. (I don't see it as the only card up their sleeve, or always the best one, for sure). That's all less important to the more main point I was interested in.

                        Whether or not they purely use punitive expeditions beyond the borders or a more flexible set of strategies, I'd contend regardless there are probably circumstances that create the formation of fair sized populations beyond where they actually rule, and that they try to avoid overreach in where they actually rule*. Whatever they do probably has some limitations in its effectiveness! Those circumstances lead to the formation of "barbarian armies" and I suspect that the Lunars (or at least the Lunars as I think are well written) would try to shepherd, use and complement those, without much of a need for them to be tied down in events creating them, in a way that feels contrary to their strategy as described and impinge of travel and adventure in Lunar actual play.

                        Lots of other points in your post of interest, so will just say these are worthy factors to consider in the balance of the Realm using punitive/violent vs negotiating/allying strategies, in lieu of having more time, and given I'm kind of conceding to a bit more agnosticism on this point.

                        The only point I'd like to immediately clarify on is my mention of "Hadrian's Wall" - I was not intending a general comment on the effectiveness of walls themselves (I was intending no stance on that!), but just to use it as an example of a far frontier posting to try and emphasise a human dimension of why it's difficult to maintain a fortified frontier (whether by walls, dispersed garrisons and forts, etc.).

                        That soldiers are by default not going to be enthused about being posted to the limes far from "civilization", in some marsh, desert, steppe, mountains, tundra, etc. and that keeping them happy and not rebellious can impose high costs by ending up with a massive export of your "civilization". Example of Roman Britain - there's a lot of recreation of Roman society in Britain under Roman rule that basically seems to be quite arguably to serve officers posted there, and possibly at cost to the imperial government. Particularly that's the case if you're drawing them from your core (as Dragonblooded officers must be for example) rather than from local recruitment - e.g. again Roman use of Gaulish auxillaries in Britain.

                        * Like, on overreach, preview from the Lunars devblog post - "Knowing that overreach would play into Lunar hands, she slowed the Realm’s expansion to a crawl, tolerated greater independence in troublesome satrapies, and grew more reluctant to embark upon grand endeavors — subjugating the Scavenger Lands, reclaiming Prasad, seizing the West", which suggests some understanding of the Lunar strategem (what they seek to achieve, and how they seek to achieve it, which is relevant to some of our discussion about how much the Realm and Sidereals are aware of what the Lunars are trying to do and actively work against it) and that the Empress is mitigating against it at least by checking territorial expansion.

                        The Wizard of Oz, those calculations look pretty good to me.

                                    I'll admit that I have biases around the idea of Realm expeditionary forces, which on this occasion I'll elect not to bore people with the details of. I think I can basically summarise it with the idea that, in the absence of major rival powers for the Realm to compete with at war, and in a system in which their expansionism is somewhat limited, I kind of want the legions to consistently have something to do, and I have reservations about the idea that they're constantly on the defensive because that has connotations of problematic narratives that empires use to justify themselves.

                                    Which is admittedly complicated by the point about how the Silver Pact (and some of the Fair Folk) actually are present in the setting to attack them in a manner resembling those narratives. Hell, I would find those details to actually reinforce the importance of the Realm asserting itself pro-actively and violently, since otherwise I fear the setting would lean too much in the direction of "fantasy/sci-fi nation in which authoritarian militarism is justified by being encircled with an enemy that will continuously attack and cannot be reasoned with".

                                    But, again, even with that I have argued somewhat emphatically from a position of having certain biases in values and artistic preferences.

So when I hear "we're basically giving Sidereals what they got in 1E, and not adding anything new", I reflexively cringe a little, because...well, hewing too closely to Sidereals 1E is kind of how we got Sidereals 2E, you know?                                             * ...all of the other classic Exalts have changed and grown and expanded in 3E so far. Dragon-Blooded got to use Charms of all elements with all abilities. Solars got to speak prophecy and learn the ways of spirits, as well as a whole new category of coolness in the form of Evocations and martial arts Mastery. Lunars we've been over in this thread. Compared to all of that, it's a little hard to swallow that 1E Sidereals were conceived so perfectly that nothing could be added or improved. Especially since...

                                            * ...3E, as a play environment, emphasizes the places where Sidereals are weak a lot more than previous editions did. The low-Essence period of the game gets a lot more focus, and Essence increases substantially slower; since Sidereal Excellencies are based on Essence, that's not great for them. Add that to the fact that they haven't traditionally had a lot of Charm choice per Ability, and that slow Essence gain would limit that even more, and it seems kind of frustrating to play in a mixed party, for a larger chunk of the game than it would have been previously. (As I mentioned above, playing 2E Sidereals was frustrating for quite different reasons.)

                                            All that said...well, look. I'm just a guy who's maybe too invested in some of my favorite characters. You guys are the devs who've been doing, as I said, superlative work on the line. Really all I'm hoping for is to be blown away in 2020 when the Sidereal book comes out, to be able to say "holy shit, I never expected THAT to be something they could do! That's so cool!" like I've done for all the other splats so far. And even if I fret, I do have faith that I'll get that, in some form.
