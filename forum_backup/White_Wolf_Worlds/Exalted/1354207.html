    Thoughts on why science is stagnant in Creation

    12-10-2019, 01:52 PM
    The state of technology in Creation is fascinating. It's been over 700 years since the last major catastrophe, a catastrophe that didn't send human understanding of the world back to the Stone Age. It's a world with thousands of people with super-human capacity to invent and innovate - a Terrestrial Exalt with just a Lore and Craft Excellency is capable of feats of thought and craftsmanship so far beyond Einstein and Da Vinci as to make them look like college frat boys. Yet it's a world that hasn't really made it out of the Iron Age. Considering that the Black Plague killed ~60% of Europe's population back in the 1350s and today we've been to the Moon and are working on traveling to Mars, what's holding back Creation? Creation even has advantages that our real world doesn't have, like a longer growing season and more landmass to support large populations.

    A thought came to me while I was considering the futility of arguments for or against the existence of God: the scientific method doesn't work properly in Creation. Here in our world, we can reliably perform experiments. When we burn hydrogen and oxygen, we always get water. If we drop something, it always falls towards the center of mass. So on and so forth. We can design experiments that allow us to hold the majority of things constant and only adjust our experimental variables.

    In Creation, however, the world is animistic and it's difficult to control enough variables. You are doing Mendel Gregor's pea plant experiments, just about to work out dominant and recessive genes, and then along comes the god of pea plants, who likes you because of how many pea plants you've been growing, and then "blam" suddenly your short pea plant strain now produces children that are taller than your tall strain. Or you are trying to work out the equations for gravity, but the Loom of Fate chokes on a demon's influence in the next province over and so today feathers are falling slower than they should. The best you can get with a simple experiment is a "most of the time, this seems to be the result". That's fine for most things, but the more complicated the thing you are trying to get to work, the more you are piling uncertainty upon uncertainty until the result is a complete roll of the dice.

    Perhaps the easiest way to grasp what this means in practical terms is that in Creation, anything you hear could be true. If the lady next door shows you a gold nugget and told you she pulled it out of her oven instead of the cornbread she had been trying to make, that could actually be the case. Baking another batch of cornbread in the exact same oven and getting a loaf of cornbread wouldn't disprove her claim. Her experience could be one of those one-offs, where a god decided to influence things or a sorcerous working went sideways somewhere or the pattern spiders decided her fate needed to change.

    Figured I'd share this and see what other people thought about it.

              I think that sounds roughly correct Kyuendo. I tend to think that "science" is fine, in the sense of "The scientific method still makes logical sense in this world, and they've thought of it", and the problem is more that they don't have many scientific instruments that can actually work on Essence, and replication is much harder (largely because of much larger variability in underlying laws and conditions, like you suggest), so the scope for discovering usable and true scientific objectivity / "laws" that can generate technological innovation is limited. Combined with not for some reason due to the construction of their world not having as much scope in using fossil fuels and electricity for energy in ways that allow them to avoid the issues that Essence has (some Exalt might be able to magic up a framework where they do, but that would probably be a local exception to rules that worked as long as the Essence-craft held out).

              Their actual ability to conduct science within this scope is fine, they just can't really do energy & computing industrial revolutions with it like we can, and in practice depend more on experience based learning by extraordinary individuals than they do on scientific method.

                      From what I've skimmed of The Realm, it sounds like there's a fairly good understanding of technology at the heart of the empire-- there are mentions of printing, telescopes, aqueducts, clockwork, and so on. It generally sounds a lot more Renaissance than bronze age (apart from gunpowder). I don't the the question is a matter of science so much as it is... everything else, really. It's the Great Divergence-- why did western Europe push forward into colonialism and the industrial revolution, while similarly "advanced" societies in China, the Middle East, and elsewhere did not?

                      And that... isn't really a question that's possible to answer. Looking at Wikipedia, there are theories involving everything from capitalism to geography to the random luck of "happened to have population centers near coal deposits." But looking at some of them...
                      • You couldn't really have colonization in the same way as we had in the real world. The Threshold has always been there, there's no sudden shock of new wealth from the "discovery" of the Americas.
                      • The Realm has been a single stable entity for eight hundred years. No bloody religious schisms, no inter-kingdom ways, no competition, no pressure.
                      • The Immaculate Order is pretty well entrenched, and was pretty much built from the ground up to keep things the same.
                      • Creation is huge. If you need more food, you can just plant more fields. There's less pressure to squeeze more out of a limited supply.

                      And if all else fails, I guess blame the Sidereals?

                                  To go on a long tangent on this a little, there are apparently a bunch of complications to working this out:

                                  1) China has a bunch of imperial census going back over a thousand years and those tend to provide similar and sometimes lower totals than estimates of European population, while modern estimates can provide much higher numbers... but it's difficult to seriously take some of these that estimate the imperial Chinese government was low counting but up a degree of up to 50-100% (the bureaucracy had it's limitations, but counting every one in two or two in three taxable heads?). Though modern estimates, even if they are overestimates relative to the census, still often have China only 20% up in total population compared to estimates for Europe (as a whole) for most of the series barring some exceptional periods like an apparent Song population boom (where the peak of the Southern Song could be something like twice as much).

                                  2) For India there apparently aren't anything like the above, and more wild estimates until late Victorian censuses that give around 245 million in 1872. But again that's not much higher than Europe of the time and possibly lower.

                                  I wouldn't really endorse the whole theory of "Two heads are better than one" arguments where innovation across history is simply explained by population size, market size and social network size, but it is worth stating that the modern situation where China and India each both have something like twice the entire population of Europe (and elsewhere in Asia another amount equal again to Europe) is probably something very much of a historical aberration. Compared to one where each region was standing on more of an equal population size with Europe (while within each region the centre of population growth probably moves from more developed parts to less developed parts, e.g. population growth in Europe disproportionately centered on less sophisticated Northern and Eastern Europe compared to more sophisticated Italy, in China the South rather than the North, etc). It's very much to do with the divergences of the last two centuries. (This kind of means that some of the arguments that China and India were often un-innovative relative to their population size can easily be exaggerations. While a lot of innovation centered in Europe probably is explained by that being where quite a bit of global population was, particularly bits of the global population that had best access to innovations going back to the earliest dawn of civilization).

                                          For a more elaborate answer:

                                          First off, I don't buy into the idea that any of the Exalted are meaningfully more intelligent than anybody who has ever existed (helped by the fact that the game has never really portrayed anybody as being such). There are several reasons for that inclination on my part, among which are questions it raises such as "how would you even portray that" and "doesn't that sound exhausting". Never mind that comparisons to Einstein are difficult when general relativity is not an observation that can be made in a universe that does not have planet or a vast expanse of vacuum in which they orbit stars in which to make queries about Newtonian motion or gravitation. Or that, while Leonardo of Vinci did stand out a bit in being somebody who independently possessed and pursued a notion that mechanical principles could be applied towards developing more sophisticated vehicles even to the point of flying, none of the designs he sketched are in the least bit practical, so in some respects it's not exactly hard to show him up. You need a bit more than an Excellency to make an effective flying machine, and the fact that it will work owes a tad less to you being much smarter than it does to the fact that there exists magic to facilitate the process.

                                          Second, I'm of a mind to assume that Creation contains savants (Exalted and mortal alike) who are much like real life people making technical and intellectual queries prior to the 19th century, i.e. individuals of means who can dedicate a lot of their time to making rigorous observations in a context where those don't exactly contribute to any grand transformation of the world (although they might contribute to a number of smaller transformation that are felt by the people benefitting from them while the structure of society remains the same). I can imagine that somebody in Creation who has dredged up and made functional the proper diagnostic tools is in a position to find the local equivalent of the Higgs Boson. What is done with that information? I imagine that the intellectual who found it regards it as very spiritually elevating, but for the moment that's about it.

                                          Having given more of a look, I'll give props to referring to the fact that farmers and blacksmiths have actually possessed and implemented fairly sophisticated information about aspects of the world, but I'll point out that these things were never found under circumstances resembling a modern technical rigour or systemic process of iterative development. It's a collection of ad hoc problem solving and improvisation, often systemized in the form of things like aphorisms or a kind of mystical terminology (frequently by people who were illiterate) and transmitted on a one to one basis. There are numerous flaws in such a circumstance, such as it having a very spotty capacity to innovate (although in fairness, innovations once discovered can then spread like wildfire as people integrate what they know works better), and being somewhat vulnerable to large knowledge gaps should the people teaching it die off. That said, why would there be an assumption that steel processing in Creation has not had periods of improvement over the centuries? For that matter, why not assume that at the time before the Contagion, they were widely employing methods reliant on still functional machinery that meant there was a lack of people learned or experienced in how to do that stuff by hand. If the real life apocalypse comes and all the smelting facilities are rendered non-functional, do you know where to find a blacksmith to provide services to you? I hope that person doesn't die off before things stabilize.

                                          I wonder if the fact that they've only recently developed the means to get ships on a commercial scale rather than the personal vessels of adventuring Exalts implies any developments in ship construction and navigation techniques and technology.

                                          Still, the main thing about all of this is commercial. Scientific inquiries exploded when social models developed and there was a monetary incentive towards providing for people making these inquiries and having a means to put them to profitable use. I'd say much of Creation lacks those models, or that stability. The Realm is not intellectually static, but it's still governed by a powerfully entrenched and conservative aristocracy. Much of the rest of the world is confined to smaller and rigidly controlled polities that don't afford much flexibility, or disrupted by various kinds of devils. Life can often be taken up by pursuits that leave little time for much investigation or development. The Guild might have potential to foster innovations, but I think that for the most part their society is currently inclined towards innovations in the form of finding ways to effectively exploit currently existing resources rather than much development of something new.

                                          There's a quality to the degree to which the world is dependent on engaging with the mythic and mystical in there as well sure, although I think I've been losing some of my taste for that lately. I'm at least inclined to not argue so heavily from that position. For now I'll point to all of those other driving factors.
