                I would basically shy away from using actual numbers because I'm terrible at them and any time I nail a population number down someone else who's better at them than me seizes on it and goes "Aha, if that's true, then THIS IMPLICATION OF THAT must also be true" and then goes on to treat that (unintended) implication as gospel, but here:

                Assume in sparsely-populated "tribal" areas where people tend to gather in groups of, oh, 150, there's like one thaumaturge every ten villages or something, with a new one usually coming along in time for the previous one to take as an apprentice. That is, any given population probably has access to one, but they're sparse enough that using that access may be a pain (unless you happen to be like the one village right next to the witch's hut). This probably means two thaumaturgist every 1,500 people in sparsely populated areas. As population density increases, the natural incidence of thaumaturgists decreases, purely because it's dumb for cities to have as many as that figure suggests. This is also probably inobvious or works on inobvious principles such that it is not possible for e.g. the Realm or Lookshy to deliberately set up a low-population area to "farm" for thaumaturgists to induct into some larger program like sohei-exorcists.

                This is going to hold up about as well to rigorous analysis as vampire population figures in Vampire: The Masquerade or Vampire: The Requiem, i.e. hardly at all, and I don't care. If anyone wants to take a shot at a better set of numbers that achieve the same effect, go wild.

                                            02-05-2016, 02:23 PM
                                            It's definitely a change. 2e's thaumaturgy took a lot of cues from Glorantha, which is a mythic fantasy setting takes the position "In a magical setting, it would be cool if everyone could learn some small magics." And so you get e.g. nomadic herdsmen where the trick of creating fire with neither flint nor tinder is common and basically everyone in the world knows an effective ritual against misfortune or something. Glorantha is also a place where most people know the fundamentals of how magic and the world work.

                                            3e very deliberately departs from that entirely. 3e Creation is a place where magic, though common, is much more often used to reinforce divisions of privilege and fortune than alleviate them, and superstition and misconception about how the world works is everywhere, leading to a ton of varying cultures all based on different worldviews. Varangian astrology, for example, is a bunch of oppressive nonsense idiosyncratic to Varang, that the Varangian people nevertheless invest a great deal of meaning into. It's not some special insight into the real astrology of the Five Maisens that's been applied culture-wide.

                                            See, as always, Tales of the Flat Earth for a fantasy setting where magic is real but weird and misunderstood.

                                            If you prefer a more Glorantha-like setup, 2e thaumaturgy is there for you to adapt.
