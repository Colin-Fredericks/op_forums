    As a small example for your consideration: in Heirs to the Shogunate, there's a Dragon-Blooded Presence Charm called Heart-Conquering Prowess, which, along with giving an App-based attack roll bonus, lets you drop a level of damage from a decisive attack to count it as an instill roll to change the target's Intimacies towards you in a positive direction, and, relevantly here, ensures that an incapacitated target won't die.

    Now, that's a backer Charm, which some might argue fall under a different design philosophy than the rest of the system, since even when they aren't Apocryphal, there's an obvious incentive pulling the designers away from what they might otherwise feel should fall within the powersets and theme of the game. Still, I like that Charm, and I think it's got interesting implications for the question. First, while there might be other ways to reduce the risk of an unwanted kill, ensuring it doesn't happen seems magical, though little enough so to not be the entire Charm effect. It also lends credence to the notion of giving up damage to represent holding back, albeit a lot more efficiently than I'd wager a mortal hero could do without magic.

    For doing without magic, I'd probably want scaling penalties the more assurance you want against an accidental kill, something like sacrificing accuracy dice per lethal damage level you want to reduce to bashing or avoid applying; it's easier to go for incapacitation against someone you seriously outclass, whereas someone you can't afford to hold back against is, well, exactly what it says on the tin - if you're hesitating/wasting openings to avoid killing them, they capitalize and you die.

    If you feel that interacts poorly with the idea that a huge Initiative lead represents being in control of the fight, I'd invoke the section on initiative crash in the core rules, page 193-194, that says a character so deep in on an Initiative disadvantage that they're basically a free initiative tap for the PCs with no chance of recovery should just be written out of the fight. Basically, the PCs are so overwhelmingly in control, with no further advantage to gain over someone, that they just end the fight by fiat; at that point, I'd rule in the player's favor for how they want the enemy to go down, with an eye towards extenuating circumstances (if you wanna capture an enemy surrounded by allies, you're not gonna be able to just fiat that part).

                I think Medicine as a basis for a "applies to whatever Ability, makes it not fatal" custom Charm makes a lot of sense, combining skill with anatomical expertise. As with the example of Heart-Conquering Prowess (which costs 3m and has a single prereq), even if it's quite cheap that could probably afford to be a rider effect on a Charm that also helps you accomplish some other goal. Maybe to inflict some (temporary?) penalty through exploiting your precise anatomical knowledge, hitting them somewhere that will slow them down extra if it doesn't take them out, but also being sure not to kill if it does. That's just spitballing, though.

                Custom Charms aside, though, I will circle back around to Gambits as being probably the best way to do this, context-dependent. For an honorable swordsman, disarming them will probably be enough for them to accept the loss; for someone trying to fight to the death no matter what, grapple them down and do submission holds til they pass out; for a kung fu master, pull out your bludgeoning weapons and use custom Gambits or Charms to break their legs. That's the most direct way to translate having a bunch of extra initiative into successfully disabling an enemy without splattering them.

                        I guess for completeness, I should mention that Wisdom of the Celestial Crane also has a 100% non-fatality rate, but that's an Essence 4 capstone MA Charm. >_>

                        It occurred to me that a lot of fictional duels end with a sword at someone's throat, without anyone necessarily being seriously injured along the way. But it seems like the closest Exalted 3e equivalent to that is the "hold at bay" rules (corebook p. 204). I considered the possibility of a gambit where you attempt to put someone in that position. But, of course, being held at bay kinda sucks if the victim gets stabbed anyway, so the victim would need the opportunity to decline and just take a normal hit (which represents them refusing to stop fighting even though there's a sword at their throat, and also makes it pointless to attempt if you don't have enough Initiative for a credible chance at killing them). But, of course, that raises the question of what happens if you're not willing to actually follow through and stab them (it's probably a bit late for them to do a read intentions roll at this point). It feels very difficult to make a good, clear general rule for this idea.

                                    Depends on the experience and focus of the Dragon-Blood, and on exactly what you mean by "awfully difficult."

                                    For what the book guidelines might call the average case of a talented, well-educated DB crafter (Attribute 3, Craft 3 and a specialty, an Excellency), 2-dot artifacts are a baleful mountain they might hope to summit with constant willpower exertion and great stunts and an equipment bonus from their rich family's special workshop, never mind 3.

                                    If you buy Craft up to 5 to get access to Flawless Facet Realization and Strike the Dragon-Anvil, a starting character who's willing to pay those Charm costs can make 2-dots really consistently and view 3-dots as mountains.

                                    Go up to E3 and take one of those dice-improving, Simple Signature Charms. I think Blazing Dragon-Smith Arete is the best one, though Imago-Hatching Realization is swingy in a way I'm not sure how to quantify with the player choice aspect of deciding whether a given roll got enough failures to spend your one reroll on. 3-dots are relatively consistent for this group; the mountain of the 4-dot is distant indeed, but not unbridgeable.

                                    E5 (not applicable in most games) with Blazing Dragon-Smith Arete and a repurchase of Strike the Dragon-Anvil makes 4-dots with some consistency and 5-dots are plausible with that mountain-climber attitude.

                                    So... It's not easy being the crafter of a 3-dot artifact, that's for sure, but far from impossible or egregiously demanding in a build resource sense (never mind the in-universe life sense of never leaving your workshop because you're an obsessive savant of your craft and being hounded by prospective customers all the time). And as a baseline it feels okay to me, I think 3-dot artifacts are worth being the magnum opus that singlehandedly make their otherwise-merely-great forger a legend, something that even most Exalted craftsmen in the world will never do, and 4/5-dots can be magnum opuses for player characters or powerful DB NPCs who treat crafting as a primary focus.

                                                            Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                                                            Really, a statement like this crystallizes to me a sense that it's a mistake to have ever presented 5 dots in an Ability as denoting the GOAT as opposed to something like somebody deeply committed to understanding and application of it.
                                                            On the one hand: I hear where you're coming from, and I do take some of those guidelines with a grain of salt and context. What it means to be at a "professional level" for the general population of Creation isn't the same as what we would think of as being a professional in a context with strong educational institutions and the economic freedom to heavily specialize, i.e. Athletics 2 covering the vast swaths of manual laborers in the setting rather than a professional athlete, or Craft (Architecture) 2 representing the people who build everyday housing in towns and villages rather than Heptagram-trained manse architects.

                                                            On the other hand: the whole scale is five dots long. Whatever interpretation you decide to give "five dots and a specialty" vis-a-vis how exceptional it is in prevalence and repute within the setting, it has to fit with the fact that at the scale of mechanical resolution, there's no room for improvement. Whether or not it's exclusive to the world's most legendary savants, it certainly must include them - there's nowhere else for them to go.

                                                            As a matter of personal preference, I think it fits really well with the rest of 3e's ethos. I grumped at it when I first started playing 3e, the same way I grumped at the notion that most Exalted NPCs won't hit Essence 3 for decades or ever go beyond that, because I came in with a notion that Exaltation is already maximally protagonizing and that PCs are only any more special than any other Exalt in the narrative sense that the current campaign is about them. But 3e's done a good enough job otherwise with not treating the mechanics as the setting and giving people below the pinnacle a lot of cool stuff to do that I've come around to thinking it benefits from not correlating a serious interest/devotion to a skill with maximizing one's rating in it. The fact that even most serious, devoted Dragon-Blooded craftsmen with careers worthy of their Great Houses bragging about them can't do artifacts both emphasizes the great value and need for all that "mundane" crafting work, and rarefies (by... rare-ifying) both artifacts and the people capable of making them.
