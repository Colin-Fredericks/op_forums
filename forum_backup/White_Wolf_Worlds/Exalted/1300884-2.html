            Now it’s a description of the Silver Way. This is actually mostly familiar in component concepts from the portrayal in Second Edition, but the framing is a bit different; where in the Manual it was depicted as relatively recent and kind of a shaky code on account of being formed by committee, here it’s framed as a common set of ethics to enable cooperation between one another (particularly in the form of establishing rank and precedence) and preserve their higher consciousness against the intrinsic call to monstrosity. In that, it’s attributed to Luna as a sacred gift to maintain their reasoning. It’s described as especially important to young packs, as a value system to preserve them against the Threshold’s temptations and allow them to effectively function as a unit. It has a point about them not really preaching it from a perspective that outsiders are neither worthy or capable, with a point given to civilized folk not really being considered people anyway; I’ll again say how I don’t really mind that in this framing, since it’s not really an ethos any more terrible than what the Realm has.

            This is something where I really want to compare the two books because, on the face of it, this was one of the few things transmitted for Lunars across Editions. So the most outstanding thing that I see between them is that the First version has a lot more brevity to it, because the Second one is so anxious about justifying and elaborating each of the tenets. Where the First version keeps things fairly simple and direct, and to me effectively connects each of the details to their backstory or contemporary circumstances, the ones in Second Edition have to overload it with all kinds of caveats and conditions; in my experience, it made the latter ones extremely tedious to read. Without going into detail about each of them, I find the First Edition ones to be compelling in their details, even in cases where they’re not exactly sanitised (such as the element of “Defend what is yours” that has some aggressively attacking any neighbours to their territories for the sake of keeping their homes safe), and in some ways paints a picture of Lunars that can be more reasonable and reliable than even the Second Edition one did. As a code for guiding their values and actions in the circumstances common to them, I think it’s perfectly fine.

            Now there’s a section on other common customs. It starts with a statement on them being very diverse, owing to the range of social and geographic environments afforded by their shapeshifting (including the option to live among animals); with the stuff before and after this about customs, I wouldn’t entirely buy this on its own, but if I think in terms of the prior chapter and slot Lunars among the various societies presented, I guess I could let it slide. Conversely, for the fact that it’s about to state various commonalities despite being so far spread, I think it helps for it to be framed as standards and principles that their society has retained despite exile, because that puts it in terms of something they wilfully promote in the face of adversity.

            Elaboration on territory is the first one (and as I said earlier, I had skipped ahead a bit to check it); it’s attributed primarily to elder Lunars who have become very habitual and protective of what is theirs, sharpened a bit by mystical instincts. It describes methods that a Lunar might use to mark the boundaries of such a claim that are especially recognisable to other Lunars (such as claw-mark carvings or special scent markers), the common practice for respectfully asking permission to enter a territory by way of making loud calls back and forth, and reiterates the point of it being more of an age issue (with young packs framed as perfectly capable of co-ownership).

            There’s a description for gatherings, which I take to be both an expansion upon and slight distinction from the previously given Silver Pact meetings. I’m a bit torn on whether or not it’s redundant to have both; it makes a measure of sense for how the Silver Pact isn’t all-pervasive, but that doesn’t necessarily make it good book composition. Still, for itself it’s a decent description of a form of ritualised meet-and-greet and opportunity to have dealings, make exchanges, or generally socialise. It also includes a plot hook of upcoming annual large meetings scheduled per half-century, that would be concerned with matters of the Time of Tumult; it sounds like the kind of thing where a lot of previously established politicking about who Lunars should fight and how they should gather their strength could come to a head.

            Now there’s Blood-Debts, and I’m saying how Second Edition did consolidate some of these things into the Silver Way principles; I can understand the intent, but it doesn’t change how much I found those parts to be a tedious read back in the day. In any case, blood-debts here are a bit distinct from the Way tenet of repaying debts owed, given how it’s less about exchanges and more about conflict resolution, settling disputes by having a fight to submission or unconsciousness (the description of which implies to me that such fights are rough and unpolished by convention), after which the loser is customarily expected to perform a task in payment for transgression. It’s got some language that I’m a bit torn about; on the one hand, it attributes Lunars with instinctual preferences for settling disputes by fighting, which I’m a bit iffy on (even if the tone is consistent), but on the other it at least says that they regard fighting one another to the death as sacrilegious in a manner that compels curbing those impulses. Where blood-debt fights appear rougher and concerned with disputes over tangible things, ritual duels are seemingly reserved for more abstract conflicts, and are given as more formally structured conflicts, that are intended to be more public and take forms with more exacting stipulations (as chosen by the one who was challenged); they don’t appear to come with any formal prize like a blood-debt, more of an opportunity for prestige and claiming that one’s own position or principle is favoured by Luna.

            Now there’s a bit of an odd one to frame as a custom, the subject of the somewhat unfortunately named “mating”. I want to give it the benefit of the doubt as being intended to put the spotlight on these being more powerful, potentially long-lived Exalted than the Dragon Blooded, who are out among people of the world in a way that Sidereals aren’t, and don’t have stipulations on who they connect with as Dynasts do (as well as conveying the game’s overall awareness of sexuality and romance as drivers of history and mythology), but I think some of the phrasing could have been tidied up a bit. As it is, it’s a bit too… focused on most connections being unsentimental and driven by whim. It does have a paragraph that I like concerning more affectionate relationships, such as maintaining loyalty to a spouse(s) that is beloved for the length of their mortal lifetime, that old relationships with Solars had a love-hate thing going on that new Solars might have to deal with, and how being attracted to a sufficiently compelling Threshold denizen might make for especially tumultuous relations. It also gives word to the subject of non-consensual encounters; it at least wants to set out that most Lunars wouldn’t do that, although I think some of the premises given behind why are not the best. Like, when it’s saying that it’s part of a personal moral code, or seen as adhering to the tenets of the Silver Way, or something that can contribute to loss of reason in proximity to the Wyld… I don’t think it was the intent, but it does kind of read as a phrasing in which lack of concern for consent is the default, and characters need to have surrounding moral considerations to go out of their way to not do it. There’s also a line to the effect of “they can be so alluring that force ought to be unnecessary”, which is… significantly misunderstanding the motives underlying rape (plenty of people do it to others who would have had consensual relations, because the sex is not the point). It ends with a point about how some do it anyway, and while individual Lunars might try to take one to task for it, it’s not regarded as a deal-breaker in society as a whole; I can unfortunately at least buy that as an element of culture.

            Might as well skip ahead a little bit to look at this here: a long time ago, I had read Lea describing how this book had two sidebars concerned with the subject of sexual violence as an extrapolation of barbarian raiding stuff, and he laid out very distinct ways that they approached the matter. I’ve already seen one, short, direct, not lingering on the matter, and advising Storytellers to approach the matter with caution and consideration. This is the second one, and… I can’t exactly fault the content of it. For one thing, it’s making the point of how rape is still a distressingly common violence in reality, such that it’s possible that people around a gaming table might have some direct or indirect experience with it; all of the advice surrounding that point are made with an eye concerned with nobody in a game being distressed or triggered. It tries to place the advice into context of both the literary genre that Lunars are being inspired by, and historical realities akin to the setting as a whole. Even when it suggests a “realistic” approach, it advises being carefully restrained with how much detail is gone into. It also offers the alternative of leaning into the dynamics offered by the distribution of powerful figures in the setting to regard sexual violence as a much more infrequent occurrence, including with regards to use in warfare. It ends with a note of how these things ought to be found out between a group long before they’re brought up in play, because it isn’t a fictional issue the way that things with dark magic or large monsters would be.

            I can see how one might question why the topic might be brought up in the first place, but I think I can get how there would have been writers who would have felt remiss about these subjects concerned with endemic warfare and very powerful figures situated among normal people and danced around the subject. My own ethos is one that finds that not talking about it at all is not particularly preferable, and while it might be a little heavy handed (and redundant to have two sidebars placed at different points) I really do find that it’s made with the best of intentions. Honestly, I find it a bit ahead of the curve for the time in which it was written; I can imagine how people in the moment might have been struck out of left field by it, but considering the discourse that has developed in the last couple of years, I feel as though the culture of today would receive such inclusions very differently.

            To step back again, last on the topic is the subject of where beastmen come from; namely the union between Lunar in animal form and human partner (or vice versa), while in the presence of energies of the Wyld. It gives some of the logistical considerations for it, such as the limitations of certain animals being able to bear humanoid offspring, and how Lunars carrying children are free to shift form, but not sex, as well as some of the premises for how such offspring propagate. For the idea as it is at the time, it’s fine; I think it covers all the important bases, and it’s not a concept I’m against in principle.

            Moving on from all of that is the subject of raiding, which… looks less like a Lunar custom and more like something that should have had its place in the first chapter. It’s a basic overview of the seasonal considerations for raiding and general warfare, as well as the cultural and practical fruits of doing so; how they’re often living with scarcity, or could do with acquiring people with specialist skills. That includes a note on how those peoples aren’t as likely to value gold as a means of exchange more than as something decorative and displaying prestige. The significance to Lunars comes towards the end; how some (especially Full Moons) find it a good opportunity to face worthy opponents to prove their strength to themselves, how it can be a good means of testing enemy procedures and defences (as well as taking informative hostages, who can occasionally be converted; there’s an amusing note on how sometimes the children or lovers of Dynasts are kidnapped, only to next be seen fighting at the Lunar’s side), and how it is cathartic for Lunars to get another hit in on the Realm that took so much from them.

            Following that is a section on spirituality. After everything set up so far, I would have expected this to be a bit more focused on the meaning and expression of their connection to Luna, but although that’s referenced a bit, it’s more about common trends in how Lunar religious sensibilities evolve following their Exaltation. Most are said to incorporate Luna and associated understandings of the spirit world into pre-existing belief systems (particularly placing the moon goddess at the top of the pyramid), although adherents brought into the fold who had previously subscribed to Immaculate beliefs are liable to have those countermanded with a bit of harshness verging on violence and the persuasive arguments of No Moons; interestingly, it doesn’t say outright that no Lunar would hold Immaculate beliefs, just that the most exceptionally rare and stubborn would retain them under those conditions (although considering the criteria given for Lunars, such a person would not exactly be out of place). The main exception given is for ancestor worship, here characterized not just as ghosts but any kind of reverence for a powerful and storied progenitor, who may or may not have literally existed; it’s said that in a lot of cases, Lunars lose such reverence, as they themselves are basically elevated to that same status and role (with a note on how some surrounding people might regard the Lunar as a personification of the honoured ancestor). Last are First Age Lunars, referred to as sufficiently powerful and jaded to view powerful spirits as peers rather than objects of worship, limiting their reverence for Luna (if even her, as it’s pointed out that she’s a kind of trickster or warrior goddess who doesn’t necessarily need ritual devotion). It’s not quite what I would have looked for, but I think it’s another valid piece of advice for the topic of these Celestial Exalted having fitted into the world.
            That’s followed by describing the specific devotion entailed by the ritual hunt; it’s not a thing tied directly to what they do for form acquisition, just a form of self-expression and thanksgiving to Luna engaged in by basically all Lunars. There’s a breakdown of the chosen timing, the traditional frequency, the preference for not employing weapons (which not all adhere to, regarded as nigh-heretical by the more conservative) and other ritual elements, and ways in which it might be further personalized (often by self-imposing extra challenges or limits). It’s also said to be one of the few things that a territorial elder can let intrusions slide for, although they’ve got to be convinced that the other Lunar wasn’t just using it as an opportunity for spying (said to be a thing that has actually happened). It’s a little uniform, but it fits the image they’re going for, and fulfils my interest in them having a rite specific to their adoration for Luna.

            There are a couple of mystical connections given for them. The Unfettering is presented as a characteristic form of meditation for improved mote respiration in a demesne or manse; they mark a circle around themselves, bow to east and west, and then settle down into a state of continuous subtle undulations of form, described as letting them assume a state on the path between reason and instinct, momentarily liberated from both. It has no greater mechanical benefit than the usual for being in the most relaxed state, but is said to be the most soothing for Lunars in character; it’s a touch that I like, for incorporating the particulars of their nature into everyday management of themselves. It then goes to the subject of working moonsilver; reiterating the significance that the metal holds for them, how they’ve retained some of the sacred forges in which to do so, and the ritualistic manner in which it’s forged (has to be gathered and purified without man-made tools, only worked at night, and quenched in cold water that has never been touched by any light besides that of the moon). While it’s said that such methods aren’t strictly necessary for working moonsilver, a suggestion is given that Storytellers could rule that items made without it wouldn’t be truly realized enough to provide attunement bonuses.

            Last in these sections concerned with Lunar customs is a note on the subject of manses; while a lot of Lunars are said to have demesnes, it’s stated to be difficult in most cases to cap with manses, owing to how several are in places that were unsettled even in the First Age (and have otherwise decayed or been wilfully destroyed), and exerting the effort and resources to build a new one is described as challenging and often a low priority. Sorcery is described as the most common method for raising manses, although it’s hard to get sufficiently powerful conjurers into one’s debt for the purpose. Otherwise, Lunars just need to be patient about it, building a manse gradually over a long time whenever circumstances afford them the freedom.

            I’ll break it here with all of this customary stuff, and see if I can’t finish the second chapter a bit later on.

            I have approximate knowledge of many things.

In the spirit of interrogating conventional wisdom concerning the book, I'm starting to wonder if that point, to the extent that it's true, has any greater significance than beta components such as the Terrestrials coming first or ideas for all of the types of Exalted to just be different skins.

                                                            It makes me wonder if somebody took an original statement about an early iteration of the Lunars, framed it out of context, mixed it in with an initial hostile reaction to its source book, and then became echoed over following years into a go to narrative for why Lunars "don't have a place in the setting", even though that's not exactly true.
