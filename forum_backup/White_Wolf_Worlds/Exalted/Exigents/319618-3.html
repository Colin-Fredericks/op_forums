    Plus of course, it depends a lot on how much your character is built around combat. A combat-based DB is clearly going to beat up a Twilight crafter with no combat skills.

    While I'm happy for them to not think in terms of "tiers" where non-Solars can never have charms as good as a Solars, I think they'll have to be very careful with Exigents. No one's going to enjoy being the ST who suddenly finds everyone wants to play a super-optimised Exigent who's totally balanced because, like, he's slightly better at combat than a Solar but he's utter rubbish at cooking. Sure, you can blame the players, but that doesn't help.
    Game balance after all, is one of the two main reasons you buy mechanics books rather than just make up all your own charms for free (the other being inspiration). It's pretty difficult for an ST to judge the balance of every charm or effect a player wants for their fancy new Exigent, and once you allow it it's difficult to later take it off them if it turns out to be unbalanced. So I hope there's a lot of guidance in the book.

    STing Bronze Age Exalted

                To add to the Exigent of Healing, a comment from hatewheel on the rpg.net forums,

                (in response to “Can I make an Exigent Chosen of Loss, or would that concept be better served by Abyssals?”)

                Originally posted by hatewheel
                You can do that, but it behooves you to pay attention to the character of the god when making the Exigent. The best way to do an Exigent is to start with a Terrestrial or Celestial god and work from their purview and themes. If the god doesn't exist you should invent one and fold it into the Divine Bureaucracy.

                Siakal's Chosen is invested in Sail and War and empowered by bloodshed. But she can also swim to ridiculous depths, breathe underwater, and bite through steel with a mouthful of fangs. We feel like the added touches make her more flavorful and interesting than just giving her the powers of an indefinite god of war.
                Siakal's Chosen would, I think, be limited in expressing war in ways that a non-descript god of war wasn't, but at the same time would have totemic and metaphorical shark powers and mastery of her domain (and that's a brief and simplified way to describe her style) in ways that generic god of war wouldn't, so it would be a trade off.

                Re; breadth, I don't think many flavourful Exigents will be as broad as Solars as a splat, but many will be as broad as the Solars people actually want to play, who are archetypes of a particular kind of person.

                In general, I hope the idea with Exigents is not to push them along a rail where they have a “tiny living space” in the way of the 2e Infernals, unless that emerges naturally from the concept and god the player is interested in. Just whatever falls out naturally from the god plus a level of power of the Exigence that feels right for the game (probably, but not necessarily balanced against others in the group).

                Re: balance being hard to call, particularly because some advantages just aren't commensurable, that's true, and one of the good things about the Exigents is because they are one offs (or a small "The Gang") for one table, it is even more not really a reason to be timid about a particular power just in case it may end up with the Exalt being too strong, because it is only for one game.

                            I don't want to write another long post that over old ground but ultimately how high material technology and the magic which is Creation's analogy for it (in many ways) can go without the Solars is really something I hope 3e doesn't waste to much time trying to nail down in a book.

                            My impression is the idea that the question of "What is ultimately, eventually, in all possible paths, possible without the Solars?" (as opposed to what is likely without them and what has been possible so far) was not even considered important, solvable or worth addressing, through the First Edition of Exalted.

                            They were back, they were mighty, they had wrought grand works in the First Age. They could make a difference to the world, a world in danger and need. Their loss was a meaningful, hard, questionable decision. The question of what in the tumble of possible futures for Creation, the ones without Solars could look like was not answerable, or relevant.

                            With the Great Prophecy, the answers the Sidereals had were true at the time, for the value of the questions they asked, and the choices they took were hard, and now they place faith in them which is not certain - their foresight was not infinitely great and perfect. It has limits. That doesn't make them bad prophets unless you (in a way that I feel is questionable) argue for a level of foresight for good prophets which is numbing to a future which is a vast sea of the possible. Creation contained the ruins of multiple high magic civilizations which, if not the heights of the First Age, were not products for which the Solars were necessary (that of Dragon Kings for one).

                            But this is what I said I would not do and treading over old ground.
