                    Mental health is a very sensitive subject, so hope I don't make too many missteps with the following...

                    Conan isn't cursed.... but I wonder how many modern shrinks would actually pronounce him a psychologically healthy specimen, with no "inappropriately" extreme personality traits?

                    Likewise for Hercules, Elric, Qin Shi Huang, Achilles... all those standard Solar inspiration guys(another model for a Solar might include a certain Galilean fellow who talked about being the son of God).

                    I don't know if you can really go into looking at the heroes and villains of pulp, epic and history, the way Exalted intends to tell stories about them, with a modern view of sanity and insanity that places extreme passions and boundless ambition and fatalistic bouts of depression as "insanity", because then you would end up questioning the kind of protagonists that are intended, or ascribing them as "ill", whether they are actually like that due to the exaggerating effects of a curse or not. (Hope this doesn't seem like I'm getting all "Dianetics! Don't trust psychiatrists!" on you).

                    Like I say, I'm all for the Exalted as heroes, all of them, who labour under a curse, no optionality and choice on that without you breaking from the canon and deciding to defeat it or excise it from the setting in your game, because that is what they have been since the first second of day one. It *should* be a default topic that is always there and in your face unless you decide for it not to be, consciously, I think.

                    I do hope though that the mechanics Rob Vance has written up for it in 3e really intentionally allow measures of personal growth, for heroes to conquer and defeat a manifestation of it, even if they will never be clean of it, for heroes to grow spiritually in ways that allow them to put it more at bay. People will love it more if it is a challenge they can do, even if making it totally defeatable should be so obviously a thing to do that people always just do it.

                            To Solar:

                            I entirely understand your point, and your reluctance to accept the Great Curse as a thing. I entirely understand the personal reasons for it you outlined earlier in the thread. I also understand that you might at this moment feel antagonized, so let me first assure you that's not my intent before I make my point.

                            I entirely agree that the Great Curse has not been modeled well mechanically. I don't necessarily agree with you on why it is not modeled well--I suspect that were you and I presented with alternate models we would disagree quite a bit on which is more acceptable. You have stated, in a roundabout sort of way, that you don't care for the Great Curse because it models mental illness that take control of one's character away from the player. I'm sure you have other reasons too, but I'd like to use this one as a specific point.

                            I used to play D&D quite heavily, and while I still enjoy the game itself on occasion, I have found over the years that it encourages a specific style of play that I do not care for. I have made this point in other venues before: D&D presents a combat simulator with a few extraneous rules added on, and from the very beginning the game has intended to model a very specific "kick in door, kill monsters, get loot, achieve plot coupon, get XPs" style of game. It CAN be played in other ways, but the way the rules are designed and the way the books are presented, this is the game as it is clearly intended and this is the game that a new player sees when they pick up and play the game, sans experience with other games. This tends to color the experience and the actions of new players, as they tend to take up the mantle of whatever they idealize their character as, be it the superintelligent wizard of ultimate power, the brave and unflappable fighter, the shifty rogue, whatever. The point is that they idealize these characters, because the game supports it and in fact in some ways almost tells you to.

                            By the same token, other games are presented differently. Vampire: the Masquerade presents a setting full of political intrigue and the slow, inexorable slide into monstrosity, and while many players eschew focusing too heavily on that latter part, it is nonetheless presented front and center as one of the primary themes of the game. Mage: the Ascension is a game of creativity and discovery, where the primary focus is on learning and exploring a world you are just beginning to understand.

                            Exalted, on the other hand, attempts to model, via the Great Curse, a world where heroes are tragic and flawed. It forces you to accept, from the first time you build your character, that your character will, on occasion, act in ways that are counterproductive or detrimental to their goals and those of their allies. It is an attempt--an attempt that I believe did not succeed entirely, but that still nonetheless has merit--to tell the player who first picks up Exalted that your character, no matter how great, epic, and 'perfect' they may be, has flaws, and that those flaws will express themselves whether you want them to or not, because this is a game ABOUT flawed heroes and the mistakes they make, not a game about perfectly succeeding at every task one after another with no complication, even if your magic kind of supports trying to do exactly that. But that's the point of a flawed hero: no matter how powerful you are, it is your own failures that will bring you down in the end, if nothing else does.

                            I've spoken on this topic before, on the old forums. The existence of the Great Curse is important precisely BECAUSE it makes it clear that one of the core central questions of the game is how will you learn to manage those flaws, given that those who came before you fell to them?

                            I can entirely understand, as I said, why you might not personally like it. Why you, and others, may feel it is unnecessary for the game, even. But you are all experienced roleplayers, I presume, who have also played quite a bit of Exalted. You have made your decisions about whether you want to represent that flawed and tragic heroism in your games, and if so how you want to go about it. Perhaps you feel that you can do so without an external curse involved, perhaps you feel you don't want to do so at all. Both of those are valid viewpoints.

                            But the existence of the Curse, in some form or another, is important to the game itself because it makes the statement, right up front, that your heroes are not flawless, they are not perfect, even if they believe they are, and they are going to have moments where they act in ways that may even come off as scary or monstrous or weak sometimes. Whether you use the Curse or not, whether you like how it is implemented or not, I believe, personally, that having it as a part of the game, even if it is not as well mechanically represented as I might personally like, is important, because that statement is part of what forms the core of Exalted's theme, of heroes that can do great things, but that sometimes, great things can be horrible things all at the same time.
