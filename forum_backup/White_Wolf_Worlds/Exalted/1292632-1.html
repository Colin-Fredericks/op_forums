                I started out liking Abyssals b/c Solars had a lot of in-setting historical baggage attached to them that I thought would get stale really quick. Its not that the Solar fluff was bad, its just that Abyssals had this whole new avenue to work down while still being (in fluff but not in crunch) just as powerful. Like, the Solars had Limit Break and that occasionally took away something that's really important for a heroic character to have, self-mastery. Too much limit and a solar turns into a horrible caricature of themselves, but Abyssals stayed in control of themselves, though Resonance ended up being a horrible mechanic. I also liked the Neverborn and how alien they were, Yozis did this too occasionally, but the fact that they were so neurotic made them seem more like over-powerful adult-children than eldritch unknowable god-monsters. But the Neverborn stayed pretty alien throughout the fluff, and the parts of them that were humanized tended to be the parts full of pathos.

                Chivalry of Death seems really interesting, and I really hope the 3e devs do more with the concept of Oblivion, the Neverborn, and the Underworld. Like, maybe being a ghost shouldn't be portrayed as an objectively horrible unnatural tragedy when the alternative is having your identity wholly scoured away Lethe followed by reassignment into an arbitrary reincarnation unless you bribed the right god. This where 1e and 2e failed in that regard b/c IRL reincarnation doctrines tend to have reincarnation depend on your previous life actions(and both of the two most prominent ones portray escape from the cycle as the ultimate goal), whereas Creation seems to just randomly assign reincarnations(despite Immaculate Order propaganda) unless the individual in question worshipped the right god like Ahlat, who would reward their loyalty by turning them into clothing ornaments or something equally servile. But ghosts were always portrayed as being "wrong" for holding onto their identities, engaging in transhumanism, or just wanting to retain some measure of agency(all of which are things I find admirable). Like, even Oblivion warped spectres have this weird Abyss-"enlightenment" whereas Lethe is ultimately a recycling plant intended to churn out blank slate prayer batteries.

                Another thing is that 3e (I hope) seems to be making the Neverborn less overwhelmingly awful with Chivalry of Death. Their existence is one of constant pain and misery, maybe self-righteous contempt shouldn't be the right way to fight them. Though this is probably hoping for too much.

                Also, as evidenced by my username, i've a fondness for fantasy undeath.

                        Granted, I think the reason Gwyn feared and hated the Dark Soul was because it was the only Lord Soul that grew larger, and the larger it grew, the more power it drained away from the First Flame that birthed it, the weaker the First Flame grew, the weaker the other three Lord Souls became.

                        This theft might have been a trick the Furtive Pygmy deliberately wrought, given that he explicitly split the Dark Soul among all of his descendants specifically to encourage its rampant and unchecked growth. Which definitely would have angered Gwyn fiercely, such that even those humans who joined the gods in their war against the dragons were branded as slaves and given no honor for their great valor and achievements.

                        The Curse of Undeath was likely a collaborative effort with Nito, the First of the Dead, and possibly also the Witch of Izalith, both of whom would also want to keep from losing their powers. Thus, the Dark Sign is a burning ring of fire that consumes an Undead's humanity when they die, then reviving them as hollow monsters driven to kill other humans to fill themselves with a new piece of the Dark Soul.

                        And the Linking of the Fire is a ritual to feed an Undead loaded with much of the Dark Soul back into the First Flame, reducing the Dark Soul's size and returning its stolen power back into the source of all four Lord Souls.

                        But the longer time marches on, the larger the Dark Soul becomes, and the more impossible it is for any Undead to gather enough of it to return to the First Flame. Eventually, an Undead (or specifically, an Ashen One) will consume the remains of the First Flame itself, taking all of the power into the Dark Soul, extinguishing the other three Lord Souls, and ushering in the true Age of Darkness.

                        Finally free from the curse of undeath, the Dark's more peaceful nature would win out.

                            Its somewhat ambiguous whether or not Dark is dangerous inherently despite being useful(like say, fire). Interestingly, beings of Dark seem to be drawn to the fire they lack. Humans even in the Ringed City were in absolute awe of the gods(who in turn treated them as chattel and vermin), humans linked the fire, even Nashandra from Dark Souls two was obsessed with the First Flame (whether the prospect of snuffing it out or simply possessing power over it as a function of being the personification of Manus's Want). But any power, used incorrectly, can cause harm. It happened with the Witch of Izalith accidentally creating Chaos, it happened with Seath's obsession with immortality. Humans and the Dark Soul got slapped with "designated villain" quite early on by Anor Londo and the association colored nearly every interaction with the Dark from then on.

                            As for Hollow Knight, The Void is primordial darkness, equally capable of creation and destruction. Powerful, unknowable, and terrifying, but not actively malicious. Its not actively anything, it just IS and IS NOT. And because of that, you can pull all sorts of things from it: magic, shades that can animate automata, vessels for holding power, etc. At the same time, those qualities make it dangerous for anyone who isn't careful and its power can be abused. This contrasts it with the Pale King(resident Vendrick expy, who is associated with both light and dark) and the final boss(resident Gwyn expy, who is associated with searing hateful solar light):

                                The first thing from Dark Souls that I would draw upon to inform Abyssals would be... the aesthetic. It's a rich aesthetic, that can readily inform things like the Underworld itself, the look and mythology of Abyssals themselves (lot of broken remains of fallen heroes in Dark Souls), and the manner in which they might perceive the world.

                                The cosmology, not so much, it's too different. The closest thing would be the vague idea that, while the Dark is scary, there's also a certain comforting serenity to it.

                                No, the second thing that I would draw upon would be a thing that pervades the whole series but never actually serves as a direct focus and so also happens to be one of its most ambiguous elements; the nature of undeath itself.

                                By which I mean various interpretations of the origin and significance of undeath, and the tension arising from the ambiguity.

                                Why are there undead in Dark Souls? Is it because an artificially prolonged Age is basically warping the nature of souls, causing them to cling to living vessels past the point where any meaning or purpose or vitality is retained? Is it a consequence of the Age coming to an end diminishing souls to the point where they're imbalanced and the remaining dark force in them becomes a mindless and insatiable hunger? Is it the true nature and legacy of humanity revealing itself as the power of divinity fades away? Is it just people becoming contaminated by the general wrongness of the world to give them a degenerative condition that is threatening to those around them? Is it just that the person really did kind of die, and something messed up keeps the body going until it forgets all semblance of its life?

                                Who knows? Never firmly examined, and different characters in the setting have different opinions about it, not all of whom are entirely forthcoming. The third game introduced the intriguing notion of a religion saying that being not merely undead but actually Hollow (i.e. the final stage where basically all human semblance has disappeared) is actually empowering, and they want a new age in which the cosmological Fire is still around, but the undead have the reins of it.

                                In Exalted terms, I wouldn't be thinking about zombies specifically, but I think it's a source of idea and an underlying tension from which various Abyssals could draw.

                                So things like a question of whether or not undeath is a superior state to life, or at least will be by the time that you're done with it. Or the process of dying and state of being dead having an aesthetic or philosophical value that is unique to it, and they're going to be the advocates and champions of that.

                                In addition to ones of the attitude that the state of the Underworld, or state of Creation for that matter, constituting a perverse attachment to life long after it should be apparent that things have come to a conclusion, and they're the ones empowered to see it through to its end.

                                As well as some more death cult-y ones, whether they're in the mould of "life was never all that great, I'd best ease it along" or the more fascistic "life is only truly affirmed by ending in an impressive and meaningful way on your own terms".

                                Plus the more straightforward ones, as champions within and over the world of the dead itself, being well-suited to functioning as heroes and Lawgivers in that environment while having no special concern for extending that power over the living before their time, or ones who see Creation as a dying world, that they see no power to save but also no desire to help along, and that it deserves heroes who won't try to sugarcoat that decay beyond trying to help people find ways to accept and internalize it, as well as opposing the figures trying to give it false hope.

                                Right down to the ones who aren't deeply philosophical about their own place or implications, they're just empowered by their own wounds and the magic that flows around that image, and wield it to what ends suit it.

                                I wouldn't necessarily view these as villainous, but I would think that most of the more established, conventional powers would, and that can be compounded a bit by attaching a bit of traditionally villainous imagery to the Abyssals.

                                    I'd be interested in them making the Void more than "horrible death pit forever." That's simplistic and has the effect of placing Lethe into "designated right choice" when, again, its really just a recycling plant. The Dowager was pulling all kinds of Abyss linked crap out of the Well of Udr and The Emissary of the Righteous Victory is implied to be a "Saint of the Void" i.e. someone who entered it and came back. In Dark Souls III, the Abyss isn't implied to be emptiness so much as a different mode of existence.

                                    If one wants to reincarnate, that decision should be respected, but the 1e-2e text has always condemned decisions to the contrary, whether that's remaining as a ghost or escaping the cycle through oblivion. Which is really jarring given A) the reincarnation is arbitrary unless you sell yourself to a god like Ahlat or cough up the right bribes and B) the reincarnation process is corrupt from both bribery and things like Taru-Han's soul collection. The Lumina from the Ink Monkey Daystar write-up were interesting because oh-my-god they were different, a positively portrayed transhuman afterlife that didn't involve becoming some god's furniture or menial servant or something equally degrading. Ghosts were always portrayed as being in the wrong somehow just by being present and not submitting themselves to what Yu-Shan wants. Even when the entire reason the reincarnation cycle is broken is because the gods and the "proper" exalted created the Neverborn.

                                                    Oblivion - emptiness, nothingness
                                                    Void - emptiness, nothingness
                                                    Abyss - an emptiness so deep that the light does not reveal an end such that it seems to descend forever

                                                    Kaos - the Grecoroman and even Judean concept of emptiness and nothingness of formlessness, before defined forms were created. To the Greeks, Kaos was both a state of pre-existence, a place where things still didn't really exist, and a female Primordial figure who birthed other pre-Titan Primordials like Nyx, the deity of the night, as well as Gaia and Uranus. To the Hebrews, the world was formless chaos of (or merely like) a large body of water (and the sea/ocean has always symbolized godless chaos to the Hebrews) devoid of light and solid forms before God began creating the Earth, starting with light.


                                                    What might be interesting is a new take on the Underworld where it's actually something of a throwback to an "original Wyld", if the Wyld were Primordial Chaos thrown into a riotous mess of echoes of Creation by the act of making Creation and imposing direction and juxaposition upon nothingness.
