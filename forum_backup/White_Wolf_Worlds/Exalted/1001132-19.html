                        The Jianghu Marches: Xia’s frontier

                        The Jianghu Marches, formerly known as the Jianghu Gap, is a region of Xia scarcely talked about in the esteemed halls in Teriake. Distant, underdeveloped, poor and harsh, the region has been largely “under the radar” for most Lords for quite some time…. until now. With the recent elevation of Kasai {x} to the Lordship, and the peace agreement with the Hill-Tribes to the north, the fate of the region is of sudden and immediate concern to all involved, most of all the people who actually live there.

                        History:

                        The Jianghu Gap has been inhabited for almost as long as the Hundred Kingdoms and the Hill-Tribes have been around. Before the Hundred Kingdoms, the proto Hill-Tribes reared sheep across the hills of the region, and when the Vanehani came, they kicked out the Tribes and built terraces where sheep once roamed.

                        The region would pass through many hands as the nascent Hundred Kingdoms rose and fell, until about 200 years before the present date. The region was owned by the Shiba clan, a comparatively-important Oathsworn family, who served under the royal family of Zhou. Zhou was a powerful kingdom that ruled over most of the Red River valley, a state that builds grudges like weeds.

                        To make a long story short, the Shiba rose up in rebellion against Zhou, and along with others, annihilating the kingdom and, through both leadership in war and clever politicking, established the kingdom of Xia. Once the Shibas took control of the region, they basically abandoned their holdings in Jianghu in favor of Teriake, but never relinquished the region to another. Either because the family wanted a fall-back point if things went south, or it just was never considered, Jianghu has been held by the Shiba family since the founding of Xia, but visited only infrequently.

                        Yagura Castle was the clan’s ancestral stronghold, but even still it has never been popular with the family. The current Lord, Iysen Shiba, only visited the castle 3 times in her life (after she was given ownership of it as a wedding gift), and before her the castle was functionally empty for 50 years. None of the younger members of the Shiba clan has ever been to Yagura, or visited the region.

                        As a result, the region has been de-facto independent for almost as long as Xia has been a state. There have been castellans responsible for maintaining Yagura Castle, as well as Oathsworn retainers living in the region, but neither made much effort to govern the entire region. Both the Oathsworn and the commoners are largely used to doing what they want….. what will happen now the region has a Lord who actually will live and govern it?

                        Geography:

                        http://imgur.com/hLDrK4y


                        The Jianghu Marches are roughly V-shaped, following the banks of the upper reaches of the White River. The river falls over fast-flowing cataracts and rapids, steeply descending from the surrounding hills and bluffs of the mountains, and the White River stops being navigable at the market-village of Liangzhou. From the river’s edge, the land rises quickly into ridges and outcroppings of granite and slate, making conventional agriculture difficult at best, and leading the farmers of Jianghu to make heavy use of terraces and suchlike.

                        Abutted up against the mountains as it is, the region is harsh to say the least. While still temperate, Jianghu tends to be windier and colder than the lower river bottoms, and people living in the region make more use of wool and quilted garments than is usual.

                        Due to the geography of the region, and the mountains to the north, west and east, Jianghu tends to magnify the weather and climate of the seasons: Spring has truly astronomical amounts of rain, Autumn has fog and mists in abundance, and Winter often sees several feet of snow. On the other hand, summer is comparatively pleasant, with milder temperatures and diminished humidity compared to other regions.

                        In Dragontongue, “Jianghu” roughly means “rivers and lakes”, and ….. well, Jianghu has a lot of rivers and lakes! The two branches of the White river are the major ones, but there are many subtributaries as well, not to mention Yari Lake. Yari Lake is located at the head of one of the branches of the White river, and is where Yagura Castle is located. Deep and cold, Yari Lake is home to many fish, from trout to bass to pike, the last of which can reach sizes as long as a man. In addition, there are many hundreds of smaller lakes and ponds scattered throughout the domains, several of which are sacred to the Hill-Tribes that inhabited this area.

                        Vegetation and animal life is representative of the general Hundred Kingdoms, if skewed towards species that prefer higher elevations and colder temperatures. Maple and Ash, Oak and Pine are all common, with pine and other conifers tending to cover the upland ridges and hills. Wild animals include white-tailed-deer, turkey, raccoons and skunks, wolves and bear and boar, as well as many others. Berries like blueberry, cranberry, raspberry and others are extremely common, serving as food for both animals and man.

                        In autumn, the forests and fields are resplendent with a multitude of colors, making for quite the beautiful scenery.

                        People:

                        The people of Jianghu are an odd lot, influenced mainly by their isolation and harsh living conditions.

                        Phenotypically, they mainly exhibit traits from the Vanehani, but Hill-Tribe characteristics (darker skin tones, taller statures, red hair, etc) are common as well, and in the northern parts of Jianghu one can sometimes see actual mutations signifying direct descendance from intermarriage. The main language spoken is Riverspeak, but the region has a distinctive accent with the lilting flavors of Forest-tongue, and many people know a few words in that language.

                        People have a tendency to be pragmatic and taciturn, hard-working and focused. Self-sufficiency is prized, and people that can come up with solutions to problems without the assistance of others (not that others won’t help, of course) are valued highly.

                        In Jianghu, the social order is diminished in importance, relatively speaking. While Oathsworn landlords are still nobility, and therefore treated with respect and deference, the various social classes interact far more often, and work together more closely, than in other domains. This is necessary: if everyone didn’t band together, surviving the winter would be almost impossible. Oathsworn more often serve as direct mediators and judges (even though they don’t have the technical legal authority to do so) than as distant and disconnected authority figures, and it isn’t uncommon to see a landlord directly talking with their tenants. Peasants serve more as jurors and village headmen than as an ‘insulating’ class between the nobility and the dirty serfs, and in many cases can claim descent from their Oathsworn landlords. Some particularly-wealthy and powerful peasants might even have tenants of their own, making them a quasi “peasant aristocracy”.

                        In addition, the “commoner clan” is of more importance in Jianghu than what is usual in other domains. They function as support networks in the face of the harsh terrain, and many an Oathsworn landlord has to balance the needs of their fief and the wants (if not demands) of the commoner-clans inhabiting it.

                        With the absence of any real authority figures, most of the usual laws and customs of the Hundred Kingdoms are modified or outright ignored. Some of the more prominent examples include the owning of weapons and the preponderance of hunting.

                        Due to the high level of bandits plaguing the region, most every household will have at least one person familiar, if not skilled, with arms. This includes serfs, who by-and-large are prohibited from owning weapons in other domains. Common weapons include the spear, the club, and the bow. Club-fights are a common way of sorting out issues with neighbors, and asides from some broken bones and teeth, are usually nonlethal.

                        The ownership and usage of weapons is important for another reason: hunting. Almost everyone in Jianghu hunts, for the simple fact that to not make use of an ample food source would be foolish. Again, this often includes serfs, and said hunting often takes place in the privately-owned forests, most of which are owned by the Lord of Jianghu. Hunting in these without the Lords express permission is considered poaching, largely a hanging offence in other domains, and in said domains most nobles viciously retain their right to hunt. In Jianghu, things are different, and almost all Oathsworn landlords turn a blind eye, at the very least. As far as they are concerned, the fact that the common folk aren’t starving to death as often is enough benefit to give up some of their noble rights, and the fact that they are better able to defend themselves against bandits and raiders is a plus on top of that.

                        Most people live in the comparatively-less harsh river bottoms, and all settlements of any size are located along the White River. Both settlements and farmsteads are compact, with little wasted space. Houses and outbuildings are solidly made, almost-always with footings of stone and steeply-sloping roofs to shed winter snow, and buildings tend to be built close together, if not touching, to prevent snow and wind from whipping between them. Many of the isolated farmsteads are fortified, with palisades and thorn-walls, to protect them from both bandits and wild animals.

                        Economy:

                        Like in most of the Hundred Kingdoms, most of the people in Jianghu are agriculturalists.

                        Crops grown in Jianghu include potatoes, maize, beans, onions and leeks, pumpkins and other gourds, carrots, cabbage (which is easy to preserve for long periods of time), oats and wheat, etc etc etc. Cotton doesn’t grow well in the thin, rocky soil, so most clothing is made from linen or nettlecloth.

                        Fruit trees are especially common, with many houses having at least one tree, and great orchards stretching over the upper terraces. Most of these orchards are technically owned by the Lord of the region, but in the absence of said figure, most of them are cultivated freely by the commoners. Fruit raised in the orchards includes cherries, plums and apples, all of which are eaten both fresh and cooked, as well as turned into cider. A common method of making alcoholic beverages stronger is through freezing, leaving barrels of cider outside over winter and regularly scooping out the ice that forms. Such “ice-wines” are usually around 25-40% alcohol, rivalling wine in potency and in the hinterlands is commonly used as a currency.

                        Asides from fruit orchards and farm-fields, much of the arable land in Jianghu is never put to plow, instead being used as pasture for animals like sheep, goats and (rarely) cattle. The milk is used to make cheese, including particularly fine cheddar, and the fiber is used for clothing, warm against the wind from the hills.

                        Except for fruit, dairy and wool, the region doesn’t really export much besides the occasional timber, which is floated through the rapids to Liangzhou. There are deposits of iron, workable stone and coal, as well as great stands of lumber in the forests, but these aren’t exploited mainly due to the bandits infesting the region.

                        Liangzhou is the largest settlement in the region, and the only market town. A comparatively-sleepy hamlet of only 300 people, Liangzhou nevertheless has the only dock-facilities in the region, as well as the largest concentration of craftspeople. Everyone else lives in scattered farmsteads on the estates of Oathsworn overlords, with a few scattered hamlets, more concentrations of farms than actual towns.

                        Religion:

                        Like overwhelmingly-most of the Hundred Kingdoms, Jianghu is officially Immaculate-fearing. There is a shrine in Liangzhou, and the smaller hamlets and isolated farmsteads are (sporadically) services by roaming priests.

                        However, due to the isolation, several other religions have crept into the area. Field-gods, while not worshipped outright, are acknowledged, with small shrines present on most farms, and the animal-totems of the Hill-Tribes are often acknowledged whenever someone enters the woods, especially by hunters. There is a shrine to Luna, left over from the Hill-Tribes, that still sees regular offerings, though if they are made by the Tribes or by the Vanehani is unclear.

                        This mish-mash of religions has led to several holidays that would otherwise be proscribed by the Immaculate Faith.

                        First of these is the Blossom Festival, made in springtime when the flowers of the fruit trees are in bloom. Garlands and crowns of ephemeral flowers are laid on the graves of the honored dead and around the necks of newly-married couples, in symbolism of the circle of life and death.

                        The other holiday is the Feast of the Moon, distantly related to the Hill-Tribe holiday of the same name. Essentially a harvest festival, it takes place in the middle of autumn, after the harvest is done and the leaves begin to change. Communities gather together and welcome the coming winter, acknowledge the dead (usually through the carving of pumpkins and other gourds with faces and such, in order to ‘scare away the dead”), and pass around treats (fruit candied with maple sugar, pastries made of fruit and chopped nuts, as well as ample supplies of alcohol) to enjoy. Young children run around with masks and such, to frighten away the Fair Folk, or the dead (nobody is sure) and otherwise celebrate another year of toil.

                        Troublemakers:

                        Jianghu has a bandit problem.

                        This is mainly due to the region’s isolation and lack of central authority than anything else. The hills and forests make excellent places for the ruffians to hide, and the scattered and often divided Oathsworn can’t muster enough to meaningfully drive the bandits out. Sure, they can defend against a bandit attack on their own holdings, but the scruffians don’t exactly respect fiefdom borders and often melt away when faced with any sort of meaningful resistance.

                        Recently, however, there have been reports of several bandit gangs being forcibly consolidated into one, led by a disgraced Oathsworn warrior who calls himself “Lord Masanori of Jianghu”, who has occupied (or built up, reports are conflicting) a ruined hillfort deep in the central forests of Jianghu. Masanori has used his bandit gang to demand tribute from the outlying farmsteads on the fringes of the forest, and the Oathsworn of Jianghu are concerned he might have pretensions of glory and might try to take the region for his own. Of course, the Oathsworn can’t agree on the proper response (unify? Call the Lord Iysen? Appease?), and in the face of their indecision the bandit-lord only grows more bold.

                        Of course, the Men-at-Arms of Jianghu being called away to war doesn’t exactly help matters.

                        The Hill-Tribes are another problem. Life is hard enough in Jianghu as it is, even without screaming barbarian raiders stealing your sheep and crops in the middle of the night. However, other than the raids by young warriors out to prove themselves, the residents of Jianghu and the Tribes get along well, comparatively speaking. The two peoples trade together as much as fight, and have even intermarried in the past. As far as the populace of Jianghu is concerned, at least a Tribesman will stab you in the front, not the back, and be trusted to keep their word.

                        However, the recent rumors about the Tribes moving into the hills is ….. concerning. While they get along, they don’t get along that well. What will happen remains to be seen.

                        Yagura Castle:

                        http://imgur.com/ptwtYlt


                        http://imgur.com/C4MORiK


                        Yagura Castle is the ancestral stronghold of the Shiba family, and the single strongest point in the Jianghu region. Built of local granite and slate, and located on a bluff overlooking Yari Lake, the fortification is more than capable of holding off a force many times the number of the armsmen it holds.

                        “Yagura” is the Dragontongue word for “tower”, and indeed Yagura Castle is primarily made up of towers. The three towers, while small compared to the likes of Teriake or Azu, are solid and well-built, shedding heavy snows and deflecting strong winds as easily as arrows.

                        Since the Shiba clan doesn’t live at the castle, it is overseen by a castellan appointed by the Lord Iysen. The current holder of the position, Akio Masumi, is an incompetent fat slob who should probably be hung on principle alone. Due to his principle of noninterference (due to chronic drunkenness), the armsmen at Yagura are relatively lazy and out of fighting trim, and the armory is degraded. Not to mention the state of Jianghu….

                        Yagura Castle currently has a force of 10 armsmen permanently stationed, 6 infantrymen and 4 crossbowmen, with helmets, backswords, halberds and crossbows. The armory holds 50 spears, 10 crossbows, with plenty of bolts for the same, several dozen helmets, several shields, and several piles (several hundred or so) fist-and-head-sized rocks, for dropping from machiolations and the walls. Most of the armory is in various states of disarray, but the spears are in decent shape, and if the stuff needed to be used, it probably could.

                                                Jianghu Domestic Policies
                                                • Centralization: 4. Little centralization (law and court cases must be brought to a specialized representative of Kasai, aka a Magistrate of his choosing). I say this because it keeps us from doing all the ruling but also allows us to build loyal underlings who clearly would have to strive to appease Kasai.
                                                • Aristocracy vs Plutocracy: 2. Slightly Aristocratic ( Slightly agriculturally favored) we have a lot of natural resources that we can take advantage of right now and by having other oathsworn vassals or retainers or whatever distinction it is, we'd be building a class of people to support us as we go while being able to get our economy moving now.
                                                • Commoners: 3. Even mix (5 on 5 split, Resources (agriculture) goes to 3, Resources (taxes) goes to 3) lots of money and allows us to grow in either direction depending on whether we find the slightly aristocratic way of doing things useful. If not we could easily switch back. Either way its good to divide people so they aren't all capable of uniting against you.
                                                • Mercantilism vs Free-Trade: 1. Free-Trade favored: encourages external trade. Boost external economy. Seems to me like local economy is almost non-existant beyond subsistence. We'll need to trade outside our boundaries to grow the economy. We'll want investment from outsiders but that also comes with a risk of spies and sabatours. Either way i still think its the better option for a po-dunk territory like ours.
                                                • Attracting new settlers: A mix of 3 and 4 if possible?
