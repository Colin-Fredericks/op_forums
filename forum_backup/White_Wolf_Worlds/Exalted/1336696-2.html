        Okay, when was the last time I actually posted anything about the book in this thread...

        November?! Ah jeez, I spent three weeks slowly putting together just 2500 words or something, and that was more than a month ago.

        Well, it'll do for a start.

        [
        Oh, I forgot to say anything about the illustration of soldiers accompanying captives and plunder through the North. Errr, it’s fine. It works as an accompaniment to this particular subject, but I don’t find anything exceptional in its own right.

        Okay, so we’ve gotten the basic summation of what satrapies are, now it’s on to the underlying mechanism of how they work. This is an area in which I’ve gotten a broad sense of how detailed the rest will be ahead of time from having checked how long it was before getting to the examples. I’ve been going back and forth on whether I agree with the level of detail that’s going to be provided, since it felt in part reminiscent of styles from Second Edition that I’ve been critical of in the past. My final provisional assessment is that it’s a necessity because the layout of satrapies has in the past been a remarkably underexplored subject; not much information and what’s there is scattered among multiple spots. Especially if they’re being made more significant, it needs to develop something from the ground up, and lots of information gives a lot of hooks for how Dynasts navigate control over them or other characters deal with the challenges of extracting the imperial apparatus.

        For a start, I’m liking this flourish of the introduction being given in the voice of imperial propaganda, it conveys information about both sides of the system effectively and sets a tone, while I draw just enough of a sense of irony to think it’s not coming across as something to take seriously. Aaand this sidebar about the imperialist apparatus is immediately endearing to me with the title invoking the image of some technical census taker with a secret; it reminds me of a long time ago when I had a picture of a king in the previous Southeast who became very excited at the prospects of having taken captive a Realm mineral surveyor. Going with the assumption that this is where the information about the permanent administration in a satrapy is provided, I’ll first start that I’m glad to have the detail of such ministerial postings being laid out for us, just to give a sense of the scope of imperial presence in each territory that I felt was often up in the air. That they would tend to be infiltrated by agents of the All-Seeing Eye as part of the means employed by the Empress to ensure that her information about satrapy affairs was accurate might be the more dynamic detail, especially for the story hooks about satraps experiencing paranoia about their staff and working to root out those observers (and I think it plays well with earlier plot hooks about the Eye starting to strike out on its own), but I’m personally more drawn to the side of them as a permanent staff to ensure consistent management around shifting political overlords. It’s a dynamic I find compelling in stories of civil services run by appointees with high turnover rates, and one that might be unique in the institutions of the Realm (prefectural governors are close, but they still amount to a different layer of political appointee with a longer term and still beholden to special interests); I like the image of Dynastic masters being given such a staff and needing to learn to rely on their advice and experience, especially if it could emphasize that for all their power they can still be Exalted in an unfamiliar land. It also just reminds me of things in my history classes about the British administration in Dublin Castle, so it comes with providing an enduring edifice that local rebels can make the symbol of their ire.

        Okay, the actual procedures for forging a new satrapy, should be a lot of meat in here. I’m still reading the line about the Empress not being expected to govern all territories directly in a kind of in-character voice of the rhetoric and legal fictions of farming them out to the Dynasty, and where in the past I’ve always read descriptions of the Great Houses bidding for jurisdiction as something more like an auction, some of the framing here makes me look at it more in the manner of them all having the opportunity to put forward a proposal about why they’re best suited for or deserve a satrapy in question. Which… it occurs to me may just mean I completely misread the references in the past. It occurs to me that I’ve probably misunderstood the basic concept of “bidding for government contracts” every single time I’ve heard it. Ah well; the point here is that they get to make pitches, while final say remained with the Empress who altered or revoked their terms at her leisure. It’s a nice starting point on the history of interplay between the monarch and the intermediaries, even if most practical authority was exercised by them and they would likely maintain most leases in perpetuity.

        Now we’re getting to the subject of actually selecting the satrap, something that I’ve been feeling around the edges of since back in What Fire Has Wrought. To have the proper context for this, I went and reviewed sources on the appointment in the previous Edition, and found three distinct references: Exalted: the Dragon Blooded tucks a tiny note into the lexicon of all places on how Houses get satrapies and then select a satrap among themselves, Manacle & Coin went into more detail on how the post of satrap and Houses leases to collect the tribute were distinct and the office was appointed by the Empress (who made a specific note to typically appoint members of Houses other than the one collecting tribute to limit corruption, a practice discontinued in her absence), and the Second Edition core (my introduction to the matter) gave a very detailed list of the qualifications for a satrap (such as having served for a period as a prefect) and while appointed by the Empress (by a combination of her own selection criteria and being able to afford the preliminary fee) candidates being put forwards by the Great Houses meant that satrap appointment seemed to have been more or less conflated entirely with Houses being privileged to collect tribute. Incidentally, that description in the core book in combination with Manual: Dragon Blooded repeating information on House policies towards satrapies verbatim from First Edition meant that for the longest time, I had no idea how the empire actually worked. In retrospect, for a good deal of time afterwards I assumed something that was cobbled together from Manacle and the Second Edition core.

        The point is to establish where I’ve been coming from in surmising a revision, starting with reading a line about House leadership choosing one from among its ranks as referring to the House as a whole, rather than choosing one among the leaders specifically. I’d find that consistent with the idea that the preferred criteria of having had experience on another satrap’s staff (that reads to me as more mid-management or junior executive than established power).

        Now, this all reads to me as the background of satraps having a lot less seniority and generally being wider than the impression I’ve gotten before, and that would be to my preference. On the note of shifting the responsibility for appointment away from the Empress, I like the way that makes satrapy assignments more a matter of House political manoeuvring and standing, how it can emerge from pre-existing hierarchy within a House and success or failure can create shifts rather than being another point like the Ministries where the authority of the Empress supersedes that of family. Especially when it can be used as the basis for some promising up-and-comer or individual being groomed for higher responsibilities for being a power out in the world (i.e. a position that players might find themselves in). A less rigid and experienced criteria for appointment also relieves some of my impression that it was an office that would soak up a lot of the Realm’s most experienced, as well as a feeling of a disproportionate quantity of each House needing to have had such a career path (particularly when prefects were supposed to have been Exalted so uncommonly). I think settling on tribute collection and satrap appointment being consolidated in a single House is a much neater arrangement; I get what the separation was probably intended to accomplish, but this is an area where I think the much higher priority should be on how each of the Houses relates to their subordinate region of the Threshold rather than being another area in which the Scarlet Empress was dividing offices against themselves. I can get a much stronger sense of how a given House has been an influence on or set policies for a satrapy when they get to set those terms directly, rather than having to mediate through an imperial appointee. Even in the straight reading of the Second Edition version, the House that is profiting feels several steps removed from the satrap as representative of the throne, where it’s much easier to see this setup as being one where the boss might change but the procedures remain the same. Overall, I find it to be more flexible and give the Houses more bite out in the world.

        Oh, I also like how it shifts the criteria in the direction of appointing satraps who aren’t inclined too much towards personal enrichment or even the possibility of using the territory as their own personal fief. It adds an interesting tension and potential for suspicion and distrust of satraps, fears for how their conduct might reflect on the House as a whole, and indeed the possibility of characters actually do act like that (or at least dance on the edge of acceptability for it) but have the cunning or clout to get away with it. In the actual history of states or empires or a few kinds of drama series concerned with a workplace environment, I’ve a particular fondness for conflicts around such figures exercising too much authority while basically giving lip service to their superiors. It seems particularly relevant at this uncertain juncture of the Realm’s history, where a couple of satraps who might be pushed to the point of declaring themselves petty kings helps to round out some of the Warring States imagery, and I like it falling along an axis that might split Houses rather than just intact Houses splitting the Realm.

        The specific duties and functions of the satrap are another thing that I think in the past had to be largely inferred, and while the specifics might be largely boilerplate it’s quite helpful to have them laid out in detail. Ah, now something from the Second Edition core that I did like a lot was how the Empress was supposed to have appointed assistants to the satrap from other Houses and had a system where all of them provided separate reports. I think that’s a more interesting example of how she could maintain a balance with and honesty from the tribute collectors, that rather than them having to deal with a manager installed by herself it’s a case that they get their own managers but she sends people along to keep an eye on them in the guise of helping to execute their duties (which also feels like a more fun dynamic between herself and the Houses), so I’m glad to see the return of it here. Sure, it still has the point that in her absence the assistants have been co-opted to make sure all offices represent a single House, but I think the altered backstory gives the subject a bit more weight. I also like the added note of petulance in how such appointments are not being issued to people with close personal ties to the satrap, and especially how the more dubious reports are used as another area to subvert the Thousand Scales.

        Ah, now I really like going into detail on exactly how it is that the Foreign Office intersects with the satraps; I never would have exactly thought that the two were redundant, but limited information did not help to provide a firm idea of how they were not. A lot can be gleaned just from the range of subjects that the satraps need to submit reports on, their range and scope providing the basis for a sizable staff on both sides merely to collect and process highly detailed information on the satrapies; this is another example to me where description of what the bureaucracy does can provide a basis for thinking of things that Bureaucracy does. I’ve said before that the Foreign Office has always seemed like a solid organ of the Realm, but the information here lends a lot to make it feel more proactive and dynamic in its heyday. A central coordinating body that could direct information acquired from one satrapy to the benefit of a neighbouring one to ensure its efficiency or well-being is a major contribution on its own (and another place to infer ways that the Great Houses are shooting themselves in the foot), but the Itinerant Advisory Committees are where the real money is. For a start, the regular meetings/inspection tours make for a very compelling idea of historical or upcoming events that a satrap and their staff and household become extremely tense around, while not being strictly antagonistic with the overt function of being an advisory and feedback exercise. The process of inspection itself sounds like something that could make for an involved way of introducing a satrapy in detail during play, while the matter of how the inspectors arrive early to take a look before the official show is put on feels like a strong hook for people who want to play it from the ministry’s side. I’m also enjoying the descriptions given for the circumstances in which inspections are performed with greater frequency and scrutiny, both for helping to keep a leash on overzealous satraps and for the amusing dynamic of the Thousand Scales being distrustful of satrapies that are too successful on paper and making sure that everything is on the up and up (with the benefit of getting insights to improve other satrapies if it should be).

        And finally there’s the subject of how this is a case in which a ministry is hamstrung not so much by active interference from the Houses as the limits of its own purview. I like that presentation of how the Foreign Office never actually had direct authority to correct problem satraps, and in the absence of an Empress to inform who might rescind leases it comes down to ineffectually suggesting that Houses rein in their own appointees. This doesn’t even feel like any sort of dramatic irony of the Realm being bitten by some engineered institutional shortcoming; it just feels like a logical limitation of a ministry being thrown into relief where checks elsewhere are not put into effect, more an illustration of the Realm’s gradual collapse then a contributor to it. I find that it adds to the pathos of Ledaal Arnis, and it’s a nice point to conclude this expansion of the Foreign Office on.
        ]

        I have approximate knowledge of many things.

            Okay, what did I write back at the... end of January.

            [Finally getting a move on. I’ve been continuously stalled at the beginning of this next topic for trying to find the right words to start with.

            Even within the previously unaddressed topic of how satrapies are established and function, the matter of how that interacts with House military ventures has always stood out to me as having particular significance. It was a hanging question back when every Great House was supposed to have its own legions, and Third Edition has had its prominence keep creeping upwards with the introduction of things like overt references to House Cathak engaging in conquest of its own accord and then this book’s military chapter elaborating on the war tourism of the Dynasty. I appreciate it being given some focus.

            It makes sense to me that the Houses don’t generally get to keep territories they roll over for themselves considering the general layout of the Realm. The first value I see for that in worldbuilding is to have plausible situations in which a nation that neighbours a satrapy has had longstanding tensions with it but the House in question has never gone so far as to push for conquest if they think they’ll just end up enriching rivals (a situation that probably amounts to backstory for a conflict that might finally erupt). I think the way this lends itself to satrapies of a mixed composition serves the same use that it does for prefectures, a reminder that these aren’t merely the private fiefs of each House and a logical way to allow characters of any House to have activities in any given satrapy. I also see good potential for giving satrapies their own longstanding pressures internally between the governing House and the business interests of the conquering House. As House Cathak in particular goes, it provides the context to fully round out the description of their House economy. I also like how the descriptions of scouting out the satrapy of a rival House can provide a bit of an edge for some new graduate’s sabbatical tour, or create a background for why one might have an accomplishment that secures a high appointment a bit earlier.

            It seems to me that the point for the description of how one House attempting to sabotage the control of another might go is to create the impression that the satrapies are not all hotbeds of intrigue and one-upmanship so that people will more readily give them backgrounds of a certain stability. It does also provide a few more ideas for Dynastic intrigue and the governing methods of the Empress, mind; one could come up with some background for a bit of bad blood over things like the loss of a lease to scheming or being punished. I’d almost wonder if the purpose of House Sesus in such a sabotage might have been to hurt Ledaal rather than enrich themselves, and the possibility of it costing them a satrapy might have been considered worthwhile. Considering how much I’ve been thinking of House Ledaal recently, my mind is suddenly going off in the direction of thinking that some other Dynasts sometimes want to sabotage the Shadow Crusade because they’re wary of the possibility of unnecessarily antagonizing mystical threats.

            But, as with elsewhere, there’s still that focus on providing the contrast between how things were with how things currently stand, with the point that no Empress to leash them makes undermining one another more frequent and overt, and suggested as a possible hook for the start of civil war. I think it’s the kind of thing one would expect, but useful to lay out in a section like this.]

                All right, I’m finally going to get around to the intricacies of satrapy policy. I did have a little peek ahead, and I like the headers for each area with the hand metaphors.

                First off is the financial benefit. I’m glad to see that it doesn’t bog itself down with the details of exactly how the tribute for a satrapy is assessed, just quickly describing the process with a focus on the key personalities involved. The specifics of what is collected are more important; I don’t think I would have expected hard currency to form the majority of the tribute, but apart from anything else I suppose it helps make portraying the subject a bit easier and it does contribute to a picture of the Threshold of being financially sophisticated. The description for the occasions of providing merchandise is good in the same way that the trade output of many locations is for giving a few specifics to get a handle on, as well as how satrapies can fuel the businesses of the Great Houses. I quite like the point of how the mandate would sometimes be for things a satrapy would need to acquire from neighbours because it gives a picture of dynamic sections of the dominated Threshold, makes the reign of the Empress a bit more insidious for occasions where policies cause violence to radiate outwards, and plays off of what Fangs at the Gate will portray the grievances of Lunars and their confederates as. For the part about First Age relics, I can read it two ways; one being that the high value means that including that in the tribute means a state can get to keep a bit more of its money that year, the other being that they’re so precious that it’s taken as a given that the Dynasts will want them placed on top of the usual extraction, and there will be dire consequences if they’re withheld. The depiction of tribute in slaves seems a bit of a departure from how at least I always understood it before, as the primary means by which the Realm acquired them, but that would be necessary for the stronger emphasis on the slave trade as the monopoly of House Cynis. If it’s going to be run over quickly, I think it’s a good call to use it as a basis for a plot hook about increasing demand for indentured soldiers, and the discord this is causing in the satrapies.

                Next is the matter of where the tribute ends up going. This is actually the one details of how the empire worked that I felt prior Editions did very well, although there are some necessary adjustments here to fit the altered layout of the Realm, something that I’m of two minds on. On the one hand, I like the breakdown given for everybody who wants a cut of the take, particularly the inclusion of the Merchant Fleet and the need to account for all of the smaller officials who’ll skim (it’s fun to see how the political culture of the Realm deals with lower level corruption by simply transferring the burden to taxed rather than discourage embezzlement), as well as some of the debts that the Great Houses need to cope with from their share. It does make the Realm feel more rapacious to say that with so many looking for a share, actual tribute exceeds assessments of what can be fairly acquired, not to mention giving satrap characters more to do in locating and facilitating sources of revenue in a strained tributary.

                But at the same time, I was always very interested in how First Edition’s ethos for realism (and probably some of Grabowski’s professional background) dealt with the topic by presenting the tribute as a fixed amount of which even at the best of times the state never collected the full amount of between losses in transit and the amount that would simply never be collected in the first place. It gave the world a sense of depth and complexity, that there were too many parts for the great empire to be all-powerful. However, it does make up for it a bit with the portrayal of responses to the more uninhibited taxation in the absence of the Scarlet Empress, that many of the Threshold peasants work to try and confound the assessors with underreported or hidden assets. It should guide people towards depicting the agrarian workers with a much needed degree of agency and creativity, and sets the stage for conflicts based around investigating the countryside for concealed resources; lets one adopt the role of a Hans Landa engaged in tense scenes of trying to uncover stockpiles of grain or fibre. Something different from the norm, but still with genuine stakes (especially with the ideas for how the tax evaders might be punished), and its own incentive to go exploring off the beaten track.

                Last is the process of getting that tribute back home. I guess that under normal circumstances this would be the more routine step, so I think it’s a good idea to focus entirely on the complications produced by the current crisis. It’s all well and good that guard contingents reduced by assignment back to the Isle increase vulnerability to bandits, but what really gets me here is the idea that the accompanying soldiers might run off with their charges. It’s a fun way to scuttle some plans and facilitate others (the most obvious reference to me is the drama in HBO’s Rome around evacuating the city’s treasury) and can introduce uncertainty about whether or not a House would have anybody to spare to get the stolen funds back (assuming it’s even possible if they get divvied up and spent in the time it would take). While the description of getting it back across the sea gives a nice point of comparison between peasants and Princes of the Earth in satraps trying to underreport earnings to lower V’neef’s tolls (although I’m interested in the implication that there’s also an incentive to overreport lest it be given away that the House’s funds are weakening). Still, it emphasizes the disproportionate power that the fledgling House has with the particular vulnerability crossing the sea presents that makes all Houses willing to pay something to maximize the chance of getting anything over at all.

                Right, the policy of establishment for the Immaculate Philosophy, the kind of thing that felt like it should have been a big deal but often seemed to be applied inconsistently even by the limited standards of prior Editions depicting satrapies. Clarity right from the bat that, in all normal circumstances, conversion is to be inflexible and all-encompassing, making it the one area of real cultural imperialism for the Realm. I think it plays well with the Order’s expanded domestic role of being a culture enforcing secret police, and I like to imagine the contrast between monks on the Isle having a somewhat more placid, accepting demeanour and the ones abroad functioning as firm inquisitors. I think that’s a good playbook they have with appropriating existing gods and places of worship into their way of doing things, it’s a good way for their system to have enough flexibility to start assimilating and I think could make for compelling scenes of the gradual transition. I like the image of how they invade homes to remove and destroy contrary religious materials and use shows of force to bring people into compliance to have a mechanism for some authoritarian violence while maintaining the familiar limits of the Realm’s intrusion. Indeed, in this expanded insight into the satrapy system I’m seeing an understated depiction of the usual division of powers inherent to the policies of the Scarlet Empress; taken as a whole their control is pretty comprehensive, but it’s obscured by the separation between multiple interests who need to tie themselves up finding ways to cooperate.

                Anyway, jumps off from there to the more fantastical interplay with gods. What Fire Has Wrought introduced some very nice Occult Charms, and I’m quite happy to see references to them here as among the tools that the Realm’s Dragon Blooded employ to handle unruly spirits. The Immaculate Dragon Styles might still afford the monks the means of destroying gods that they wouldn’t otherwise have, but I like that their repertoire includes being able to handle problems with the lighter hand of putting somebody in a box for a few hundred years to think about what they’ve done. Especially if some gods might be only just coming out of confinement to find the Time of Tumult. And finally there’s a nice bit of intrigue given to the clandestine worship engaged in by locals who continue to resist (my favourite is the hidden shrines in the wilderness), and a last form for this harsh edge to the Order to take, a nice transition into their more accommodating methods.

                Huh, accommodating methods that seem to be reserved for when they lack the support of established structures of authority by which they can apply force, that’s interesting. If nothing else I’d like it for the subverted image of how it’s the people living in the harsher conditions within a satrapy who end up benefiting from the charitable programmes that the monks can bring. And the descriptions of how they make issuing some of those benefits conditional on people hearing them out… there have been relatively recent stories about religious charities (also I’ve been reading back over some of what the Church has done in this country in the last few weeks) that make that stand out and hit a bit harder, makes it a bit more insidious under the surface even if it can provide genuine good to people. On the other hand, the idea that monks convert Immaculate texts and other Realm propaganda into popular entertainment feels more sincere and charming; recalls some of the best things about the lectors of Autochthonia.

                This last section about the Stubborn Gods though, it feels a tad redundant. The basic point of “not everyone converts and people continue to worship in secret” has already been made, and the parts about the syncretism and how some festivals and practices need to be permitted seems like it could have either been distributed elsewhere or expanded a bit. Still, I like a few of the points given about how that syncretism plays out, especially how people might contrive connections or justifications for their existing traditions or venerated gods in the Immaculate Philosophy. More interesting is the sidebar on the places that are able to evade these controls almost entirely, like Fajad, introducing the premise of satrapies who surrendered to save the Realm a fight are able to make stronger demands to be left alone. The point about how the government only wants to let those arrangements stand temporarily makes me think of places where the missionaries are the more underground ones even if they’re not in danger of persecution. Operate to build goodwill towards their religious supremacy, but carefully enough to not provoke troublesome incidents.

                Now for the last branch of imperial policy, military matters. I don’t think I really thought about it before, but it is a rather distinct and unusual thing to have the armies responsible for subjugating new territories be completely separate from the private interests charged with managing them thereafter. Apart from being another example of the distributed powers of the Realm, it makes me think that a satrapy might have very different perceptions of the legions and the House garrisons, probably starting off as seeing the former as harbingers of doom compared to the lighter hand of the latter and then perhaps transitioning into a state where familiarity and abuses from the stationed troops breeds contempt while the mobile armies become something more abstract (although maybe no less fearsome, especially if faced with the prospect that they might be ordered to reoccupy the satrapy). The military chapter already elaborated on some of this sure, but here I feel as though we’re getting more of the Great Houses’ side of it, the conditions they need to operate in and calculations behind where they want military aid to deal with troubles come from. It especially gives more weight to the business policies of the military Houses to get the feel for the environment in which their services could be leased out to their fellows. It’s a funny thing to think about how the Imperial Legions are still there, but the circumstances in which they’re brought under the control of the Houses actually weakens their applicability to putting down uprisings.

                Hmm, I don’t believe that it had come up before in this book that the overall command of garrisons is an imperial appointment and typically from a House other than the leaseholder under the old regime. It makes enough sense with the general standards, and I like for the satrapies to still have a few parts of the imperialist apparatus fall under the purview of Deliberative and Empress. And sure, it’s important to make satrapies into hotbeds of intrigue and backbiting between appointees of differing loyalties. The example if given for House troops who chafe under an alien leader, but I think there’s a lot of ripe drama to be wrung out of those who find that they like their garrison commander more than authorities of the House that raised them. It’s understandable that most Houses should be using political manoeuvring and murder to consolidate power in satrapies, but I think there ought to be at least one in which the House needs to put up with an imperial appointee because they’re not sure the garrison won’t mutiny in the event of dismissal or worse.

                The image of some garrisons being a preferred arm of the Realm’s control is strengthened by the point that satrapies that lacked the means to defend themselves from either direction find such a military establishment desirable, although what interests me is the idea that the priority of the governing House might be how much the locals are capable of rebelling and how that can lead to cases of less well-defended people not being assigned as many troops as they might like. The idea of a local ruler who tries to foment just enough dissent to keep the House investing sufficient forces to feel secure from what is actually scary… eh, that’s probably playing with fire too much for most people. Ohh, but the description of how this is handled with a satrapy that had to be conquered and has a stronger native military tradition has a lot of the things that I like, with both the integration of their own troops into the local fabric and bringing some of the existing structure in line with their own. It would seem to me that a garrison in a troublesome and dangerous satrapy is the closest that the Realm gets to colonisation, albeit more in the model of the aristocrat adventurer than the plantation settlement. It actually makes me think of occasional models in which the format of a garrison is not that of the mass of troops centralised in forts but spreading them out as a kind of yeoman landowner who maintains traditions of supremacy and can take to their horses and deploy in the event of mass unrest (there’s a lot I can get from Irish history for these last few points). You know, I’ll admit that from about the time I saw it being introduced in What Fire Has Wrought, I felt as though the House garrison in the satrapy was a lesser military force than the Imperial Legions, static and uninteresting when set alongside the more widely campaigning force of fearsome repute. This section is short, but it does a lot to make the premises behind garrisons shine, draws more interest with the complexities of them being part of the system of Realm control and the diversity that can lend them. It rounds out my earlier observation with an inspiration for how the garrisons might view themselves; thinking that the legions get the easier job of rolling in and dismantling the Realm’s enemies, while they’re the ones who have to live here and make those people into neighbours. After all of that, the descriptions for how the Realm executes enforcement feel fairly standard, with the exception of making a note of how their effectiveness is improved by the visible inclusion of local troops. Certainly that’s a thing that happens across history, I just mean in terms of bringing that to the attention of more people. I like the sidebar suggesting that the deployment of auxiliaries abroad improves Realm control by means of implicitly holding both sides of the equation hostage, although I think a distinct implication of that is that failures on the Realm’s part can severely compromise them in far-flung theatres. I can picture some unscrupulous Lunar who targets the relatives of some auxiliaries deployed across the world and then relates the fate of their families to provoke mutinies from troops enraged at having been removed so far from people that needed their protection, against an empire that failed to do so.

                Time to finally finish this section off. Ah, now here’s something I quite like; it’s become standard up to this point to illustrate ways in which the conventional models are breaking down in the current crisis, but I find this to be an excellent (indeed necessary) place to mention issues with the system even prior to that. I think there have been a few hints dotted throughout these books that the imperialism of the Realm was something that had been decreasing in profitability over time, and I very much appreciate it being clearly spelled out here (especially since I’ve been reading a few things lately that have necessarily included points about the lack of long term viability to the typical imperialist motive of “expand or die”). Oh sure, the impact of Lunars is given its due credit, but we’re also given the point of how a mindset in pursuit of constant growth inevitably yields diminishing returns and incentivizes increasingly desperate (if discerning) searches for areas to wring value out of which has… let’s say a lot of resonance with current life. It would certainly seem to form part of the background for why the Realm so recently started directing more attention out West (which would lend further irony to the fact that doing so awakened the ire of an ancient Lunar in possession of a sizable polity to turn against them). And I think there are very interesting settings that one could make out of the satrapies that have needed to be recently merged to remain profitable or thoroughly abandoned when they fail to maintain even that (an idea for them I’ve had in the past, but funnily enough on an occasion where I suggested it was informed that this wasn’t really the way the Realm handled things).

                Heh, I was ready to dismiss the reminder that the crisis is rendering the satrapies more vulnerable as almost trite in comparison to those considerations, but it recaptured my interest by opening with the perspective of the satrapies that the increased power acquired by the Great Houses would transfer to their interests and that such hopes have been dashed by greater deployment to secure the Blessed Isle. There are always issues inherent to portrayals of empires as necessary defensive forces, but from this I can readily imagine people who always took issue with the legions and garrisons and tribute payments but have realistic assessments about the consequences of those things being very suddenly pulled out while they have nothing in place to take up their roles. Such a strong picture of how some satrapies are being left to fend for themselves creates a starker image for how many are being so much more severely drained of their capital, and the human side of that stands out the most. I’ve often thought that, despite legions at the outset, the actual unfolding of civil war contains the possibility of a dramatic transformation of Realm military organisation, but I had never before considered the idea that incentives to not disrupt the Blessed Isle’s economy and having access to those people anyway might mean that the burden of mass conscription would also be transferred to their foreign holdings. It’s the kind of thing I imagine would quickly shatter the satrapy system, but it feels capable of leaving an utterly ruined Threshold in its wake even before any unleashed aggressors get to them, and I think it greatly increases the stakes of a Realm Civil War for a world at large that might otherwise be perceived as happy to stand back and watch their overlords knock the shite out of each other from across the sea. But sure, the actual danger that Lunars and bandits (and Fair Folk, and mysterious empires from beyond the scope of the Realm) pose is no slouch either, and I think it’s especially good to show how the problem is exacerbated if satraps get to the point of trying to raise defensive troops solely from locals.

                With all of that in combination with points about how the increased extraction leaves more depletion in its wake and how the people at the top are too focused on one another to recognise the unsustainability of their policies, it certainly does paint a bleak picture (one that I find ultimately more condemnatory than validating for the empire). I also think the line about how those positioned on the ground have limited power to actually control policy (in addition to a few of the earlier references to satraps forced to adapt around what is being done to their charges) lends itself to my feeling that satraps can benefit dramatically from being more junior appointees of the Scarlet Dynasty. A bleak picture, but in the wider scope of the game one that really sets the stage for heroes to appear and try to develop systems that can save it from the brink, even if they’ve got complex and challenging issues to deal with.

                The chapter isn’t finished yet, but considering the fact that from herein it’s profiles of a few example satrapies I feel confident in giving my overall assessment of it. All in all I’m impressed with how much it’s managed to do with what was previously such a remarkably overlooked part of the setting. The policies for how satrapies are established and governed feel like they effectively walk the line between verisimilitude and serving dramatic purposes such as being focuses for a cast of compelling characters and conflicts, they’re detailed enough to cover pretty much all of the bases while not getting bogged down in minutia, and it approaches with a reasonable amount of nuance without drifting too far from the core problem issues. I especially like how much it develops a sense of impending crisis arising from wider systemic issues, that not only is the danger a matter of widespread systemic collapse but how each driving factor behind this danger is itself part of a larger web of drives and interests. I think it makes for a very good framework for the nature of how the power of the Scarlet Dynasty and its greatest luminaries practically radiates out over the world as well as a skeleton off of which a number of distinct and interesting locations can hang, distinct but bound together in a way that many parts of Creation have not felt so much before. I look forward to seeing more of the places that are made out of this.

                I have approximate knowledge of many things.
