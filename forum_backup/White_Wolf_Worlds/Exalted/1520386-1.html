            Anyway, what I think the Martial Arts section is saying.

            I think that it's calling out the initial decision back in 1st edition to split unarmed combat into Brawl and Martial Arts (because there's a lot of pre-existing baggage there) and wants us to critically examine when we're picking one of those abilities over the other. There's brawlers who learned to fight in a formal school and self-taught martial artists.

In any case, I look at it this way, having money or resources opens lots of doors, but it's useless if you don't know how to properly use it, it's a support tool for the skills where money or resources could play an important role; Backing is more simple, define who or what exactly is backing the player, what can they do, and what are they willing to do for the player; Influence is the most nebulous, that's why it's very important for the player to tell me how and why do they have that influence, also it's not mind control, like resources it's a tool to support skills if it's relevant, with an emphasis on social skills.

                                            I'm still reading the book and am working on a Youtube video about it (I like the book overall), but I'm waiting for the post-errata version before I finish it.

                                            I haven't read the essays on slavery and orientalism in great depth yet, but I want to point out that there are valid reasons to include it beyond the player experience. Exalted is a gameline that people either love or hate. It's hard to find nuanced takes on it. It's also been criticized for hypersexualization, orientalism, and a history of generally bad takes over the decades. Those who are on the hate-side of the Exalted-take polarity have concrete things to point at and say "game bad!" Including these kinds of essays help to defend the game against those criticisms, present a solid stance, and shift the game towards a better place in the larger RPG space.

                                            It's impossible to control how players approach things at their tables, and it's not a publisher's place to police what people do in their own homes, but taking larger stances helps the publisher distance themselves from certain problematic takes should those takes be public. You don't want to be the "orientalist game" or the "slaver game," especially not after you were publically called the "rape game" in late 2E.
