                I see Exigents role primarily as plot devices. For example:

                - Exalted of God X wanders into the PCs lives, causes trouble or enlists their help in the pursuit of a goal, then fades into the background.

                - The PCs are too specialised in Y, so along comes an exigent who is immune to Y and forces the PCs to adapt and broaden.

                - antagonist Z turns out to be a secret Exigent - surprise!

                While I'm always interested in seeing what a new player option looks like, my expectations re that a group of Exigents would be like a D&D group of entirely chaotic alignments. They all have their own goals and agendas and their divine (parent?) will have shit that needs doing the PCs can't ignore.

                                        Honestly, the only issue I see with Exigents is the fact they're basically legitimized homebrew. And frankly, homebrew making or breaking your game is something you'll hear about from any number of stories, good and bad. At that point, it's a question of how well your GM and players handle things and dealing with what everyone wants to have fun with.

                                        Anything you can do with a godblood, you can do with an Exigent, but anything you can do with an Exigent, you can do with a Solar/DB/Lunar/Whatever You Want. I think Exigents open up easier paths to getting what you thematically want, but they're just homebrew generated content with a coat of legitimacy paint. They're not going to break the game any more than Nocturnals or Terrifying Argent Witches or Infernals or whatever, because when it comes down to it, what something does to "the line" is secondary to what your table decides on. So if you and your players are having fun, there's no problem.

                                                It seems certain that there will be standards and guidelines provided in the Exigents book for how not to break the game by unleashing Invincible Sword Princess and her four identical siblings, but "power level" is really contextual and hard to quantify unless everyone's powers are super generic, at which point the game fails to deliver on its promise (e.g. how in 2E Lunar Charms seemed a lot like someone had gone through the Solar Charmset and said "these, but less efficient for the magnitude of the effect"). My point was just that "Essence rating of the god in question" doesn't seem likely to be extremely relevant to the power of the Exigent; it felt to me implied in the fluff from the 3E core that the Exigence, the portion of the Unconquered Sun's power that he made available for this purpose, does most of the work, since the god's own Essence is referred to as "catalyzing" the process.

                                                Also, the Terrestrial-Celestial divide seems like less of a Giant Thing that will be mechanically enforced. Many 2E Dragonblooded Charms literally have riders saying something like "but Celestial Charms beat this automatically if there's a contest," as part of Ye Grand Plan To Make DBs Seem Terrible And Sucky In Their Own Book. That's gone in 3E; the differences between Terrestrials and Celestials, in terms of how the one is generally weaker than the other, is in the Charm pool (the ways in which Dragonblooded Essence expresses itself) and the weaker Excellency, but that same difference also causes DBs to be contextually stronger (they're repeatedly called out in the core as masters of Evocations second only to the Solars, and in some respects their peer).

                                                There will always be arguments about homebrewing, but I worry that systematizing it too much leads to the issue one sees in superhero games, where powers are generic things to be point-bought and then refluffed, but fundamentally the same thing underneath the surface. Rejecting that route makes it harder on everyone to make good material, but I think leads to better games and stories.

                                                            *This is the issue. I can't really say "You guys can play an Exigent, but that guy can't." I mean, some players wouldn't mind: if I said "Iozz-Sothoth, you're a fluff man not a mechanics man, you can play an Exigent if you've got a cool concept, but only if someone else writes the rules for you", I don't think Iozz-Sothoth would mind, because he doesn't care that much about mechanics anyway (and probably wouldn't want to write them up, just come up with vague ideas), but others might mind. And if I say "Owen can play Exigents because he comes up with really cool ideas for bizarre but not overpowered characters, like when he wanted to play a magic sword that controls the guy wielding it, and I know he doesn't care if the charms are powerful or not, but the power-gamers in the group can't because they'll have concepts designed for getting max power.**"

                                                            **I include myself in this group. I would not let myself play an Exigent.

                                                            As Aya Tari says:
                                                            the normal arguments associated with homebrewing
                                                            This is actually one of the things I dislike most about running Exalted (well, any games, but with Rogue Trader that I'm running now, it's not really something that's come up). Arguing with players about what their homebrew stuff should do. I found adjudicating sorcerous workings (which are, essentially, homebrew with guidelines, so a little similar to what Exigents are likely to be) quite irritating (the absolute worst thing is when the player eventually convinces me to let them do the working they want, but I nerf it in various ways so it's not too powerful, and they grumble that it's too weak, and then other players complain that I've let that player have something really overpowered and it's so unfair, grumble, grumble).

                                                            And Exigents seem like the same thing, but far, far more.
