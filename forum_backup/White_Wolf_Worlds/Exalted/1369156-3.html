                            Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post

                            Armies can be no bigger than you can afford.
                            This is a key point.

                            With a few exceptions, the issue for most states hasn't generally (except after really brutal) been that they don't have enough people to be soldiers. It's that they don't have enough money and food to support such an army. If we look at medieval armies (a time whose governments were extremely focused around the military), the number of soldiers is actually tiny compared to the population. They could have levied more troops... but they wouldn't have enough armour, weapons, and most importantly food for them.
                            Saying "women can join the army" might have let them levy slightly more people... but then those women aren't farming, repairing soldiers' clothes, farming, repairing their boots, farming, etc, etc.
                            Actually, most men weren't levied either, because then there's not enough farmers. (Also, bringing a horde of unskilled, untrained peasants isn't very effective compared to a smaller number of skilled professionals.)

                            The only time medieval leaders tended to get as many people as possible to fight was when their city was being stormed by an attacking army. And that was one of the small number of situations in which women actually did fight.

                            So Creation's more egalitarian armies might be slightly bigger, and a bit more effective, but there's still the key issue of what a leader can afford to put into the field.

                                                I've heard some suggestions that sort of a myth, and that the likes of the Scythians and Mongols probably really had a substantial "commoner" population which did not fight and generally provided logistical support, than is commonly thought.

                                                The Mongols' could mobilize more population than many comparable agricultural populations though. IMO that really does have more to do with the facts of life before the state though, really.
                                                Before you have big states in play, necessarily every young man (and perhaps young women in some instances), would have to have some degree of "military" experience, to defend their own property. What else can they do? There's no army, and there's no police to protect them against theft.

                                                Once big states are in play, the army and police (or watch or what have you), enforces the law and individuals no longer have to be trained.

                                                As well as this Malthusian tendencies mean that any spare capacity to mobilize tends to be used up. Primitive farmers tend to not indulge in too much farming effort, as they can't afford to, so pursue strategies that limit their population size but leave them plenty of time to fight. There's slack. But once peace is assured, people can afford to engage in a lot more high effort and committed agriculture, but end up with very little room to deviate spending a lot of time in this, without much time to train, and less time to go away and fight.

                                                So once all that's used up, groups like the Mongols often found it was actually more of a pretty even fight with very large agricultural empires than you might think, particularly if they were willing to recruit. Head-for-head, they could built pretty competitive sized armies, if they could unite.

                                                Pastoralist invading armies like the Mongol Hordes are, IMO, more like "invaders from the past, with modern technology", in their context.

                                                From what I understand, there were some states who were "civilized" and still went really hard for mobilizing big armies - the Roman Republic, the Warring States (particularly Qin), maybe the Greek city states. But they had to be pushed to do so by a pretty tense local geopolitical situation.
