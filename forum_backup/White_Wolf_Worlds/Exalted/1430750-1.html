        Originally posted by Kelly Pedersen View Post
        I'd note that the simultaneous actions stuff is all about when actions are resolved, not declared. In general, I'd just use whatever method would be used in any case to decide when the actions resolved, no matter who got to declare first.
        This is a good thing to keep in mind. I was trying to use the simultaneous action piece mostly in an attempt to triangulate the authorial intent on declaration, but you're right that they are not the same thing. At the end of the day I think the rules are conspicuously silent on how normal actions get declared; I say conspicuous because Charm usage is very specific (page 251; "Players must openly declare which Charms their characters are using, and all Charms (unless their text indicates otherwise) must be declared, and their costs spent, before any dice are rolled. Attackers declare their Charms before defenders."

        For those with the context, this is pretty clearly an unambiguous answer to old transparency arguments from previous editions on this very forum. It's just funny to me that a similar paragraph doesn't appear in the base combat engine too.

        Originally posted by Kelly Pedersen View Post
        So, for example, say that A and B are acting on the same tick, after A has delayed. B declares he is going to run across the rope bridge to the next platform in this Castlevania-esque battlefield. A declares in response that she is shooting a blazing arrow at the rope bridge in an attempt to destroy it and cause A to plumet into the void. I'd still have A and B make opposed rolls of some sort (probably (Wits + Athletics) for B vs. A's (Wits + Archery)) to figure out who ended up doing their thing first.
        Yeah, I could see running this as essentially the movement action equivalent of a Clash, if that felt right in the moment. That said rules-as-written I think this is pretty clearly covered by that sidebar; two characters act on the same tick, but whose turn resolves first matters, so you either decide amicably or (in this case) coin toss. But in this case, if A goes first B should get to change their mind about what they were doing; their previously declared action (running across the rope) is no longer valid (because the rope is gone).

        Since A's intent is pretty clearly to cause harm to B by shooting the rope out while B is on it, I would probably skip the coin flip, and instead say that A's action is really a Gambit directed at A. I'd probably model it after "Unhorse" assuming the result would be A prone on the ground.

            Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
            Also, whats the lowest you can actually delay to? If someone's Crashed at -3, and I want to delay to Clash them, it's not like there's an inherent bottom number here. Setting "one last tick" is a house rule. Probably not a bad one, but it's less important if you can't continue a delay after you say you want to act that tick but your target delays instead of acting.
            I don't see it as a house rule, more like the only sensible limit to the "waiting game" function, as intransigence approaches infinity.

            Ticks just control who is eligible to act. If everyone is already eligible, and no one is doing anything, then advancing the tick counter is meaningless. So defacto, this is the last tick; if you're all waiting until infinity, then you all act at the same infinity. Which is convenient, because you get to leverage the "agree amicably or flip a coin" rules to resolve the stalemate. Note my framing as "one last tick" is only for the garbage collection case where everyone is delaying indefinitely; as long as anyone is choosing to do anything, time still has meaning, ticks can still progress.

            Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
            Clash has a few things that probably need looked at that are particular to it. I mean, the reason why delay to Crash is something that's even on people's radars is how effective it is to attack and defend with on roll on your mote economy.

            I'm not sure it's a high priority, mostly because it feels like, to me, to more of a white-room issue. If I'm the ST and my players are delaying to Crash all the time, it's not hard for me to shut that down in any number of ways if I think it's being abused: I can stat my NPCs however I want, and I can just talk to my players if they're relying on one combat strategy so much it's getting unfun for me (whether that means house rules or they want me to send them against stuff that works within the framework set in the books to counter it).

            Even with a more solid right of way ruling, I think there's a big question of whether delay to Clash should be as good of a tactic as it stands.
            It's a fair point, particularly the mote-economy bits. I might even say that the fact that it is so readily addressable may make it a higher priority; down that road lies one-true-builds and the ghost of paranoia combat. But I digress.

                            Originally posted by Zen_r0b0 View Post
                            We choose fun over the letter of the law (See: Orichalcum and Golden Rules, EX3 pg. 182), and work together toward the best outcome for everyone, so we don't decide things in secret. This is thanks to a lot of time playing together, and would be harder to do with unknown quantities who might abuse such lenience.
                            And under no circumstances would I suggest you do anything else at your table!

                            The way I see it is this. Understanding the letter of the law, as you call it, serves two purposes and the first is the Golden Rule itself. The "executive override" rules are in reference to the other rules; that's what they override! Invoking the Golden Rule is saying "I know the rules and I chose to change them because it was better for my group this way." It covers deliberate choices. Doing something fun without knowing that the rules say something different is also totally cool and no one should tell you otherwise, but it's not the Golden Rule's department. Given all that, anything that helps folks understand the rules helps them know how to change them for their group. Note that the rest of page 182 talks about learning the rules

                            FWIW the second is communication at a scale bigger than your personal table. When we're having a conversation about the rules (or when we're creating content to share with one another, or...), the rules-as-written are the common ground. Deviating from them is totally fine at your table (and supported within the rules by the Override rules), but it's useful to call out those deviations as such when they apply, or we can end up talking past each other. Case in point, your local house rules (and your local choices for how to fill in ambiguities in the rules) that govern who declares their actions when might make a big difference if you're discussing how powerful delaying to provoke a Clash is as a tactic, where this thread started.

                                                        First off, thank you so much for your help thinking this through. Sorry if it seems like I'm talking past you but your post gave me a new perspective so I'm kinda coming from there more than responding directly.

                                                        So the thing I'd focus on from the simultaneous action sidebar's example isn't that they're allies, it's that both characters still get to take their intended action as planned. There's no question about invalidating or changing actions, as you say the only thing being rolled for is who gets the Initiative Break bonus. This may be a logical leap, but I'd infer that the spirit is that simultaneous actions don't interfere with one another's execution, they can only interfere with each other's ultimate resolution. If I wanted to codify this in strict rules I'd say something like "the effects of actions taken during resolve at the end of the tick", or "simultaneous actions are taken simultaneously, but if absolutely necessary the resolution can be serialized in whatever order the participants agree to with a coin flip as tie breaker".

                                                        This way, amicable agreement even between opponents is totally believable for most cases, because where things end up has already been determined and you're just deciding the coolest way to describe how it played out. The only time you'd go to roll-off is if the order makes a mechanical difference in future ticks, like who gets the Initiative Break. And that kind of thing is usually going to be between allies because the rules are generally pretty crisp vis a vis opponents.

                                                        I think this is supported by Clashes being the one special exception, since it cuts back the number of times a same-tick duel would end up with both combatants dead, as would be the case were they handled with "normal" simultaneous actions (). I wouldn't be surprised if this was the initial rationale for Clashes before someone got the idea to also use them to model counterattacks. EDIT: And, I think it's supported by how stunts work, which is to say that the stunt the player described (and got the bonus for) is what they are attempting to do, but may not be what actually happens, if the resulting dice roll doesn't back it up. This is just that, but factoring in other players' simultaneous actions too.

                                                        Say we have A and B (who are allies) and Z (their opponent). A and Z act on the same tick, B acts later (or has already gone, doesn't matter).

                                                        Z launches a decisive at B. A doesn't like this but he's not close enough to Defend B. So instead, he shoots at Z with a decisive. Like in the rope-running example, in principle if A "goes first" and kills Z, Z won't get a chance to attack B. But A isn't going first, they are going at the same time. So no conflict, no roll-off; Z gets to attack B as planned, and A gets to attack Z as planned, and then at the end of the tick we see who is still standing.

                                                        Or perhaps even simpler. A and B go on the same tick and both want to attack Z, who acts later. Z is low on health and A and B (after discussing it) both choose to hit him with Decisives. Unless there is some sort of Abyssal-style effect that cares about who dealt the killing blow, there isn't actually a conflict. A's decisive may be irrelevant if B rolls well enough to kill Z, but it's not invalid. It still "happens" if anyone cares to roll it out. The possibility was inherent in the strategy; one of them could have chosen to Feint, or spend 2i to Delay until the outcome was known so as to use their action differently if Z died. But they didn't, presumably for some good tactical reason, and here we are.

                                                        Originally posted by Kelly Pedersen View Post
                                                        I agree it should be a gambit, I'd just have the contested roll first, or rather, have the contested roll be the question of whether A can roll initiative for the gambit or not.
                                                        Upon reflection and in consideration of the above (granted, with the kind of thinking time I wouldn't have in the moment at the table!) I would just let the Gambit be "knock B down to the ground", and "shoot the rope" is a good contextual stunt. But both actions get rolled and happen as planned. I'd call for the tiebreaker (probably the contest you outlined, to keep it interesting) only if the players need help deciding how to describe the aftermath. Maybe A shoots out a support under the second platform after B does their thing instead of burning the rope. Or maybe if that second action didn't strictly need to be made from the second platform, maybe A suggests that B actually did it from the rope on the way, or from the air while falling... whatever the table thinks is the coolest way to let both things happen. If A really wanted to prevent B from doing whatever it was, he should rolled a higher initiative.
