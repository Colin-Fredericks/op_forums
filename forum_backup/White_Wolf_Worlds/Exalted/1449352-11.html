    Originally posted by Ncolic View Post
    I just realized something about ExEss combat system.

    There is now an opportunity cost baked into making a withering attack due to introduction of Build power action. (I really like the build power idea btw).

    We will understand more once charms are available, but as it stands now assuming you can use the same dice pools for both, it is more beneficial use build power than attack with your weapon the harder / skilled your opponent is.

    For Example; As the Dawn of the circle lets say you have 10 dice to attack with your sword and 10 dice to move athletically.

    If you are fighting Crimson Dusk of the better days to bury, who has a defense of 7. It is much easier to beat the default difficulty of 3 for the build power (which your opponent cant do anything about) than try to hit him with your sword like a good little sword princess. So choosing to make a withering attack is almost certainly is a bad decision in combat, or a trap choice if you will.
    There are still some points to consider though. Having 10 power is useless if you can't hit your opponent, and using withering to use Concentrated Attacks (formerly onslaught) is a way to hit the opponent more easily. The point about Overwhelming has already been made. Atracking the ennemy can also force him to consume motes, while BP doesnt cost the enemy anything at all from a resource perspective.

    I think if necessary it shouldntm't be too difficult to slightly twist Build Power actions to increase Base Diffiuclty : if 3 is too low, just getring it to 4 or 5 should be enough for most purposes.

            My main issue is less that Withering attacks are always weaker than Build Power, as that which is more powerful is a boring calculation to make.
            I can, given knowledge of my pools, my weapon, and the enemy's defense, calculate pretty easily what the optimal action to gain power is.
            In fact, I can when building a character calculate the value of defense where the average switches over. There's a bit of depth in gaining for yourself/giving to others, and in amount of randomness, but it's generally easy.
            Withering attacks should always be directed at the lowest-defense enemy on the field, there's nothing but defense that affects this.
            I worry that at the start of the fight you can determine the one or two actions you'll be taking (something to gain power, and decisive attacks if you aren't feeding it to an ally), and calculate when you'll use either based your power and nothing else

            In contrast, in 3e you need to think about whether to try to reduce an enemy's initiative to prevent a decisive, whether to try to crash someone, how to avoid being crashed yourself, etc. Your decisions are based the interactions of the initiative of everyone on the field.

            I get that it's simpler, but it lost all of the depth that 3e combat had, and still isn't exactly simple.

            Charms and gambits and whatever might add something, but I'd be a lot more confident in the ability of charms to be interesting if I was more confident in the base.

            I think I've probably let pessimism take hold here, and tomorrow I'll probably be optimistic. I also haven't actually played any fights, so take it with a grain of salt.

            I do really like that you can build power for allies, but I worry that you'll always just build power for the person who's best at hitting with decisive attacks

                    A lot of what I'm worried about is what it means to "ignore" the Deathlord. What else can you do? You can try to kill them with decisive attacks, but if you could do that you'd do that. You can try to get a wound penalty on them, or hit them with gambits, but a) those are slow, and b) those feel much less dynamic to me than withering attacks. There's no change over time in how effective they are, gambits you either have enough power or not.

                    I think that the war rules are confusing partially because they are an extension of the venture rules, but before where it explains venture rules.

                    How I understand it:
                    A war is a sequence of opposed rolls, separated by time and potentially by dramatic scenes.
                    The number of opposed rolls is (highest War among all leaders)+1
                    Each opposed roll is (some attribute based on stunt)+War+modifiers
                    After each opposed roll, the winner counts their extra successes
                    At the end, the person with the most total extra successes wins

                    Dramatic scenes are like, the highlight reel of the war.
                    About 1/main opposed roll, you have a Dramatic Scene
                    Both people pick what they want this scene to be, then the leader of each side rolls (some attribute+ability appropriate to the scene they picked), and the winner's scene happens
                    Then you run that scene, possibly modified by the scene-picking roll, and whoever wins the scene gets bonus dice on their next opposed roll
