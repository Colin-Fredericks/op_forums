                    Originally posted by Einlanzer View Post
                    Can someone give me some good examples of how to use Lore to introduce a fact? I feel like I can't wrap my brain around how to use this without it being rather dumb or just making something up on the fly.
                    Well, "making it up on the fly" pretty much is the point of introducing facts - the whole idea is that the player gets a bit of narrative control to introduce something to the setting (subject to GM veto). So, for example, say the party is crossing the mountain range between the southwest jungles and the Dreaming Sea, heading for Volivat. The Lore-expert with a specialization in "Geography" could roll to introduce a fact by saying "I've read descriptions of this area in my texts, and there should be a pass near here that comes out close to our destination". If they're successful, the pass is there, and their journey gets easier.

                    Or say that the party needs to find an elemental for whatever reason. The Twilight with the Lore speciality of "Spirit courts" could roll to say "there are many wood elementals in the nearby forest, although they're rather hostile to humans, generally."

                    And so on. Basically, introducing a fact can really be anything that's a) reasonably covered by a speciality that the character has in Lore, and b) that would improve their situation somehow. The GM can veto suggestions that completely short-circuit the plot, but most will just make it easier for the characters to accomplish their goals, but not trivially so.

                                    Originally posted by Epee102 View Post


                                    I have adoptd the "this would be a good time for an introduce fact" when their stuck on a puzzle, or ask me a question about if something exists.

                                    Yeah, this is good advice. Don't be afraid to remind your players of what they can do.

                                    Other suggestions:

                                    1) Make sure your players understand that you're willing to let them co-create the setting. This includes both telling them so, and being lenient about what sort of details you allow them to roll to introduce facts on, both difficulty-wise and in what you outright veto. Say, for example, the party is entering a small nation in the Hundred Kingdoms. If the Lore-specialist decides to roll to introduce a fact that the state is a mercantile republic, with a lot of stuff that's outright for sale (and thus easy to bribe their way in), and you had originally conceived of the place as a despotic monarchy, you should think hard before vetoing that declaration. Try to be open to the idea of revising your plans for the campaign to fit introduced facts.

                                    2) As sort of an adjunct to #1, there, be willing to offer suggestions to gently edit the players' suggestions, rather than outright veto them. So, using the first example, say you decide you absolutely can't live without that despot you were envisioning. So, instead of totally vetoing the "merchant republic" declaration, suggest that you'd be okay with changing the suggestion to the place being a monarchy, but with a strong noble caste largely derived from great merchant houses, and the profit motive still being strong. The player gets to keep the effect they were looking for (easily-bribed people), and you get to keep your plot on track.

                                    3) Make sure to mechanically reward creative fact introductions. This can be as simple as awarding stunt bonuses for introductions that give more detail or provide more interesting hooks for the plot, but larger rewards are easy to work in as well. The Twilight role bonus for Solar XP is the most obvious one ("solving a significant problem or crisis through the application of knowledge..."), but pretty much any Caste can benefit from introducing a fact that then furthers their goals. A Dawn Caste, for example, could introduce a fact about an enemy army's culture ("they have a great fear of the nights of the new moon, due to centuries of predation by spirits tied to Luna"), then use that fact to win a great battle (by, say, attacking at night on the new moon).
