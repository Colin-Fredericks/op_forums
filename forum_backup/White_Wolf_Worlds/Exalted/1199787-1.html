                With the onset of the Great Contagion and the broad collapse of urbanized society across Creation, the economies of the survivors may be quite different than what a person living in ‘modern day’ Creation would expect to see. Below are some of the trade goods one might expect to see across Creation in the aftermath of the Great Contagion.

                Precious Metals: specie, bullion, and other forms

                Before the Interregnum, the Shogunate had a Creation-wide series of specie (in gold and silver and bronze), the value of which was tied to the price of staple agricultural goods and artificially controlled through government purchases of said goods. These valued over the course of their existence, mainly due to climate change between the Principate and the Dominate, until they completely collapsed during the Interregnum.

                The most common of these coins were the silver Denarii, valued at a kilogram of wheat or 3 kilograms of barley at the height of its value (and usually measured as a ‘standard’ day’s wages for a common laborer), and the bronze Assari, valued at 1/10th a Denarii, with multiple denominations. Other values were retained as bullion and used as units of account instead of currency

                During the Interregnum, with the fracturing of the Shogunate Provinces into competing realms and the accompanying breakdown of stable trade and infrastructure, the value of Shogunate specie took a nosedive. Between the different Provinces minting their own, usually disgustingly debased currencies, and the ceasing of controlled values of goods, the coinage of the Interregnum was not worth very much at all, with supplies of grain often taking several times its worth in denarii to purchase, when it was available at all.

                After the Contagion, the value of specie and bullion wasn’t helped by the fact that there was just so much available relative to the surviving population. There was no more governing body to remove excess coinage from the market, nor control the value of the coins.

                To combat this, most survivors do not accept pure coinage as currency, instead relying solely on the weight of the metal as its value. This process, and the accompanying bits and pieces of metal, is commonly called ‘hacksilver” after the most common examples, and is a common means of exchange in the North to this day, outside of the larger, more centralized states. Traders will measure out specific weights of silver (or other metals), and if a piece of silver weighs too much for the transaction, the trader simply cuts up the piece to get the correct weight.

                Generally speaking, most hacksilver across the North had the following “exchange rates”, keeping in mind that many, if not most, trading was done without seeing a single piece of silver.
                Hacksilver was used for large-value trading, between tribes and as tribute, not the small-time exchanging of shiny-bits between neighbors
                • 1 ounce of silver (Skytongue: aurar) = approx. 30 or so grams. 1 Shogunate Denarii usually weighed about 3 grams, regardless of the actual silver content
                • 8 ounces of silver = 1 ounce of gold
                • 8 ounces of silver = 4 milk cows
                • 8 ounces of silver = 24 sheep
                • 8 ounces of silver = a bolt of wool cloth 72 meters long by 1 meter wide
                • 12 ounces of silver = a healthy adult male slave
                • 4 ounces of gold = a pattern welded sword
                Values were measured in the ounce and the pound, the latter of which is still widely used across the North as a measurement of land-value in the Hide: a hide is the amount of land that will produce goods, almost always agricultural, equaling a pound of silver in value over the course of a year

                (Yep, shit be expensive, especially weapons and armor.. In 8th century England, a coat of mail cost the modern equivalent of about £11,000, while a sword cost about £2,000 for a no-frills blade. A hide of land was worth about £5,000/year.)

                Iron

                Iron is by far the most important metal in human history, and the collapse of society after the Great Contagion is no different. Without iron, there would be no plows to plant grain, sickles to harvest it, nor spears to defend it.

                During the Shogunate, most iron was sourced from shaft-mines, often state-run and staffed by slaves (with hideous costs in human lives) and refined in advanced refineries, resulting in high-quality iron ore, and by proxy tools and weapons, that were relatively inexpensive. After the collapse of the Shogunate, most iron was sourced much more locally, in far smaller amounts, and in much lower qualities.

                In the North, most post-Shogunate iron was sourced from bogs and marshes, which, unlike mines, were sustainable sources, but in very low quantities. In addition, the less-effective bloomeries used to refine said ores were small, meaning finished iron was available only in small quanitites, and took a large amount of labor and time to produce said iron. As a result, most Northern households had, on average, about 100 pounds of iron goods, most of which was tools and cooking equipment.

                Iron weapons and armor were therefore of immense value, and usually passed down from generation to generation until they were no longer usuable. Weapons that used little iron, such as spears and axes, were the most common, while swords, helmets and mail armor were rare and highly sought-after.

                Areas that had lots of bogs and marshes were highly prized as sources of iron ore, and regions where iron mines could be found were, with little exaggeration, worth fighting and dying over.

                In trade, iron was often worth its weight in silver, if not more, due to its direct usefulness. Finished iron bars, axeheads, knife blanks , etc, all were eagerly traded between surviving peoples.

                Salt

                Salt is the most common form of food preservation across Creation, making salt extremely valuable. While smoking, drying, jugging and pickling are all known, none are as simple and as effective as salting.

                That makes salt that much more valuable in the aftermath of the Contagion, and regions where salt can be found that much more worth finding (and by proxy, keeping out of the hands of others.)

                Food:

                With the breakdown of urban infrastructure and logistics, those who wanted to survive were forced into the countryside and made to learn farming. Foodstuffs are the most common trade-good seen post-Contagion, and even though iron and salt may be worth more due to rarity, foodstuffs are still extremely valuable, arguably the most valuable trade good.

                Everyone has to eat, after all.

                In the North, most foodstuffs take the form of dried grains (wheat, oats, barley, rye, maize), preserved root and leaf vegetables (cabbage, onions, potatoes, turnips, carrots, etc), dried legumes (beans and peas), gourds (pumkpins and squashes) and fruit (most commonly apples, but also pears, cherries, peaches and grapes). The trade in alcohol is also common, as it serves as an effective means of preserving grain or fruit for long periods, as well as making water safe to drink.

                Livestock

                Livestock are valued not just for their meat, but also their milk, their hide and fiber, their horn and bone, and for their labor. The most valuable Northern livestock is the cow, but horses aren’t that far behind, and pigs are the most commonly eaten. The most common by far, however, is the sheep and goat, both of which are often kept in the same pastures.

                The milk from a single cow over a year can keep a family alive through the winter, and the wool from sheep can be easily the most valuable thing a household produces. This is especially important for migrating peoples, since they often do not have the luxury of settling down and putting a crop or two of grain into the ground. Some tribes measure their wealth in the heads of cattle they possess..... and livestock stealing is often a target for young men to raid, eager to attain wealth and status, since the livestock are so valuable on top of being able to walk themselves.

                Cloth and Furs:

                Nobody wants to be naked, even in the warmer parts of Creation. In the North, wool is the most common cloth seen, followed by linen and nettlecloth and hemp canvas. Cloth is exceedingly labor-intensive to produce (the average weaver can produce about half a square yard of cloth a day, with the thread for that day of weaving usually taking about a day and a half to produce), making it high in value, and in many households, the women that produce the cloth are often the ‘breadwinners’ of the family, making them indispensable and giving them a great deal of influence. Most cloth is spun using drop-spindles and woven on warp-weighted looms.

                For trading purposes, cloth is measured in ells, a Northern unit of length that roughly corresponds to the length of the forearm, or about 20 inches.

                Asides from cloth, the trade in furs is also brisk. In the frigid North, the pelts of fur-bearing animals are prized for their ability to retain warmth even in the face of the hardest wind. All sorts of pelts are traded, from rabbit to beaver to wolf to bear.

                Other goods

                Of course, the above are not the only goods traded. Locally-produced goods like lumber, pottery, baskets, etc are valuable.

                Economies:

                With the collapse of the urban, semi-industrialized Shogunate economy, and accompanying cash-money based currency, most surviving groups fell back on barter and gift-based economies. This is especially common in the North, where most tribal groups already used gift-based systems internally before the Contagion, and still use them today. Generally, speaking, barter was used with people external to the group, while gift-giving was used internally.

                When I say ‘barter”, I really mean trade based on commodity currencies, like what I listed above, instead of a government-backed fiat currency. Silver is generally the medium of exchange, with goods being roughly valued in their worth in silver, but most transactions occur without hacksilver trading hands, only the direct goods.

                Gift-based economies may seem a little weird to the modern observer, so I’m going to try to explain the concept as much as I can, keeping in mind that I am not an economist.

                Let us take, for example, a Swadian clan. The clan-chief is granted a parcel of land, measured in hides (aka, a hide of land is expected to produce goods equal in value to a pound of silver in a year) by the Tribal chief. That, in and of itself, is an example of a gift-based economy: The tribal chief grants land to the clan-chief, in return for assumed support in war and peace.

                Let us go on.

                The clan-chief, in turn, divides out the parcel of land to the subsidiary families of the clan, with the largest parcels generally going to the chief’s primary supporters. In return, the subsidiary families support the chief by supporting the chief in peace and war, and by paying of tribute so the chief can support an armed retinue for their defense.

                In between the families of a clan, there is also gift-giving. Different families may focus on different products and processes. These families make gifts of their produce to each other, so that the clan survives and prospers.

                Example: you have one Swadian family who makes pottery. These pottery pieces are given out to the members of the clan, “free of charge”, and in return, the potter-family doesn’t have to worry where their food comes from. So on and so forth. Outside of the clan, the pottery will be ‘sold’ (bartered, for commodities), not given freely.

                Other gifts include the giving of weapons and armor, the fostering of children, the arranging of marriages, the supporting and patronage of skilled labor, and even the giving of loyalty

                For example, let us take Young Dude Swadian, a member of a clan. He is 16, and a man by the standards of his people. According to Swadian law, all men between 16 and 60 must be armed. Since he is of free status, his parents gift to him a seax, the signifier of freedom in the North (seaxes are to be carried everywhere, and to take away someones seax is to make them a slave), but since they can’t afford other weapons, the clan-chief will symbolically present Young Dude with a shield and spear, to signify he is now an adult and ready to take on adult responsibilities within the clan. Young Dude –and- his parents now owe the clan-chief a debt of loyalty for the gift, and may offer some form of tribute in thanks.

                Generally, gifts are intended to be ultimately reciprocal, not one-sided. You don’t have to repay the gift immediately, but it’s expected that the gift is returned at some point. Failing to reciprocate a gift, or even reciprocating with a gift in return that is felt to not be equal in value to the original gift, is a good way to cause a falling-out between the parties, if not an outright blood-feud if the insult is strong enough.

                For example: Young-Dudes mother is known to be a highly-skilled weaver, and she offers to take on the youngest daughter of another family as an apprentice. The other family now owes Young Dude’s mom a gift in return, and to repay the gift, they offer to arrange a marriage between Young Dude and their unmarried cousin, who comes from a poor family and isn’t that skilled. Young Dude’s family is enraged, and, to save face, the other family then offers their eldest daughter in marriage, as well as a parcel of their families land.

                The act of gift-giving can be traced all the way up to the top of Swadian society, and influences most social interactions. Political support for a crucial proposition in the tribal Things or even the Althing are common forms of gift-reciprocation among the Swadian nobility, as well as marriages, the ceding of lands, so on and so forth.

                            Northern Warfare: Aims, Methods and Means





                            -Evidence of Northern Warfare:

                            Most descriptions of warfare in the North come from three broad sources: the saga-songs written by the Northerners themselves, the literary accounts of the Shogunate and Shogunate Successor-States, both military and civilian records, and in physical evidence found in the grave-goods exhumed from warrior-burials found across the North and East.
                            • Saga-Songs: these sources, found in both oral histories and in written records (often in the form of runestones commemorating an event), are the most common descriptions of Northern warfare, but also some of the least useful, being often-fragmentary and written in poetic, often euphemistic, verse relying on insular cultural concepts as opposed to more descriptive terms.
                            • Shogunate/Post-Shogunate records: Surprisingly, both the Shogunate and the Successor-States neglected to record much in the way of information about the Northern ways of warfare. What few records exist are broadly denigrating, either describing both mass-combat tactics and individual combat styles in forms that, through both physical evidence and the studies of current Northern combat, were discovered to be greatly exaggerated, or, alternatively, describe mainly the aftermath of raids on civilian populations long after the Northern warriors retired from the field.
                              • It is important to note, perhaps, that most of these records date close to or after the Interregnum, when the Legionary system was breaking down and largely unable to respond effectively to examples of Northern aggression. Most military records detail Shogunate defeats, with military authors perhaps showing bias and attempting to downplay their own culpability in their defeat, overstating their opponents savagery and underhandedness. Civilian accounts were often taken “after the fact”, neglecting to consider the effects of trauma on the recollection of such an event.
                              • In the few pre-Interregnum records that exist, mainly when the Northern warriors were fighting as allies to the Legions, shows that most Northern cultural groups fought with at least a modicum of discipline. It is unknown if this style of warfare was native to the North, or if Northern Legionary veterans (Northerners made up a surprisingly high number of Legionary recruits) brought the ideas of discipline back with them. They describe their allies as fighting in a shieldwall, as well as being skilled at the charge, albeit the mass-charge, not the individual “scream and run” described in later accounts.
                            • Grave-good physical evidence: The most useful forms of evidence, but by far the least common, both the equipment found in burials as well as the condition of any bones that remain often give indications to how the warrior fought in life, as well as how they possibly died. Keep in mind that in many cases, only the rusted remains of iron can be found, with wood, leather, bone and other organic materials long deteriorated.
                              • Shields (as indicated by their surviving bosses) appear to have been used by all warriors. Earlier graves seem to suggest that shields were smaller in size, indicating that formations were not so close, while later shields appear to be larger, about 1 meter in width, implying mass-combat was more common. Most shields were round, but in areas heavily influenced by the Shogunate, oval shields are common.
                              • Seaxes (that is, the pan-cultural Northern knife) appear in all non-slave graves, men and woman, adult and child. Either as a utility-tool or a weapon of war, it is unknown, but the larger examples would be a handy reserve weapon
                              • Spears, both thrusting versions and javelins, were the most common non-seax weapon found, in both noble and commoner graves. It is unknown if these represent weapons of war or hunting tools, but if the North follows the convention seen Creation-wide, it is likely they were both.
                              • Axes were the most common hand-weapon. Stereotypically associated with the North, axes were used for both melee and throwing, as evidenced by physical evidence (stained soil or wood residues) in graves. “Throwing” axes exhibit handles around a foot long, “melee” axes about 2 feet, and axes suited for both somewhere in between. Axeheads are small, single-bitted and tending to have short cutting edges and weighing only about a dozen or so ounces, as opposed to the gigantic double-bitted monstrosities described in post-Shogunate accounts.
                              • Swords, helmets, and metal armors (mail is the most commonly found, but lamellar is found in areas influenced by the Shogunate) are exceptionally rare, being found in only approximately 1% of all graves. This indicates that the shield was the primary form of defense for most warriors, at least going on what physical evidence remains.
                              • Most injuries appear to occur to the legs, arms and head of warriors, with the shield serving as a very effective defense. Wounds to the torso are rare, and are found mainly in the back, suggesting they occur mainly when a formation is broken and individuals attempt to flee and are cut down.
                            -Battlefield Tactics
                            • The shieldwall: The “typical” battle involved both sides forming shieldwalls to protect against the launching of missiles, and standing slightly out of range of each other.
                              • While many, if not most, Northern saga-songs invoke the sight and sound of shieldwalls crashing into each other, it would appear that, based on the fighting styles of current Northern groups, that each shieldwall would make a large effort to gain as much of a terrain advantage over the other as possible. Many “real world” battles were decided by one side withdrawing once the commander realized they could not push terrain advantage over the opposing force. Common terrain advantages include: holding hilltops, occupying the space below a hilltop, with ranged support on top of the high ground, on opposing sides of a ford or bridge, or with one or both flanks covered by impassible or even only difficult terrain, such as a swamp, a stream , a cliff, etc.
                                • As much effort was spent attempting to gain terrain advantage as was spent in a battle. Forced marches were undertaken to secure a strategic ford or ridgeline, or to circumvent the same. Entire wars were won by one side securing a critical terrain advantage over the other, with the disadvantaged side either unable or unwilling to spend lives in effort to dislodge their opponents.
                              • Once battle was decided upon, it would appear that most shieldwall-clashes occurred in the following steps:
                                • Preliminaries: lines are drawn up and leaders give pre-battle inspirational speeches
                                • Advance to close quarters: a battle-cry is given, and one or both shieldwalls advance
                                • Exchange of missiles: As the range between shieldwalls close, skirmishers exchange slings-stones, arrows, javelins and axes, in effort to wound or kill opponents and disrupt the formation.
                                  • Ideally, enough damage was done to the enemy shieldwall through missile attacks, so that any melee combat was merely a ‘mopping up’ operation instead of a risky back-and-forth. Depending on the ranged weapons used and the discipline and morale of the opponent, this could be common or all-but impossible.
                                  • It is unknown just how much focus archery was given. Based on the physical evidence found in graves as well as the saga-songs, the javelin was the most common ranged weapon, and, according to the both the saga-songs and witness accounts, melee was generally preferred by the average Northerner. However, bows, being made of wood and horn and sinew, all materials that degrade, probably have not survived the intervening centuries, and there are several culture heroes (including Hjartr Arrow-Odd, who famously killed 20 men with as many arrows in one of the sagas), as well as law-codes that make reference to the use of bows and arrows, evidently expecting the listeners to be familiar with the terminology. It is a worthwhile guess to assume Contagion-Era Northern archery to be similar to modern-day Northern archery, wide-spread, but used for hunting and home-defense as opposed to open warfare in most cases.
                                • Shieldwalls connect: one or both sides close the gap and attacks, using shock weapons (spears, swords, knives and axes) and the shield in effort to collapse the enemy line. If the enemy line holds, one or both sides withdraws to rest, and the exchange of missiles continues again.
                                  • It would appear that there were two main “methods” to breaking the shieldwall of an opponent, dependent on equipment.
                                    • If a shieldwall is armed with spears, more space would be left between shieldwalls, to allow for the front row to attack.
                                    • If shorter weapons are used, shieldwalls will advance until the shields actually touch (described in the saga-songs as a ‘crash of thunder’, so that swords, axes and seaxes may be used
                                      • It would appear that both tactics could/would be used at different points in the same battle, depending on the conditions of both shieldwalls and available equipment.
                                  • Most bouts of close-quarters combat lasted only a few minutes, up to a quarter of an hour at the most. The effort was the break the foes morale and cause them to lose formation and flee, not to kill everyone outright. By fighting in short bouts, the combat effectiveness of your own shieldwall will be kept as high as possible, allowing for a pursuit when the enemy breaks and flees.
                                • Rout and pursuit: Eventually, one side would begin to give way, either from wounds, loss of morale (especially with the loss of a leader a warbanner, etc), or exhaustion. A last stand may be made by some, but most will tend to flee. The victors would pursue as much as they were able, killing and capturing as many as they could catch.
                                  • It is during this routing that it is the most dangerous for the victors. Saga-songs tell of victors routing an opposing shieldwall, breaking their own formation in order to chase, only to get crushed by reinforcements moving up or hidden in reserve.
                              • Individual combat: Most accounts go into great detail with individual combat, due to it being highly visible. They suggest that most individual combat occurred after a warrior left the safety of their own shieldwall to throw missiles.
                                • Individual warriors would leave their own shieldwalls in order to gain velocity for javelin and axe throws. This would leave them hideously vulnerable, as they left the cover of their own shieldwall and were fully open to a missile from the opposing side.
                                • If a warrior was killed in the ‘no-mans’land’ between shieldwalls, often someone from the opposing shieldwall would rush out to capture their highly valuable or useful equipment (shield, helmet, sword, extra missiles, etc). The one to do this would in many cases be the one who cast the fatal missile in the first place. Individual or groups of warriors could come to blows between their shieldwalls because of this.
                                • Any and all available weapons would be used during these fights, up and including unconventional tactics and improvised weapons (one saga-song recounts the tale of Grettir the Strong, a culture-hero, crushing skulls in an ambush by swinging a cauldron full of soup around by its handle, blinding opponents with the boiling liquid)
                            -The Horse in War
                            • While there are many references to the horse being used in saga-songs, the physical evidence doesn’t bear it out. The Northern breeds of horses are, to this day, largely small and stout, more large ponies as opposed to warhorses, and grave-evidence suggests it was much the same back during the Shogunate.
                            • Most Northerners fought as infantry, and while the nobility/elite often rode to war, it would appear that they too dismounted to fight.
                              • The horse was mainly used to maneuver the comparatively-heavily armored Northern elite around the battlefield, an arduous task if you have to walk it. On horseback, an elite retainer can quickly shore up a wavering battleline, flank around an enemies shieldwall, or charge a routing foe.
                            • A notable exception can be found in the Vlandians, a tribal confederation that moved to the banks of the Yellow River in -174 RY. There, they adopted the larger warhorse of the post-Shogunate successor kingdoms and ended up being very decent shock cavalry.
                            -Supplying War
                            • It is suspected that a good deal of the tribute passed up to tribal chieftains and elites from their sworn underlings was used to support their retained warbands, both in peace and in war.
                            • It is known that many/most Northern groups participated in raiding, the sacking of the countryside for food, wealth and slaves. This would allow them to live off the land of the enemy, at least for a while, as well as intimidate the local opposition.
                            • Most Northern groups require the ownership of arms by all free peoples (or, alternatively, the ownership of arms means a person is free), and therefore each warrior would be expected to supply their own arms and armor. A chieftain also might gift weapons and armor, especially luxury versions of the same, to retainers in return for their loyalty.
                            -Aims of War
                            • Northerners enacted warfare for many of the same reasons peoples of other Directions waged war, that is: for defense, for vengeance, for loot, for tribute, or for conquest
                              • Defense: It is said that ‘the best defense is a good offense” and one way to prevent yourself from being attacked is to hit them before they hit you. A raid on an enemy may remove their ability to attack you, as well as intimidating them with your strength
                              • Vengeance: The need for vengeance is a strong one seen across most cultures of the North, and closely ties in with the concept of honor, both individual and clan-based. If someone wounds you, or your clan, honor dictates you wound them back. No exceptions
                              • Loot: Even before the Contagion and its associated climate change, the North was one of the more inhospitable Directions in Creation. The land was poor in many places, and the people looked on the riches of others hungrily. Raiding an enemy and taking their stuff may ensure your family survives the harsh Northern winter.
                              • Tribute: Closely related with loot, waging war for tribute is common. Essentially, raiders would attack a region, stopping only when the owners would pay for the raiders to leave them alone.
                              • Conquest: Seen during the initial stages of the Contagion, when barbarians migrated across the River of Tears, this is when an entire people marches to war, in order to win more lands.
                                • It is important to categorize Northern warfare into two broad groups: raiding and declared war. The main difference is the intended result: booty, in the form of loot, slaves, materials or tribute, or land for settling
                                  • Raiding is when smaller groups of warriors attack enemies, usually on an unofficial basis. Whether young men banding together to raid a neighboring clans cattle, a clan gathering to enact bloody vengeance, or a tribal chieftain orders an enemies countryside harried in return for tribute, the attacks are usually unsanctioned by the higher leaders of the group (and may be reacted to with a wink and a nudge or outrage, depending on the consequences and relations between the parties). Stopping raids is usually very difficult, as they usually happen without the knowledge of leadership outside of the raiding party, and raids almost always do not constitute war, at least between Northern groups (they are viewed as the natural inclination of young men, who will stop once they get older and settle down, and serve as effective training in arms and combat. Tribal leaders will generally only step in if raiding between groups gets too violent.) Non-Northern groups tend to …… disagree.
                                  • Declared war, on the other hand, is when warfare is openly declared by a clan-chief, tribal-chief or King. This, essentially, declares ‘open season” on the opposing group, and can be very bloody, as the combatants see no need to restrain themselves and prevent undue violence and/or damaged relations. In many cases, ‘open warfare’ is indicated by an upswing in the occurrence and brutality of raids, as one group seeks to weaken the other before marching in and wiping them out.

                                As your companions gather and order the men of your warband into a cohesive whole, you stand apart, looking across the empty fields and running river to the abandoned town, more lost in thought than actually examining the site. As you turn back to your men, the wind changes direction with the oncoming twilight, shifting from the riverside, losing its tang of salt and taking on something….. unexpected. You freeze.

                                Woodsmoke.

                                Heartbeat hammering in your ears, you whip back to the town, eyes straining to catch a glimpse of something, anything, that might be burning….. there! From the center of town, a small, thind column of white woodsmoke rises into the sky, hidden against the backdrop of the building clouds. You wouldn’t have been able to spot it if you didn’t smell it beforehand.

                                Quickly, loud enough to attract the attention of the men closest to you, but not to carry,” Quickly, get off the shore, and drop to the ground!”

                                Confused, the men do as they are bid, and your retainers crawl up to you a few minutes later.

                                “What do you see, Rechila?”, hisses Hildebald.

                                “Smoke. Woodsmoke, and the cooking of food.”, you reply with relief. “Last I heard, the restless dead don’t cook their meat, so we are dealing with living people, and the town is clear.”

                                Fridr, another of your companions, chances a look above the line of waving grass, then ,”Or, it could be a dumbass refugee, that gets eaten as soon as the sun goes down.”

                                You think on that, and then shake your head, “I don’t think so. This area has been abandoned for, what, close to a century, and has gods-know how many refugees move through it? Refugees or not, they would know enough to avoid a potential haunting, and that town would be a good place to make camp if it weren’t haunted…….”

                                You think some more, glancing up at the slowly-sinking sun, then call up some of the skirmishers. You bid them to get as close to the town as they can, without being spotted, and try to catch a glimpse of who or what is making that smoke, and to report back before the sun went down. Silently, they slip off, the grass making no sound in their wake.

                                The wait is agonizing, and the sun is but a hands-breadth from the horizon (meaning, for those not in the know, there is about an hour of sunlight left. Useful wilderness survival trick), and you are just about to call for a general retreat when two of your scouts suddenly reappear, dragging a heavy burden behind them. As they get closer, the burden is revealed to be the unconscious figure of a man, roped by his ankles. He looks like, well, most other Easterners you’ve seen (not that you have seen many, Easterners being rare these days):,short, skin the yellowish color of the grass surrounding you, black hair and facial stubble. He is dressed in what approximates a….. military uniform? With a beige canvas tunic underneath a boiled rawhide lamellar vest and helmet. Around his waist is a baldric with an empty dagger sheath, and your skirmishers are carrying the blade, a spear and a shield. These they present to you, and as you look them over, the skirmishers talk of what they saw.

                                “Well, warlord, there are definitely people in the town. At least two dozen in the center of town, and sentries around the edges.”

                                “How long until this one is missed, or should we already be moving?”

                                The skirmisher grins, “At least two hours, after dark. They switch off every four, and the shift just changed when we came up on him. The main camp should be finishing up dinner about now.”

                                Hidebald frowns, “Single sentries, in unfamiliar territory? I know a stupid enemy is a gift from the gods, but are they totally incompetent?”

                                The scout shrugs, “It looks so. The main force in the center of town is completely unorganized. It isn’t even fortified! They are holed up inside a hall-looking building instead, and some of the troops were out of armor, playing some sort of ball-game!” He chuckles, and your companions exchange meaningful looks.

                                “That could either be really good, or really bad. We could have either stumbled across completely useless troops…. Or be in the center of their territory where they feel like they can lack picket-discipline.”, you muse.

                                Lugios interrupts, “Shield-brother, if we were in the center of their territory, I feel that the town would have been rebuilt already, or would be being rebuilt.” He turns to the skirmisher ,” Did you see any workers, any signs of construction?”

                                The scout shakes a negative, “No, my lord. We didn’t see any signs of building. The buildings are in good shape for a Contagion-ruin, but not recent good shape, if you catch my drift, and they don’t look lived in. Grass in the streets, roofs stripped of thatch down to the base level. Only the center-plaza and buildings are maintained.”

                                You take that in. “Tell me more of these troops.”

                                The man shrugs again, then points down to the slowly-awakening man. “They look like him, pretty much. Probably two dozen sentries around the town, most on the south and east-sides, another two dozen in the center plaza. Most armed like him, spears and shields, a few with bows, but not many. I didn’t see any horses. “

                                “Okay, excellent, but….. why bring him back with you?” gesturing at the bound sentry.

                                “Ordinarily, I wouldn’t warlord, but when we snuck up on him and dragged him into a bush, he must have heard us speaking Swadian, because he switched from whatever gibberish he was trying to talk in to Eridanian. Its awful, and I only got a word in ten, but he speaks our tongue, after a fashion. Thought you might want to talk to him.”

                                “Good. Good.” You turn your attention to the writhing man at your feet. “Drag him down to the riverbank, behind the strand of reeds.”

                                There, you order him stripped, then cold, salty riverwater dumped on his head. It takes but a single helmet-full to bring him to full awareness, and his shout of surprise is only stopped short by your foot pressed firmly onto his chest, forcing the air from his lungs. His eyes open as wide as saucers, even more so once you draw your sword and put the point under his chin. You let him sweat there a moment, in order to recollect just how to speak Eridanian.

                                (The Eridanians are a Northern people , sorta like the Kievan Rus, at least in ancestry, and Eridanian is largely based off Skytongue. There are several loanwords and inflections from both Low Realm [Low Realm proper doesn’t really exist yet, its still largely a dialect of High Realm) and Dragontongue [Dragontongue was the Low/High Realm of the East, a Greek to the Latin if you will, and died out to the Contagion], meaning Rechila has to parse out what he says, especially since it has been some time since he spoke it. The difference between Swadian-Skytongue and Eridanian-Skytongue is sorta like English and German, if German has more Greek influences, for those interested.)

                                “So.” You start, looking him full in the face. “I have been told you speak Eridanian. If so, nod if you can understand me.”

                                After a second, he nods, careful of the blade prickling his throat.

                                “Good”, removing the blade and lessening up the pressure on his chest. “I don’t have to tell you what will happen if you get too obnoxious, correct”?

                                “O-of course, my Lord.” He stumbles over the words, his fear not helping his thick accent.

                                “Wonderful. Now…. You are going to tell me everything about what is going on in that town. Where you are from, who sent you, how many warriors you have, and, most importantly, why you are here.”

                                And that was all it took for him to spill everything.

                                Apparently:
                                • The mans name was Artemas, and he was a soldier for the Shogunate successor-state of Turontum
                                • Turontum was a week and a half’s march to the East, beyond the mountains and on the banks of the Durian River
                                • There were 4 contubernium, or about 40 fighters, in the town, and they were there to watch on the movements of several large refugee camps located at the mouth of the Durian
                                • River, a few miles to the south. If the refugees moved en masse, warning was to be sent to the main Cohort in Turontum proper by messenger pigeon.
                                • Discipline was lax. Cohort I Occidentalis was, by all accounts, the smallest (half-strength), underappreciated (away from the main threats) and underequipped division of the Turontum Legion. This migrant-watching posting was viewed as a punishment-detail, and both the soldiers and the commander didn’t really give a damn.
                                • The commander of the post, Optio Vegetius Renatus, was a young, puffed-up noble from the capital, chosen for the rank not for his skills but for his connections. He mainly walked around shouting useless orders that nobody followed anyways, or stayed in his office. Other than that, the soldiers were largely left to themselves.
                                The man stammers his way through his description, then waits silently as you pace back and forth on the beach, cringing whenever you or another of your men look his way. Suddenly, he bursts out,

                                “Forgive me, my Lord, but…… there may be a way we both profit from this!”

                                As your men curse and put their weapons away, looking uneasily at the town, you walk up to him. Towering over him, lying on the ground as he is, you ask, in a quiet voice.

                                “What do you mean, profit?”

                                “W-well, there are the refugees to the south of us, and the whole reason we are here is to keep an eye on their movements and prevent them from moving into Turontum proper. I-If you…..if you were to take out these refugees, we would be able to go home, and I am sure you would be paid very well.”

                                You raise an eyebrow, “You have enough something in that abandoned settlement to be worth my mens while?”

                                He freezes, realizing the implications, “N-No, my Lord. You would have to go see the Dux himself, at the Castra Sinica, but…. he has issued public proclamations of the threat refugees and migrants portray to the settled realm, so I am sure he would be willing to offer whatever sort of payment you want. Money, food, land! Gods only know there is enough land now…. Hells, he might even offer you a long-term contract, to fight off the Khergit raiders!”

                                “….Khergit raiders? Who are they?”

                                “Horse nomads, Lord. They live to the East, and the South, and they raid anywhere their horses take them. We try to bargain with them ,pay them in silver and cattle and iron, but they just keep coming. There are a line of border-forts, along the eastern edge of Turontum, but they just slip around, to the south and the north, and nowhere in Turontum is safe! In fact, the Dux has a bounty on Khergit scalps, an ounce of silver a head. Easy money!”

                                You don’t reply, stepping back from the man to look out over the grass. You could do something about these migrants for these pretend-Shogunate fools, get in their good graces so that when your people arrive, they meet friends and not potential enemies. On the other hand….. you doubt they would be happy to hear about almost 15,000 people arriving on their western shore. On the other other hand…… it really sounds like these people already have their hands full, and your people could carve out their own territory without too much issue. However, the Khergit nomads sound like they could be a problem.

                                You:
                                1. Offer to meet with the commander, to talk terms about doing something about the migrant-camps. “As the saying goes, take me to your leader!”
                                2. Move to take out the post. You don’t want anyone knowing your people are going to arrive soon , much less are already here.
                                3. Leave. You have too much to do to get caught up in local squabbles. You will just have to find a different place for your people to land.
