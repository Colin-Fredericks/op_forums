        To me, the far more concerning thing about superhero media - Marvel and DC - seems to be the relatively consistent message that rocking the boat makes you a bad person, even if the boat is absolutely awful.

        ​Gotham is an absolute hole, and everyone who tries to clean things up and isn't named "Bruce Wayne" either is revealed to be a villain, or ends up becoming one. (And for all the millions that Bruce Wayne has supposedly spent on improving Gotham, it's still a hole.)

        Reed Richards has a cure for cancer, but he refuses to share it, because "we're not ready yet"; scientists who actually try and make things better end up getting corrupted by their experiments and mutate into monsters, or have to rob banks to fund their research.

        It seems the primary way you can have power and be a good person in superhero fiction is to either be born with it or acquire it entirely by chance, and then only fight to keep things pretty much the same - if you work to acquire your power or try to use your power to change things, there's a good chance that you're a morally reprehensible person.

        Is this surprising to see a message like that being amplified by a company that owns 40% of the world's entertainment? Probably not. Is it still a problem? Oh, hell yes.

                            Re; Sundar and Alucard's comment, I don't know about the contrast between popularity of manga and American comics (I mean, could be right, maybe not - I wouldn't really know!). Fully do get the impression from little samples that a lot of American superhero comics (possibly unrepresentative samples!) do go in weird places as narratives from this frame of being oriented around conflicts between extremely super-powerful long established characters who can't really die or change or change their world, or even really change each other.

                            It seems like you often have these long "epic" overblown fight scenes interspersed with a long accompanying monologue that either expresses what a character *means* to the author (Superman expressing his Superman-ness, etc.) or serves as an excuse for an author to expound on their own personal philosophical musings on the nature of X (life, humanity, 'the Good and the Evil', parenthood, dignity and triumph in the face of adversity, etc). For several pages of panels.

                            It seems then like sometimes this does not really accompany *any* idea of what purpose this character or conflict really serves or means in a wider story where the author really has something to say subtly through subtext and themes (rather than just shouting or preaching their ideas at you through a monologue!). Rather it just serves to express the character's brand personality, or it serves as an blood pumping epic conflict accompanied by an often feel good "totally awesome Fuck Yeah!" speech or supposedly profound sermon for a quick endorphin rush.

                            But then, this seems like a constraint of characters and plots having to be these eternal, unchanging monetized brand icons. It doesn't seem like necessarily a constraint of making modern day stories about super powered people who want to achieve things in their world, or even lots of the particular tropes of the particular US comic book incarnation of this idea.

                            So going back to the specific point about heroes who do or don't change the world, I'd say from my perspective it's totally fine to make stories about heroes who don't. Maybe this is because the author is making an argument that powerful supermen shouldn't change the world, and maybe the subtext is because this is the author trying to avoid certain ideas about authoritarian and elitist minorities imposing radical ideas on society through force, or because they want to explore grounded stories that stick with the world as we know it. Or it's some other reason. However, whatever the reason, ideally that should be because that's the story the author wants to tell, sincerely, and should not have the issue that the audience can't really believe them because they know that this is hollow and the storyteller is ultimately just motivated by keeping the world of the story intact in order to sell it again in the future.

                            (So possibly this is either me just figuratively shouting at a cloud, or tl;dr-ing on the value of sincerity in expression over commercial constraints. But it was fun to write!).
