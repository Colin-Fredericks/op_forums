    Originally posted by Blaque View Post
    Even granting Aristotlelian was kind of important for the West, that Aristotle himself was nearly lost a couple times along hte way is kind of an example advancement being as much a matter of random dumb luck as anything.
    Yep. He was really a quite secondary philosopher in terms of influence from when he taught until the Arabs conquered half the Roman Empire and got their hands on him, ever in the shade of Plato. And of course Plato taught that the world was a shadow of reality, and a proper philosopher looked at reality directly through his mind rather than through the shadows it cast in matter. Under that sort of paradigm, proto-science is low prestige.

    It's really an obvious matter of historical contingency that, first, a useful quantity of Aristotelian philosophy still managed to survive, and second, the efforts by some Catholic theologians to ban "the new Aristotle" (after the Arabs transmitted it to the Latin West) in favor of maintaining neo-Platonist dominance didn't succeed. if it had been theologically crushed like it was in the Arab world by The Incoherence of the Philosophers, what study of "natural philosophy" would there have been anywhere on Earth? Technology would have continued to accumulate, and mathematics as an intellectual toy, and maybe that eventually would create the proper takeoff by its own accumulation, but, well, all we know is that it didn't take off except in the one civilization that called Aristotle "The Master of Those Who Know".

        Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
        Encouragement for investigation of natural phenomena has often been interpreted as being written right into the Qu'ran, and might be associated with the fact that the Islamic world has had, for instance, sophisticated treatises on the science of optics.
        And resulted in a Scientific Revolution in the Muslim world, right?

        Oh, wait, no, it didn't. Islam had a true Golden Age of natural philosophy under the influence of Greek philosophy, but it died. Because the theologian Al-Ghazali (honored by the title "The Proof of Islam", the most important theologian in Sunni Muslim history other than Muhammad himself) said to believe in the operation of the world by natural law rather than by direct divine miracle was heresy. And thus even if some bits of the Qu'ran could be interpreted as encouraging investigation into nature, it didn't matter, because it was socially difficult to try to investigate laws of nature in a society where people were predisposed to see that as evidence of heresy.

        So the great intellectual heirs of Ibn al-Haytham (the author of the Book of Optics who died about twenty years before Al-Ghazali was born) accordingly were natural philosophers in medieval Catholic universities, not Muslims, whatever the Qu'ran might be argued to say. Because what mattered was the attitude of the society.

        Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
        I don't know, what god is terribly concerned with you making observations that can be used to form precise models of how pressure works?
        You don't need a god concerned about that. You need a social environment that isn't crushing people interested in that, in the nascent period where 1) it's actually possible for small numbers of investigators to actually learn important things, and 2) learning those things isn't translating into major technological breakthroughs yet, so it isn't obviously of practical value. When 1 accumulates to the point you get 2, then you get the prestige to actually get active support. But that seems, based on the historical models we have, to only come after natural philosophy is tolerated as a respectable sub-field of philosophy.

                These intellectual history -> science ideas, tend to seem to postulate that ideas of scientific methods were either really uncommon, or common but commonly suppressed / not transmitted. So taking Verzio's example, you need Aristotle (unusual) and then for his ideas not to be suppressed (again unusual).

                (This specific one seems in some ways like the flipside of ideas, in a way, that early Medieval Europe and European Christianity was uniquely suppressive of scientific ideas?)

                But I think it's really hard to know through if this was the case without very thick knowledge of what people thought that even specialists would find challenging. This tends to be why I would tend to go more materialist and estimate that these ideas probably were not that unusual, but that the instruments and tools (printing press, optics, mathematical notation) may have come together more in Europe in a way that allowed the ideas to be useful. These instruments and tools (used to test and communicate) themselves not necessarily coming from science, but from experience based learning. So this is a much more materialist idea that tends to stress a more general ability to produce tools and mechanisms across the society that then made scientific ideas, a minority in any society, more useful and practical to apply.

                If we're looking for intellectual currents, I would guess you may also see more of the influence of a mechanistic view of the cosmos in Europe, connecting with a generally greater use of mechanical technology over the late Middle Ages (mills, clocks, etc.) to early modern period than what we would see elsewhere in Eurasia (certainly from what I know than in the world of Islam and Near East, and also China and India), which in turn is possibly driven by different subsistence ecology and economies and state structures, etc.

                Ultimately though it is very difficult to know what is correct.

                                    Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                                    Hmm, even implying that this is the extent of what has been said by another is my own instance of being reductive, and I can't in good conscience or intellectual honesty let it pass without my apology for becoming heated enough to hint at putting words in somebody's mouth.
                                    Okay. To make it absolutely clear, I don't think there was anything inherent to Christianity or Islam that explains why Aristotle became the basis of learning in Catholic Europe. The fact that Aristotle was known and ignored in all the rest of the Christian world (all of it pre-Aquinas, and always in the Byzantine, Russian, and Ethiopian areas), that he wasn't initially ignored in the Muslim world, and the virtual coin flip in the case of the University of Paris as to whether the "New Aristotle" would be banned make it clear there's nothing about the religion itself that drove it. It's easy to imagine a world where Ibn Rushd (Averroes) won the argument in the Muslim world and Aquinas lost in the Catholic, and the dominant Muslim civilization talks about how Christians were held back because their religion fosters superstition and ignorance. It didn't happen that way, but why it didn't is a matter of social currents too subtle for examination of history to reach definitive conclusions.

                                    Now, is it reductive to point specifically to a single point cause with the Aristotle-baptized-by-Aquinas? Well, maybe. But the thing is, when X is universal in all cases except one, it is usually because of what was different in that one case, rather than a bunch of single accidents in all the other cases. (Not always, but . . .)

                                    So when people, like in Aquilion's link, start dragging out "causes" like Confucianism (which of course didn't apply in the Muslim or Indian civilization-complexes), I think they're nuts. Same with the people blaming India's caste system, which had so many parallels in Christian Europe (nobles/townsmen/serfs/Cagots-or-nightsoil-workers-or . . .) that our theoretical advanced Muslim world could blame it with more justice than we could. Or blaming political unity, which doesn't characterize the Muslim world much more accurately than it did in Europe (Persians versus Ottomans, weakness of Ottoman rule in Egypt and North Africa compared with weakness of Hapsburg rule in the HRE, the Ottomans clearly being in competition with the same Europeans anyway, etc.), and what unity was there in India, and . . .

                                    My core is, by a contingent accident (Aristotle becoming the basis of its theology), western Europe wound up with "natural philosophy" as field of (consistently) respectable intellectual inquiry, while nobody else did. And then it's where natural philosophy evolved into science, an evolution which would seem to require that you have a vigorous tradition of natural philosophy. However fertile a farm is, it won't grow wheat unless there's wheat seed in it; a less-fertile field that's been sewn with wheat will grow more than the rich-but-fallow one. And the availability of wheat seed has little or nothing to do with the quality of the soil.

                                    Whether science then drove the material Great Divergence is then its own question, but I note that the "late date" view (which I favor) puts it downstream from the Scientific Revolution.

                                                You know, at times like this I kind of wonder exactly what people mean by science, and what their baseline for how fast its investigation is.
                                                The discovery of truth in its predictive form. The ability to tell the future through observing the present and past. An understanding of our world that matches the course of future events. As an example, both the necessary knowledge to know the chemical reaction that will drive a piston, as well the materials science to contain and direct that force for locomotion. The ability to coax agriculture into yields our ancestors would have considered magical. And how shit-slow any of that type of thing was when there were fewer of us, and most scratched subsistence from the earth all day.

                                                I was being glib talking about electricity. To risk being boringly serious and earthbound, mostly it was the rare eccentric who made scientific discoveries in the pre-modern world. Today, being a scientist is a respected profession staffed by more people today than there were even people at all in most of human history (setting an arbitrary flag down at, say, 2 million years ago). Having achieved a better ratio of people discovering things to things to be discovered, we're making more progress per unit time (assuming a constant progress per unit person-time).

                                                Creation leans hard into the 'rare eccentric moving the world' theme.
