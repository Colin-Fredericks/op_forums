        The core problem (as I've been repeating time and time again across three editions to no avail) is that Sorcery in Exalted offers no real sense of progression beyond the three initiations, which are extremely non-granular, take forever to reach in-game, and are partially or completely unavailable to many splats.

        A master of Melee or Stealth or Lore gets better and better charms the more they invest, expanding their options by progressing through the tree. This encourages investment and makes dedicated masters of those arts reasonable builds.

        Sorcerers get nothing. Giving people a free spell with their initiations, while it seemed like an obvious step, actually makes things worse because now there's simply no down payment to becoming a sorcerer at all - anyone and everyone is encouraged to buy one or two spells, but there's no point (as with high-in-the-tree Melee or MA charms) where you can say "alright, here we can put some really good stuff to reward people who have dedicated themselves intensely to Sorcery." The most powerful spells you can possibly make can effectively have, at most, a max of two prerequisites, and those two can functionally be any two spells you want. Worse, Sorcery is front-loaded by the control spell system - your first spell is worth the most; others offer less, rewarding dabblers over dedicated Sorcerers.

        The core book didn't even give us a single evocation tree to progress along, it just vaguely implied it exists. If your Sorcerer knows a thousand spells, there is still absolutely no incentive for you to pick up your thousand-and-first spell rather than buying a Melee charm to balance things out (and in practice, since you grab the best spells first, it's less "a thousand spells" and more "two or three spells.")

        I understand the desire to have different things work mechanically differently, but having every spell be its own disconnected thing with no prerequisites is dumb. It doesn't reflect how Sorcery is portrayed in fiction, where study and progression and research and accumulated knowledge are absolutely central. It doesn't provide any support for playing a sorcerer as an archetype rather than someone who just picked up the few most relevant spells.

        I'm unsure how it can be fixed at this point - it's really something that should have been fixed in the basic design for Sorcery. I guess you can put all the weight on evocations that boost Sorcery, but making and balancing generic Sorcery boosts is extremely difficult and you still don't really have any sense of knowledge-based progression when playing a Sorcerer.

        What I would probably do is just allow spells to have any sort of prerequisites you want (charms, evocations, and other spells), and write off the corebook spells as introductory stuff. That would be a bit crude, but I feel it really is necessary. Without prerequisites there's not really any sense of progressing as a Sorcerer or personalizing them in any particularly deep way - the initiations and control spells just don't cut it compared to the in-depth progression offered by trees in every other part of the game.

        Evocations for sorcery-boosting artifacts might cut it, but again... having some way to progress and benefit from extended mastery of Sorcery in the same way that a warrior can progress and benefit from extended mastery of Melee is a central part of playing a Sorcerer, so it's baffling that it was left out of the core book.

Maybe some of the capabilities one might acquire from their original initiation are a bit unusual as something that would extend from a single Charm purchase, but I'd say that's an area that can be fudged.

            ​Still, judging the utility of sorcery based on the mechanical conceit of initiation taking the form of a Charm purchase is ludicrous.             You appear to know enough about sorcery to make reasonably detailed comments on how workings function, and yet seem to be neglecting to actually look at what it can do in making your assessment of it.

            ​Sorcery, as it has been provided in the Third Edition core book, is perfectly capable of being a fully realised central aspect of a character and, even if a player makes minimal investment into it, conveys enough capability and weird quality at the most basic level to let a character be recognizably a sorcerer.

            ​In contrast with prior Editions, in which somebody actually could just buy Death of Obsidian Butterflies and Emerald Countermagic, hardly ever use them, and thus be nigh indistinguishable from any non-sorcerous character.
