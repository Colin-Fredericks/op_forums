    Mira's Bell -- a short Exalted story

    06-27-2018, 08:57 AM
    Once there was such a city called the Springs of Mi-Solla and it was a city filled with joyful music. At first it was a small temple dedicated to a god of musical instruments and only craftspeople and musicians lived in the Springs. One day many days ago, a certain flute-maker carved a flute that rang the stars upon the sky and changed the temple’s destiny. Their god grew curious as it never remembered having blessed the flute-maker to craft such a miraculous work. Thinking this to be a sign of true heaven-granted genius, he descended to the temple to reside in it. The god called himself Chun’m and only then did the musicians of the Springs know what kind of music they should make. People flocked to see this god and study under him. The Springs of Mi-Solla was then a euphonious place.

    But this story is a sad story in the end because a child and his father die burning; Chun’m abandons his people; and all the musicians die by a bell’s side.

    For many decades the Springs of Mi-Solla grew and did not stop to grow. At first people did not believe the story of the Springs – a place where musicianship granted citizenship and citizenship granted well-being. Not only that, people who did not show musicianship would be taught it. This was all because the god of that place sincerely enjoyed good music and wanted to see more of it. At first people doubted, saying “Chun’m is actually an evil god and has evil plans” or “Chun’m is using music as a bribe to a god above him” or “Chun’m eats the souls of musicians”. Later on these sayings disappeared, because Chun’m was neither an evil god, a sly god, nor a hungry god. It was simply he truly enjoyed his duty to the world (and I think it is very sad that people refused to believe it, because the attitude says a lot about Creation nowadays).

    The God of Mi-Solla welcomed everyone who would come to him to seek tutelage. He listened to them and knew whether they should pluck strings, strike bells, or melt bronze. Chun’m advised everyone how to pursue his favor. People would listen back and live to meet the god’s expectations. And once in a while Chun’m would tell a great musician to leave the Springs, not because he was displeased but because he was very pleased – he wanted them to spread good music across Creation. Of course to each of them Chun’m gave a staff-flute which sang pretty melodies when it smacked unruly brigands in the head.

    But one day something terrible happened to the Springs and Chun’m went to do something about it. The city was saved, but its god fell into wakeless sleep. For the first time since his arrival, the people of the Springs did not want to make music. Instead they gathered at the temple and asked, angrily, how to awaken him. His priests answered fearfully “we do not know; we hear neither his dreams nor nightmares.” The Springs of Mi-Solla had grown complacent under a benevolent god’s guidance over the years and, without his direction, the entire city was confused. To arouse Chun’m, the people tried to orchestrate their music and play reverently for three days and two nights. When that didn’t work, they instead tried to raise a commotion and make the biggest noise that Creation has ever heard – which only stopped when Malfeas almost grew interested. The people of the Springs did everything they knew to help the god who helped them. Nothing worked.

    The city was in mourning after the last attempt. That day a sorcerer was walking by. She was surprised to hear that the joyful Springs of Mi-Solla was not joyful at all. The sorcerer asked the people and Chun’m’s priests what had happened. Once she found out the sad fate that had struck the city, she earned the people’s favor with small miracles and asked to attend sleeping Chun’m herself. Now, by the god’s bedside, the sorcerer heard something that musicians of the city could not. She told them “I know how to awaken your god but it requires someone untainted by the world to sing to him; he is a god of musical instruments and a wicked song had gotten to him. He needs a pure song to purge that wickedness from his Essence.”

    The advice was true and the sorcerer wished to help the people of the Springs, but she had to leave as the Wyld Hunt was after her. Chun’m’s priests came together and discussed how to follow the plan that this kind sorcerer shared with them. You see, they were used to strings and bells and flutes and drums, but the music of the voice was unfamiliar. They sought out singers among the citizens. Nobody could awaken Chun’m. They had experienced life too much. Too much greed. Too much lust.

    Once again, the people of the Springs were at a loss. But in another angry meeting, a bell-maker remarked in passing “the child of Mira the Thug has never felt the world and his voice must be purer than anything – if he could speak.” You see, though I never mentioned him yet, one among the citizens who were no good at music was Mira the Thug, who was called the Thug because of his thuggish demeanor in youth. Let me recount his story first.

    Mira never cared for much and did whatever he wanted during his youthful years. He had beaten up many to get what he wanted and, in turn, he was beaten up by many to lose what he kept. His life was garbage. Mira did not always live in the Springs of Mi-Solla – he moved there much later in life – and once he was living in Nexus. A few decades ago, though Mira did not remember the occasion, a starry-eyed stranger came to him and told him to do a weird favor for her – to deliver a bottle of rice beer to a certain recipient. The starry-eyed stranger chose Mira specifically because his life was garbage and in need of disposal. But she was a fool to think a garbage-living Mira was a fool. Mira realized how dangerous this favor was and gave up on it, forgot to do it.
    The Sidereal was very mad about it, because Mira’s not being a fool resulted in many deaths and, not only that, her loss of status. So one night the Sidereal plucked out Mira’s star and cursed it, saying “nothing you love will truly last; anything that lasts will last only to give you pain.” Then she put it back to the night sky before anyone noticed. The Sidereal forgot about this curse for a time (she had a habit of cursing many people who got in her way.)

    Over time Mira’s star never helped Mira. His life grew worse, and then even worse over time. He lost everything. He almost reached a point where he could not care. Then someone cared for him somehow. He was afraid to love her, because he had grown to suspect that nothing he loves would truly last. It would kill his soul to see her wither and die. But how powerful is the heart! Mira made a compromise; he would only love her half as much as he would want to.

    The couple had many children but all of them died within a week after birth. Mira and his wife sought many gods and even demons to help them, but always the answer was “there is nothing even I can do.” But they did not give up. One day Mira’s wife had heard of this great god called Chun’m and they decided to plea to him, too. She knew she did not have much time left and she had grown desperate to see a child between them live to stay with Mira.

    Chun’m, too, knew he could not help the couple very much. But so wide and broad was his heart that he blessed them nonetheless. He decreed that their next child will live long and bring beautiful music to the world. Perhaps because of this, Mira’s next child lived to be eight days old. At this moment, the spiteful Sidereal noticed something was wrong and looked at Mira’s star, saying “well, you get to keep one thing and one thing only, but don’t you think I will allow you happiness.”

    So Mira’s wife died.

    Mira the Thug wept. He wept again when he found out Nangi could neither see, hear, nor speak. Mira was happy to be with Nangi but Mira was never there for Nangi. Accepting that he was thankful even for this state of affairs, Mira worked hard to keep Nangi happy – he particularly loved honey-dipped cookies. Mira was thankful for Chun’m, too, because in his mind it was Chun’m who granted him this child.

    And again then was when someone said Nangi could sing Chun’m awake. But how? Nangi could not speak. Hearing his beloved child’s name commonly spoken in the Springs, Mira wanted to find out how they would make Nangi sing. Well, the bell-makers who were enlightened in musical arts decided to give Mira the Thug’s child a voice. At another temple meeting, Mira was told this – and that it would happen as Nangi would fuel the melting of bronze for the bell that will awaken Chun’m.

    Enraged, Mira picked up an iron flute and struck dead one priest and showed how he got his appellation to others. He escaped the city with his child. He was angry and fearful that the people would want Nangi dead. But he never cursed Chun’m himself. Though Mira put all his strength to his legs and ran, he was one and there were many people in the Springs of Mi-Solla, some who were faster than Mira.

    As punishment, Mira was bound and forced to watch the bell be forged. The bruised and bone-broken priests consecrated the bronze and the fire within the furnace. The city prayed to Chun’m. The smiths threw Nangi into the fires; he died without even letting a scream out. But Mira could hear his child cry and he was compelled to hug him, to embrace him so he would not be afraid. He summoned such strength that the rope binding him tore and exploded. Before anyone could grasp him, Mira too leapt into the fires.

    But he did say one thing before: “whoever tolls this bell to break it will be blessed with the strength of the typhoon, the wealth of the Imperial Mountain, and the wisdom of Heaven.”

    And then the bell was cast. It was a great temple bell, as tall as a house and as wide as an elephant. At the dawn of an auspicious day, the still-bruised and still-bone-broken priests tolled the bell together, as the ringer was too big for a person. Chun’m rose from his sleep.

    Then the god heard the sound, which was clear and sonorous to mortal ears, but cruel and ragged to divine ears. Chun’m screamed invectives at his blessed priests and instrumentalists. He spat out words of anger and spite at the whole city for allowing the bell to be cast. Then he returned to Yu-Shan without even looking back.

    The city was silent for days.

    One night, then, Mira’s Bell tolled again. The sound filled the entire Springs. The priests ran toward the bell-gazebo and saw that someone was throwing all his weight to toll the bell, to the point that he died. Luckily the bell was too finely crafted to break. They carried the carcass away to give it burial. Then the bell tolled again. And again. And again.

    From that night to months later, Mira’s Bell never stopped to toll. All the musicians had forgotten their virtues and fought each other to throw the ringer, sometimes to death. Clearly after this many strikes, it should be the next strike that breaks the bell. Then the strength and the wealth and the wisdom would be theirs. The priests (who had not thrown themselves at the bell) gave up and departed the Springs of Mi-Solla. Corpses began to pile up at the gazebo.

    In the end, the city broke before Mira’s Bell did. But Mira’s curse has become a legend and even to this day the bell’s beautiful sound fills the countryside.

    ---

    So I was on a bus yesterday and had this scenery in mind. This story is heavily inspired by the legends of King Seongdeok's Bell and the Bell of Avici of Awawa (not sure about the name!). I don't know how this story could be used in Exalted games, but it sounds like something from Creation.
