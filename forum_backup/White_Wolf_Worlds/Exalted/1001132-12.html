                                You know, if I spent as much time actually working on my Quest Thread as I did gleefully writing world-building lore, I would probably be done by now……..

                                The Oathsworn: Arms, Equipment, Training and Philosophy

                                The Oathsworn, as they are so called, are the quasi-professional warriors of the Hundred Kingdoms feudal aristocracy. Stories are told about them, homage is paid to them, and battles are won or lost through their skill at arms. To be given the title of Oathsworn is to be held in great esteem, at least technically.

                                But who are they really? Noble warriors upholding a valorous code of conduct through personal integrity and skill at arms? Vicious bullies that trod over the dignity and lives of those beneath them, demanding obsequiousness under threat of violence?

                                In many cases, they are both. They are, after all, only human. Regardless, this treatise will attempt to decipher if only the smallest aspect of their traditional (and often confusing and contradicting) code of conduct, origins, and means of training.

                                ---Origin:

                                The Oathsworn, at least as an example of a professional warrior caste of society, is partially derived from two different and opposing forces: the ancient, yet still extant Vanehani martial tradition called "Kenseiden" (derived from the Shogunate Era Pre-High Realm terms for 'sword/means of aggression' [ken], 'paragon/saint' [sei], and 'tradition/philosophy' [den], many followers/practitioners of which accompanied the first Sword Prince, Kendorei, on his fateful final invasion of the proto-Hundred Kingdoms, and from the Chosen warriors of the proto-Hill Tribes warrior chieftaincies.

                                From the Kenseiden warrior-cum-monks, the Oathsworn inherited their tradition of martial excellence, and from the Chosen warriors, the first lieges of what were to eventually become the Oathsworn took the idea of gathering a solid group of loyal, skilled and driven warriors (the Kenseiden practice largely being individual), and using them as heavy infantry in battle. After experiencing the brutal effects of the proto-Hill Tribe cavalry attacks, a beautiful idea was born, and the heavy infantry soon took up horses and turned into heavy shock cavalry.

                                As the Hundred Kingdoms progressed from infancy to adolescence, and warfare changed from "skirmishes between Lord and their retinues" (scrums more akin to brutal back-alley gangfights than warfare) to "massed combat between kingdoms", the proto-Oathsworn retainers changed from the primary combatants on a battlefield to supplementary heavy infantry and cavalry. For the price it takes to maintain and keep a single Oathsworn warrior, Lords could instead levy 5 or more peasants. This, coincidentally, was the start of feudalism in the Hundred Kingdoms, as when the lands of ruling Lords expanded, and they therefore needed more and more proto-Oathsworn to defend their holdings, many Lords were unable to support keeping such large retinues out of their own wallet. Therefore, each pre-Oathsworn retainer would instead be ceded land, so they could support themselves on the backs of the peasants that lived on their estates, and in turn swore loyalty to their liege, to support them in peace and in war.

                                These oaths of loyalty would give these military retainers the title of Oathsworn. Initially, this was the only "Oath" the Oathsworn made.

                                As the Hundred Kingdoms progressed from adolescence to adulthood, these Oathsworn warriors (now landed nobles), ever hungry for more lands and riches, became increasingly brutal in their search for the same. Initially, any warrior with enough equipment and skill could make an Oath of Loyalty to a lord in return for wealth and respect, leading as one might expect to plenty of bad apples in the bunch. The plundering of villages, brutal mistreatment of peasantry, and overwhelming preponderance of uncouth and rough behaviors led to a general mistrust of the group among the Hundred Kingdoms in general.

                                One nameless Oathsworn, apparently in disgust at what their fellow comrades were doing to their reputation, rebelled against the then-current social norms; instead of swearing ill-kept Oaths for riches and homage, this individual instead traveled the Hundred Kingdoms, righting wrongs and behaving in general opposite what was expected of them.

                                When an Oathsworn party attempted to raid a village for loot or fun, or stood by idly as bandits did the same, this Oathsworn was there to defend the peasants from violent death. Where a peasant might be beaten down, or even killed, by a passing Oathsworn for presuming to speak to them, this Oathsworn instead replied civilly and respectfully to people of all social status. Where most Oathsworn were concerned only with weapons, women and wine, this Oathsworn took up the arts of poetry, calligraphy, music and other arts.

                                Eventually, this Oathsworn attracted followers, eager to learn from them. In turn, the Oathsworn bade them swear additional Oaths, instilling in them codes of conduct, a sense of honor and dignity, "correct" methods of training for war and peace, a sense of appreciation of the arts, as well as many other aspects, in a sense codifying the modern Oaths many Oathsworn swear. After making them swear these Oaths, their instructor had them meditate for a night and day in an Immaculate temple near their place of encampment, in order to fully reflect on what they were so swearing to do. If anyone felt, after a night reflecting on their Oaths, they they could not uphold them to their best, they would be allowed to leave, but they could not call themselves Oathsworn.

                                The majority did, and thereafter left, travelling the Hundred Kingdoms much as their master had done, behaving in much the same way and speaking about the "new" Oathsworn. These warriors, in turn, attracted followers of their own, who attracted their own followers, etc etc etc. The Lords of the Hundred Kingdoms, happy to see a warrior-class that wasn't all about burning down villages indiscriminately, eagerly supplanted their earlier retainers with the 'new and improved" Oathsworn........ who proceeded to rapidly become very similar to the rough-and-tumble asswipes they replaced. It would appear that material greed and desires beat out philosophical promises. However, the system of Oaths, methods of training and appreciation for the arts remained ingrained in the new class, and has remained so to the present day.

                                ---Training:

                                The Oathsworn are, first and foremost, semi-professional warriors. They are not fully professional (a good example of a professional warrior would be the Legions of the Scarlet Empire, or the maniples of Lookshy), since they do not receive a wage for fighting, nor do they spend the majority of their time at war. Instead, Oathsworn are instead retained (hence the term "retainer") by Lords of the Hundred Kingdoms to serve as the heavy backbone of a fighting force. When they are not engaged in battle, many/most Oathsworn are either engaged in the running of their enfeoffed estates or training for future battles.

                                One thing that might not be apparent to the modern, gentle reader, is that combat with weapons other than a crossbow or firewand is difficult. Using weapons like swords, spears and bows requires a great deal of physical strength, long periods of training, and a sense of aggression and drive that might be lacking in those not versed in combat.

                                In response, many Oathsworn start training with arms at a very early age. The "traditional" age one might start "official" (start as a page to an Oathsworn instructor) is usually given as seven years of age, although many Oathsworn start physical training as soon as they are able to walk and run. As a youth, games and play that build stamina and strength (footraces, wrestling, whacking sticks together)are favored, and often chores involving physical labor are given as well, such as carrying heavy loads or gardening.

                                At the age of seven, most prospective Oathsworn are apprenticed to an Oathsworn warrior for instruction and training, usually in how to maintain weapons and armor, some sparring bouts with blunted weapons, serve as go-fers and general labor (tents, firewood and water will not sort itself out!) as well as training in etiquette and other niceties (mainly: stand up straight, shut up, and listen to me when I am talking to you!). At this age, most pages do not accompany their lieges into battle, as they are far to young to be effective combatants, nor do they likely own weapons/armor.

                                At the age of 14 (traditionally), or when the liege-Oathsworn deems them ready, usually several years after first starting as a page, the page becomes what is termed a "footman" (which comes from where the trainee sits, aka at the feet of their liege), which is basically a senior page. At this point, the footman will take a more active role in caring for the liege's equipment, as well as accompanying them both to battle and to court. In battle, they will usually hold their masters unused weapons ready for use, give up their horse if their masters is killed, as well as passing messages or bringing water (in battle, footmen are traditionally bare-headed and without hand protection, ostensibly to prevent them from being valid targets, although shit can always happen.) In court, they traditionally sit behind their master at the table, offering bowls of washwater or clean cloths when required, serving their master food and drink, and cleaning up after them.

                                When a footman is deemed ready, both in combat skill and courtly skill, which is traditionally the age of 21, the liege-Oathsworn will ask the footman to accompany them into battle, fully armed and equipped. If they prove competence, they are thereafter allowed to swear the Oaths that make them Oathsworn, then stand a vigil overnight in an Immaculate Temple, in order to reflect on what the Oaths mean to them. Of course, the footman actually has to own their own weapons and armor, which isn't guaranteed. Some might be given equipment by their parents, others might be given arms and armor by their liege. Some, forever too poor to actually get equipment of their own, perpetually remain footmen to their masters.

                                --- Equipment:

                                --Armament:

                                The three traditional "noble weapons" of the Hundred Kingdoms are the sword, the spear and the bow, and all Oathsworn are expected to be at least proficient with all three. There are also several important means of actually bearing the weapons that identifies an Oathsworn as a Oathsworn, as opposed to just a well-heeled retainer or noble.

                                The main difference is: while most warriors in the Hundred Kingdoms will wear armor/clothing bound shut by a sash of cloth, only Oathsworn may wear their sword and dagger thrust through (aka wrapped up in the sash and bound to the body) the sash, while non-Oathsworn are to wear the above weapons hanging from (aka suspended via a cord or tie) from the sash. This difference is to symbolically represent how the Oathsworn is ever-ready to defend their Oath-sworn honor, and how the Oaths cannot be removed from their person, yadda yadda yadda. The difference might be seen as trivial, but bloody duels to the death have been fought over such disrespect.

                                -The sword is a direct derivative of the ancient Vanehani-style straight sword (usually a concave diamond or flattened hexagon in cross-section), itself a descendant of a Shogunate cavalry blade adopted, amusingly enough, from the ancestors of the Hill-Tribes. In the Hundred Kingdoms, only those of noble birth are allowed to wield the double-edged straight sword, while those of lesser birth are to limit themselves to "lesser" single-edged backswords. Traditionally, the sword is made of three "plates" of steel, with a hard plate of steel sandwiched between two of softer steel. The softer steel plates form the flat of the blade, giving flexibility and protecting the inner spine from shock, while the hard central plate forms the hardened cutting edges and central spine. More expensive swords use 5 or seven plates instead, and most sword-steel is imported from Vaneha anyways.

                                Most Oathsworn swords are about 3 feet in length, of which 24-30 inches of which are blade (depending on the size and preference of user) and the rest is hilt. The hilt is long enough to fit a hand and two or three fingers, allowing both single-handed and two-handed use as needed, and is usually wrapped in cord for better grip. The crosspiece is traditionally oval-shaped, although wing-shapes projecting both towards the blade and back towards the user are common. The hilt, handle and pommel are usually stacked onto the tang of the blade, which is in turn peened over the pommel, holding everything nice and tight.

                                The blade usually exhibits both a profile and distal taper, meaning it both gets narrower and thinner closer to the tip, which itself is usually needle-pointed for piercing armor. Also, the blade is traditionally divided into three "sections" based on intended use: the section near the tip is razor-sharp, for stabbing, slashing and whip-cuts. The central portion of the blade is knife-sharp, for cleaving cuts or draw-cuts. The section near the hilt is either "chisel-sharp" (meaning a definite edge, but you can touch the edge with a finger) or left as an unsharped ricasso, and used for parrying, binding or other defensive moves.

                                While the sword is traditionally the most "respected" weapon of the Oathsworn, and in fact serves as the symbol of their social class, in practice the sword is usually a backup weapon, both on the battlefield and elsewhere. The "main" workhorse weapons of the Oathsworn are the spear and the bow.

                                -The spear is, by far, the most used weapon of the entire Oathsworn arsenal. On foot, on horseback, in offense and in defense,the spear largely cannot be beat in practicality.

                                The Oathsworn spear was actually invented by the invading Vanehani armies before the Hundred Kingdoms were formed. As the campaign progressed, the swords of the warriors broke in battle, and it wasn't like they were able to go home to get replacements. Therefore, in order to allow their armies to remain combat-effective, the clan-leaders of Vaneha ordered their warriors to reforge the broken blades into shorter weapons, suitable for use as spearheads or daggers.

                                The spear is traditionally about 7 feet in length, or as high as the warrior can reach up with arm outstretched. From that point, the spearhead is traditionally one foot in length, with a dagger-shaped blade ending with a tang several inches long. This tang is inserted into a slot carved into the end of the shaft, over/through which a peg is inserted and a socket worked, to prevent the shaft from splitting. The dagger-shaped blade is long enough to allow for slashing and stabbing attacks to be worthwhile, while the shaft is long enough to be usable from horseback and short enough to not be unwieldy on foot.

                                Traditionally (and pragmatically), the length of spearheads and dagger-blades allows one to reuse broken sword-blades in their construction.

                                While the Oathsworn spearhead is dagger-shaped (due to them mainly being made from cut-down swords and daggers), spearheads used by commoners are simpler in construction, usually diamond-shaped and making use of less steel and more iron.

                                -The bow is the final aspect of the Oathsworn arsenal, and is a blend of the traditional Vanehani asymmetrical composite bow and the laminated wooden longbow used by the Hill-Tribes. About 3 feet in length when strung, the Oathsworn bow is symmetrical, with deep limbs and a long draw-length, usually to the ear. Constructed of wood, sinew, rawhide, horn and bamboo laminates, the bow is capable of punching an arrow through both sides of a deer at 50 paces, and loosing an arrow faster than the eye can see out on a relatively flat trajectory.

                                The arrows themselves are usually about 3 feet long, as long as the bow is, and made of coppiced willow and bamboo. The tang of the arrowhead is usually inserted into a tight-fitting section of bamboo, which is left free around the wooden shaft. That way, the arrowhead will be left behind when the penetrated enemy pulls on the wooden shaft.

                                Usually, the string is made of linen or silk, and drawn via the aid of a carved ring worn on the thumb of the dominant hand.
                                Like most composite/laminated bows, the glues and resins of the Oathsworn bow do not play well with water and moisture, meaning many battles are decided by the vagaries of the weather.

                                --Other weapons

                                - The dagger: the dagger is basically a shorter version of the sword, about a foot long and thrust through the sash. It is primarily a backup to the sword, but is retained for use in interior-combat, where a sword or spear might be caught up on the surroundings, or for use in a grapple. Traditionally, the sword and dagger are made in a matching set.

                                -The glaive: Another Vanehani descendant, the glaive is basically a curved backsword blade set on a spearshaft. In the early days of the Vanehani invasion and the formation of the Hundred Kingdoms, the glaive was a favorite of the proto-Oathsworn, but is no longer very common. It requires too much space to use in the tightly-packed blocks common to modern Hundred Kingdoms combat. Instead, the glaive (and the spear) have found a new home in the hands of the daughters of nobility (and the nobility-aping nouveau riche town-dwelling middle class), who are trained to use the weapon in defense of their homes, and as a grace-building training tool.

                                -The backsword: basically, a single-edged sword with a “back” that is perpendicular to the face. They come in many forms, from straight-bladed copies of the Oathsworn sword to curved blades for use from horseback. The backsword is used as a backup hand-weapon by wealthy Men-at-Arms/armsmen, and as self-defense tools for travelling merchants. Additionally, many nobles wear them as “walking about” weapons, for when they don’t want to possibly damage their expensive straight-swords, or as hunting weapons.

                                -The shield: The shield is used primarily by Oathsworn that wear lighter armor than their peers, or by Oathsworn intending to get into melee combat. The frame of the typical shield is made of steam-softened bamboo bent around wooden support laths. The shield is faced with more slats of bent bamboo, then covered with linen and sometimes laccquer. Convexed in final form, a well-made shield will cover from the shoulder to the knee, offering protection from arrows and melee weapons, all while being relatively lightweight.

                                --Armor

                                Oathsworn armor is derived from both the Vanehani styles the Hundred Kingdoms inherited and the Hill-Tribe styles they subsumed.

                                Usually, in the wealthier river bottoms, nobles will wear lamellar supplemented by mail, while the poorer nobles from the hills and forests will wear just mail. Both are equally protective, all things considered, they differ mainly in the levels of maintenance required and maintenance methods.

                                Lamellar is laced together, and if it gets wet must be unlaced then dried before the lacing starts to rot. Otherwise, it is largely self-supporting in the sense that it will bear its own weight.

                                Mail, on the other hand, can get wet without (much) regard towards its welfare, as the links are open to the air and will dry comparatively quickly. However, it does not support its own weight, meaning it will hang from the shoulders if unsupported.

                                Both mail and lamellar are worn with underpadding, usually made of wool or cotton stuffed between two layers of linen or canvas. This underpadding is what prevents spears, arrows and swords from breaking bones when impacting the armor, all the metal does is prevent the user from being stabbed/cut. And a strong enough cut/stab, or narrow enough point, will penetrate regardless. The underpadding prevents these from being lethal (most of the time). Mail, by and large, is probably going to be sewn directly to the underpadding so it doesn’t just hang loose.

                                Other than that, most armor worn by Oathsworn/nobility throughout the Hundred Kingdoms is going to be largely the same, and put on in much the same fashion. The heavier the armor, the more difficult it is to put on, hence why Oathsworn/nobles tend to have pages to help.
                                1. As a base layer, the warrior will don a loose shirt and trousers of linen or silk
                                2. Over this, a tightly-wrapped sash of linen or silk is wound about the midsection, to give support to the torso. It is wrapped around itself with no knots
                                3. The greaves are put on next, tied around the ankles and calves
                                4. Next is the underpadding (lamellar) or mail-jacket.
                                5. Around the waist go the thigh-guards, which are laced to the inside of the underpadding/jacket
                                6. Next, the armored gloves and sleeves are donned. They are laced to the inside of the sleeves of the underpadding/jacket.
                                7. If the warrior is wearing a heavy helmet, a padded shoulder/neck pad is worn. If not, skip this step
                                8. Now the jacket is closed (through buttons, usually), or the lamellar vest is donned, and bound tight to the body with another sash. Through this sash is thrust the sword and dagger.
                                9. Now comes the head protection. A coif may be donned, or a full helmet may be placed on the head. It is bound under the chin, while coifs are laced to the shoulders.
                                10. Any extraneous armor, like pauldrons, poleynes, gorgets, face-masks,, crests, Coats-of-plates, etc are put on
                                11. If the warrior has a tabard showing their personal heraldry or the heraldry of their lord, this is worn over the entire ensemble. Most of the time, these will be made of linen, but particularly-favored or wealthy retainers might wear silk.
                                Wealthy Oathsworn might very well own multiple sets of armor, a “parade set” of their finest pieces, and “battle sets” of stuff they can accept being damaged.

                                (For an idea of what the armor looks like, look up various Japanese armors.)

                                --- Tactics:

                                Oathsworn are, by and large, individual warriors, at-odds with the current means of warfare in the Hundred Kingdoms. Most fighters on a battlefield are going to be peasant Men-at-Arms, standing shoulder to shoulder in a tightly-packed formation, fighting in unison. Oathsworn, on the other hand, are probably going to be in a looser formation, as they require a bit more space to use their weapons to full-effectiveness. And, even when they do stand “in formation”, Oathsworn are almost always going to fight as individuals anyways, seeking out individual targets and fighting for their own honor and glory.

                                The one exception is a full-on cavalry charge, which is a thing of blood-curdling beauty. Take several dozen heavily-armored warriors, put them on the backs of horses that weigh upwards of 1000 lbs, and pack them together tighter than sardines in a can. Now, point them in the general direction of the enemy.

                                Contrary to popular knowledge, the Oathsworn charge isn’t a full-on gallop. Only the last 50 or so yards are at a dead run, the rest is a trot or even a fast walk. The goal is to impact the enemy with maximum shock, with no gaps. Therefore, Oathsworn warhorses (and only warhorses. Horses untrained in this maneuver cannot be relied on) will charge literally shoulder-to-shoulder. Infantry will get run over and trampled underfoot, which is more than capable of killing the poor sap, especially when there are a couple more horses yet to step on them.

                                Of course, the same goes for the Oathsworn who fall off their horse mid-charge (usually after getting hit by an arrow), or horses that get killed during the approach. Therefore, a heavy cavalry charge is reserved for the deciding point of a battle, not the opening stages. Infantry or light cavalry is usually used to sweep away missile troops.

                                --- Arts and Etiquette

                                Part of being an Oathsworn, or a noble in general, is a general appreciation for the arts and things other than battle. Someone who fixates wholly on warfare is generally viewed as a savage (which is one of the many reasons the Hill-Tribes are looked down upon, as they Hundred Kingdoms don’t view their cultural aspects all that often, if at all).

                                Traditionally, the “noble” arts are calligraphy, poetry (spoken and written), musical skill (with the guzheng, the flute, or the drum) , painting, flower-arrangement, and dancing. Oathsworn, as nobility, are expected to at least be familiar, if not proficient, especially if they want to marry into their own social class. Other common pastimes include board-games, but they can be viewed as uncouth (especially cards and dice-gambling. UGH!)

                                Also, as nobility, Oathsworn are expected to behave with “decorum suitable to one of their social status”, aka not be an asshole. Nobility in general are expected to be rather stoic, with outward displays of emotion being looked down upon, and rudeness or crude language being highly discouraged. Hence, why most nobles won’t react all that much when a peasant gets uppity, even though it is within their rights to kill them. Using proper diction, grammar, and honorifics are very important when it comes to courtly life, and the system of when to give respect, and how much, can be quite confusing. How deep one is supposed to bow, and how long, is dependent on the relative social status between two nobles.

                                --- Oaths:

                                Here we come to the meat of the matter. What makes Oathsworn, Oathsworn.

                                When Oathsworn are made to stand vigil (again, traditionally in an Immaculate Temple for a full night and day), they are required to swear a number of oaths to as many witnesses as there are present (usually, these witnesses include their master Oathsworn and an Immaculate monk). The exact wording and the number of oaths vary among individuals, but the general gist is:

                                Oath of Honor: I will bear myself with the dignity according to my station. I will treat others according to this dignity, and the dignity they deserve. I will not undertake any action that will bring me dishonor (aka fail to uphold an Oath, rob/steal/cheat, lie, commit a crime), and will not tolerate dishonor among my peers and servants. I will honor the gods and the Immaculate Faith.

                                Oath of Compassion: I will protect the innocent. It is the duty of the strong to oppose those who threaten the weak. Through my own actions, I will not bring others to harm, or through my own inaction, allow others to come to harm. I will treat all with dignity and respect, and help whenever possible. I will punish the wicked.

                                Oath of Loyalty: My word is my bond and my sacred honor. To break my word is to bring dishonor on myself. I will uphold my promises, to my superiors, to my peers, to my servants and to myself. I will work within the bounds of the law to bring the wicked to task, not subvert it for my own ends.

                                So on and so forth. Generally, the “big 4” standards one is trying to uphold when swearing an Oath is:
                                1. Protect Innocent
                                2. Punish Wicked
                                3. Uphold law and order
                                4. Maintain Honor
                                Now, as you can see, most Oathsworn do not uphold the intent of their Oaths. Many do not try. Kasai does, but Kasai is an idealist in a pessimists world.
