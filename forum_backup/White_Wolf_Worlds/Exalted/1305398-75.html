        Originally posted by Chejop Kejak View Post
        That's Exalted all over, though. Like, in every edition.

        The downside of the "CCG RPG" paradigm is that all of these little mechanical widgets reward you for heavily specializing... but either that's total overkill, or anything calibrated for specialized builds just destroys anyone who's not in their element. Which is tough enough when it's like "the social character gets pwned," but much nastier when someone gets disarmed or something and then abruptly cannot do the thing they built around.

        I'm not sure there's much to be done without giving up on "loads of exception-based powers" as a concept, however.
        I've been doing a lot of thinking about this lately, and came to the conclusion that I think you can have either loads of exception-based powers or granular base stats or a balanced system, but not all three. If you have exception-based powers and you want them to be balanced, those powers need to have some foundational math that they're working on, which means characters need to all be working from the same statline. For an example of a system that does this in a kludgite way, there's D&D 4E, which bases all its system math on the assumption that if your class is based on Strength then you're going to max out Strength at chargen and raise it more at every opportunity. The consequence of this, combined with 4E being a relatively well-balanced tabletop, is that if you don't max out your key stat then you're objectively building your character wrong and everything becomes much harder than the devs intended. At that point, though, there's no real reason to have base stats at all other than tradition. They're not adding anything besides punishment.

        The same goes for Exalted, except in this case the punishment is that the game becomes too easy instead of becoming too hard. If you strip out the fiddly numbers and set the base dicepools to a static value, then you have more room for the little mechanical widgets to have a meaningful impact because they're no longer overkill on their own. You can still have a character who specializes in taking combat Charms to the point where they're unstoppable, but then it's the product of a long-term decision to make that specialization, not just because they picked higher base numbers than the published opposition can really handle.

                I'm not sure if this is exactly right - at least, depending on how high your standards are for a balanced system. You could imagine that characters of a certain type could have a "main combat stat", like Intelligence for Wizards in D&D or Dex/Melee for a typical Exalted character, and a number of options for special powers that would be based on various noncombat stats - like certain combat Charms in Exalted will reward you for Manipulation or some other stat. If you balance things so that characters can't max all their stats, you would have a few different options that could theoretically be balanced against each other - e.g. you could max Dexterity and Manipulation and take all the Charms / special powers that reward you for having high Manipulation, or you could max another noncombat stat, or you could take a few stats to medium levels and choose the best Charms for each type. (I'm imagining this as being like in TCGs where you can play decks of a single color to make your individual cards as reliable as possible, or multi-color decks, which will make your individual cards sometimes hard to cast, but have more options for cards, making it easier to play the most powerful cards or take advantage of synergies between cards).

                But it's certainly the case that these things make the game harder to balance!

                I would say that the biggest issue in Exalted is that the game is intentionally unbalanced in a lot of ways. Like, it's intentional that you can make a character that is just bad at combat and will lose a swordfight to a single human soldier or whatever, or a god of combat who can beat thousands of soldiers without breaking a sweat, because the game developers want to let the players tell stories about how the first character uses their other amazing abilities to get out of the situation where it looks like they might have to fight and about how the second character deals with the consequences of winning that battle. It's intentional that putting armor on makes you much more effective in battle.

                And I think the intentional unbalanced nature combines with the unintentional unbalanced nature, which to an extent is unavoidable, to lead to a lot of imbalance.

                The same goes for Exalted, except in this case the punishment is that the game becomes too easy instead of becoming too hard.                 Yes, although "too easy" is not quite the right description of what happens. Like, you might be able to build your character to the point where they can defeat anyone in Creation (or just about anyone) in a duel, but it's still trivial to find something that can challenge you - like, just step off a boat on the Blessed Isle and declare yourself Empress (or declare your friend who took 15 Bureaucracy Charms and would be able to run an empire as Empress) and you will be quickly killed by 25 DBs and 3 Sidereals, or whatever it takes. Or you can head into the Wyld, fight a behemoth, and use it as bait to fight a bigger behemoth.

                The issue is playing these scenarios where a powerful character is challenged (1) makes combat drag on for too long, in the length of a given character's turn, the number of actions in a round, and the number of rounds in a battle, (2) requires enemies (powerful behemoths) of which very few are statted in the books, (3) doesn't involve hardly any of the enemies which are statted in the books. The "fight DBs" version will have a bigger problem with (1), while the "fight behemoths" version will have a bigger problem with (2).

                Unlike the "starting characters can be combat gods or below-average human warriors" thing, there is nothing intrinsic to the setting of Exalted that makes these true. But it's unsurprising that (1) is true as it's hard to design a system that is fun at wildly different power levels, and it's unsurprising that given this, they would focus on writing enemies where their system shines the most (although even given that, they probably aimed a little low with some of the HDNP enemies).

                    So in the Godlike series of RPGs, you have three types of dice. Normal (which are d10s), Hard and Wiggle. Hard and Wiggle dice represent your godlike powers and mean you'll basically always beat out mere mortals. But, when you fight other supers, Hard and Wiggle dice cancel out.

                    So if you have Super Human Strength (6 dice, 3 Hard Dice, 1 Wiggle Die) and fight a Super Human God of Pankration (5 Dice, 2 Hard Dice, 3 Wiggle Die) you don't both roll the maximum (10) number dice. Instead Strength would roll 6 dice (trained human level) with 1 Hard Die and Pankration would roll 5 dice with 2 Wiggle Dice.

                    Exalted could really use something like that to simplify high level fights so they don't drag for 33 rounds.

                                        In part, this will be hard because you're struggling against the design goals of the game.

                                        It often gets forgotten, but if people didn't have infinitely-spammable perfect defenses, 2E fights tended to be over very, very quickly. The whole reason why Withering and Decisive attacks work the way they do is to slow things down and create a lot of back-and-forth before the possibility to end the fight comes online. If you want faster fights, you probably just want to do away with that technology. My first thoughts would be:
                                        1. Withering attacks just damage people's health, 2E style. You'd have to make some decisions about combining Withering and Decisive Charms on the same attacks. This is, obviously, the most extreme choice, and would have the 2.5E effect of making everyone need lots of Soak.
                                        2. Whenever someone gets Crashed, they cannot fight. In a group fight, you must become Incapacitated, Withdraw, Go To Ground, or try to take actions as a trivial combatant who will die to any hit. If you're alone, this is covered by the Hold At Bay rules. Paradoxically, this makes serious fights less deadly, because taking people alive is the default and there's relatively-little incentive to make Decisive attacks. On the other hand, it also opens a powerful combatant up to a lot less vulnerability, because they're not forced to reset down to 3i and maybe get Crashed by a lucky hit.
                                        3. Increase all damage from successful Decisive attacks by 5 levels or so. The idea here is to keep the flow of combat mostly the same, but make it so that it seldom takes more than 1-2 Decisives to finish off even supernaturally-tough enemies. It also would obviously make deadly alpha strikes a lot easier, so you might want to start everyone with less Initiative, or make this a bonus you "unlock" for the rest of the fight the first time you crash someone, or instead outright double Decisive damage.
                                        4. Withering hits give you something like 5+damage Initiative, instead of 1+damage. Often, fights go long because both combatants keep hitting each other for roughly the same amount of damage every round and nobody can build up enough Initiative to accomplish much (or because the Gm thinks it's cute to have enemies with mediocre stats but decent soak outnumber the PCs without collapsing them into a battlegroup), so just globally speeding up Initiative gain could be a solid hack. It would make poor Heavy weapons even more worthless than they already are, though. You might instead give every non-crashed person 3i per round automatically, which makes Withering accuracy less of a godstat, but also makes it less important for people to make Withering attacks at all. But maybe that's a feature?

                                        I do think it's also worth noting that Mata-Yadh being impractical for a one-on-one duel isn't necessarily a bad thing. 3E is in the unenviable position of making it cumbersome to run lots of worthy enemies, whilst also having very few tools to make boss fights work, so the best patch that OPP has come up with is enemies with a ton of -0 HLs and decent attacks, who can soak up attacks from several PCs at once. If even those can be nuked down quickly by a lone non-specialized combat build, there are even fewer options to make a fight climactic without just learning to play 10-15 Exalts at once.
