                    I'm sorry, but this is not how Stoking Bonfire Style works as written. You seem to have forgotten the crucial last sentence of the effect: "Missing an attack or failing to parry resets the discount, as does attacking or defending against another enemy, or going a round without using this Charm."

                    In our example of a single unoptimized Parry 5 Dragon-Blooded going up against three claw striders, this is what would happen:

                    DB wins Join Battle, activating Stoking Bonfire Style on the attack. Hits, 1m discount on SBS goes online, reflexive activation of Flame-Borne Interception happens before Claw Strider #1 would attack.
                    Assuming we're extraordinarily lucky and CS #1 is the one we hit, still leading in Initiative despite our massive blow, we get a 1m discount on SBS. We full excellency for 3m, putting us to Defense 8 with a stunt; another 1m discount goes online, FBI gives us a further +1 enhancement to Parry which is immediately cancelled out by the onslaught penalty.
                    CS #2 comes a-knocking. Since the Charm text of SBS actually says "When she lands an attack against an enemy or blocks one with this Charm, the cost of using this Charm against him is cumulatively lowered by one mote on subsequent rounds", our cost reduction ain't worth jack right now, so we full excellency for 2m, putting us at Defense 7 with a stunt. At this point, the discount on SBS is also immediately reset because we defended against a different enemy.
                    Good news though, we're at full excellency thanks to FBI anyway - so now we're looking at an attack against Parry 6. Successfully parrying this gets us another 1m discount if we attack CS #3 next turn though; question is, do we want to do that or are we better off pressing CS #1 in the hopes of getting a Crash and wasting the 1m discount?

                    At this point, we should take stock. Assuming a 4m full excellency on our first attack, we've spent 4 (first attack) + 3 (FBI) + 3 (full excellency on Parry vs. CS #1) + 2m (full excellency on Parry vs. CS #2) +0m (we were already at cap when CS #3's turn came up); total of 12m.

                    If we didn't use FBI, we'd be at 4m + 3m + 4m + 4m = 15m, so activating FBI did get us 3 free motes. Note that as the number of enemy combatants decreases, the gap between having FBI and not having it closes as well - if we only had two attackers, we'd be at 4+3+3+2=12 versus 4+3+4=11, so even if FBI works perfectly, it is only worth activating if you're looking at the possibility of getting attacked three times in the same round at minimum. This also means enemies can bait you into wasting motes by cleverly positioning and selecting their targets or actions - had CS #3 decided to take an Aim action instead, you would be looking at the same number of spent motes, but he'd gain a +3d bonus on his next attack while denying you a mote discount on your next attack against him (which is fine, you get it against CS #2 instead).

                    However, a more pressing concern with FBI is that its bonus only procs on successful parries and resets if an attack goes through.
                    Assuming our opponents have 8d on their first attack, they stand a mere 4% chance of hitting us; however, with our Defense steadily decreasing due to onslaught penalties, this initial slim chance goes up to 9.97% on the second, and 20.94% chance on the third attack.
                    So, how things may go down looks like this:
                    Initial roll - 96% to successfully defend; we've spent 4+3+3=10m thus far.
                    Second roll - 90.03% to successfully defend; we've spent 10+2=12m thus far.
                    Third roll - 79.06% to successfully defend; we've spent 12m total. (0.96*0.903*12=10.40m) // (0.877*0.767*12=8.0719)
                    Second roll - 9.97% to fail to defend. We've spent 12m thus far, but the bonus is reset, so we'll need to dump another 4m into a full excellency on our third parry.
                    Third roll - 79.06% to successfully defend; we've spent 16m total. (0.96*0.099*16=1.52m) // (0.877*0.233*16=3.2694)
                    Initial roll - 4% to fail to defend; we've spent 10m thus far, but we gain no Parry bonus on our second roll, forcing us to full excellency for 4m.
                    Second roll - 90.03% to successfully defend; we've spent 10+4=14m thus far. Parry increases by 1 due to FBI, so we only have to spend 2m to hit our excellency cap on the third hit.
                    Third roll - 79.06% to successfully defend; we've spent 14+2=16m total. (0.04*0.903*16=0.57m) // (0.123*0.767*16=1.5094)
                    Second roll - 9.97% to fail to defend. We've spent 14m thus far, failing to get FBI to do anything, forcing us to spend yet another 4m on a full excellency on our third parry.
                    Third roll - 79.06% to successfully defend; we've spent 18m total. (0.04*0.099*18=0.07m) //0.515
                    If we total the weighed costs, we come to the conclusion that the actual cost of using FBI against three opponents with 8d attack pools is closer to 12.5m than it is to 12. By itself, this does not seem significant - but against an opponent with just a 10d attack pool (literally a stunt's difference), we're looking at 87.7/76.7/61.2% chances of successfully defending, respectively; by plugging these numbers into the formulae above, we can calculate a total cost of 13.36 motes or thereabout, meaning that in a real scenario against attackers with 10d dice pools, it saves us less than 2m per turn even if we assume no counterplay.
