    By The Pricking of My Thumbs – John Doe’s Guide to Demon Summoning (3E)

    03-30-2020, 05:42 PM
    Introduction

    Recently I saw a post about someone struggling with summoned demons and thought there’s scope for my to provide a mini-guide.

    I’ve been playing RPGs for nearly three decades, have a strong mathematical background and came to RPGs from strategy games. I’ve been a big Exalted fan since the game first came out. Third Edition is significantly different to previous editions, and this is decidedly a Third Edition guide – some of it is based on my Second Edition experience, but this guide is definitively not intended to be for Second Ed.

    Because I’m old, I still see the need to put these disclaimers in: Exalted is a game of fantasy. You cannot really summon demons to do your bidding. Exalted’s demons do not exist. This guide will not help you summon demons in real life, and even if it did, you shouldn’t summon demons in real life. In Exalted, you can pretend to solve world hunger. It doesn’t actually solve world hunger. You can pretend to summon demons. You’re not actually summoning demons. Some games let you pretend to be a championship football manager. Some games let you pretend to be a Native American fighting invaders. Exalted lets you pretend to summon demons.

    What’s a demon anyway?

    From “δαίμων” meaning “spirit” or “divine power”, the word “demon” is inexorably linked to Christian Apocrypha, which describes demons as evil beings in thrall to Satan (the very personification of evil and adversary to god).

    Exalted, which has spirits, gods, elemental beings and fae creatures. Exalted’s demons are not named coincidentally.

    Whilst Exalted’s demons have little in common with the demons of Christian Pop Culture, they are invisible, intangible beings, cast out from the world to a prison of eternal torment.

    Demons descend from the Yozi: ancient enemies of the gods, cast down and imprisoned within the body of their King, Malfeas. Each of the Yozi possess a number of souls, the Demons of the Third Circle, who define and express their being. Each Demon of the Third Circle is in turn reflected in their souls, the less powerful the Demons of the Second Circle (who express facets of their over-souls). Demons of the First Circle are the least powerful kind of demon, serfs created by greater demons in order to perform specific functions. All demons from greatest to least are slaves, trapped in a land of eternal torment.

    Demons are not humans.

    Demons are alien beings. Most (not all) are sentient, sapient and at least as intelligent as the average human. But they do not think and behave as humans do. A demon might be driven into a killing frenzy by the sound of laughter, or be dependent on playing the harp to live as a human depends on breathing, be obsessed with abducting and swaddling babies. A demon isn’t necessarily inherently evil as such, but they are inherently different to humans.

    That said, most demons possess at least some similarities to humans. They may not be human but they’re still people. The Yozi that demons descend from are ancient, powerful and broken things: whilst each demon is an individual, no demon completely escapes this legacy.

    What are demons for?

    Exalted is a role playing game. It’s meant to be played by a group of people (dare I say, “group of friends”) for enjoyment. It is not a mathematical simulation of a world. It is not a solo exercise.

    Demons, like everything else in Exalted, are a tool designed to help you have fun by telling an interesting story.

    If demons aren’t helping you have fun, stop using them. The entire reason they exist is to help you have fun. Go have fun with something else instead.

    Okay, but what can demons do?


    Being people but not human, demons lend themselves to narratives about the morality of slavery, stories about nature vs nurture, tales about forgiveness, punishment and redemption, and building legends about right and wrong. All of these stories could be told with humans, of course, and they’d be more powerful and harder hitting with humans.

    And that’s why people use demons instead.

    Just as Marvel Comics used fictional mutants with extraordinary powers to talk about racial issues, making a story more powerful and more moving does not always equate to being more fun. Hitting people where they live by reducing their daily struggles to a game for your entertainment can be… not fun.

    Not always. Sometimes people want to tell a narrative about the struggles they face. In which case, don’t dress the story up with demons, give them humans who they can identify with and go to town. But if you don’t want to treat these themes with a light touch, maybe push them to the background, then demons are your go-to.

    I feel like you’re just being obtuse at this point...

    Good. This is a primer on how to deal with demons after all…

    Why might my character summon demons?

    Why wouldn’t they?

    Malfeas is a vast and sprawling land, larger even that Creation. Within it dwell demons hand-crafted to serve any manor of purpose that the Yozi and their souls can divine. Any task you might want to perform, there’s a demon for that.

    If you’re playing one of the Exalted (and, let’s face it, you probably are) then demons are mystically bound to serve your will.

    The question you should be asking is:

    How do I have fun summoning demons?

    A good story starts at the beginning. A great story starts in the middle. Hold that thought, we’ll return to the beginning in the middle.

    First go here and read this.

    I’m just kidding, I know you’ve already read that, and if you haven’t read it before, you’re not going to read it now. Let me sum up.

    Treantmonk surmises that there are archetypal “roles” that get filled in role playing game groups (he also contests that blindly trying to fill these roles is a bad thing, but admits people do it anyway): The Fop (or party face) does the talking (not always, but when it’s important); The Corpse (or rogue) sneaks ahead of the rest of the group, opening locked doors, disarming traps and running face first into monsters; The Gimp (or healbot) who’s role is to “service” the rest of the party between combats; and The Lump (or tank) who exists to beat down enemies in combat.

    Treantmonk then goes on to describe a fifth archetype: God (The Wizard), who controls the battlefield and the narrative, primarily through the use of minions that do god’s bidding. Treantmonk enumerates six primary reasons why summoning minions is a good idea: they help you damage enemies, they help stop you being damaged by enemies, they can help control the conditions of the battlefield (slowing enemies down, and reducing their defenses), they help with your action economy, they can be used as mounts (making you faster, yes, but also giving access to mobility abilities like flight), they can see things you might not be able to see (such as invisible, immaterial spirits). And, from a purely stimulationist, mathematical point of view, all these things hold true in Exalted.

    Sounds fun doesn’t it?

    Your poor wife!

    The problem, with god’s approach to demon summoning, is that those pesky other players (including the Storyteller) who also want to have fun. Treantmonk talks about it in his guide, but the problem is sooo much worse in Exalted.

    The first lesson in demon summoning is simple: just because you can doesn’t mean you should.

    Ask yourself two questions:
    1) Is it fun?
    2) Is it fun for everyone else?

    A lot of the time, one of those questions is going to come up “no”, and those times you should not summon demons. For many people, this can be a difficult lesson to learn. Too often RPGs condition players into a mindset of antagonism between the “players” and the “dungeon master”: The DM sets a puzzle that the players have to solve, and the players go all-out, using all their skills to solve it. Exalted really doesn’t (at the default level) work like that. Exalted is about working together to tell a story. That story is going to involve solving puzzles and using skills, but Exalted is played at a level where the story can’t survive open antagonism and outright competition between the players and the ST. All RPGs require suspension of disbelief, and all STs can beat the players: but STs generally don’t beat the players. Getting into an arms race with the ST is going to create a weird, clunky quasi-story that’s only barely supported by the accountant’s wet dream (the 662 page Exalted rule book).

    You said something about a beginning?

    Summoning a demon requires a six hour ritual, conducted with precise timing.

    In prior editions, said ritual needed to be tailored to the demon (or at least the type of demon) you are trying to summon and involved exotic ritual components and sigils.

    It’s a demanding feat whereby you draw a being from an eternal prison across the boundaries of reality, then break its will to yours. It requires ancient knowledge from a time before memory. It’s fraught with the danger of unleashing (a small part of) hell on earth.

    And you wanted to reduce it to a dice roll, didn’t you?

    Chances are, you’re playing a Solar. A being in league with the Yozi. A demon clothed in human flesh set out to destroy the righteous. Anathema to the natural order of the world.

    There’s a pretty good reason why… well just about every NPC in existence doesn’t want to let you conduct your precisely timed, mentally exacting ritual to bring another demon into the world.

    To have fun with demon summoning, I suggest, you know, actually playing out the summoning of the demon. Devote an entire session to it. Every time.

    Where are you doing it?
    Why are you doing it?
    How are you doing it?
    Who is trying to stop you?
    (And yes, we know "when" you're trying to do it -- but use the when to set the scene.)

    And now for something completely different!

    And on that note, I suggest not actually playing out whatever you want to use the demon for. Read over the Skip It sidebar on page 212 and the Resolving Battles sidebar on page 205. Not every fight is worth playing out in full, and not every fight is going to end in death.

    If you summon a blood ape to go assassinate someone, then set a difficulty using page 185 and make one roll using the ape’s grapple accuracy pool – if it succeeds, then the assassination works, if it fails then your blood ape is driven off, on a botch the blood ape is killed. Simples.

    If you have a blood ape bodyguard then narrate what that bodyguard does in combat without busting out the dice (or, alternatively, play using the blood ape and narrate what your Solar does). If the ST wants to win, then they’re going to win. No matter how powerful your character is, the ST controls the gameworld: they can throw three “you but better” characters at you whilst you’re sleeping. So put aside thoughts as to whether having that bodyguard is “worthwhile” from a combat optimization point of view: you’re already Exalted, you’re in the top 1% of the gameworld power-wise. Consider if having a demonic bodyguard is interesting from a story point of view.

    Remember your blood ape bodyguard is a hulking, cat hating monster. A living, feral embassy of hell who lives to feast on blood and gore. It’s not Vaarsuvius’ Raven. It’s always there. Snarling, hungering, barely held in check by your indomitable will. It’s not a power-up: it’s a story hook. Lean into that.

    Split the party

    Chances are you have five (or, more likely, fewer) mighty Solars in your Circle. To roam a land-mass larger than Earth’s. Maybe you want to tell a story about how you save your tiny backwater village from the Realm, but Exalted lends itself to telling grand stories about the fates of nations: mighty heroes, roaming the world, fighting creatures of legend.

    Split up. You’ll have more fun.

    But what do you do when your character is five thousand miles away?

    Play a demon.

    Which demon should I summon?

    This is not always obvious.

    In Second Edition (2.0), neomah were vastly better combatants than erymanthoi. And they had a bunch of useful powers. And they didn’t randomly murder cats. Third Edition is a lot better at making demons good at what they’re supposed to be good at, but really look at their stat-lines and read the descriptions before making your choice.

    Remember that demons are characters in their own right. Treat them as such. Like Vaarsuvius’ Raven. And remember that whilst they might be people, they’re not human. Find a demon that compliments the story you want to tell who also doesn’t squick-out the entire table. I can’t tell you which demons to summon, because I don’t know your character and I don’t know the players at your table.

    But you should have an open and honest discussion from the players at your table about what they expect from the game, and which themes (if any) make them uncomfortable. Play to that.

    Demons are incredibly versatile. If you’re looking at Exalted’s back catalog and/or homebrewing as you should, then there’s a demon for every occasion. An agata might not be as good as Chariot of Aerial Conquest, and an erymanthus might not be as good as Supernal Melee Dawn, but you can have as many agata and erymanthoi and neomah and firim and sesseljae as you like for the low, low cost of 8 Solar XP. Like Lunar shape-shifting, demon summoning is crazy versatile.

    As an ST, you want to build a story around a summoner in a similar fashion to building one around Lunar shape-shifting. The labor intensive (and more rewarding way) is to give the players multiple paths to go down. If we have to rescue the princess from her tower, then let the PCs summon a couple of blood apes and attack the front gate, or let them summon a neomah to seduce the guards, or let them summon an agata to fly up to the top of the tower. Better yet, have a princess atop her tower, and put her sister on the battlefield fifty miles east, and have the players choose whom they’re going to help in person, and whom is getting demons to come help them. The less labor intensive way is to simply raise the bar: the princess is a half-cat neko-girl so blood apes are a no go, the guards are eunuchs, and the tower is actually an underground dungeon – so demons can’t actually help you. You’re going to want to mix approaches, and encourage spot-light ceding.

    Fortunately demons aren’t an insurmountable problem. Almost everyone in Creation is a mortal. And a blood ape can beat just about any mortal. A blood ape can’t beat Ahlat. There’s a lot of scope for making demon summoning useful, but not game defining. The demons are more powerful than 90% of the enemy opposition. The PCs are vastly more powerful than the demons.

    Answers will I only chime, when questions put are asked in rhyme

    These are my first thoughts.

    I’m sure many of you disagree.
    I’m sure many more of you have read this far, and found that none of the advice I’ve given you is useful.

    Good.

      I think summoning demons for combat and then not using them in combat deeply undermines their use. If I summoned 30 blood ape demon body guards as a battle group, I’d assume I wanted them to do some fighting and some fighting with dice—presumably that I the player would run, not the ST. This is extra so for the Second Circles. Now with things like single tasks, like the assassination example, I agree. Or crafting, or other parts where player interference doesn’t matter and time isn’t pressing.
      I think your approach to demons as primarily narrative elements is interesting even if I think demons actual talents and potential numbers are their value—They provide logistics support and multiply the force for the party, which in 3e is vital.
      I’m also...not sold on the idea that demons are there to explore slave morality stories, although I guess they could. That sort of narrative seems better suited for elementals, who have a chance at freedom—while a demon you do not bind lives out an equally tyrannical if slightly more pleasant life in Malfeas.
