                        They aren't "Mary Sues" (God if I could kill one phrase from reality forever...), they're a really advanced society who have used their incredible technology to effectively solve the vast majority of problems we face today as a species and are run by stupendously intelligent machine Minds, this is kind of the point of the Culture. They don't generate the traditional narratives of storytelling, although they do involve themselves with other, less advanced societies and try to prod them towards certain views.

                        The first Culture book is about a sworn enemy of the Culture (this is thousands of years before the later Culture book) who has allied with a roughly equiv-tech expansionist Empire who the Culture oppose on ethical grounds, and even he admits that he fights the Culture because of very broad, universal ideological reasons, and the Culture is still a great place to live (he's also kind of a machinophobe, if such a word could exist). Two of the most highly regarded Culture books are about a Culture agent messing around with a society they are trying to prod in a better direction (Player of Games) and an exploration of the psyche and actions of a non-Culture mercenary who is employed by the Culture as an agent in their schemes (Use of Weapons). So the Culture books do very much recognise that the Culture is only really interesting when you put it alongside something else, something the Culture isn't, and see how they interact.

                        And there's nothing wrong with putting it alongside Creation, except that Creation simply works on different narrative rules, so there's no real way to do both settings justice IMO. If someone disagrees with that, and wants to discuss how the two settings could slot together and interact then fine! yes! Cool! Let's talk! But I don't think it's unreasonable to want you to, you know, have read a Culture novel and not be trying to poke holes in how it works as a setting without any kind of actual knowledge of that setting.

                                        I feel that Solar has accurately represented the Culture novels, I can't find any fault in Solar's representations of them. In fact, Player of Games in its opening chapters very accurately conveys why a novel set only in the Culture proper would be boring - because nothing really wrong/bad/conflicting ever takes place within the culture. Even when a faction decides to split off from the Culture (like the Zetetic Elench), the Culture is generally, "Well, okay, you're free to go if you'd like. Keep in touch, though, because we still care about you." The Culture only becomes really interesting when it's dealing with something outside of itself, an Outside Context Problem so to speak (I just started reading Excession and this book is blowing my mind).

                                        In Excession, the topic of Sublimation is discussed. This is basically Stargate's Ascension. It's transcending material existence to exist as pure consciousness on a "higher plane of existence." The Culture could Sublimate but chooses not to because, it's noted, that none of the Elder Civilizations which have Sublimated have chosen to deal with harmful hegemonies or save populated worlds which are little too close to supernovae. They're content to remain in transcendent bliss and abandon the lesser forms of life and civilization to cruel chance. The Culture isn't comfortable leading behind the universe in which they do a great deal of good. The Culture is a civilization of Space Bodhisattvas, in this way. They believe in maximizing happiness for all living beings, but they also believe in the freedom to choose. So they'll never force a civilization to change, but they'll encourage it in interesting ways. Player of Games shows a very interesting way the Culture destabilizes an objectively horrible Empire in which rape and slavery are the most mild of their daily atrocities.

                                        If the Culture were to encounter Creation, their approach would belie their abilities. They never allow lesser civilizations to know the full extent of what they're capable of because they're worried that when the average person learns that an egalitarian utopia is possible, that it exists, and that they're not in one because their leaders are selfish, greedy assholes, they'd revolt. There'd be widespread violence, death and new power structures would emerge. This would create more problems than it would solve. So the Culture would play at money, creating wealth to use in Creation's economies - enough to interact with it, but not so much that they'd be considered wealthy. Once they've inserted themselves into Creation's socio-economic fabric, they'd examine its power structures and see very quickly that power in Creations comes from money and essence. Essence-Users (including the Exalted) tend to make their way into positions of wealth, influence and authority. This entire top-down power structure would stop being effective if everyone could use essence, and so the Culture would find a way to allow everyone to use Essence. It wouldn't necessarily offer that to Creation's average citizen at first, but everyone who is part of the Culture would have that ability.

                                            ...and that brings us back around to the core problem of trying to mash a high-fantasy world and a hard sci-fi world together.

                                            In Creation, according to Exalted's default assumptions, not everyone can use Essence because the world was not made with that possibility in mind, literally. The most powerful essence-users are the Primordials. Those beings that made the world, decided the rules that would govern it, and populated it with their progeny and creations decided they weren't interested in competition, so they didn't make any - except the gods, who were bound to obey them. The kind of magic it would take to turn all sentient beings into essence users would be completely unprecedented, if it were possible at all - the Working that made it possible for everyone to learn sorcery would pale in comparison. And even if everyone could use essence, not everyone would be able to use it equally because your ability to wield essence is dependent mostly on how much effort you put into it (leaving aside details of your approach, be it meditation or hard training or persuading large numbers of people to worship you).

                                            To make the whole thing even remotely plausible, you'd probably have to hack together some kind of motonic physics model of essence that would mesh with real physics instead of the narrative conventions essence follows now. Knowledge of the power of essence would entirely cease to be a sign of your transcendent understanding of the world and instead be something like knowledge of gravity or electromagnetism - a force that anyone can manipulate with the right tools. In short, the Culture's setting assumptions would totally override Exalted's setting assumptions.

                                            As has been talked about before, you can pick and choose elements from one setting to put in another if you reskin them a bit - I still think Autocthon is very Mind-like - but direct crossovers seem to inevitably result in this unresolvable clash of ideas.

                                            I also disagree with the assumption that Creation's societies are categorically "lesser" than the Culture. Probably the Minds and Contact humans would think that way, but that doesn't make it less subjective.

                                                Like I said, I feel that people are ignoring two things:

                                                1. From Creation's perspective, the Culture's Minds are not substantially more powerful than the Primordials. They pose certain problems (lacking what a Solar would consider a physical body, say, and to the extent that they have any physical presence it is utterly unapproachable), but the Solars have killed things like that repeatedly before. Therefore, any crossover that doesn't allow the Solars to kill at least one Mind before they realize they have to defend themselves is IMHO completely ignoring everything that makes Creation what it is; I don't think it's reasonable to even discuss such a crossover. The question in your write-up is therefore "how does the Culture react when its minds start dying, feeling fear, and so on?"

                                                2. On the other hand, from the Culture's perspective? The culture and its minds are explicitly not transcendent. The idea that the Culture could encounter a civilization that looks primitive but which has some technology that even Culture minds are unable to comprehend is not outside of the scope of their genre. (From the Culture's perspective, the Primordials and perhaps the Incarnae are to an extent paradoxes, in that they're transcended beings who have not completely lost interest in the world... although I guess the Incarnae mostly have, now. The Exalted are people who are otherwise primitive but who were given chunks of technology far beyond what even the Culture can begin to analyze. Encountering weird situations like that is, as people have said, what you do when you want an interesting Culture novel.)

                                                That interpretation fits in with both the assumptions of the Culture novels and with the assumptions of the Exalted. It's the most interesting way to look at it, beyond that. It feels like some people above are saying "no, no, the Culture always wins", which is definitely not in the books; or even just "Creation is primitive, of course the Culture wins against primitive people", which is, again, not what Creation would look like to the Culture itself once they encounter Exaltation. In certain specific ways (that is, when it comes to Exaltation), it's reasonable to assume that the 'technology' behind Exaltation vastly exceeds that of the Culture; that's the only way you can really run the two of them side by side and get a meaningful story that respects the assumptions of both.

                                                    I don't know if this will help, just to be clear though, on my post, a lot of the point of what I posted at least was *not* exactly that the Minds would for sure instantly solve Creation's problems.

                                                    That all depends on interpretation to a degree. The Great Curse might (in a crossover) be something that they just genuinely couldn't crack or get a handle on - there might be magic that goes "No matter how smart you are and how much you can work out about history, you won't see this in plain sight" or if they suggested solutions, the answer might elude them. For instance. You'd have to decide.

                                                    The point really was that they aren't transcendent intelligences like Primordials or the Neverborn, who are hostile or aggressive to humanity and regard it as at best a resource to be exploited. They're not ones with weird, "alien" or robotic blindspots that prevent them understanding spirituality or ethics or human potential (or potentially understanding Essence).

                                                    They're transhuman intelligences who are kind and well intentioned and have a great understanding of people. They don't blunder in and attack others. They work out what's happening, how the world around them works and adapt to follow suit. And because they don't believe in scarcity or zero sum situations or winner takes all and are genuinely committed to making a better world for humanity, they try and help, without just straight up controlling people to do what they think is right.

                                                    They wouldn't, by their own intention, probably be a foe for the Solars at least to fight. And if they are, chances are you're not necessarily going to be rooting for the Exalted as the champions of humanity against a force that would oppress it (rather the opposite of anything). Without a great deal of saying they can't do what they normally would be expected to do because magic (and if you do this a lot, why include the Minds anyway?) then should achieve a good deal of progress.

                                                    Their levels of intelligence are very consistent with being able to figure out the history of Creation very quickly and they certainly do have a huge amount information on a vast number of cultures from across space in reality for comparison. Many of them (it changes as the Culture books take place at different times in the Culture's timeline) are meant to be civilization scale intelligences equivalent to entire space opera civilizations in their ability.

                                                    To use an (perhaps shaky) analogy, if I said, for some reason, "Mohandas Gandhi suddenly appears in Creation. Except he has the speed of thought many orders of magnitude faster than any human can, and access to as much knowledge and intellectual capacity as an entire far future space opera civilization" and the response was "Well, Gandhi will be all at sea in Creation, as it is immune to his scientific analysis. And the Exalted will totally be able to kill Gandhi before he realises what's up". Then wouldn't it be like "Seriously (leaving aside the assumptions about why Gandhi wouldn't grok Essence) why are you killing Gandhi again? Are you Tyler Durden? He is a nice man with, in this scenario, to say again, the intellectual and productive capacity of an entire far future space opera civilization at his command."?

                                                    Although, in a crossover, I could actually see forces like the Deathlords or Fair Folk taking on or trying to suborn the Minds - which could work because magic if you want, and then they might need some magical assistance as a hard counter.

                                                    (If a Mind did die to a Creation born force, my mental image doesn't really involve them feeling fear exactly. More like acting like Hange Zoe does to the Titans).

                                                    As to the actual Culture books, yeah, like others have said the main questions that come up IRC (and you'd be better off looking at synopses of the books for this, as it has been a while) are hard ethical questions on how the Culture interacts with other civilizations and personal level drama between people within Culture societies, and particularly the Culture's malcontents, usually in remote areas away from where the Minds are. They were very compelling books, when I read them, as everyone has said. Banks generally does all this while respecting his premise of including strongly godlike friendly AI.
