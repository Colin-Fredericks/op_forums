    From the magic side of things, the way to avoid Dreams of the First Age is with a few very high level artifacts or workings doing a huge amount of heavy lifting each. Even effects with lots of small uses should all obviously come from the same thing.

    For example, instead of streetlights, central heating, and blast furnaces you build a giant red jade temple that makes on demand minor elementals for that sort of thing.


    What makes these schemes start to look technological is the ubiquity of individual pieces of technology. Giant gangs of slaves building a magic pyramid doesn't violate genre aesthetics even if that pyramid happens to also be wireless bank that teleports people's ritual money around. So you can get credit cards and wire fraud without looking too modern.

                    That's not a bad point, but at least for Solars, I think it's a surprisingly small problem in most cases.

                    3E works really hard to present Solars with the Superman problem - they can do almost anything, but can't be everywhere at once. All of their society-altering tricks, then, get balanced along ideas of scale - an even semi-focus Solar should be able to treat any disease/cross any distance/steal any object/persuade any person, but can only work on so many patients, only bring so many people on fast travel, etc. That's a compelling area to explore, and it does reintroduce many of the issues which were trivialized so quickly in 2E.

                    But it also means that a Solar ruler is about a billion times better at getting a few dozen powerful people on board with her vision than she is at getting specific results from the general populace. In particular, any individual dissenter who (1) is known to the Solar and (2) lacks some MASSIVE supernatural protection can be swayed from a bitter enemy to a (literal) worshipful lackey in under half an hour. Adjusting the attitudes of the upper class as a whole is trickier, but ultimately doable (gather them together regularly for some impressive Performance recitals).

                    Generally, where Solars are involved, the "old aristocrats who impede your works not out of malice so much as scorn for the lessers you uplift" seem less likely to be the upper class of whatever society you're working on, and more likely to be local gods, rival nations lacking a dialogue with you, and (of course) the Dragon-Blooded.

                    "For me, there's no fundamental conflict between really loving something and also seeing it as very profoundly flawed." -- Jay Eddidin

                        I agree with the broad point, although I personally dislike the term "social engineering" because I associate it with high modernist disasters. I prefer to speak in terms of creating incentives; you create an incentive for a cash-based market economy in which the agrarian classes devote a lot of time to producing profitable goods by requiring them to pay taxes in cash. You attempt to engineer a city into being some uniform thing built according to a rigid grid plan in which there's a place for everything, and everything in its place (and then look in on bewilderment trying to figure out the inevitable failure on several fronts).

                                            "Piers Mitchell said: “Modern research has shown that toilets, clean drinking water and removing faeces from the streets all decrease risk of infectious disease and parasites. So we might expect the prevalence of faecal oral parasites such as whipworm and roundworm to drop in Roman times – yet we find a gradual increase. The question is why?”
                                            One possibility Mitchell offers is that it may have actually been the warm communal waters of the bathhouses that helped spread the parasitic worms. Water was infrequently changed in some baths, and a scum would build on the surface from human dirt and cosmetics. “Clearly, not all Roman baths were as clean as they might have been,” said Mitchell."
