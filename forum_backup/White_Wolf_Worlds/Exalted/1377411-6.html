    Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post

    For a start, because it's an element of my cultural background; I was brought up learning about English religious persecution in Ireland.

    But expanding from that, the degree to which England drove all other religions underground stands out to me just that little bit more from the manner in which they persecuted even their own offshoots of Anglicanism. That's the major thing, the manner in which the Immaculate Philosophy is the only permissable religion, more than its specific theology.

    Mind, there's also the fact that Anglicanism is entwined with its state to a degree that stands out even among European religions. The monarch is still head of the church, and as the monarch is "advised" by the prime minister clerical appointments are a religious topic. Bishops remain prominent peers in the House of Lords, and I'm familiar with the history of when that meant something. That resonates with the kind of authority the Order has, where it sanctions and is sanctioned by the state.

    And the manner in which it was incorporated into the colonialism stands out because, in addition to the local examples, there's just so much of it that the British dotted around the world.

    Plus the way that I find the Perfected Hierarchy to feel kind of like dharma by way of Protestant Work Ethic.
    Some observations: you seem to be suggesting that this is the lens through which to view the Immaculate Order and the Realm, because the US is sufficiently drenched with the influence of Anglicanism to the extent that it can't help but be the lens through which US authors would view it. This strikes me as implausible for a variety of reasons, particularly the different relation of Church and State in the US, where most players and authors of Exalted will be from.

    It's true that the British empire (and 19th century imperialism in general) *is* an influence on aspects of the Realm and its imperialism in some ways (but not others [1]), but it's also the case that the original setting developer claims to have taken the relationship between Buddhism and the state in Imperial China as his model for the relationship between the Immaculate Philosophy and the state. There is *plenty* of precedent for suppression of rival sects by the politically dominant, state supported sect in Chinese history -- a good summary of this happening during the Tang can be found in the chapter on religion in Lewis' China's Cosmopolitan Empire, for instance. Another important dysanalogy between Anglicanism and the IP would be that the Empress is not the head of the order in the same way the monarch is the supreme governer of the Church of England and the Church of Scotland [2, 3]; , which is again closer to the case of the relationship between Imperial throne and a powerful sect in China or (I assume) to the relationship between the Catholic Church and the monarchy in the Spanish and Portugese Empires (aka the reason why there are a lot of Roman Catholics in South America).

    I'm not saying that you're wrong that it's a lens through which to view the Realm and the IP -- it clearly can shed some useful light on it (but then, so can Roman and Persian imperialism) -- but the authoritative way you state it as if it were the primary lens through which everyone else should view it just seems off to me, especially in that it seems to place some fairly specific aspects of the history of the British Isles at the centre of the influences on these texts and their interpretation that the authors might reasonably be expected to know very little about.

    Also, some general points about Anglicanism: While much of what you say about the political structure of Anglicanism is probably fair enough when talking about the period between Henry VIII wanting to get his divorce on and the end of the 19th century, it's really not true in the way you make out anymore. To begin with, the state church in Scotland is Presbytarian, not Anglican (Scottish Anglicanism is represented by the Scottish Episcopalians from whom US Episcopalians are descended); neither the Church of Ireland or Church in Wales (both disestablished, one in the late 19th century and one in the 20th) regard the monarch as their Supreme Governer, nor does any province of the Church outside of England (though the Queen is a Canon of the Cathedral of St Davids, which if it weren't a meaningless honourary title would just mean that she's technically subordinate to the local bishop). It's also worth noting the contributions of Welsh and Scottish non-Anglican Protestant missionaries to the imperial project in addition to those of Anglican missionaries (this was a big thing in 19thC Welsh nationalism, for instance), but also that Catholic and Eastern Orthodox empires were prone to exporting religion in a similar fashion. While the presence of (English) Bishops in the House of Lords is a constitutional anomaly worth fixing, you could argue that the Church of England is entangled with the state to far less an extent than the Lutheran Churches in Scandinavia were until comparatively recently, where clergy were direct employees of the state and church buildings were state property, neither or which are the case in the UK [4]. (In fact, legally clergy aren't even employees under UK law.)

    The Protestant Work Ethic (to the extent that it exists at all as a thing) strikes me as more a non-Conformist or at best Low Church Anglican thing rather than describing the denomination as a whole (there exist a whole bunch of Anglicans who dislike being called Protestants, as they regard themselves as more Catholic than the Pope), but I'm willing to be corrected on that.

    I mean, yeah, the Anglican Church has a pretty awful history that it needs to account for, but it's one that's bad enough by itself without any need for overstatement.



    [1] One dysanalogy would be that the Realm doesn't seem to do settler colonialism to the same extent as 19th century empires; it tends to prefers tributaries.

    [2] Specifically the Church of England and the Church of Scotland (the latter is not Anglican, btw). Not the Church in Wales, the Church of Ireland, the Scottish Episcopalians or any of the other provinces.

    [3] Even if it were, it would be hard to argue that that was the specific influence and not -- given Grabowski's statements -- the relationship between Qing Kangxi and Tibetan Buddhism.

    [4] All of these pale in comparison to Imperial Russia post-Peter the Great, where the Orthodox church wound up a government department in an absolutist state par excellance and contributed in the settler colonialist rampages around Siberia and the Cacausus.

                                In my Creation, the Twilight Caste aren't the bigtime sorcerers. That role belongs to the Eclipse Caste, to whom the role of Sorcerers fits much more naturally. Twilight Castes are sages, academics, architects, artificers, doctors, savants, detectives, and the like. They are the overwhelmingly intellectual Caste of the Solar Exalted. Sorcery is not an intellectual affair in Exalted, the way that magic is in many other games. Tying magic to study feels like a sacred cow of other rpgs brought over when Exalted was first written.

                                Sorcerers in Exalted are mysterious and enigmatic figures who gain their powers through the trauma of the Wyld, or through pacts with Demons or Elementals, through bonds with powerful and strange items or places. The Eclipse Caste literally has an Anima Power that lets them seal bargains and make pacts with strange forces. It seems so obvious that Sorcery should be a thing that they're known for, rather than Twilights.

                                            - The ruins of Rathess are within the borders of Ixcoatli, and it is a holy site to them. They (erroneously in the biological sense, but accurately in a cultural sense) claim the Dragon Kings they know relatively little of as their forbearers. They don’t realize who the beastial claw striders in the jungle really are.

                                            - Coral is a matrilineal patriarchy with little sense of marriage. Men have little responsibility for their own kids, but rather for their sisters’ kids. The patriarch of most children’s homes are their maternal uncle, or maternal-maternal grand uncle.

                                            - Wavecrest has chain marriages with every individual allowed to take two spouses. There is cultural rhetoric about a single great chain, but this is not literally the case.

                                            - the river Elidad starts in the Fire Peaks near Gem and flows north to break into a fertile river delta at the sea that includes Lap. Kingdoms like Zephyr are spread up and down its annually flooding banks. Besides being Gem’s main traderoute, it also is the course by which the Realm reinforces its legions through the Satrapies.

                                            - If the above evokes the Nile, than the river that flows from the Summer Mountains to the Dreaming Sea that Kamathahar sits upon should evoke the Niger, in how it forms the spine of the Prasadi Empire the way the Niger did the Mali, and allows for a capital so far inland while the bulk of its empire follows the river’s course to the sea.

                                            - Creation has a slight curve, as all of it is built on the slopes of the Pole of Earth continuing outward and downward to form the fundament. Dawn in Wavecrest is roughly five hours after dawn in Ixcoatli.

                                            (this is necessary as the alternative is that on the flat plane, dawn is simultaneous everywhere, but morning is short and bright in the East followed by a loooong slow dimming evening, while the reverse would be the case in the West, with the brief evening as the brightest time of day. A curve allows everywhere to have the same length mornings and afternoons, but requires that they aren’t simultaneous)

                                            - Creation’s stable Pole Star always points centerward, not North. The dome of heaven turns on its yearly axis around the Imperial Mountain, as a literal axis mundi. This means all the known constellations are always in the sky together rather than rising and falling over the horizon. Relevant signs are thus highly influenced by where you are in Creation - as two children born the same night are born under different signs in different corners of Creation. Of course where the five planets (literally wandering stars) are among the constellations at any given time are of extreme importance to any given reading.

                                            Rename Shepherd of the Northstar as Shepherd of the Polestar
