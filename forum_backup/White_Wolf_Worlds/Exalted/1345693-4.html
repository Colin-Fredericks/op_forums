    Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
    You're shifting the goalposts away from "read what's in the book" to "I don't actually buy into what the book depicts".

    That said, on top of Lux consistently being portrayed as quite the little giant city diva, and the idea of a grandiose hero opting for a dramatic and showy method over the practical one (see the very fact that Lux has paramagnetic catapults in the first place as opposed to... bridges), you're just kind of overlooking details such as it being a described as a last resort tactic at a time when the city's usual defences had been shut down (a good faith reading of that might conclude that flinging regular projectiles was off the table) and, again, Estasia was facing a war that was unlike the norm for conflict within the limiting confines of the Realm of Brass and Shadow.
    This sounds a lot like "I don't agree with you, but I do agree with you". Which is it? Was Lux flattening a raiding party or was he flattening an invading force that could threaten his security and his people?

    Hard is good enough to make something uncommon.
    Uncommon multiplied by thousands of years equals plenty of examples.

    And always failed.
    You are missing the point that the Octet has had many reasons and plenty of time to develop the same sorts of logistical practices we've seen developed in the real world. The German military in World War 2 went from centralized tank repair at German factories to a developed and decentralized doctrine of forward operating depots by the end of the war, largely due to circumstances analogous to what the Octet would have to deal with, such as round-trip travel times and logistical problems like disconnected rail lines.

    Whether the wars were successful or not means little to the incentive structure to find ways to ameliorate the challenges a people are facing.

    It doesn't come out of every wall, and even if it did, that wouldn't mean there is no value in possessing a surplus while also denying rivals the same.
    You are thinking of nutrient slurry like a farm. It isn't. It's like a river. The only way to deny it to your rivals is to divert it entirely. Such things are easier said than done, especially if you want to divert it to your own territory. That's a major infrastructure project in hostile territory, justifying the need for extended logistics.

    Like, of barrels of slurry are easier to carry then pallets of steel, and you steal them from people that will pay you steel to get back the slurry (because people need to eat every day and time spent increasing production of slurry is time taken away from some other industry), which might the raiders go for?
    I don't think you've thought this through. If there's barrels sitting around somewhere, you are either dealing with some outpost community that exists for a specific purpose and is being supplied by delivery due to distance from the most convenient nutrient slurry tap or you are dealing with surplus stored against disruptions of normal supply - a surplus that exists because the community produces enough to eat and then some. Only the first group is likely to negotiate and only if they do not have enough remaining to tide them over until the next supply delivery through some measure of rationing. Also, what's to stop them from shooting you when you come to make the exchange? After all, they'll know where you'll be, they'll have time to bring in reinforcements, and a raid often relies upon the element of surprise to be successful.

    Besides which, barrels of nutrient slurry are probably one of the lightest things you are likely to get through raiding and they are still going to be hefty. Liquids like water are a kilogram a liter or over 8 lbs a gallon, making a twenty gallon drum of slurry somewhere in the range of 150 lbs. If you get lucky it might be bales of cloth that you seize - the Octet do have ways of producing that - but a lack of new clothes is less compelling than a lack of food, so that's going to be lower priority on your "take stuff and then sell it back to them" plan.

    Any which way you slice it, you'll need to move a large volume of goods from where you can get them and off to where you can use them, so you are looking at wagons, carts, trains, trams, etc. that need guarded until the plunder is shipped home. While that happens, you'll have an army in the field in hostile territory.

    Excavated? Why are they attacking mines instead of warehouses?
    Because mines aren't located inside sentient cities that can have automated defense grids. Outlying warehouses will be near the mines and only have enough space to store what's going to be shipped to the factories on the next tram if the system is in any way optimized, maybe a little more if they have surplus production that will be eventually result in a double shipment. If you want more than a few days worth of stuff, you'll have to hold on to it and work it yourself, just like has happened in every modern real-world war. Want oil? Well, the enemy isn't going to work the derricks for you once they are dead or run off.

    Shorter supply lines are generally why you don't want to be locked down in one place. If you're going after something that will take long enough to load up that enemies will surround you, you're doing residing incorrectly.
    I assume you mean "raiding" not "residing".

    That's why real-world raids are over things that were mobile in some way. You seized wagons of goods, livestock, slaves, or small high-value items like gold coins or silver plates. However, Autochthonia largely doesn't have livestock, slavery doesn't get any mention that I can recall, they don't really buy into the idea of value beyond utility, and internal transit systems are more akin to trains than wagons and so not easily diverted. Smash-and-grab raiding isn't a thing that functions well in Autochthonia on the scale of the Eight Nations and the conflicts between them.

    I mean, it's not as if we lack historical example of this. Vikings, Saracens, the Anglo-Scottish border, pastoral nomads out of China's northern frontier, all examples of societies that enriched themselves on a sustained process of using high mobility and good planning to get in, grab lots of valuable materials and people, then get out before an enemy force could be mobilised against them and cut off their line of retreat.
    Note the small size of the groups doing the raiding for small volumes of stuff. One man can carry away enough to feed himself for weeks or a tribe for days, but not a nation.

    True, in many of these cases, sustained campaigns of raising gradually evolved into straight up colonisation, but the Scandinavians were a bit dependent on things like Britain not eventually being completely inaccessible from Scandinavia. It also being a lot more accessible owing to the fact that there is a while sea to sail across, rather than being to approach via tunnels.
    So in other words, raiding for small volumes of goods only works if you can hit whenever you want, easily move what you take, and can be relatively immune to reprisal. Not things that apply to city-dwelling nations, nor the kinds of resources that said nations want, nor to the terrain that the Octet live in. Instead, their warfare over resources is going to require that they get substantial amounts of resources out of the venture, require significant manpower to mount the expedition, and require a period of time spent fending off reprisals (because as long as you can reach them, they can reach you). In short, it's going to be a war and require logistical planning.

    Well, some kinds of operations. Lightning raids still function today, facilitated by things like planes. They're not concerned with the acquisition of commodities, but their opposition have advantages that Autochthonians will generally lack, such as wider implementation of more accurate and destructive ranged weaponry.
    The fact that modern raids aren't about resources means that they aren't comparable except in duration of initial combat.

    Alchemicals can be the military equivalent of a lot of things. Sometimes they're commandos, where you outfit them for stealth, speed and precision that increases the time they can stay ahead of and harass enemies before they need to go back to the vats. Maybe they sometimes go outfitted for maximum destruction because the person planning the raid decided to go for a blitzkrieg approach, all "go in and obliterate everything, sustain fire while the troops pick up the goods and then fall back". Maybe there's an Alchemical build that is the equivalent of anti-armour rockets, but if it takes three days for them to get there then a viable tactic is to not be there anymore. Retreat into those tunnels that are hard to be followed into and easier to set traps in. Perhaps use your blasty powers to cave in the entrance.
    You missed the equivalency. They are the equivalent of tanks because they are high-power assets that require regular maintenance and upkeep if you expect them to function at optimal capacity for extended lengths of time. Your commando eventually needs to come back because the mission is accomplished or irrelevant and once he does you need to refit him if you don't immediately have another commando thing to do. You can try to do a blitzkrieg thing with a commando, but it won't work as well, much like a tank that's got some holes in the armor or a gun that's jamming. If you have to send your Alchemical all the way back to the factory for refit, you incur tons of logistical overhead in the field use of Alchemicals.

    Compass: Autochthonian depicts a world where a fairly uniform resource constraint is the inability to mobilise the vats needed for Exalted to respec, so they need to plan around that.
    Honestly, it does a poor job of depicting warfare between the Eight Nations with any level of substantial detail beyond "they use crossbows as primary weapons" and "they use magitech stuff and Alchemicals". About the only place that it is actually addresses the question of building vats complexes outside cities is the sidebar about building them in Creation, where the stuff mentioned is for a full sized complex capable of servicing arbitrary numbers of Champions and Collosi. Something in the field, able to service one Alchemical at a time, has significantly smaller power and resource needs.

    Interestingly, the entry for blightborn, IIRC, mentions that they often find ways to build vats complexes in blight zones, guided to service Apostate Alchemicals, establishing that vats can be created in Autochthonia outside of Alchemical cities (a thing we also know because the first Alchemicals were built before there were Alchemical cities). A mobile vat option, either a standalone set of artifact gear or a Collossus Charm usable only by Champions, would fit fairly seamlessly into the setting without disrupting the existing depictions of Autochthonian warfare, provided there was some level of time constraint on setup and redeployment of such.

    It's going to be hard in any event to have pitched battles in this world, there is often not the room.
    I don't expect pitched battles, although there is apparently often room. Various nations inhabit cavities measured in the miles, especially Claslat, Nurad, and Jarish, and those aren't depicted as bizarre oddities.

                        Originally posted by Ghosthead View Post
                        Separately, on playing a Lunar to play a "hero with a home turf that makes them better, stronger, and seems to empower them with the people and the land", this is where it kind of feels to me like I want to protest that this should be possible within a Solar's aesthetic and story. But! This is probably where even *very* flexible character types as in Exalted run up against limits, and that's just kind of the way it is.
                        Well, it totally is possible as a Solar. If you've got a concept for a character, 99% of the time a Solar can do it. That's one of the reasons they're the corebook splat. A decent amount of Solar charms involve intimacies, like Fists of Iron Technique, that gets bonus damage if you're striking a hated foe, defending a beloved friend, or simply enjoy the thrill of punching. You could easily use your intimacy to defend your home or your people in that and it would make you stronger by virtue of defending your home.

                        It's not really the same though, because you could be using any intimacy for that, and there's ones that are probably a lot easier to apply more of the time, depending on where you are.

                        You could always write your own charms that do, of course. Especially especially if you end up getting to Essence 6+, those can start to break out of the mold of "Solar charms" and much more into "Charms for just my character." The only thing really holding you back is balance. Solar charms are already the absolute limit of Exalted power, so any you make shouldn't really be any stronger than the ones printed, but you also probably want to make them competitive with the printed ones too. Nobody wants to buy a charm that just performs worse in some situations for no benefit.

                        It's a fine line to walk.
