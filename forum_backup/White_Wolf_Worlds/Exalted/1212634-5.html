            Quentyn and Oberyn/Oberon are not notably Welsh; Tristan is Welsh, granted; Lewyn and Arianne are only Welsh in the bad pseudo-Celtic fantasy sense [1]. Names show no sign of a voiceless alveolar fricative (the notorious 'll' which is a particular feature of Welsh) or similar consonantal complications; something like 'Blackmont' would be 'Mynydd Du' (in modern spelling; medieval orthography would be different) though there could be a translation convention for epithets, granted; there are no surnames, only epithets or patronymics, the latter of which you must be able to recite several generations back in order to establish a claim on land or as part of a family. (Eg. Gruffydd ap Owain [2] is from House Aberffraw, but you'd not call him Gruffydd Aberffraw unless it's his nickname and it certainly wouldn't substitute for a recitation of lineage in a legal context.)

            In addition, Dorne diverges greatly from Wales in terms of inheritance law -- it has gender neutral primogeniture. Wales had a gavelkind system -- and medieval Welsh politics makes no sense through the lens of primogeniture. Dorne seems to have no obsession with bards, and not nearly enough messy internicine warfare resulting from the inheritance system, so I'm not really seeing the alleged similarities beyond a sketchy 'well, this has a political set up that's kind of like Wales under Llywelyn the Great, but really nothing else is the same at all'.

            EDIT: Some example Welsh names can be found here, though some of them are a bit archaic, some of the given meanings/origins are suspect and the spelling isn't always reliable.

            [1] There are Welsh names similar to Arianne, but they're usually more like Arianrhod, Arianwen or something similar. 'Arianne' also conflicts with Welsh orthography; my non-expert guess is that it's more Latin in origin.

            [2] 'Gruffydd son of Owain'.
