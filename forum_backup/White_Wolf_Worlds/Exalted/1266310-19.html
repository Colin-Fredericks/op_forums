                                                What are the actual mistakes and what were limitations of the narrative there, though?

                                                It's not like the characters were shown much outside "complete wilderness", "a school that clearly is built mostly by people that think otherwise" (which makes sense considering its cultural roots and the fact anyone there is a terrifyingly strong fighter and highly likely to be willing to act on the offence), "a haven that the people fighting against discrimination got for themselves and reign absolute" (menagerie) and, briefly, mistral and argos.
                                                These are evidence that such racism is not ubiquitous, and that it follows diverse cultural lines. Which is close to how it works in the real world, you know? Peoples' ideas shift differently based on the different circumstances in which they live.

                                                So I agree it's not very visible. But it didn't feel, to me, nonsensical. They almost never went to places where it'd make sense for such discrimination to be pervasive, much less the norm, but not in a way that denotes "the decried discrimination is not as common as they complained it is".


                                                If anything, what really broke sense for me is why it persisted the way it did before a threat like the grimm. Common enemies are excellent ways to persuade different people to band together. Precariousness is a magnifier for both violence and selflessness. Few things are more precarious than literal boogeymen indistinctly eating both standard human and faunus human. Whatever happened to overcome that huge (both magnitude and duration) stimulus to band together requires explanations that were not given (our sampling of hunters correlates well with this, for example). The alternative would be making such prejudice a recent development, and that's not how it was portrayed, thus demanding further explanation as well.

                                                        Yes, it absolutely makes sense that if a story takes place in locations where a setting element doesn't exist strongly, it won't be very visible.
                                                        But then you have to ask yourself: Why is it a setting element, if you're setting the story away from it?
                                                        Either a setting element is important, in which case the writer should take steps to ensure it has enough visibility, or it isn't important or shown much, in which case it won't be a strong source of drama to use.

                                                        I personally found the Faunus discrimination was reasonably strongly portrayed, by the standards of this show at least, but I do understand that some might feel that a story including a faunus as a protagonist should devote more focus to what that means.

                                                        As for everyone banding together against the grim: Humans are great at unifying against outside threats as long as the in-group doesn't run out of resources (room, food, jobs etc). As soon as there isn't enough to go around, we are all to happy to splinter into ever smaller in-groups and exclude ever more people into new out-groups. And then all it takes is one Adam and a war starts.

Society has, by and large, been ignored in favor of the mission or for the character drama, and not so much on the character vs society aspect of the story, even going so far as to have a lot of it's world building handled by the World of Remnant sections rather than finding ways to integrate that information into the show.
