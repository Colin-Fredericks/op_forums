    Having watched this discussion spiral around the same themes for the last nine pages, I decided, as my first post on these forums, to post out my view on the matter. Especially because I find this thread having potential, should it be directed more constructively.

    First I shall talk about the most likely reason why everyone keeps on runnign to iron as an abhorrence for the fae in the Exalted. I would not like to do this kind of wall of text, but as the originator of this post wants a DEFINITIVE answer on a feature in a fantasy RPG setting, and has refused to consider whether this feat is impossible, I feel I need to try and explain this from the beginning, as these themes have been touched in this post before, but they have either not been understood or they have not been explained well enough.

    Contrary to some strange notions on this thread, the idea of harmful Iron has been around longer than the Kipling poem that was mentioned. Kipling wrote this poem on 1910, but beliefs and superstitions regarding iron have been around much longer. I am unsure why the originator of this post pointed this poem akin to a source on the "cold iron" myth on modern days. I am unsure if he ment it as so, but it came across like it. However, it is a good example about the problematic position on the nature of iron.

    The abhorrent nature of iron has been around far longer. The Bible's Book of Judges has a fairly famous verse (Judges 1:19) where God is unable to bring the men of Judah victory over an enemy for the stated reason that they had "chariots of iron". Many historians consider it an anachronism by the author writing centuries later, since at the time of the battle iron-wrought chariots would have been unheard of. As it turns out, other mythological sources have similiar takes on the nature of iron as something that repels the magical and the unexplainable. The Islamic djinn were also said to dread iron. Also in the norse Saga of Hervor and Heidrek from the 13th century, King Svafrlami catches two dwarfs by using iron. Thus, the idea of iron as harmful to supernatural has been around for good thousand plus years, and in different cultures.

    I would argue that even the idea that iron was harmful only to faeries is not technically so. Or in actuality, our perception on what counts as a "faerie" has changed. Djinn, for example, are not "faerie" in the sense that we understand. I argue, that the term was used in medieval times to have more widespread meaning than what we give it today. For example, in the book Faerie Folklore in Medieval Tales – an Introduction, it is mentioned that during middle ages such creatures as banshees were counted among the numbers of the fae or fair folk. Creatures which modern popular culture labels as undead. Also during much modern times, in 1910 novel Phantom of the Opera, a book from Gaston Leroux that later inspired the musical of the same name by Andrew Lloyd Webber, everyone at the Paris Opera relies on iron for protection against "the ghost." I point out these examples as a reminder, that even though Exalted "fair folk" are not faeries as we understand such notions, neither are all the creatures of the mythology that people of those earlier times considered as such. Thus the term "fair folk" is more akin to "otherworldly" and "unnatural" than faeries with wings. This, I think, Exalted takes very well into account.

    Many modern fantasy writers have used iron as harmful to faeries since Kiplings poem and Phantom of the Opera. Most notably such masters as Terry Pratchett in his Discworld series, Neil Gaiman in his Stardust book, Robert Jordan in his Wheel of Time series and David Eddings in his Elenium and the Tamuli series. Many more slightly less- known fantasy authors fall into using cold iron rather than steel as a weapons against the supernatural. Patrick Rothfuss' The Name of the Wind, Josepha Sherman's A Strange and Ancient Name. T.H White's Once and Future King and many many more. Many RPG:s such as Dungeons and Dragons, the one that lift the RPG genre into prominence, have similiar notions on cold iron - material harming the fey creatures. And Exalteds previous company, White Wolf's RPG: Changeling worked closely on this faerie theme and the abhorence on iron. WoD IS relevant, read on.

    Examples above testify, that the idea of iron as a harmful force against the supernatural, rather than steel, has been engraved into our conception of mythology. Steel is used in the modern times, and thus automatically is more "real" and as such less suitable in creating such mythologically epic narrative as iron. Whether the iron in the ancient times or the stories really was iron or steel, IS IRRELEVANT (this is very important). The important is that it was PERCEIVED as iron by MODERN writers and the majoritys interpretation on mythology and folklore.


    Why is the above relevant? The narrative, the story, rather than scientific metallurgy explains the usage of cold iron in a fantasy work better. Exalted was not invented let alone written in a vacuum. It is, as is every book and text, influenced by others that came before it and the cultural norms and understanding of its writers and desingers. Being a combination of all kind of features from different mythologies, I find it not strange at all that our take on mythological "iron hurts faeries" trope has found its way to Exalted. In my opinion, it is best to not wonder why, but instead how to incorporate the questions that rise from contradictions into ones own games'. Into the mythological and epic stories that are Exalted.

    In short: the real answer to the question "why" is: Because it has been so in our collective mythology and popular culture for a long time and has formed the majority of our mythological understanding to be such. It is because it is.

    To get this post REALLY on the subject that I think is most important here. While demanding a DEFINITIVE answer might be a bit silly to ask from someone other than the developers, and can also be very restrictive to peoples playstyles, should one demand the existence of one (for it implies that this is the one true way). I find it better to find a BUNCH of answers and then everyone to decide for themselves. Again, the narrative, the story, needs to take precedence over metallurgy and physics.

    Having said that, the question IS an interesting one. WHY iron in the first place? Why would the fair folk, the raksha, be especially hurt by any material AT ALL? We might find something to guide us by from the 2nd edition book Graceful Wicked Masques.While game-mechanically horrible even at the time of 2nd edition, lore-wise the book is of good quality. As the originator of this post generously commented, 2E is perfectly valid source, and some fair folk aspects from 2E have already been suggested to be transferred to this edition in Arms of the Chosen. We must wait and see, but for now, this is with what we must go by:

    The originator of this post has the notion that the raksha are some sort of beings akin to the Exalted gods or a form of demon. Beings who happen to hate creation, but from different reasons as the other enemies of the gods. Let me be absolutely clear so that the nature of raksha (according to the earlier editions of the Exalted ofcourse) does not come into question again.

    The MAIN thing about the fair folk is that they indeed do not understand. (As someone pointed out).

    They do not understand any of it. Raksha do not understand anything that lies within Creation, nor any concept inside Creation. They do not understand the concept of sustenance (even though they require it while in creation), they do not understand the concept of "life" (even though they are by some standards, alive) and even their understanding of "existence" is so alien as to be unfathomable to mortals. They do not understand any of the concepts of Creation.

    Perhaps the raksha understand permanence least of all. Raksha are definately not permanent as there is no such thing as "permanence" in the wyld. Passing of time as a concept is the only thing that raksha can even remotely relate to due to the first Shinma, but they did not have any deeper understanding on history, and thus of growing old. To Raksha, there was only "now" and a passing of infinite "now":s. The matter of being permanent was not an issue because they would not cease to exist in the future since there was no future. Nor did they contemplate their beginning since there was no past. There was only now. Thus they are not by any means "ageless", yet alone consider themselves as such, since there is no age in the Wyld, not fully.

    It all changed with Creation somewhat. In creation there is such concepts which raksha did not previously encounter, and the appearance of Creation changed the Wyld in the process. Wyld still is not permanent and there is no history as we understand it, but the concepts of them appeared. While these concepts were unfathomable to the raksha, they still saw them in action in Creation. Thus the raksha are able to work akin to these concepts even though they understand them not at all.

    For the raksha are excellent at pretending. Everything a raksha does, it pretends. EVERYTHING. The fair folk in Exalted are endlessly malleable beings and should you encounter one, it pretends all its actions, from breathing to eating (if it remembers to have such things), from rage to hapiness and other feelings. Even the fact that it falls towards the ground when dropped from high is pretense. It understands not why these things should happen, yet alone how, but it knows that they are "just how things happen" in creation and thus need to abide by them.

    Most simple to imagine a raksha is that the raksha are beings of infinite potential and possibilities, who in wyld can do anything, and be anything by merely a whim. They are bundles of conciousness in a sea of infinite potential they alone can control. Raksha in Creation are trapped in a game where there are rules. They do not understand the rules or see the point of the rules, but they will calcify if they ignore or do not heed the rules. Thus they play by the rules. They would stop at any time, should they wish to, but then the Creation would impose on them and calcify them ("death" is not a good enough word of what happens to raksha when it "dies" for it is not alive as we see such things, thus the word calcification). This is one reason why they hate Creation so much. It imposes on them rules, and in the Wyld there is no rules. They do not understand how, why, when or where or anything of these rules. They just know the rules are (if they truly grasp the idea what "are", "is", "being" or "existing" even mean).

    When a raksha, for whatever reason, comes to creation, it enters through the Gates of Sundraprishna. This is not a place, for the Wyld has no specific place, and I understand that neither is it a specific method of entering Creation, for the raksha have no grasp of a single way to do anything. Gates of Sundraprishna is a methaphysical choice. The raksha chooses what kind of being it will pretend to be. What kind of skills it pretends to have, what kind of goals it might want, what kind of person it is and what it hopes to achieve. Most importantly, it chooses what kind of story it wants.

    THIS defines the raksha IN Creation. It defines everything the raksha is from that point on until it returns back to the Wyld and its being of potentially anything at a whim. A courtesan raksha has chosen to be a courtesan, and cannot suddenly choose to be a spouse, a mother, father or anything else than a courtesan. Creation-born entities might give it these labels, like in our game where one of our players married one. But it will never BE a wife. It has chosen to be a courtesan and have a courtesans story. As a courtesan it will remain until it changes its role, changes it's entire being in Creation.

    The Gates of Sundraprishna allows the raksha to work in the creation without instant calcification by agreeing to follow the rules. Some rules are more universal than others. Such as no raksha can enter Creation indestructible. Is this a defense mechanism of Creation left by the Primordials, or because nothing in Creation is truly indestructible and as such Creation imposes this rule on everything in it? Your guess is as good as mine. However, as you can see, there are many MORE rules than just iron-vulnerability that applies to ALL the fair folk.

    Nothing else in Creation bears such a vulnerability to iron as the fair folk, however. This is extremely interesting. Because Creation lacks any other such distinctive ruling on a metal being especially harmful, it must be something the raksha choose when entering the Gates of Sundraprishna.


    Thus we come to the question that this post should have been asking all along: What could it be that has gotten and continues to get ALL the raksha to CHOOSE that iron, cold iron, master of them all, is a substance that specifically hurts them when they pass through the Gates of Sundraprishna?

        You appear to have fundamentally misunderstood my point. I was not arguing that Kipling originated the idea that iron was a bane against the supernatural. That would be nonsensical, since the poem doesn't actually mention any sort of harmful effect iron has on supernatural beings. I was pointing out that the idea of "cold iron" as some sort of special iron, as distinct from regular iron or steel, seems to stem from a misreading of this poem.

        My main point, however, is that the folklore about iron, which definitely does date back to long before the 19th century, doesn't make any distinction between iron and steel. I'll reiterate the example of burying a knife to ward off supernatural threats: knives would have been made of steel, at least any decent knife blade would have. And the superstition doesn't say "bury a knife to ward off evil, but only a cheap knife. If it's good, it won't work".

        You've provided lots of examples of iron working against the supernatural. That's great, but that's not the question anyone's really been asking. The question was "why do things named iron work against the Fair Folk, but not steel, when several of the materials that do work share more properties in common with steel than "pure" iron, or are more divergent from "pure" iron than steel itself is".

        I don't believe the idea that steel is somehow perceived as more "modern" is particularly true - myths and legends about steel date back a long, long time (some of the claims made about Wootz steel, for example, are barely centuries younger than the general introduction of iron). Even if I conceded that point, however, I don't accept that, when creating a narrative or setting, have to accept only ideas that are prevalent in modern culture. It's okay to use older narrative structures, if they're interesting or just make more sense with the setting. To use another example, Exalted puts a lot of emphasis on the ideas that the protagonists are "heroes in the Greek style", focusing on their larger-than-life exploits and divorcing the idea of "heroism" from "morality". That idea of heroism is definitely not a particularly modern conceit - most modern fiction pretty firmly attaches the idea of a hero to someone who is a good person. We've created a word, "anti-hero", to describe the opposite, in fact, a protagonist capable of great deeds but who's morally conflicted or just outright bad. Exalted says "No, the Greeks were right, those are just 'heroes'".

        So, Exalted as a game line and a work of fiction is entirely capable of using non-modern ideas and integrating them into the setting and narrative it's trying to build. So, I see no reason why the modern, artificial distinction between "iron" and "steel" should be preserved. While it's certainly possible to come up with some justifications for it, and some people in the thread have definitely done so, I think it's just easier to dump it entirely. Why keep it, if it isn't historical, and just requires convoluted explanations for the difference? What narrative purpose does making the distinction serve? Are there great tales that can be told when iron and steel affect the Raksha differently, that can't be done if they're the same?

                        Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post

                        I'll take a stab at this (though I feel I'm repeating other people):

                        It's not iron that's special, it's steel. Steel inspires people with the possibilities of what metallurgy can accomplish as the useless or limited utility forms of iron are transformed into one of the most potent technologies humanity has ever discovered. But the awe and wonder of what steel can do, means the Raksha can interact with it safely unlike other forms of iron.

                        This is consistent with the setting, because of the dream-narrative nature of fae creatures. While steel isn't to the level of the standard magical materials, it could easily be called the first magical material that humans could access without thaumaturgy, sorcerery or those that naturally work Essence. Like the major magical materials, and the other minor special materials out there, their special standing in human imagination is strong enough that fae creatures aren't harmed directly by it.

                        It makes sense because the idea of steel being 'special' in some fashion beyond chemical superiority is something we see in real world human mythology. Multiple cultures have myths about the steel produced in meteors, or the Greek concept of "shining iron," so on. While steel was in the phase of becoming commonly understood as a thing to make, but still before mass production, it grew to have a special place in the minds of people. Even today people have illogical assumptions about how special Damascus steel and Japanese folded steel are (I mean, they look cool, but they're about turning mediocre steel into good steel, not good steel into awesome steel). In Creation there's no reason to assume there wasn't a similar psychological process. Steel is special, steel is what elevates metal working to a civilization growing tool.

                        If that's not good enough... I'm not sure what any of us can really say.
                        Honestly I think the above idea is the most consistent and satisfying answer that's been articulated in the thread so far. This discussion has gone over and over how it doesn't make any sense, chemically speaking, for steel not to count when cast iron and wrought iron do, so obviously it's not the chemical structure of various forms of iron and steel. Attempts to justify iron as a paragon of Earth essence stumble over why iron has this special property and not other contenders, and explanations to do with adding too much Fire essence during the process of creating steel run afoul of cast iron's inclusion in the list of faerie banes.

                        The only consistent difference is in the way human culture thinks and speaks about iron and steel. Steel is a technological marvel that represents the current peak of metallurgy, and its production is regarded as an arcane process. There's an element of wonder there.

                        So you have iron. It's the bane of the Fair Folk, for whatever reason. I don't think the "why" of the iron bane was ever the main point under discussion, so we'll take it as read. Some innate property of iron makes it baneful to raksha. You melt and cast it, still baneful. Heat and hammer it any way you like, alloy it with whatever, as long as it's still predominantly iron, still baneful. So steel should count, yes? Yes. Except that because of cultural attitudes to steel, for the purposes of the raksha what you're doing when you create steel, with those cutting edge production methods that produce this marvelously useful and valuable material, is alloying iron not just with carbon but with human wonder. And that shit is delicious. That's what deprives steel of its banefulness.

                        What I wonder is, in that case, would steel become a bane again when it becomes easier to produce and more widespread, and thus less wondrous? Or does its history as a marvelous metal of arcane craftsmanship mean its bane status is lost forever?

                            I'd say steel left a mark that industrialization didn't kill. That's why I referenced the modern myths around things like Damascus and Japanese folded steels. Mass production simple shifted the "specialness" of steel to techniques that are more commonly done by individual smiths (generally just mistaking the time and energy of someone working something as an art) as superior to the techniques used to facilitate effective mass production.

                            I think the bigger question would be over time does iron vs. steel move on to "artisan" vs. mass production. We see, for example, cast iron getting a huge cultural boost these days as people relearn that it makes great cookware that lasts far longer with far less issues than chemically coated non-stick steel. The shift is more towards "old ways were better" thinking than, "steel isn't special."

                                                Honestly, the OP says it's nonsense, but to me the whole "iron is much more associated with building and cold materialism while steel is much more associated with killing people" thing that someone mentioned at the beginning rings true enough to me to use it. It might be a cultural thing, but I know that in my language, if you have a poem talking of "cold iron", it's most likely going for associations of impersonalness, duration, unfeeling - iron doesn't give a shit. But if a poem employs the words "cold steel" at all, someone is getting murdered before the stanza is over, end of. "Steel" is often used as practically a kenning for "weapon" in more florid poetic language. So for me at least, playing in my language, it works, and I'm stealing the shit out of it.
