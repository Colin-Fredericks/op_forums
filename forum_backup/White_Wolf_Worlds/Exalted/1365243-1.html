        Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
        Hadestown seemed to manage.
        I must admit that I don't quite understand the reference. Quick googling says that Hadestown was a musical based on the myth of Orpheus. I don't whether there is something particular about the musical, but I assume that you refer to the drama that can be present in the myth of Orpheus even though everyone know how it ends.

        I think this is indeed a deep question about how deeply specific role-playing games are as a medium. I could imagine a drama of a character trying to bring someone from dead fueled by dramatic irony: we know that this is futile and yet we try. Arguably, this is the drama you experience retelling the story of Orpheus.

        However, I think that the fundamental feature of rpg is that you retell the stories from the perspective of involved characters. Properly retelling a story of Orpheus in this particular medium would require you, to actually retell the hope that your journey to Hades can be successful, and then failing. I really think that in roleplaying game such an adaptation would be more true to the original story than bleak certainty that Eurydike will stay in the Underworld.


        Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
        People always bring up reincarnation as though there is a single real world religious system that acts as though it contains an assumption of personality continuity.
        Well, ok. First we would have to make it clear how relevant to our discussion is continuity of common-sense personality as opposed to selfhood or personhood. I could imagine a nontrivial resurrection which changes personality. Indeed, it is the case in many stories depicting resurrection as an act of hubris.

        Second, at least some Greek philosophers (was it Plato?) made quasi-empirical case for reincarnation based on the anecdotes of people who apparently had memories that clearly belonged to recently deceased (everyday memories, not specifically Platonic anamnesis).

        Third, what is relevant here is how reincarnation functions in the setting of Exalted and it is explicitly allowed to alter reincarnation so that people retain their memories. I know, you mentioned personality, but the case should be made that this is impossible in Exalted. (If this impossible precisely because that would be resurrection then we go back to the question why this is more disruptive to the setting than retaining your memories.)

                I have always assumed that when the Primordials created Creation, they were limited by certain preconditions that made certain ways of creating Creation impossible, and certain ways possible; certain ways resource-intensive and certain ways resource-cheap; certain ways labor-intensive and certain ways easy.

                In much the same way that I, personally, here in the real world, could build a house any number of ways but could not build one that hovers in the air unsupported by anything, the Primordials could have made Creation any number of ways but could not just make it any way they pleased with no limits to how they did it or what the final outcome was. And Creation and everything in it works the way it does because of the set of choices they made before the dawn of time, between this way things could have been and another way things could have been that they decided against because, given the bounded number of choices available to them, this was the one they thought suited their purposes and resources best.

                So, in that respect, death probably works the way it does and not some other way not because "death as it does work" just happens to be the concept as they envisioned it, but rather because in order for it to work a different way they would have had to make tradeoffs they didn't want to make.

                (I'm not at all interested in exploring what those initial boundary conditions were, though. Something something mumble shinma.)

                You might as well ask "Why quarks?" or "Why the higgs-boson?" There's probably a reason and it's probably even interesting but it's probably not going to be existentially fulfilling, and will probably boil down to "Because math has to work this way for things to be self-consistent."

                    Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                    When I think about things like this, I find that they all go even simpler than Theseus' ship, depending on the answer to a single question:

                    Is this a setting that integrates an apparent concept of a soul?

                    If not, then no, it's not resurrection. Nothing you do well change the fact that the actual person in question experienced a cessation of consciousness from which there is no return.

                    Still, a key subject in several narratives about the subject is who the resurrection is being done for the benefit of, the person whose life was lost or those they leave behind. There might well be people who are quite satisfied with this identical copy as a means of getting the list person back in their lives, but you've but really done the person that died a favour.

                    ...Wait...

                    {Levi, in a cosmology where there's no existence after death, do you not think that an instance of reviving life in the original brain and body of the lost person constitutes a return of life? If so, how is that functionally different from replicating those patterns otherwise? Doesn't a purely materialist world view become one in which the only meaningful definition of life is the instance of the person that is present and active?}

                    Okay fine, I'll grant you that, but that still raises a question about the value and attachment that we place to the lives of others. It's hard not to feel as though that focus on a person as a material component has implications for how we conceive of and interact with people in general, alive or dead. There's something very transactional about it to me.
                    You assume that, in the absence of the soul or an afterlife, a perfect copy isn't a resurrection because the person in question "experienced a cessation of consciousness from which there is no return," but you have to admit that from the subjective point of view of the copy, there was no cessation -- the original isn't around, and from the copy's point of view they are the original because it's all just memories. So, followup question: If the cessation of consciousness that we call sleep, or even the cessation of consciousness that we call losing our train of thought, were equivalent to death, and we're all just copies of ourselves walking around inside our own heads already...

                    ...would we have any way of knowing?

                    And what would that mean for resurrection via perfect copies?

                    (The concept of the self is socially constructed; because we are educated on our worldview and perspective by our peers and the social norms they teach us. Destructive teleporters would catch on within less than a lifetime because some people would use them, and then everyone else would be powerfully incentivized to see the teleporter-copies/copy-resurrections as the originals, and then peer pressure would take take care of everything else and soon enough the socially constructed version of identity we all use would include the-copy-is-the-original. Except for a few holdouts, who’d quickly become disenfranchised from the global economy.)
