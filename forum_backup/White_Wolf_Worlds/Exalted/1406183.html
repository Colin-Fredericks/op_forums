    Trying to understand fay

    08-03-2020, 07:56 PM
    I wanted to use a fay in a scenario, probably the manipulative kind.

    I managed to get my hands on some fluff, but don't quite get the feel of how they should work mechanically (the 2E stuff revolves around character traits that no longer exist, and 3E only gave us a couple of bestiary entries.)

    From what I see they look more or less like this:

    Creatues from the primal chaos, stuck into more or less stable forms, in a love-hate relationship with reality.
    Very high stats that are very hard to further improve.
    Don't lie. (why? do they need to remain true to themselves to maintain a stable 'self'?)
    Feed on personality traits by making their victims turn back on them (seducing the pious, scaring the brave, etc.) gradually lobotomising the victim in the process. (how would that work in 3E? would they erode principles?)
    Super rich, capable of spinning dreams into gold if they need to.
    Obsessed with narrative devices, as they are the most intuitive thing for them in reality. Try to pusposefully become Mary Sue.

    - What happens if they are killed in Creation? Does it just kill them, or do they need to be finished off in some special way?
    - Where are they power level-wise compared to exalted?
    - Beyond the abilities described in the bestiary examples, are there any other iconic things they should be able to do?

      If killed in Creation, that particular Raksha is dead. A different fey might take up their story though, killing who they were to become the dead fey. They could possess healing abilities that make them hard to kill without iron, but as of 3E they probably don't.

      Fey nobles (Loralie, Cataphract) are a little bit above a starting Dragon-Blooded but below a starting Solar.

      I think you've described the iconic things fey do in your post. They're formless monsters spun into stories by the power of reality. They have whatever powers the story needs them to have. There's fey in Adversaries of the Righteous if you wanted to expand your reading.

          Honestly, I don't think it really needs to target something specific on the character sheet. For the moment I think it's enough to just run with a story that extended proximity to a raksha and acting in the manner most responsive to them weakens one's soul in a manner that affects personality or gradually reduces motive and motion, while the raksha is sustained in Creation by it.

          Originally posted by Braininthejar
          Obsessed with narrative devices, as they are the most intuitive thing for them in reality. Try to pusposefully become Mary Sue.


          I think the narrative device thing is played out, and that it's better to look at a raksha as being focused on a mythic or mystical vision of themselves or of the world that all efforts are focused on bringing to life. The "become Mary Sue" thing in particular ought not to be pursued, because it's a bit of a caricature and probably cuts off a lot of interesting raksha ideas. Like, imagine a creepy self-flagellating mendicant monk who goes around with a flail of iron, obsessed with being punished for his sins real and imaginary while purging them in a manner that causes them to rise personified from the ground where the blood from his back is sprinkled. I would find that in keeping with our example nobles, and I wouldn't call that trying to become a Mary Sue.

That said, as Isator expands below, fey nobility still exists in some form as evidenced in the Raksha Corebook write-ups. But my point, as Chausse rightly deduced, is that in straight interaction with the PCs fey nobels aren't meaningfully more challenging. Just as Essence 6+ Exalts develop personalised, unique powers rather than becoming more powerful as a flat upgrade, so too does it seem that Raksha nobles are characterised by their unique quirks rather than "more dice" -- and thus remain at the level of "above Dragon-Blooded but below Solars".

                  By contrast, the fey created by Marching Fever Dream Nightmare, Hobgoblins and Silverwrights seem meaningfully weaker and more limited. Much more like the old commoners. But YMMV.

                          In terms of power level it's actually quite useful for them to be absolutely all over the place, and mostly as powerful as their narrative role demands them to be. Exploring that range is pretty useful to make them feel more integral to the setting (especially since bordermarches can appear just about anywhere). This is how I like to stratify them:


                          From the tiniest trickster spirits that pull pranks and entice children to dangerous places and can be scared off by a stick to the dangerous predators that sniff nightmares and enter unprotected houses at night you have your goblins and hobgoblins.
                          These tend to be weak, and most mortal warriors should at least have a good shot at tackling them (a small militia can take on a few hobgoblins or a couple silverwights, but it's deadly).

                          From the gallant mermaid luring fishermen to love-devour them to the Road Warrior that tags along caravans to protect them from bandits and wilderbeasts free of charge (and the beasts likely were put there by his partner in crime) are your raksha proper and the meaner beasts you'd start calling "monsters". A starting combative exalt should make quick work of them, but underestimating them will hurt.
                          These should be the most you'll find in any given trek near the marches.

                          Raksha nobles often have their own entourages of lesser fae, and have earned many a wyld hunt themselves. Nobles have a gravitas to them that kinda takes over the story. Either you are trekking deep to find them or they are on a rampage, and can offer wonders of glamour that might as well be called miracles. They are the warlock in the woods whose songs cure Flamerot; the rampaging warlord wiping out multiple villages before disappearing into the ocean; the keeper of a fortress that can only be safely visited by the pure of heart; the queen so beautiful you have to give up your eyes as tribute as soon as your gaze lands on her form. These guys go as high up as a second circle demon in power level, though usually it's less than that.

                              My overall impression from 3e corebook is that the idea of Nobles and Commoners - and more importantly, one kind of being eventually evolving into the other - are gone.

                              Raksha now resume themselves to everything that we would put under the "fae noble" in previous editions, the Cataphract being a hint of that on itself.

                              The idea of "commoners" has been replaced with masses of varied inhuman beings, created or native to the Wyld, taken as bedazzled servants, disgruntled slaves and more, that the Creationborn, specially mortals might classify out of gauche ignorance as "fae" along with their masters, but are in no way Raksha.

                              Goblins, silverwights and more compose the ranks of such chimeric beasts - and might form bands and maybe even whole societies of their own with particular agendas and interests independent any Raksha or other groups of oppressors. That said, bands of some of those beings may do the opposite and flock of their own volition to some sufficiently charismatic, puisant or strong-willed being that attracts their attention.

                                      My basic idea for the adventure was like this: the ruler of a desert city is throwing a party to celebrate his son's coming of age. A young girl from the city dreams of going to the party but is not nearly on the level of wealth that would allow it - and a rahska decides to 'help' her, but of course it can't just be a party.                                       Yeah, that idea, of a raksha whose obsession concerns spinning these grand romances that cause social upheaval, I would find that perfectly fitting. Matches the commoner with the prince, star-struck teenagers from feuding houses, a third iconic doomed romance that I thought of when contemplating this yesterday and that I should have written down... probably wasn't Helen of Troy.

                                      Are they doing it to be helpful, truly heedless of the inevitable disasters that ensue when realpolitik is injected? Hard to say; the raksha are made of lies.

                                      I think it's perfectly suitable, necessary even, to draw on inspiration from broad, archetypal stories to find what any given raksha is about; it just goes a step too far to have them consciously, deliberately acting in emulation of stories. I like the idea that there's ultimately a measure of artifice and play-acting to what they do, particularly in relation to one another, but it takes me out of it when an intro fiction has them stand up and describe that artifice outright.

                                      As for your character, I would have reservations about outright mind control being involved, that the people playing their parts are purely puppets, if only because I think it takes a lot of the fun out of it. I think the powers of the raksha should be concerned with creating incentives, contriving scenarios, laying down glamour and enchantment, all to guide people towards assuming the roles and working through their conclusions of their own accord. It is the nature of such things that the more people immerse themselves in the fantasy, the more they'll lose of themselves, and eventually much of their wills shall be in the holding of the raksha, but it happens after they've ceded much of themselves up to the dream that is more compelling than reality.

                                      Originally posted by Braininthejar
                                      Now I need to tweak this idea a bit - first because there are a couple dragon-blooded likely to attend and I need to plan how their possible intervention could affect the plot
                                      I expect that their intervention can present a decent risk to the raksha's designs, with their competing powers to sway the hearts and minds of others and to disrupt faerie magic with the stabilising influence of Earth, not to mention their person. One Fair Folk might look upon such people as unwanted competition or an obstacle to their godchild's happiness or other such thing and wish to try removing them before the major proceedings even start. But the other will see the presence of the Exalted as a grand prize worth risking much for, and try to work towards their lending their power and status to aid the rise of the godchild. Try to present the Cindarella as a person of surpassing purity of character, such that these Dragon-Blooded pledge their fealty and swear to see such a fine heart placed at the side of the throne, for the good of the country.
