            Originally posted by Flare View Post

            The way I see it, there is a difference between Heartbreak and what Rakan Thulio is doing.

            When I think Heartbreak, I think two people who love each other but can't be together because they're like Oil and Water, or who were in love once but have fallen apart, someone who loves someone but, through either their sin or their partners, have broken their trust and their relationship. Things that represent a long period of investment only to be turned down or put away, and how they react to that. That's sad. It's sad when two people cared for each other and don't anymore, or someone cares for someone but they can't be together.
            Well, the only thing we have really is: "But when the love of his life chose another, he came to believe that Heaven had done him a great injustice." I would interpret that these two men were lovers, and then his lover "chose another". But as you say, open season on interpretation.

            Though will say, heartbreak is not just because of people who are in love, but find the relationship doesn't really work. Sometimes one of you falls *out* of love, or falls for someone else harder, while the other remains attached. (Like this sounds patronising as I think you know this right? But the guy/girl, I'm quoting doesn't seem to, from, what I'm quoting).

            Originally posted by Flare View Post
            I don't think 'I liked this guy then it turns out he had a boyfriend, and that's a betrayal to ME.'. That's not sympathetic. Getting turned down for a date and responding by writing screeds about how Chad took your Stacey and you're gonna kill all the Chads is not sympathetic, because it's a difference between putting forth effort and trying and thinking that you deserve it. Rakan Thulio doesn't come across as someone who broke up with a guy he liked because they couldn't be together, or someone who fell apart because of intense disagreements
            I don't think it's really a case of anything that can be analogous to that, or conversely, a "That handsome, successful guy doesn't seem interested in me... it's because society is wrong about what makes women attractive, the ideals it presents, and has such high expectations for women! Bad and toxic social messages must be to blame and I must war against society through activism to create change!" type. If you look at the text, I don't think the analogy really works, and requires assumptions to make it work that I don't think are warranted... or if they were originally what John intended, probably would be a good idea to shift to one of two lovers whose relationship has broken down, yes.

            This is getting kind of a bit peripheral to the argument as well, but as depicted in the art, Thulio is a tall, muscular, handsome, tanned, dark haired man who basically wouldn't look out of place in Jen's art. Add to that what we know from his character, what he'd have to be like to do what he'd has done and he's a charismatic, decisive, powerful, worldly and experienced leader. So I think, while he may voluntarily abstain from romantic relationships, he's probably been able to "get" a lot from other similarly handsome men, over the years and if anything he's a "Chad" (accepting for a millisecond only these foolish, distorting terms with very little value). And that confirms to me that really we should definitely be thinking more of a case that he's lost someone who he loved and not anything to do with general sexual frustration or an inability to form a relationship with anyone at all.

            And of course we may decide that it's sympathetic or unsympathetic, and even if it's not sympathetic, whether Thulio's wider ideology that grew out of this and really drives his actions is still sympathetic. But I do strongly feel we should begin from the point of thinking about love and heartbreak and how those are viewed within our culture (and applying the same standards to men and women and that both genders have the same rights to express and feel!), and not the standpoint of trying to make these analogies with this current vocal subset of young men who are sexually frustrated, can't find anyone to have any sort of romantic relationship with and have adopted some strange and alienated ideas about society because of it (mostly because of almost nothing to with how our culture has for time immemorial viewed heartbreak).

            ....

            Anyway, actually getting back to thread topic, also wanted to chime in that reading the 3e text again did help me on some Getimian related matters: "Rakan Thulio has been at war with Yu-Shan for centuries. He is far from alone. A handful of Sidereals have bolstered his ranks, each with their own reasons to betray Heaven. Collectively, the conspirators bring to bear intimate knowledge of the divine bureaucracies. They’ve turned the political landscape of Yu-Shan into a battleground, and the city itself into the site of a shadow war between those loyal to the Celestial Bureaucracy and those who wish to see it torn down. Dissident and outcast gods flock to his banner; in exchange for their service, Thulio and his allies promise them new roles in the order to come."

            So that resolves part of my original questions about Getimians from my first post in this thread, in that tells me that the Getimians are going to be mixed game OK with Sidereals (with a particular flavour that the Sidereal players get to be double agents) and that part of play is going to be winning dissident gods over on side as allies. Should have read it first!

                I think that you should watch more romance movies, because the idea of 'person falls out of love with someone to be with someone else', has been presented from both the side of the person whose lover abandons him and the side of the person who is abandoning their lover, and I feel like right now we're at least in a phase where the second one is more generally given as the viewpoint character. I haven't done any scientific rigor, but I can think of a lot of movies off the top of my head where the climax of the film involves the protagonist or the object of affection leaving someone they've been in a committed relationship with to go be with the person they truly love, and that the person they are already in a relationship with is portrayed generally as an obstacle, though generally not an out and out villain; this does usually have a lot of dramatic soul searching on the part of the person who leaves, because they are the protagonist, but it is not like such a thing is uncommon or never happens, and you can't go 'well society still loves heartbreak and respects it' but ignore that, in a lot of ways, this seems to instead follow the plot of that kind of movie, instead of a more flat 'two people meet each other and slowly learn to be together'.
