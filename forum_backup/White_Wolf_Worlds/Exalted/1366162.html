    Was Creation post-apocalyptic?

    02-07-2020, 08:02 PM
    I know that recently there was a thread about how postapocalyptic Creation is. I was hesitating whether to post it there. I decided not to, but I'd be absolutely fine with merging the theads.

    Encouraged by the general opinion on this board, I am just reading Exalted 1st ed corebook and I have to admit that it is indeed really solid. It has very distinctive mystery vibe straight from old Wolrd of Darkness which I find very appealing. However that's not what drew my attention the most.

    It's not hard to notice that in 1st edition together with anime tropes (enhanced in the 2nd edition) and bronze age tropes (enhanced, in my opinion, in the 3rd edition), there is a very strong post-apocalyptic feel reminiscent of Dying Earth or The Book of New Sun. Let me two quote two passages :

    [M]ost cities are fed by land within a weeks’ travel of their walls. The land between these oases of safety is sparsely inhabited. All settlements are built with an eye to defense, either walled or with a defensible central structure the community can retreat to if attacked. [...]

    Between these great cities and walled settlements lie great swaths of untamed wilderness, crossed by the trade routes of the Guild. Only armies and the massive convoys of the Guild can cross these areas with relative safety. Others who travel must rely on their wits or steel to protect them from the countless perils of the wilderness, from elementals and the fey to simple human robbers.
    Most of the Scavenger Lands consist of walled towns and cities and the farm lands immediately around them, surrounded by sparsely populated wilderness, connected by trade routes and a common heritage of immigration and restoration of the ruins that surround them.
    The book consistently makes the impression that the apparent scarcity of dots on the map of Creation is not a negligence. Creation, so it seems, is huge, but really mostly empty. Or rather, filled with ruins or badlands full of inhuman weird danger. It's a full-fledged post-apo setting.

    I have the impression that 2nd edition was making this picture less and less consistent: while the Creation was still relatively empty, it was trying to fit in more and more cool stuff and, as a result, the setting felt generally weirder. At the same time 3rd edition tries actively to repopulate the world and make it full of interesting cultures and struggles. Generally, it also tries to make Creation more "realistic" and to steer away from the extremes of previous edition. I understand why it's desirable and yet, I feel really compelled with the bleak, desert-like vistas of 1st edition, where "The Age of Sorrows" was not a joke (actually, I love pure gonzo of Ink Monkeys, but these two are indeed very different settings).

    Inspired by that vision of the Creation, I started thinking of a (Dragon Blooded?) campaign set either in this alternative 1st ed Creation or, say, 10-15 years after the Great Contagion, when the Creation would still be genuinely postapocalyptic and still retain that feel (in order to avoid the clash of expectations with players). Has anyone tried something like this in this or previous editions? Either playing in such a "shard" or setting the game right after the Great Contagion? Do you feel that Creation used to be post-apo? And finally, do you agree with this depiction of 1st edition?

      Yeah, absolutely, I don't think you're totally wrong. Exalted 1e was always doing a balancing act between sources which were:

      A) "Post-apocalyptic*" or "frontier" / exploration oriented - Dying Earth, New Sun, Zohthique, Hawkmoon, and lots of post-apoc-ish/frontier anime about at the time (Vampire Hunter D, Nausicaa, Trigun, Fist of the North Star, to some extent Xenogears or FFVI's World of Ruin, etcetc).

      (And of course, much of early-mid 1e was fairly oriented towards lots of exploration in fairly deserted ruins overgrown by jungle and the like. The original tagline of the game, which probably few people on these boards remember, was "Before there was a World of Darkness, there was a world of Savage Adventure", right?)

      The sort of thing where you have art exploring the Sengoku Jidai or post-Roman Empire period or the Bronze Age collapse as a sort of tonal apocalypse are also part of this.

      and

      B) Sources which are interested in talking about fantasy in urbane, cosmopolitan, established locations which are not the simple kind of monster huntering, far removed from civilization fantasy gaming of much of stereotypical D&D.

      This is the kind of where you get the incredibly rich (relative to most settings!), almost LeGuinian ambition towards cultural anthropology and diversity, or Exalted's explorations of Dynastic politics and intrigue which evoke Herbert's Dune and historical fiction like Robert Harris's Cicero Trilogy, or its attempts at weaving economic verisimilitude into world and narrative which evoke fantasy versions of "hard edged" steam/cyber/etc.punk explorations a la Gibson (I mention Gibson as recently reading him, but more in genre, Black Company is perhaps kind of a fantasy version of what this is gesturing at? Economically grounded genre about skilled, mercenary characters for hire who inevitably get caught up in events where they are required to be more than simple mercenaries.).

      There's no inherent complete conflict between these flavours (and each is arguably a bit of a set of disparate strands I've pulled together as a construct for my argument) but they are different currents which can be emphasized or de-emphasized and it does seem like a bit of a balancing act to me.

      Over time, I do think that there was probably a bit of a leaning in to (perhaps consciously or unconsciously) the sense that Exalted's selling point was having more emphasis on being a world full of politics and people.

      I do think that the 3e devs and core writers were and are certainly quite aware of this element of tonal shift and have tried to reintroduce some element of this simply by expanding the map so radically. But yeah, I think at the same time, recreating that same tone is really quite difficult, or takes a bit more. I love the feel of the world as presented in 1e, though I'm not saying that the perhaps "busier" feel of the world as 3e seems to depict it does not have its own charms.

      *in the sense of "a major regression of a civilization, probably more 'advanced' than ours today", not necessarily totally coterminous with a sudden death apocalypse, or total collapse apocalypse.

                      I think it's common wisdom that Exalted is Post-Post-Apocalyptic. (Like the Legend of Zelda games.)

                      The Divine Rebellion, The Usurpation, then the Great Contagion are all apocalyptic events which irrevocably shaped Creation, but the most recent of which happened over 750 years ago.

                      Creation hasn't so much "healed" as it has "scarred over".

                      So it is filled with badlands and lost ruins and weird inhuman danger. But the space between the map dots isn't empty.

                      There are minor settlements in thrall (culturally or literally) to the map dots. There is space for minor kingdoms, and cities comparable to the map dots, of your own devising. Those "badlands" might have a village like any other... or the locals might lick their lips whenever strangers roll into town. There are wild animals, and perfectly human dangers. A ghost town is just as likely to be the result of a mine drying up and the people moving on as it is to be a literal "ghost" town formed from dark magics.

                      In 2E, even though city-states thousands of miles apart apparently had thriving trade relationships, this trade was facilitated by "armies and the massive convoys of the Guild", and that's not overly dissimiliar to the Silk Road IRL.

                      And looking at Bronze Age Real Life... we're not exactly talking massive population densities.

                      ****

                      That said, if you wanted to run a game within a decade or so of the Empress turning back the Fair Folk, I think that'd be awesome.

                                  Mind, even empty space is something that can have different connotations to what some of us might expect.

                                  I know so little about the history of Africa, and yet what I have read seems to suggest some recurring patterns; rising and breaking apart empires, coastal mercantile cities, and most significant of all ethnic groups whose oral traditions maintain that they migrated to their most recently known long term territory a long time ago in response to some rival group (often backed up by decent archaeological evidence).

                                  I look at the interplay of these elements and the landscape of the continent and it creates a picture of people who are often on the margins of expanding empires who then just... pick up en masse and relocate elsewhere, because there's the space to do so.

                                  One finds a similar thing in history across Asia, although with somewhat more difficult environmental obstacles. Still, a significant portion of the history of Europe is the history of it being a verdant jewel lying at the destination of westward migrations in response to several factors (although the policies of Chinese dynasties features prominently).

                                  The thing that I'm getting at with all of this is that "empty space" can mean that a lot of the world can be open and inviting to large groups of people. Perhaps especially in this Time of Tumult. Unclaimed territory can provide one with freedom to select an area and start something entirely of their own there, or at least generate plot complications from running into other people in the process of doing so.

                                      Beyond being post-apoc, one of the things that I find about Exalted's* version of post-apoc is that it is relatively... hopeful?

                                      A lot of post-apoc settings tend to emphasize the misery of people who are squatting in the aftermath of civilization collapse, the brutal authoritarianism and cruelties of political order (or disorders!) that crop up in the aftermath, and the fallen ruin of the world from its once greatness, ever diminished and diminishing until the fading sun blots out entirely. Creation has a fair bit of this, it's true.

                                      But I think there's also a lot of emphasis on: Exploring a world which is still pristine, vibrant and beautiful. The self-reliance, and the pioneering and positive mindset of the survivors rebuilding the world, and of vitality of the resilient cultures they've built**. (There's kind of clue is in the name: Scavenger Sons, emphasizing the heroism and agency of Second Age humanity). The 'Spirit of Adventure' that has been reborn in a world where there are still re-discoveries to be made, people to reconnect, new cultures that have not known each other and which can join in friendship.

                                      Kind of something I would tentatively call, "Blue Sky Post-Apocalyptic". I feel that's something somewhat common in some of the particular JRPG and anime movie influences of the early Ex1e time** that use "Lost Fallen Age of Glory" aesthetics and setting elements, and perhaps is a bit less common in a lot of the pulp and S&S (where perhaps the spirit of adventure tends to be tied more to early 20th century, a la Indiana Jones and similar two-fisted adventurers).

                                      (I guess also feels in common with the Age of Classical Greece when presented as a sort of sun-drenched age of new philosophy and ideas and Greek colonization following the Greek Dark Ages. Or maybe more tenuously the slightly dramatized end of "The Long Pause" that I saw in Moana when I caught on TV this Christmas, where this historical event was dramatized as a rebirth of the human spirit of adventure through the heroism of its culture heroes, Maui and Moana).

                                      It's a vibe that tends to be supportive of the idea that Solars who wander Creation and "adventure" - helping people across the world connect and fight off local dangers for just long enough to build something better for themselves - are actually doing something meaningfully heroic for the world and in the Unconquered Sun's vision. That wandering characters are not merely their shirking a proper duty - to seize power and leadership in a base and then try to reform political authority and fight unjust government.

                                      (Side note: There were a couple of other game settings that Exalted bore comparison to when it first came out; Earthdawn and Dawnforge, both of which had a similarly strong emphasis on the er... bright "dawn" of a new world, recovering after an apocalypse / after its first creation).

                                      Part of this is probably a bit of a niche differentiation when Exalted 1e came out - I think Geoff Grabowski clearly loved the idea of 'dying earth epic tragedy' that combines epic tragedy with a Dying Earth settings (so that's there!) and clearly they want to write in a way that an adult audience can respect and which touches on ideas they care about. But did they want to do another gameline *primarily* in tones of shadow and sepia, after the World of Darkness? Probably not so much.

                                      Again I kind of feel like 3e's clever writers are probably not unaware of this at all, and we'll see this coming through in the emphasis in the 3e version of the Scavenger Sons book, whose name I can't remember right now. (I feel like at some point this was literally called New Horizons or something like that, which would pretty damn on the nose, but this is possibly my delusion as I can't find anything like this online).

                                      *I originally wrote "1e's version of..." but I don't want to deepen and exaggeration any perception over what's in the books.

                                      **Without getting into negative "Supporting the 'weakest' in society is bad and we should not do it!"... unless you're a certain type of 1e Lunar, who is probably a villain or of ambiguous sympathy anyway.
