                        Originally posted by Ghosthead View Post
                        I'm going to have to check the text out, but Lunars as warriors for freedom and individuality (with its unfortunate overtones of Hollywood style William Wallace) seems at odds with the discussion that Lunars vs the Realm is pretty much all solely about revenge, removing a personal threat and an ideology free beef with the Dragonblooded in particular? (Actually the whole thing I thought 3e was trying to do with the Lunars was remove any ideology that the players would find questionable or doubtful in particular.)
                        I didn't say that Lunars are warriors for freedom and individuality. I said that they're about freedom. As a part of the setting, Lunars are the group best suited to games that explore themes of freedom--what freedom means, who deserves it, what it's like to lose it, and what you do with it when you have it. Many Lunars lean into these themes and become warriors for freedom and individuality, but the Raksi/Ma-Ha-Suchi schism showcases how that's not a simple thing to define. They both want a world without the Realm's oppression, but they disagree on what role Lunars should play in the aftermath. And many other Lunars choose to fight to maintain only their personal freedoms, or run away from responsibility, or simply enjoy life to its fullest.

                        Revenge Against The Machine is a terrible cover band name. Revenge may motivate many elders, and may motivate a significant number of younger Lunars, but the keyword is rage. Lunars in the Age of Sorrows are divine tricksters who break what would cage them, and change themselves to frustrate and evade what they can't break. Many, but not all, are enraged on behalf of others as well. Lunars upset the established order because they're good at it and it needs doing, and when that's done, they'll figure out what happens next. (Also, "revenge" is not ideology-free beef.)

                        No idea who told you that "Lunars without any ideology that players might find questionable or doubtful" was any kind of goal the game might have. The Silver Pact is a society, and societies by nature have ideologies. More importantly, games require structures and goals to either play toward or against. Lunars have more in-setting freedom to play against type than, say, Dynasts, but if we go too far in in the interest of giving players more freedom, we'll run into What Do Lunars Even Do, mk. 3.

                                                Originally posted by Dex Davican View Post
                                                Also, "revenge" is not ideology-free beef.)
                                                Honestly, I think you'd be pushing at the limits of the term to describe "revenge" as ideology. There's perhaps a sense in which "I must find the man who killed my father... Prepare to die!" or "Win back the lands unjustly robbed from us by unfair treaties" or "I should be the caliph instead of the caliph!" are ideology.

                                                But clearly in a very different sense from "Civilization is a complex machine, doomed to failure states", "Hierarchy is immoral", "Property is theft" and the like.

                                                Originally posted by Dex Davican View Post
                                                No idea who told you that "Lunars without any ideology that players might find questionable or doubtful" was any kind of goal the game might have.
                                                Not something I've been told, as such, but certainly an inference from the early 3e devs talking about the problems of ideological barbarism, which 1e and 2e presented in different ways - 1e from a sort of perspective of anarchist resistance to the hierarchies and division of labour in civilization, 2e from a perspective of the Thousand Streams River as a sort of champion of encouraging and sowing cultural diversity as strengthening humanity. Anarchist barbarians, cultural diversity barbarians, anti-imperialism barbarians.

                                                3e's intention, as I understood it, was to cut through the idea that the Silver Pact was in a sense an ideologically motivated vehicle opposed to civilization. It doesn't seem like a good idea, then, to in any way recreate a conflict around the Silver Pact having an ideological conflict with anything the Realm represents as the force of imperial civilization (that seems like going back to anarchist barbarians again).

                                                This frankly this is all kind of tangential though, as after rereading and these comments, I understand better now that you were talking not about a conscious ideology inclined to prize freedom among the Lunar Exalted, as I'd thought, but more that they and the Dragonblooded Realm represent opposing themes which should be brought into clash. That said, I do disagree that this is actually a good lens to bring to stories involving both and conflicts between them though, or useful themes to hang on these splats and factions.

                                                    Originally posted by Epimetheus View Post
                                                    But that wasn't the case before. The Realm's boundaries used to exist due to the fact that the realm has limits. Maintaining an empire as big as it is takes effort and spreading it out even further would have just caused a collapse.
                                                    Yes, it changed. Because "we've been hiding for over a millennium, should we maybe do something?" and "I want to build a better society, but the expansionist military regime keeps declaring war on me and/or assassinating my friends for doing so" are way worse starting points for a game. As we've seen, following two editions of complaints about Lunars.

                                                    And because when we look at what "the Realm has limits" really means, I think we can recognize that when part of an expansionist military regime "collapses" due to "spreading out" too far, that generally looks like a bloody rebellion, a bloody conquest from an outside power, or a failed conquest of a would-be satrapy (or, sometimes, brutal pogroms that leave nothing left to govern). Though the Realm has significant corruption problems, much of what outsiders would view as corruption is the system working as intended from a Dynastic perspective, because the outside world is rightfully theirs for the despoiling. An expansionist military regime recognizes its limits in terms of what it can't yet conquer, and recognizes problems like corruption, poor communication, and resource shortages as impediments to total military/spiritual domination.

                                                    "An empire ruled by ten thousand demigods can't conquer a world mostly full of mortals and maintain cohesion" is a thought-provoking and harrowing statement about the limitations of centralized control and the challenges of bureaucracy, but it left the Lunars doing nothing in particular on the world stage for fifteen hundred years, and it frankly makes the Realm seem like a paper tiger waiting for the Celestial Exalted to come knock it over. I think "An empire ruled by ten thousand demigods can't conquer a world full of mortals when undercut by the persistent sabotage of reincarnating culture-hero demigods" makes for a more interesting game for players of Dragon-Blooded and Lunars both.

                                                    This frankly this is all kind of tangential though, as after rereading and these comments, I understand better now that you were talking not about a conscious ideology inclined to prize freedom among the Lunar Exalted, as I'd thought, but more that they and the Dragonblooded Realm represent opposing themes which should be brought into clash.
                                                    There's definite overlap between what I think a Lunar game is about and what Lunar society on average believes in. Lunars are suited to being freedom-fighters, they're all given personal attention and reinforcement of their identity by their iconoclastic patron, their first experiences with peers will likely encourage them to interact with the Silver Pact, and they all have reason to fear the loss of their own freedoms (whether freedom to worship, maintain their way of life, or simply survive) at the hands of the Realm, so it makes sense to gravitate toward that life. But none of them are required to live that life, and can play against type with relatively few consequences.

                                                    That said, I do disagree that this is actually a good lens to bring to stories involving both and conflicts between them though, or useful themes to hang on these splats and factions.
