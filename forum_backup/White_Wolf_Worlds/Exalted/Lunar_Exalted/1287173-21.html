            I just want to say from the outset lest it get overlooked: I know that I'm not disagreeing with you specifically. The following is concerned with why I found no sense of contradiction or unfulfilled expectations in the write-ups.

            Originally posted by The Wizard of Oz View Post

            Ideology.
            Raksi's intimacies don't include any devotion to the ideology she apparently fell out with Ma-Ha-Suchi over. No intimacies towards "a Lunar Realm is impossible," or "every Lunar should rule themselves" or "localism is best", or whatever.
            Ah, but you may recall from our conversation in the thread about Raksi that I believe was either several hours or a couple of decades ago that I never really presumed her investment in that idea that be particularly ideological in the first place.

            To me, Raksi's Intimacies have more or less confirmed my expectation that she's a rather pragmatic creature. Not in the crude sense of "pragmatism equals having no sentimentality or moral constraints whatsoever", but just that her motivation are driven by straightforward practical considerations for her own benefits.

            So, while Raksi may hate the Usurpers, the read I get on her motives is simply that the Shogunate and its heirs are a threat to her life, and she likes being alive, so those enemies need to be destroyed. The main thing that it's contradicted is my tentative idea that there might be circumstances in which she's willing to die for the sake of others, which even then I said was provisional depending on what her Intimacies were like.

            I just want to reiterate that I really like these ideas that Raksi is presented as being ruthlessly opposed to something that is unambiguously intent on killing her, without that being presented in terms of her constantly living in some kind of sympathetic fear. She's not Cersei Lannister, there's not a vulnerable person underneath all of that who is living under a perceived Sword of Damocles. I mean hell, between her Intimacy towards herself and the description of her general outlook, Raksi is supposed to like herself more as somebody who's been fighting for her life for a thousand years.

            With all of that in mind, I see her perspective on how Lunar governance of the world should be distributed largely in terms of something that would thoroughly demolish institutions that could be threatening to her, while also leaving her with boundaries in which she can be supreme, thus fulfilling the other Defining Principle about the importance of that amount of power.

            Originally posted by The Wizard of Oz
            "Ma-Ha-Suchi and his adherents (Disdain)," "I don’t care for the petty details of governance."
            These are the parts that I think effectively encompass why she would oppose Ma-Ha-Suchi's ideals; she doesn't invest in a model that she's certain is superior, she just holds his sentimental model for the future (which is described in his own write-up as something that many other Lunars think of as too romantic) in contempt, with a hint of thinking that it would demand activity from her that she regards as tedious.

            Overall, my perspective on Raksi from the beginning was to wait until the write-up gave us the full picture of how the writers intended to portray her, and I was careful to not form a full picture of her until then. It helps that I find what they wound up doing with her fairly interesting.

            Originally posted by The Wizard of Oz
            Ma-Ha-Suchi has "I will raise up a new Lunar Realm where I’m admired and respected by all," and "Raksi and her adherents (Loathing)." He has no principle of "Lunar unity is the best way to defeat the Realm," or "We must raise a new Lunar Realm to rule the world with justice." Or something like that.
            That one was a bit more surprising, but in a way that I like, and a bit of a reinforcement of what sets the First Age elders apart; that they're often fighting from a perspective of having lost something, compared to the second and later generations, who are trying to build something better than the only world they've ever known. I still think he's got some positive attitude towards the actual people he might govern in his current Tie to the inhabitants of the Tangle.

                                                    People still looking at this thread when it's this far down?

                                                    All right, I’ve finally worked up the energy to start looking at these chapter fictions. Doing them all in one go.

                                                    {I'm posting them all in one go, but between lack of focus and conflicting obligations, actually took the better part of a week to bother going through them all}


                                                    Okay, introductory piece. Right off the bat, the first paragraph gets a few things right; there’s an actual group, continuing my point from What Fire Has Wrought about the benefits of focusing attention on something like a game group rather than all solitary characters, the group itself starts off with some cute dynamics between members reflecting the diversity of their origins, and it introduces a sense of some basic experiences distinct to being a Lunar (I like the idea of just casually lounging in the form of an animal in particular).

                                                    Nice follow up to that with the idea of conversing about the relative value and utility of shapes (which also looks like a good organic way to tell us more about the characters and where they came from), and I enjoy the presentation of one shifting between forms as though slipping clothes on or off. The ensuing discussion is rather endearing, with the idea that a character (perhaps a bit facetiously) would hold the greatest issue with being killed by the Dragon Blooded be letting down the teachers, and the two sharing a laugh over a fun boast. The actual subject of the fiction strikes me as an effective way to introduce the Silver Pact and a signature circle; a pair being in search of a newly Chosen Lunar is an activity that would be less abstract or brutal, and is a ready framework to bring in some exposition about what they’re about.

                                                    Heh, okay, talked a bit too soon about it being less brutal. Touching on that element actually does have an appropriate place here, considering the significance that edge has for them, and it’s good to present some of the duality inherent to them. Yes, maintains that theme with the interrogation scene. Lays some of it on a little too thick for me, but I like the added element of horror in the assumption of the war form.

                                                    Nice bit of more solid backstory for that Tegama character that ties things to the general associations of the Silver Pact, and then an enjoyable twist in the Lunar they’re searching for coming out at them swinging. I like the image of realisation in the form of seeing an opponent change shape as well. Oh, and is that Mirror Soul here as well? Wonder what they’re doing so far afield. Oh, wait, no, that’s what’s their name… Shining Pearl? I’ll know in a moment. So, I’m guessing that means the signature circle consists of a round four; Sazay as Changing Moon, Pearl as No Moon, Tegama as Full Moon, and Tula as Casteless. I would not have expected that last one before, but with how it’s been developed now, it’s perfectly fitting.

                                                    Always like the image of a Lunar conveying threats through smiles. Tula does a lot to endear me to her with the idea of her being protective of a fellow whom she’s not yet sure if it’s appropriate to designate as friend. Ah, Silent Pearl, was close. If this was my introduction to the Lunar Exalted, it would help to set up the nature of the Silver Pact to have a list of luminaries, and it’s an interesting choice for a signature circle to include one of them. Heh, yes, and Silent Pearl’s enthusiasm helps to round out endearment towards the circlemembers.

                                                    Switching to Tula’s point of view is a little abrupt, but it’s nice to end on this note of the youngest Lunar making observations about the rest, both to give a sense of her freshness and a perspective to help further characterise them. I find something distinctly endearing in the idea of the four of them going up together in the forms of birds to scout things out. The characters’ contributions to the planning session are a bit on the nose, but that’s again something that you probably want for an introduction such as this; I do like the subtlety in the implication of Silent Pearl trying to revive some spirit contacts, though.

                                                    And ends on a nice hint of the revolution that these characters are in for.


                                                    It’s a good introductory fiction for Lunars; gets across an evocative sense of who and what they are, and a nice sense for each of the Castes. I find the dynamic between the characters charming, and I like how the premise of one from the Shogunate allows them to cross a wider gamut, provides an in-road into the circle for the fact of their stretching back further. It’s more low key than I would have expected, although I find that helps to make it a bit distinct. The Dragon Blooded intro fiction is a tough act to follow, but I find this one to acquit itself well, and think it would provide some useful foundation to people not carrying a decade or more worth of ideas about the Lunar Exalted.


                                                    All right, next is something that actually does feature Vanamaithri Mirror-Soul. Yes, after having already become enamoured with Skandhar-Bhal, I’m liking the idea of depicting one utilising an asset of it to engage in a mystical and spiritual

                                                    experience. It’s not necessarily a thing distinct to Lunars, but I like the bit at the beginning where a bit of Exalted hardiness allowing one to endure a snow storm without shielding eyes can allow one to have a distinct visual experience.

                                                    It looks like this is giving me my answer about what Mirror-Soul would be seeking in finally leading warriors out of the valley (even if its order in the actual book is paradoxically placed before the question would be asked), what with those visions of people in the outside world who continue to suffer. It’s interesting to imagine a Lunar that has forsworn violence seeing their war form for the first time in centuries by way of a vision, rather than simply shifting into it.

                                                    Oh dang, a conversation between the two. The very premise of that feels rather intense, let alone the specific content of it. Somehow, I feel sorry for the metaphorical representation of the war form, between its query about being abandoned and Vanamaithri’s statement about hoping that it might die out there.

                                                    Hmm, interesting bit of interplay there, and ambiguity lent to the idea that Mirror-Soul wants to leave to redress actual injustices (and new dangers from the Solars). But the resolution is great, with Vanamaithri asserting that they can harness the rage and violence for greater ends without their serenity being compromised by it.


                                                    This is another good fiction piece, effectively pulling double duty of developing (or setting up) this one character’s compelling personal conflict, and demonstrating a tension that can exist within any given Lunar.


                                                    Next is a story set in the court of queen Watevra Wa’nabi, specifically following some of the travails of Weyna Who-Sees-Much. Oh, I see, this is covering her being tattooed (and also explains one of those art pieces, which I’m going to look back on and see if we finally get a depiction of Raksi to be proud of); that’s a good, evocative scenario to open the Silver Pact chapter on. I like the idea that, in Mahalanka, such an intimate event is made a matter of public spectacle. I like the description of the priest administering the tattoos being in the shape of a silver-haired older man, since it gives me the impression that it’s not necessarily what he really looks like, but an adoption viewed as fitting to the moment. That kind of makes me think about many Lunars who use Many-Faced Moon Transformations or the acquired forms of elderly people when they want to make a point commensurate with being centuries or millenia old, even if they’re still barely halfway through their Exalted lifespan and naturally retain whatever youthful appearance they might have had when Chosen. The idea that sometimes you’ve got to look your age to be taken seriously.

                                                    Nice to see Raksi herself make an appearance, with a bit of dignified bearing (and at this moment, I thank God that she never showed up in a comic). I like the idea of her making a point to stand out and be uncanny by appearing in the form of an out-of-place white person. I’m also liking this Names-the-Wind character, and how he’s serving to reduce some of the intensity of the situation with pleasant reassurances and colloquialisms. There’s a nice image to the idea that Raksi would come down and offer some mild participation, even if somebody else is handling most of it; there’s got to be a certain prestige to being tattooed in the presence of one of the key originators of the process.

                                                    I enjoy the sense given there for the mindset one would be in when selecting one’s Caste; looking back on the circumstances of Exaltation works very nicely here, although now I’m thinking of interesting things that could be done with a character who goes for a Caste very different from that.

                                                    Nice to see some interchange between Raksi and a prospective student. I think it’s a bit cute for her to be asking after her reputation, and like the idea that Names-the-Wind is comfortable enough to find that funny in her presence, even if she reprimands him for it (albeit in a very indirect and not particularly harsh way). For anybody who wanted to focus more time in Mahalanka, I think one could draw a lot of good cues from this story to make the character dynamics more interesting. Yes, that sense is furthered by his choice of favourite story of what Raksi has done; like, he comes across as more than just being comfortable laughing, he’s lightly mocking her to her face, and her response is to criticise and correct his tattooing. It’s adorable. I also like how Weyna tries to dodge out of giving an answer to the question about reputation, and when held to it attempts something more flattering.

                                                    Ohoho lord, Raksi asking a prospective new student straight up what they think of cannibalism, and then implying that she might try to get the newbie into it.


                                                    I like this one a lot. It covers thoughts I had when reading through the Silver Pact chapter of shahan-yas and their adherents often having the dynamics of dysfunctional families, and I find a voice actually being given to Raksi’s attitude to make her highly endearing (if no less terrible as a person).



                                                    Now, the one before the Dominions chapter. Yes, I would say that of them, the situation for the Bronze Tide would lend itself well to a little shapshot, with its more dynamic situation. I think the description of the falling city is very evocative, and there’s something ironic in the idea of a modern Lunar conqueror facing somebody else’s scorched earth tactics. I very much like how that transitions on into the subject of the Bronze Tide’s own flight, and the horrors inflicted by Fair Folk at their back, it reinforces the chain of tragedy in the region that I’m finding so compelling. Not as much to say about this one, but it does well with what it’s handling.



                                                    Oh good, I’m quite glad to see Seven Devil Clever show up again in one of these, and back on her home turf. Heh, it’s a fun place to start out, being hunted by a Water Aspect in the filthy river while she hides in the shape of a catfish; seems appropriate for somebody so firmly rooted in that city.

                                                    Oh, I like what that hunter did to discern her from the other fish; laying out bait that’ll make the others swarm away and reveal the one with a different agenda is very clever (although I imagine it’s the kind of tactic that will only work on a Lunar once). Conversely, I like the way that she opts to go on the offensive when she’s found out, and a dolphin’s torpedo-like body seems like a good thing to open up with.

                                                    Nicely written action scene going on there; the Immaculate attackers seem credibly dangerous. I’m particularly fond of the idea that the Water Aspect would give her a bow before pressing the attack, especially after getting his dang nose broken (that’s the worst). I like the description of her running and navigating back through the city to her bolt hole, although it suddenly makes me wonder if Changing Moon was ever exactly the best Caste for her. Ends on a nice note of her commiserating with her troops. The note on how she’s been building pacts with the local spirits reinforces my sense that No Moon is more fitting to her.


                                                    I think this one makes good use of the more interesting elements of Seven Devil Clever’s story, being reminiscent of the parts I liked from her comic in the Second Edition Manual (everything to do with it besides the chimera). I also think it’s good to present a Wyld Hunt with a bit of victory, or at least genuine threat, in one of those. It helps to reinforce the nature of the danger that the Silver Pact has been dealing with for centuries.


                                                    All right then, back with the signature characters with a story concerned with Tula. That’s a nice image of one assessing their army’s drills in bird form, and having trained them to recognise particular songs and tones as simple commands. I like the idea of her being so direct and serious that she has to cut through any sense of awe and wonderment that her shapeshifting might instil.

                                                    I think it does a good job presenting the situation of this little guerrilla force as a bit desperate, and this fresh new Lunar struggling a bit with the weight of the responsibility she’s taken upon herself. I can readily sympathise with them here.

                                                    Oof, that’s before getting into her flashing back to the scene when her bandit gang was wiped out. The imagery of her positive memories of old friends being wiped out by the grim recollection of their deaths are particularly heavy; the one with the strongest impact has to be the contrast between a companion who was a brave leader reduced to begging to be put out of her pain (and Tula needing to be the one who did it). This girl needs to find herself a centuries old nautilus Lunar immediately.


                                                    Interesting… the Dominions chapter did a lot to add depth to the concept as seen in the corebook, but in the person of a signature character, I think it’s good to have this idea that there’s a cause and a vendetta that she’s viewing as bigger than the lives of those fighting under her. She’s not callous or indifferent to them or their families, but still ready for them to lay down their lives to do harm to the Realm. It’s a nice reminder of the edges persisting with the Silver Pact.


                                                    Now we’ve got Sazay again to open up the Charms chapter. It’s a bit obvious, but I do get a kick out of the “wasn’t herself” line. I like the way that it characterizes her choice of human target to sacred hunt for this infiltration, her liking of ironic juxtaposition and harsh sense of justice. Considering the advancement of the Changing Moon Caste in my esteem, I like how the sig seems to be the most dangerous member of this circle; the Full Moon will probably just straight-up kill you. I’m also interested in her intention to recount a story of the events she’s involved in.

                                                    Nice breakdown of the social scene that Sazay has inserted herself into, and I really like the way it’s describing her methodology for shit-stirring. The social Attribute Charms have been very evocative, and I like how this fiction really feels like it’s pulling those out, and it makes for a very strong sense of how assuming a stolen shape can function in an environment and with a goal like this. She’s really living up to the Immaculate epithet, there. I especially like how her method for polarizing her shape’s opponents are playing both sides of local politics against the middle.

                                                    Ohh, I especially like the description of what she’s doing with that one honest aristocrat that is loyal to the local prince compared against the Cathak satrap; that playbook of using innuendo to plant a suspicion, disappearing from the target’s memory so the suspicion feels as though it originated within oneself, and then coming right back to further capitalise upon it. That’s a very clever procedure. And I’m always a real sucker for narratives based around manipulated memories, particularly with that idea of somebody opening the proceedings in the same way with each go around.

                                                    And the whole thing is tied together by the point of the entire proceeding being to reveal her own murder (in several senses of the term).


                                                    This fiction is great; it’s a compelling narrative beat in its own right, and another feather in the cap of the Changing Moon Caste. It conveys the sense of an Exalt wielding their more subtle powers without having to explicitly break down the particular steps, which is a very good introduction for this chapter. And I’m really enjoying the character of Sazay, this delightfully nasty and dangerous and clever trickster figure, cheerfully revelling in the subversive use of her power to achieve what she would reasonably view as a noble end. She’s going to be the one for the other two circle members to beat.


                                                    It looks like Shining Pearl is about to get their chance. I like how it starts with a description of how they approach gods, set as a contrast to Immaculate priorities. Their nautilus shell also sounds very pretty.

                                                    Another nice description of the use of a Charm baked right into normal text, good. And that’s a particularly good description for both the entry into a sanctum, and what the place actually looks like. “Saturn had claimed you” sounds like a neat kind of idiom or euphemism to employ, especially for a being that is not directly and personally accustomed to death. I like the bit of back and forth in Pearl using some of their own idioms to refer to evading death. Really, the whole exchange has a fun tension to it; I especially like the leading line of “you haven’t changed” as a setup for the god.

                                                    Oh dang, they’re really giving it their all in bargaining with this Boryg in the… characteristic Lunar manner. I particularly like how there’s no rancour in Pearl’s observations, just pragmatic acknowledgement before getting right to business. And it’s great to hear one tempering their threats and demands with the idea that, if they wanted to, they could simply claim the god’s iconic power for themselves. Also really fun to make the point to an immortal being to not merely end their existence, but any hint of legacy; it’s not something I would have been looking for, but Fangs has been doing a good job in several places of examining the consequences of gods literally, verifiably dying.


                                                    All right then, Shining Pearl you go ahead and make a nice comfortable place for yourself right there next to Sazay, you’ve earned it.


                                                    Okay, next up there’s somebody playing cards with some of the satrapy’s local heavies. I think a couple of those card games sound as though they’d be distinctive to the North. And I often like when fantasy settings have a bit of ambiguity, such as whether or not the presence of a magic dagger is a threat or is literally capable of uncovering cheating. Hmm, interesting that Tegama appears to be here openly, but then, he was a mortal nobleman, so I’m taking it that he can still partially operate under his own identity while keeping his secrets.

                                                    Ahh, no, Tegama wasn’t there openly, and was indeed using magic to not seem out of place despite the lack of an introduction; another great low-key description of Charms in action, combined with a good feel for a character coming to their senses. It is a curious approach for a Full Moon, mind, although with how Favoured Attributes work they can of course be more varied, and Tegama’s background probably lends itself to a social edge.

                                                    Ah, it took me a few moments to realise that the references to cavalry lines are because Tegama proposed playing a Marukan-themed card game. A nice description of drawing a weapon from Elsewhere, and then it’s a fight scene. Ah yes, I believe that there had been a reference before suggesting that Tegama practiced Single Point Shining into the Void Style, glad to see it showing up here. It’s a good fight scene overall, with my enjoying particularly the description of how Ledaal Haraku is calling up a protective whirlwind, and it’s great how Tegama is effectively fighting without needing to shapeshift (lining up with how enamoured I became with the Dexterity Charms).


                                                    It’s a nice fiction, although I have to admit that Tegama winds up being the weak link in the signature circle; not bad, just not outstanding. The one distinct thing I like is how he maintains a cordial attitude at all times, which feels like a nice synthesis between his upbringing and his new role. Certainly, it’s good to have a different kind of Full Moon than might be expected.


                                                    All right, and now that all of the pieces have been put into place, we’re given a fiction in which it all comes to a head; the planted bodies and the groundwork of conflicting accusations arising from high society and spiritual double agents. A nice little mess that they’ve created. I wonder what further part they might play in it.

                                                    Ah yes, I’d forgotten that Tula had been out there training guerrillas, so they get to stroll in and pick off the weakened infighting forces at will. Delightful. Still like how she’s commanding in bird form, as well as sparing a thought to getting civilians out of the way.

                                                    And Sazay there gets to continue her reputation as a little shit-stirrer. Of all the things she did, I’d have to say my favourite was taking a tax collector’s shape in order to steal official documents; it speaks to an interesting sense of foresight, since if they win, she gets to make sure that the administration can continue functioning smoothly, and if they need to retreat, can help make sure that it doesn’t. Mind, after the manner in which she and Silent Pearl met, it’s also a treat to see the idea of the two of them fighting side by side as wolves. Silent Pearl’s action of luring troops out to meet their death with cold gods is simpler in comparison, but after how they had conducted themselves in their own piece, it has a certain gritty texture to it that I think Lunars being portrayed as commanding spirits in the past has lacked.

                                                    And finally there’s Tegama, living up to the name of his Caste as he fights his way towards their primary target.


                                                    Yes, this is a nice bit of payoff after all of that setup. I find the descriptions of the fighting throughout streets, and Sazay’s view of the attempts to keep control in the halls, to be very reminiscent of the best parts of The Black Company. It’s nice to see the activity of the Silver Pact in action, and I’m looking forward to their epilogue.


                                                    Oh darn, the Cathak satrap got away. Still, it’s hardly all been for nothing, and if one wanted to follow the story of this circle, they’ve even been set up with a personal antagonist. I like how Tula’s priority in particular should be that the enemy march home with nothing to show for it, in disgrace. And for a Cathak, that’s got to sting especially (even if she could very well have fellows who overtly praise her prudence).

                                                    I’ll admit, in the manner in which I’ve been reading these, I’d lost the thread a bit on where the factional divides lay, so I appreciate having a reiteration of how the circle’s grievance is with the forces of the local prince as well, since they had been collaborators whose recent animosity was provoked by Lunar provocation. Which leaves Tula in the awkward position of needing to decide what to do with captives. Well, I suppose if one’s going to take responsibility for starting a revolution, one needs to accept everything that comes with it. (I adore how the fiction is focusing on some of the messy and difficult aftermath of a successful uprising).

                                                    Sazay continues to be my favourite with her eagerness for murder, although Tula’s assessment that they can cause more damage by sending a satrap back to have to answer for failure, and uncertainty about the extent of Lunar activities, raises her in my esteem a bit. I like the interplay between these two, and how Sazay is ribbing her new friend a bit for association with her old one.
                                                    I enjoy this vignette of the scene in the aftermath of the battle, and its focus on the various activities of the people; a nice reminder that it’s not all about the Exalted, and their lofty conflicts, there’s some mud and sepsis down there as well. Huh, they’re burning those official records? Why even bother saving them, then? Eh whatever, if it gives the people something to crowd around and make a big symbolic gesture of their liberation.

                                                    Aww, Silent Pearl tending to the exhausted Tula in the middle of her ministering to the families of the dead is super sweet. I also like the contrast between their actions with the stone god and serving here as a tender for the wounded. Nice window into a bit of their backstory, and a sense of obligation to the much younger Lunar who found them. Dawww, and they’re trying not to be and look scary in front of some young people, who make the effort to offer a token of thanks, that’s adorable, I’m genuinely grinning over it.

                                                    And what’s Tegama doing amidst all of it? Hmm, doesn’t quite seem suited to matters of state, but is probably the most qualified out of the four of them, even if the majority of it needs to wind up with the mortal leaders. And a meeting with a Dynastic prisoner, this should be interesting. Ah, we don’t get the specifics of that, but Tegama’s presence is elaborate on for being a Lunar with experience in installation of a new government and knowing what assets to inquire after. Oh, but she does get a bit of a word in; I like how brazen she is directly to the face of a deadly Lunar, although it’s apparent that he’s good for sparring with more than just his sword, with that riposte about how Realm retaliation is likely to be delayed by the succession crisis.

                                                    And Sazay’s there starting to put together the story she’ll tell of events. It’s cute how she considers embellishing it a bit in her own favour, but silently relents when Pearl actually comes along; my initial take was that she felt a bit of a threat, but looking back it feels more like an honest decision to properly respect her other new friend.

                                                    (Hmm, a few accidental misgenderings of Silent Pearl, those can be fixed in post)

                                                    Finally a note on how they’re… not quite formalizing their circle, but solidifying their commitment to one another. It’s an interesting choice how Tula is looking to apply her talents elsewhere, although it makes sense; this whole line of fiction has been about building up the story of Kulinth, and while it’s not exactly finished, it is resolved. It’s more fitting to Lunars to not be tied down to one place anyway. I do love how, for how ad hoc this whole thing is, Sazay resolves to spruce it up a bit in the retelling.



                                                    Yesss, this has been a good sequence of fictions. It started off lower key than the Dragon Blooded got, but it’s escalated very nicely, and there have been very evocative steps along the way. Strong snap shots of what each Lunar is doing in accordance with their Caste or other associations, consistent sense of their powers at work. And it built very effectively to a major final war scene and effective sense of what there’s to do afterwards. It’s a good story and a good look for the Lunar Exalted. Reading through all of these has been a solid finisher for what has been an exceptional book; I’ll collect my proper final thoughts on it tomorrow.

                                                        The Lunar Exalted have always been kind of the black sheep of the game line, haven’t they? First Edition had to kind of jury rig them into the setting, and decided on an approach that was a bit too much pulp and not quite enough revival, and Second Edition was too concerned with being a reaction to how that was received and not enough with really figuring them out. It’s certainly something that stood out to me, because the concept of animalistic shapeshifters has a certain personal resonance, at the same time as they were one of the earliest things that I found something off about; ages ago, before I even participated in the forums, when it was all still the preliminary research via the White Wolf Wiki, there was that moment of “wait, exactly how does the shapeshifting help with all of this social engineering stuff?”

                                                        And in the years since then it’s been such a unique source of contention. So many arguments about what they could be or should be, attempts in some semi-official sources to explore the edges, writers and developers trying to continuously promise that there was going to be a major overhaul that would finally realise their potential… heh, it’s funny to think about how, for me specifically, the agenda for Lunars that emerged out of Masters of Jade has been around for far longer than any of the previous uncertainties, and even that’s something that’s had time to evolve (as well as needing to be constantly defended).

                                                        Still, all of that wait has been worth it, because the book really knocked it out of the park.

                                                        Given what I said before about the resonance of animal shapeshifters, it’s immensely satisfying to me that the ultimate result didn’t stray away from that, and finally brought together everything that makes it so compelling as a kind of Exalted. I’ve also got to say that there’s something really beneficial to Lunars having finally emerged in this environment, for what it lends to the picture of their patron deity and how they interact with the Chosen, as well as the application and comments that it’s able to make about what mutability lends to identity.

                                                        The Silver Pact is a part that has been talked about for so long, such that the basics of their agenda and methodology aren’t really much of a surprise (although the detailing of the specifics is very fine). But what this book really did that was transformative for them was sweeping away the last vestiges of the World of Darkness Garou in their iconography and social structure; to make them feel coherent at the same time as it emphasizes variety by way of removing a uniform ceremonial structure. And more than that, how it uses the shahan-yas not merely as compelling figures who lend credibility to how the Pact promulgates itself, but as nuclei of intrigue and society. We’re given so many cross-sections of their group to show us the variety of Lunar Exalted, how they’re varied personalities and purposes complement and clash against each other, which really helps them to feel present and meaningful in the setting. There’s something to point to within the actual Lunars book in the form of “this Lunar or that Lunar” rather than just the nebulously defined Lunars as a whole.

                                                        Then there’s the Dominions, which would damn near justify the entire book on their own if the rest of it had been wanting. That they’re more than just a solidification of the idea of what the Lunars are up to in the Threshold, and how varied their approaches can be in a manner that challenges their enemies to form a playbook against them. More even than just a number of interesting new locations added to every part of the setting, every one of them on par with the much vaunted First Edition introduction of An-Teng or Second Edition’s expansions of the Octet, and at a fraction of the word count (helped by them being remarkably on point, made especially noteworthy for how they’re packed with so much detail; there’s culture and history, there’s fashion and factions, there are interesting personages to make the focus of intrigue and adventure, and there’s somehow still room to define them by their relationships with neighbours, who still wind up being interesting snapshots of the larger world). No, the grandest thing it does is, as Vance put it in an article with Escapist magazine, challenging the premise of what barbarianism is, as it is culturally defined both in and out of character, and has been represented in its genre. It does such an amazing job of taking all of these things that, in so many other settings goes broad and arch even when trying to be complementary, and gives them such depth, nuance, and goes out of its way to acknowledge their capacity for ingenuity within their own context. I especially love when it keeps having examples of how being a common culture, whether a centralised empire or a disparate group of associated nomads, doesn’t make them homogenous. And then it goes and gives so many of the associated Lunars places and relationships with those societies more sophisticated than “I’m the strongest/have supernatural powers, so everything I say goes”; it takes their societies and local leaders seriously, and provides guidance on how players can do the same. The Dominions are an insight into the setting that I’ve been waiting seven years for.

                                                        Even something as banal as the Traits chapter has something to recommend itself; a total overhaul of shapeshifting to give it more value and meaning than just vaguely gesturing at the idea that turning into a wolf is helpful for tracking, mutations that are more interesting and worthwhile than they’ve ever been, and a Great Curse that emphasizes Lunar themes by way of twisting them, in a manner resulting in compelling tragedy that might just outdo that of the Solars.

                                                        And the Charms. What more can I say about the Charms that I didn’t realise as I was reading through them? They’re magnificent. They reveal tremendous amounts of character for the Lunars, both as a whole and the specificity of the Castes. They’re filled with a delightful amount of variety even for character concepts focusing on specific Attributes (never mind the permutations that can be found in cross-pollinating). They’re remarkably powerful without feeling a need to compete directly with the Solars, meaning that even in cases where they distinctly lack something that those other Exalted have they don’t feel short-changed. There’s a lot in there that is freaking terrifying, which I recall being the first thing I said I wanted the Lunars to be in the wake of Masters of Jade, although it’s also nicely offset by a few things that are deeply moving. And best of all, they didn’t need to give in to every sentiment that felt as though the very concept of the Lunars was unworthy of them; it got to bring them to life, harnessing animal references without being bound beneath them, without needing to resort to a lot of really esoteric weirdness. It’s hard to effectively summarise or rank the triumphs of this Charm set, but one that I have to end on is the point of how they took everything that should have felt as though it was a weakness of the idea of Attribute Charms (the sharply limited number, the not necessarily clear line of distinction from being Ability Charms with serial numbers filed off, and so on) and replaced it with a strength in a manner that is virtually seamless.

                                                        After that, everything else in the book just feels like a bit of a victory lap; a few neat martial arts and some sorcery (although the initiations there took a few things unique to the Lunar background and mythology even from prior Editions and really made them pop), some more very nice Artifacts (which still manage to fulfil the duty of characterizing the mythology of the Lunars, such as the spiritual landscape they navigate, the trials that they’ve faced, and the relationships underlying the Silver Pact), and rounding it out with some more detailed pictures of a few characters that gets to be the staging ground of not only redeeming some problem elder characters, but placing their power into a context that is meaningful without being completely overwhelming to the setting.
                                                        All of it tied together by another compelling narrative of some like-minded Exalted in action and union.

                                                        It feels like… like having had a nagging feeling for so long, and then somebody finally comes along and provides the lexicon with which to describe and understand those feelings, and it’s so eye-opening. You see character concepts you’ve been imagining for ages, and finally have the clear tools to realise them. And then you look at the tools themselves, how they fit together, and entirely new concepts can leap fully formed out of them. What more can be asked for in a game book, in this game book that people have waited for longer than there even was a Third Edition, what more than this?

                                                        I’m overjoyed to have seen it. There’s nothing more to say.
