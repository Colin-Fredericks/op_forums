                        The term "Terrestrial Exalted" exists in an odd kind of nebulous space. On the one hand, it's simply the alternative term for the Dragon Blooded (and, within the setting, one that is largely archaic and unused), and basically stands as their equivalent to titles like Solar or Sidereal or Liminal Exalted. On the other, it's held up as a distinct category equivalent to the term "Celestial Exalted", (which itself just really forms a convenient shorthand for Solar, Lunar and Sidereal Exalted, who are held together both for the facts that the things that they're named after are very far up, and that they have certain similar assumptions in their power and nature such as the idea that they're selected from an otherwise unknown group and can't multiply), despite the fact that it amounts to a category consisting of one. I feel the latter one is something held up more by the fanbase than the books themselves, manifesting in some rather odd qualities such as a perceived need to add additional Terrestrial Exalted or to reassign some existing one to enter it, not so much because it accomplishes anything functional but because it validates the term as a category. So the term "Terrestrial Exalted" is not entirely meaningless, since it still serves as a name for the Dragon Blooded (and an easier thing to use to refer to those parts of martial arts where they struggle, since it's one word), but doesn't really need to mean anything beyond "these are the Dragon Blooded" in assessing the details of any given Exalt. Now, if one were to take the tack of "there are tenuous links between moon imagery and beasts", that would have less cruft, even if it's still something I would argue against.

                                        Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                                        That leads into the final point; that the Solar Exalted are at the core of the game, meaning they're at the forefront of fulfilling that central narrative premise of "passionate, motivated people have the capacity to change the world"; I think it would represent a failing of that premise being used by the core of the game to say "actually, you can only do it in a particular way".
                                        Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                                        The game is supposed to grab people's attention and give them direction for play with the idea that they're playing characters whose return to power can be truly hopeful and righteous if they succeed.                                         Isator I am not sure this is exactly what you are saying (this whole post was fairly intricate in argument), but with legitimacy, I would note the books pretty much say "will destroy or save" the first time the Solars are mentioned. That is the deal. The Exalted don't get a guarantee that they are a necessary or righteous force in shaping the world. Not Sidereals, not Dragonblooded, not Abyssals, not Lunars, not Alchemicals. To a degree, it up to groups to assert their belief in their characters and the splats do (again to a degree) exist to challenge one another and their stories*.

                                        The setting is not there purely to get players gassed and optimistic about what their Solar PCs can achieve and excited to see what different futures they will shape! It's built on a history tragedy and failure! And yes, questionable legitimacy. The idea that the returning Solars may only bring glorious failure and defeat is right there early.

                                        That's supposed to be a *genuine* challenge, right? One you can rise to by looking back and saying "Yes, legitimately true, the Solars created a society dependent on them, then fell to decadent tyranny, one that treated the world as a canvas only for their eog and grand passions. The promise they held merely led, in the end, to blind ruin, chaos and loss. Perhaps that is in the nature of the Solar Exalted, or Exalted rule generally. But I still must try to strive to take power for what it can do, with hope of mastering demons of my nature and tendencies in my essence". Therein is the hook.

                                        Whether or not the TSR is the right way to approach it (and there are problems with it, as it stands, primarily I think of anachronism and the underlying cultural logic behind it), in many ways it is not challenging the Solar narrative and theme in a way that is not already present through the Bronze Faction (which in that instance is probably to their advantage as making an interesting splat) and writing within the 1e and 2e cores about who and what they are.

                                        I feel like there is kind of suggestion of fragility and sensitivity here that I don't understand.

                                        With whether the TSR implies that the Solars must shape the world a particular way, I don't think it ever did. At the most it implied there were tendencies there in the nature of their power and how they express it that skewed them towards a particular and in ways particularly unstable form of society, while the Lunars returned, with a specific cultural experience the Solars lacked were able to look at things differently not a mandated destiny. (Although if we're questioning and criticizing the idea that the returned Solars will have fairly narrow mandated destinies, at what can tie the Solars to an idea of a return to a glorious civilization, then , again, we should be really be looking at the Path of Bronze and Path of Gold).

                                        * Yes, I know there are people who will look at the idea that the other Exalted are not naturals to the task of guiding and renewing the world as a suggestion that they are worthless and incompetent compared to the Solars, to whom they are obligated.

It's not about whether anyone is worthless without a particular quality, more about what the most interesting to realise them is, and one that captures the breadth of human character and potential.

                                        "Subjective morality is what lets each Exalted type embody a genre of mythic hero and let those archetypes coexist and/or battle for control of the future:

                                        On the one hand, you have these divine bureaucrat-warrior monks who literally write and enforce the status quo. They want to preserve the strangehold of divine authority because it preserves their own power, but the nobler among them believe that they should maintain The System because it demonstrably works. Even the fall and overthrow of the Solars, an apocalyptic plague, a massive alien invasion and a cold war between superpowers armed with cosmic weapons hasn't been able to end the world as long as The System was intact. The Sidereals have the least mythic allusions behind them, unless you consider them angels, in which case it gets weird.

                                        So let's turn to the Dragon-Blooded, who are the lesser proponents of The System, in that they are warrior aristocrats who wish to spread their empire to the far corners of the world, bringing the glories (and horrors) of civilization to all. It's hard to view the Dynasty as terribly heroic, though, given their excesses. So I'll skip ahead.

                                        Now we get to the interesting dichotomy: Lunar vs. Solar.

                                        On the one hand, the Lunars champion a more elegant simplicity rooted in a lower population tribal model. Life is hard and harsh, but that means evolution can function again to help humanity grow stronger and more capable of surviving whatever apocalypse the enemies of the world can unleash. The Lunar tribal model eschews the trap of non-local interdependency brought by widespread commerce and replaces the deceptions of so-called civilized etiquette with tested meritocracy and straightforward taboo laws. As heroes, the Lunars are very Norse heroes, whose heroism is rooted in the delicate balance between bravery and cunning.

                                        On the other hand, Solars are the Lawgivers. They build civilizations, utopias, dreams made manifest. They build shining cities and skyships and geomantic power distributors for the weather machines that let the crops grow year round. They build agriculture, commerce, tiered bureucracies enchanted for efficiency and warded against corruption. They are like Gilgamesh, Odysseus, Daedelus and Aeneas, visionary god-kings of unlimited potential. But their way creates sophistication, inequity of social classes, irrelevance of mortal meritocracy compared with Solar grandeur and the power of Solar magic to elevate the lowliest but favored to greatness over the wisest and noblest.

                                        Even setting aside the futures that the Sidereals or Terrestrials would shape, the Solar and Lunar models each have their pros and cons. But they can't both win. So, subjective morality time. Who should win, the Norse or the Greeks?"
                                        Now this is actually doing specifically what Isator does not like (and I suspect this is what you're thinking of IL, even without directly referencing it).

                                        Now setting aside whether he was right with the specific identities of the splats (is it the right focus / breadth?) and how sympathetic they each actually are, that kind of view of a setting where each of the different types of Exalted have some measure of challenge to the ideal of heroism the other offers, seems interesting, to me.

                                        At the very least, evidently, I would guess, not at all out of step with the conversations he'd had with the developers and other writers up to that point, late First Edition IRC. So very questionable that an approach where different Exalted direct at times conflicting forms of heroism has a direct contradiction to the thesis and purpose of the game and setting, as it was imagined then.

                                                        You might have a Solar who is going all Genghis Khan across half of Creation with the perspective that the unification and the reopened trade routes and the renewed communications will be to the long term practical and spiritual benefits of the people for centuries to come (in terms of things like "the increased commerce will ward off the Wyld", "the better travel and communications will allow us to effectively fight off Fair Folk armies" and "people exchanging with and speaking to one another will have a richer experience"), which makes it worth the deaths of millions. You might have another Solar who opposes that one, convinced that the devastation will actually serve as the final nail in their coffin, and that it's just plain unconscionable besides and moral compromises cannot be accepted even in the face of Armageddon. Both of them are highly qualified for their respective vocations, so let us assume that it is legitimate for either of them to be taking on such tasks. Which one is correct? Does either of them being correct mean that their position can be condoned; is it right to save the world if it requires the deaths of tens of millions? Is it right to save tens of millions of doing so will perpetuate something that will destroy the world? You'd better hope these Solars solve the dispute quickly, because being distracted against one another is giving those Fair Folk a good opportunity to come in and finish things off.

                                                        I find that to be a decent enough way to preserve the idea of compelling drama and moral ambiguity amidst the return of the Solar Exalted, and a mechanism by which they may hasten the world's doom, without having to fall back on the possibility that people given tremendous power may turn out to be ungovernable monsters. Solars daunted by the tremendous size of the task ahead of them, and the possible uncertainties of how to deal with that task, without needing to fall back on the idea that they'll be uninterested or unqualified.

                                                        I think one can garner sufficient drama and complexity without having to fall back on appealing to current attitudes of authority being inherently untrustworthy and corruptible.

                                                        I hope that makes it clear that when I posit the Solars as legitimate and necessary, I'm not suggesting a game in which they're always right, or even always nice people. Just that, barring the corruption of the Great Curse, they'll be people who are trying to pursue things that, one way or another, further the well-being of people and/or the world in general.
