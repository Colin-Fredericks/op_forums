                        Originally posted by BrilliantRain View Post

                        I think I'd enjoy hearing more about Australian mythology, if you have some time.

                        I guess the major difference (to things like Greek mythology or Egyptian or whatever) is that it is an active belief system for many Aboriginal people and there are some really complex aspects of who is allowed to know what, and who is given what bits of knowledge. Mother-in-law language is common, and it is evident in spiritual practice. How knowledge is transmitted is key as well - yarning is important culturally and is beginning to be the foundation of some new sociological movements which is great (I work at a university with a large research and support unit focused on Aboriginal people and research into Australian history).

                        One of the things we talked about was the way that a lot of yarns have what feels like a fatalistic end. I was reading a kid's story once and excerpted it for them because it's about Aboriginal culture, and culture in general, but there's a small section talking about dances and what they mean. So there are dances about animals, right? The main character is talking to the white kids about what the dances mean. Except the dance for crocodiles is 'this kid didn't listen to the elders talk about where crocs are living and he got himself eaten because he was greedy', and the one about bees is 'holy shit white men's bees will kill you, run'. Absolutely vital lessons to be shared across the incredible number of linguistic and tribal lines of Australia, but they don't tend to have the same kinds of 'happy endings' or triumphs modern audiences expect (no pretty Narcissus turning into a flower for his sins, we have the Daughters running away to the sky to escape a terrible man). Which is because they aren't myths, reworked and retold and codified through centuries and millennia away from the living culture that created them, but are part of living culture now.

                        When you grow up reading these stories and hearing these songs and watching these dances, while also getting folklore that talks about this weird frozen water from the sky at Christmas, or winter being a dead season (hell, winter as a season at all for half the country), it affects how you understand myths.

                        The invasion of Australia is recorded in dances and yarns and so on, just like centuries of meteorological data, medical knowledge, things like that. So my experience of 'myth' is with a much more actively living thing than a lot of people outside Australia. Hell, even inside Australia - I didn't grow up on country or anything like that, but there are some nebulous connections around the place, and also I grew up in less white enclaves. And like I said, as an adult I have the advantage of some top level researchers to hang with.

                        Also means I know how seriously terrible it would be to lift, say, the Quinkins and use them in a game. It's less about the stories themselves, and more about how they intersect with cultures. Like it's almost impossible to talk about Aboriginal culture because it isn't a culture, a language, or even series of dialects. Aboriginal mobs are as disparate as countries, obviously, given how big the country is. There are commonalities - connection to country, some common stories, some cultural stuff like eye contact, physical contact, conversation flow, but mostly it is tied to specific places and mobs. I'm no expert, I try and sit and listen when Aboriginal people speak, and this is some of what I've learned.

                        In US terms Aboriginal culture is much more like First People's than anything else, and from what I can tell it takes a bigger part of modern Australian culture than First People in the US though.

                        (note: I am a white woman, I grew up regional and in the country, but I'm a city dweller now)
