    All right, it is way too late, and I have had some disruptions, but I still want to knock out one more Dominion before tomorrow. Finishing is going to have to overlap with starting the next (final?) Charms preview.

    I am hoping that the Nameless Lair of Ma-Ha-Suchi demands less of me.



    Okay, first off, I think it’s a good counterweight to all of Ma-Ha-Suchi’s fresher, more melodramatic qualities to still have a bit of hostility inherent to where he makes his home, although that is effectively tempered a bit by reinforcing the notion that he is not merely open to negotiation, but notably inclined to and skilled at it. I never had an issue before with Ma-Ha-Suchi’s Caste (that ire was reserved for Red Jaws), but I appreciate it being a bit more overt rather than needing to infer the details of it. And while my notion that his placement was meant to serve as a blockade between the Threshold and the Southeast isn’t exactly rocket science, I do enjoy seeing it laid it clearly here.

    That Changing Moon quality to him is also effectively reinforced with the description of how he ingratiated himself to the now named locals, the Chante-Sa. I think it would be easy to fall into a trap of using his situation to avoid really giving him much in the way of an achievement, so I’m glad that it establishes his acceptance in terms of performing a great fight of combat and negotiation in making good with the local goddesses by eliminating troublesome monsters. I don’t want to read too much into the running element of established Lunars being ascribed familial roles, but it’s a picture of authority, divinity and even celebrity that I find endearing, so I’m happy to see it repeated here.

    Also, I have a certain soft-spot for the scenario of people shunning a character who is probably kind of okay because of a monstrous appearance (it’s the basis of one of my favourite Xenoblade 2 characters), so I like that being folded into Ma-Ha-Suchi’s backstory here. That and him not being a big asshole about it.

    Oh, and there’s a little description of the Three Mothers. I think it’s a good call to not make them super long; I might even say that more cases could do with just describing a purview and iconography. Considering the environment, I think my greatest fondness is for the Lavish Mother, both because the apparent colour scheme makes her sound very distinct looking, and because how it folds together what bright colours tend to signify in a jungle.

    I think it’s interesting for the Chante-Sa culture to make unity to the point of being effective for the Lunar’s purposes an ongoing and slightly fraught goal, and I’m intrigued to learn more about what it means for each tribe to be dedicated to one of the goddesses. Sorry, bands. It’s an interesting governing setup they’ve got there; a matriarch appointed by divinity, but who delegates a lot of governing authority to chosen intermediaries and whose spiritual authority is not absolute. It sounds like a system developed on a lot of complex precedent and compromise, and I find it very believable; more than a lot of depictions of simpler systems, really. In the spirit of wanting there to often be depictions of mortal princes and chieftains that the Exalted need to treat with rather than simply dominate, I’m happy to see the idea that Ma-Ha-Suchi’s authority is considered no greater than that of the band leaders.

    There’s a word for the kind of limited agriculture that the Chante-Sa engage in, but it currently escapes me… ah, I’ll probably find it again, since this write-up is really making me want to read things about the peoples of the Amazon rainforest again. I’ve got to say, between the change of Direction, the relations with the local Lunar, and their own layout of spiritual background and challenging environment, reading this write-up is really reinvigorating my interest.

    I like the sound of the religious practices, with the reference to communal prayer putting me in mind of lavish ceremonies. And it’s nice to see a description of ceremonial cannibalism that doesn’t demonise the process or its practitioners, and gets the subject closer to its more common practice.

    For the descriptions of the individual bands, the Gap gets off to a good start. Their aesthetic, particularly the skulls on which they encourage the growth of phosphorescent fungi, sounds like it makes for some very vivid scenes, and I’m glad that they’re described as living in ornately carved towns, just because it makes for a more accurately diverse picture of the kinds of people that are probably the inspiration here, of their lifestyles and processes. Like their goddess, the land of the Chuwe sounds like it has some very compelling colour schemes. The notion that the East has flora of unique properties has often felt like an underutilised idea to me, so I’m happy to see it prominent in this band’s purview, as well as how that ties into them having the greater economic savvy; the picture of them selling goods and services to merchant outposts is cheerfully bringing me back to Masters of Jade. And for the Mahni, first off I like how they incorporate menstruation imagery into their mythology, and from there the idea that clay-work and river running leaves them as the most widely travelled and richest band. I just get something out of the image of them crafting ornate pottery in large quantities, and then having things to put it in. I really need to get pottery into my head more, considering it anthropological and archaeological significance.

    Ohhh, I like the Blood Seasons, for a few reasons. First is that it’s an intriguing way for Ma-Ha-Suchi to establish a system of resolution to allow the people to maintain a kind of unity while retaining their separate identities. The fact that one of the suggested tasks to determine authority being playing a trick on Ma-Ha-Suchi suggests to me that he has a certain willingness to be a bit open to mild indignities for the sake of keeping the system running, himself integrated in it, and maintain an endeared status. Mind, I will say that although I wouldn’t want to think of the system as being rigged, it strikes me as possible that he might occasionally confer with the Three Mothers to suggest tasks that might be favoured in one band’s favour, and perhaps sometimes send his adherents out to very lightly and subtly interfere, in cases where he thinks the good of the Chante-sa requires the season to be devoted to a particular course; he might not be dominating them, but he’s still Exalted, and there are methods for him to exert power through that. That said, I ultimately like the picture of a month of competition ending in a season of communal dedication to some particular specialty; it gives me a picture of each band having members who’ll go among collected groups to use their expertise in leading them collectively through the season’s efforts. I also like the connotations of the beastfolk only getting to lead when the Chante-sa collectively fail in their tasks, rather than getting to compete directly.

    Okay, that gets the jungle and the people out of the way, now on to the Nameless Lair itself. The backstory of being some minor fortress whose name is forgotten is certainly more fitting to his current ethos, but I also think some nice holdovers like that are given some interesting expression in the idea that Ma-Ha-Suchi has repurposed a lot of the place to be more accommodating, even comfortable. I’ve always liked to see him as being somewhat analogous to the Beast of the French fairy tale, and I’m happy that a bit of that comes through in the idea that his personal quarters are very dark and private. I also enjoy the sentimentality underlying him maintaining a catacomb for prominent descendants and fallen students.

    Considering my thoughts about the description of his initial approach, I’m glad that being considered a holy figure hasn’t completely divested the Chante-sa of wariness concerning him, just recontextualised it a bit. The way it describes him as not resenting the petitions of the bands, that they’ll only make at direst need, gives me the compelling image of him being fairly genial when holding audience with petitioners who’ll still shake a bit, won’t meet him in the eye, and hear even the mildest pronouncements in his bleating voice as carrying an edge of threat. I mean, that’s what I’d impose on it, I’m sure the basic concept is that he doesn’t mind when the people need his aid or intervention on something. Considering the difficulty of approaching, I wonder what kinds of people even would intrude upon him, but I suppose that even discounting Wyld Hunts, there might occasionally be people who seek an audience that don’t care to seek Chuwe guides are wait on his permission, and are strong enough to be capable of making it through the jungle and be a bit beyond the perimeter guards. I’m imagining something like the occasional merchant prince who might come with a well-armed expeditionary force to impress themselves upon making a deal for easier passage to the Dreaming Sea, or perhaps even the occasional outcaste or Exigent who has some words to exchange.

    Yes, with the references to beastfolk before, I admit that I briefly forgot about the capacity to transform existing people. I like the way that the whole thing is framed around and incorporated into the existing mythology, and the spiritual nature of a present religious feature, that’s all really cool. Plus not restricting itself to what might be some of our pre-conceived gender roles by ascribing Ma-Ha-Suchi the role of doula in the process. I’m glad that the resulting beastfolk are now divided into separate castes of wolf and goat (I know it was kind of the point, but I always found that whole thing of wolf-goat folk who kept feeling an urge to eat each other was going a bit too far), as well as how both of them have big communal roles; just get a kick out of the wolffolk’s purpose including additional hunting to help the people through lean times. And it’s good to give a shout-out to the admiral qualities of the goat. I get a real kick out of the basis for the premise that a lot of outsiders encounter inhabitants of the area in terms leading to an assumption that they’re all beastfolk.

    Aw damn, we’re almost at the end? I kind of want it to keep on going. Oh well. So I Mahiya and Red Cat both individually, and as a partnership; her as somebody in this context who has big mercantile ambitions out of a love for luxury (plus the idea of somebody having divine favour due to a good eye for flower offerings), him as somebody who cut his teeth from experience garnered in Guild caravans (I can just imagine him being perceived as some mysterious stoic tribal warrior type), and the sense of partnership from complementary interests.

    Hmm, the Inan of the River Band is a man? I could have sworn I read before that they were invariably women… well for now, I like how Aan Jander represents a bit of a fly in the ointment in the current peace arrangements of the Chante-Sa, leading to both some inter-band tensions and slight conspiracy within his own ranks (which I adore taking the form of wanting to petition the goddess to appoint a new leader, rather than murder him). I’m also glad of the diversification inherent to the idea of a tribal warrior leader being somebody physically infirm but a good tactical planner.

    Not so much to say about Gap leader Greenheart. Her premise, a thaumaturge encouraging her people to raid more to acquire more luxuries for their home, is all right and all, and does have an interesting connotation of their more isolated quality leading to trying to diversify their practices, causing tension as that steps on another band’s caste role… huh, I guess I did have something to say about her.

    For the beastfolk, I like the sense that goatman shaman Uakka’s authority within the dominion actually allows him to rank higher than some of Ma-Ha-Suchi’s students, as a sense of the basis for him calling upon their greater strength in dealing with some more dangerous spirits. At the very least, I would imagine that any who might disrespect the shaman in the course of doing so might receive harsh words from the boss; besides, I’m sure even he can still teach them a thing or two. And in the spirit of tensions in the homeland, I think there’s something interesting in accusing a beastfolk of retaining original kin loyalty.

    (It is just now occurring to me to see all of these systems and relationships in terms of this originally being Ma-Ha-Suchi’s retirement plan)

    And last of them is the wolfwoman general Six Day Storm. This one I actually do have the least to say about, beyond wondering if her polyandry is a practice unique to her within this society.

    Little sidebar on the beastfolk traits… I kind of feel as though the goatfolk get the slightly better deal, but perhaps I’m not giving due consideration to the benefits of Enhanced Scent and a two-dot hide.

    Now to finish it off with the neighbours who are surely the subjects of these raids and trade expeditions… huh, had not considered how the remaining separations between the bands would lead to both relations with the same group, as they do with the woodworking Vuloth; they trade carvings for petal-leather with the Flower Band, but are often subject to River Band raiding. I hadn’t considered that any of the Chante-Sa would intermarry with outsiders, but I suppose such groups would sometimes have to, and particularly the most external ones. Hell, considering some things I’ve read about African tribal trading practices… I’ve got to say, after having read everything about their layout, something about the idea of Immaculate missionaries coming to the Vuloth to offer assistance against Ma-Ha-Suchi’s supposed raiders really stings; somewhere between the manner in which they would reduce the River Band to nothing more than pawns of a Lunar, the misplaced blame for independently undertaken activities… but the worst is the fact that they have friendly relations with the Flower Band. I keep picturing some canny monk trying to keep the Vuloth onboard by excusing the Flower Band from blame for Ma-Ha-Suchi’s actions, real or imagined, and shrugging off any of their own protestations with condescension; some horrible scenario in which the petal traders are shoved to the side while their own spiritual kindred and their Undying Uncle are dragged into battle with a group to whom they have blood ties being galvanized by emissaries of the Realm.

    Hmm, nice for the jungle to have some overgrown, haunted ruins. Annnd a nice fortified trading outpost; the main thing I’m getting from that is the idea that it could make for a really neat staging ground for a chronicle, a gathering spot for adventurers and mercenaries and criminals and canny merchants, and all of the associated services, standing on a frontier with raiders from a Lunar dominion on one side and traffic flowing in and out of the opulent Southeast on another. I think I’m actually salivating a little bit.


    The Nameless Lair has done more than just a really good job of updating the features to a new milieu. The society it’s developed feels very rich and compelling, drawing a lot that is impressive and worthwhile from more accurate anthropology rather than just resorting to cheap stereotypes. It very nicely complements the dynamic shown in Ma-Ha-Suchi as shahan-ya; that side felt strong for its own internal dynamic of a dysfunctional family, and having these people and situations as the neighbours really strengthens that; alternatively, a story focused more on the Chante-Sa side of things, with the inhabitants of the Nameless Lair as the occasional weirdos who venture out on some business from Undying Uncle. Overall, very enjoyable to read.



    What Artifact is next on the docket… a set of moonsilver panpipes, what?! :0


    Made for an independent Lunar during the Shogunate, partially from the bones of a demon harpist… heh, I like the description of Nabira having succeeded in earning Alia’s gratitude and admiration with her craft skill and the gift of the instrument, but such things still didn’t bring her into the Silver Pact. I actually find something very moving in the image of her having actually saved her own precious home, and more besides, by playing the pipes; it’s not a thing I can read without imagining the kind of music that would be played. Still, poor Allia; being a demigod hero keenly aware of how many died in spite of the ones successfully saved must be a unique kind of survivor’s guilt. Even if one didn’t play out a story of seeking her out and trying to offer some comfort, I think there’s something sweet in the premise that the Silver Pact honours her by occasionally cycling her returned gift out to other independent Lunars.

    Right, it’s an instrument, so of course it wouldn’t have weapon stats… given its legend, seems very fitting that the fae are more susceptible to its performances. I’m going to suppose that the capacity to wield Silver Voiced Nightingale through it to gravely hurt them is the addition of a later wielder.

    Oh dang, first Evocation to empower music convincing an attacker, especially a fae, not to hurt the player or something they care about… general things for music to calm hostility or non-intrusively ward off the Fair Folk and the Wyld… but still a bit of edge in it to empower the Lunar magic for leading allies into battle (although still acknowledging its core, with driving them not just with rage for what they fight, but love for what they protect).

    A bit of calling upon the natural world to fight off the Raksha, if need be… being able to use a power to affect things cared about besides a claimed territory sounds very strong, and I like how it can lead to a Lunar losing Limit. And all ending in a capacity to transform a fae being into a more genial form, while still retaining its characteristic magic, but lacking a will to harm people. I think there can be interesting scenarios arising even from those who change back after a year, and not altogether vengeful, although there’s also something compelling about the idea that there are beings with Wyld powers out there who found they preferred being what the pipes turned them into, and wield their magic for more benign purposes.

    Can’t find much else to say about these, other than being really delighted to find such a pleasant little surprise amongst the Artifacts. This one is a strong contender for this book’s equivalent to Pyre of Legions.


    (Jeez, they’re really knocking it out of the park)

    I have approximate knowledge of many things.

                        Fuck I’m behind… yesterday was really weird, it messed me up quite a bit. Not sure what I’ll do to line it up.

                        Heh, might be no better mindset to approach Raksi’s domain. I’ve had some thoughts about her mannerism, I’ll be interested to see if they line up with Mahalanka.


                        For the introduction, apart from setting the scene with a picture of Sperimin’s characteristic spires, a like the description of Mahalanka as being in the former city’s shadow; even if it’s metaphorical (although I’m a bit excited at the prospect of a description that makes it feel more literal), it’s a nice image for how the nature of the place has changed over the Ages. Grounded a bit in the sidebar adjusting the backstory a bit to include Ma-Ha-Suchi, and attribute the current state of the place to inter-Lunar conflict rather than extended decay or deliberate design.

                        Mind, descriptions of Mahalanka as a living city have often felt a bit… scant to me, so I’d like to see something more comprehensive… Okay, good start with a big clearing having been made in a manner that causes the spires to stand out, and they’ve got some agriculture going. And yeah, the city sounds nice and functional; like, considering the approach before, for the core of it to be just a city is a significant step up in any case, that it’s inhabited by people with families and businesses and manner of dress just living their lives, and touched up a bit with some good infrastructure and the odd sorcerous benefit. I like the idea that inhabitants of different classes have a consistent family structure, and the idea that permission to undergo the trials to become apefolk generally require the approval of a family’s elder council. Plus the nod to the idea of a temple economy, operating at a larger scale than the Northern commune, but also existing alongside something more market-oriented.

                        Makes sense for a sorceress queen to have a proper tower, and for somebody of Raksi’s opulence to have two. I’m getting a good sense of the decaying nature of the place, which is an image I always find compelling, and I like a lot of the particulars of the descriptions; Raksi’s private laboratory, the extent of her court’s luxuries (particularly the private museum), the idea that the guest quarters contain abandoned places that they might wander into to explore. There’s a real tactile sense to this description, I can almost taste it. Oh, also that idea that the land leading up to the area has all been arranged in parks, that makes it sound like it would make for a very nice description of the journey up to it.

                        Between this and the Tangle, I’m getting an interesting sense that one of the most significant things about becoming beastfolk here is not in the actual benefits of the mutations themselves (although they’re probably nothing to sniff at, even in an enhanced experience of day to day life), but that they denote people whose dedication led them to go through severe trials to prove themselves before the reigning Lunar, and being remade a bit in that Lunar’s image is primarily a signifier of the duty and status conferred upon them for having done so. So it’s an interesting way to present this idea of a caste system that privileges the beastfolk, particularly for its contrast on how prior Editions would often default to with them (especially around Lunars). On its own, that they form Raksi’s bureaucracy continues to add to the sense of Mahalanka as a living city, and then you add their bestowed transformations and it creates a kind of Planet of the Apes vibe, a contrast between their appearance and intellectual sophistication.

                        Heh, I like the way that Raksi’s chief justice has the title of Dread Arbiter, and then the current office holder, Eskrin Fal, is somebody restrained and dignified and scholarly who finds serving under somebody as seemingly mercurial as the Queen of Fangs to be terribly frustrating. Jakun Qobal being somebody who takes the fact that they’re living in the ruin of an actual First Age archive as a basis for trying to draw upon ancient mystical social sciences and apply their principles to profoundly transform life in the city is really neat, and sounds like it could be a good example even to Solars (and I like how the opposition from his fellows isn’t just about being conservative, but argument that such lofty projects would disrupt cohesion enough to make them vulnerable to or inefficient against the Realm). And for the generalissimo Feroz Kutari to be scorned by his soldiers while he’s more of a staff officer concerned with the efficiency of logistics then an inspiring commander well, gives me a picture that these warriors don’t quite understand the Lunar that is reigning over them.

                        Does say something about the attempt to keep a military readiness by having the army sectioned off in their own city quarter. Ooh, an industrial district, that should be nice. I always like some breakdowns of manufacturing processes, so I’m happy that there’s some insight into the city’s factories and the goods they produce. It’s also nice to see that the physical prowess of the apefolk finds an application in that sector as well; feels a bit reminiscent of The Last Airbender. Honestly, a lot of Mahalanka feels more Ba Sing Se to me than some Dragon Blooded strongholds have. And I’m always a real sucker for people repurposing construction in a post-apocalyptic state.

                        The Roaring Market sounds good, but the scope of its commerce, particularly the reference to imports from the Dreaming Sea and the activities of mercenaries, does put me in mind of a certain shift of priorities. In the earlier days of Lunar discussion, Mahalanka was generally referred to in terms of a ruin that needed to be kept in a state of poor development so that the Realm Legions would be hard pressed to come in and seize it, while it’s treasures still served Raksi’s purpose. Here we have a very elaborate city-state that has trade contacts with neighbours, and the idea seems to be that it thrives without concern for Realm attack from a combination of distance, a military supremacy (and accommodating environment) that could contest the Realm’s finest, and the formidable prowess and reputation of its queen. I’m ultimately okay with that, partially because I think a lot of other Dominions are effectively covering the other image and so this place needs something to set it apart a bit, it serves the adjustments to Raksi’s character to allow more diverse engagement with her, and it is neat to get a snapshot of what an unmolested Lunar could achieve.

                        Also like that image of spoils from the official war parties being liquidated with the aid of civilian contractor merchants.

                        The Road of Idols is very neat, both as an image to illustrate the diverse extent of Raksi’s domain and for the snapshot of some of the colourful local gods (I like the cat god who defends granaries particularly). It’s also fun to use it as a contrast to the style of worship for Raksi herself, and that her own particularly brand of acting as an intermediary to the spirit world amounts to “don’t make me come over there”.

                        Does a good job of making the townlands surrounding the city sound lively and diverse and interesting; I think even some of the better parts of the game, even a bit of Autochthonia, can leave such places feeling like window dressing, but here they effectively convey being vital parts of the community and areas with their own distinct characteristics. It’s an especially good touch to give some of the local customs, triumphs and business sidelines (and I’ve got to hand it to himself, the description of Daughter-of-Fox means that his point about using one of those Intelligence Charms to bind an entity to some menial task indefinitely was right on the money).

                        Hmm, interesting that the Thousand Fangs Army consists of a mix of beastfolk, not just the ones empowered by Raksi and those descended from them. That the apefolk are empowered to punish others does speak to their caste stratification, and I find it rather fitting that they’re still wary of doing so under Raksi’s gaze. It’s a good illustration of the extent of her power for her to have multiple testing grounds, and the descriptions of what some of their trials pertain not only makes for good reading of her freaky sorcerous authority, but give a nice sense of how they’ll draw and empower people for a variety of social roles, and not merely the warriors. Also think it’s neat that, in contrast to Ma-Ha-Suchi, the ascended apefolk are expected to maintain certain family ties that help prevent the elevated caste from becoming too separated. And yeah, fun bit of internal stratification based on whether one’s state was something attained through trial or what one was born to, a kind of reversal of what is usual in depicting such internal divisions.

                        Yes, extending my renewed picture of Mahalanka is upgrading the Total Control Zone from just neighbouring tribes aware of and fearful to Raksi to a sizable empire under her sway. It’s interesting to picture how it seems to be held together less by uniform administration and more by religious awe to the sorcerer queen herself, and the manner in which they mythologize her (and how even their picture of her as a terror ascribes some socially reinforcing purpose to such). The description of how limited the process for petitioning Raksi for intercession is contrasts against my own assumption that such a thing could be accommodated regularly, but the picture of what it’s like when she actually descends to work magic to resolve some of their problems about fits what I would imagine, especially the point about her not requiring any kind of repayment for doing so; in my mind, this would still be less about altruism and more about Raksi revelling in the assertion of her own power to solve problems and exert dominance, whether those be blessing or bane to those that experience it. The actual feats themselves sound sufficiently sorcerous.

                        Good to be given a picture of some of the peoples within her Dominion… nice to see a callback to Dark Eyes’ action and influence with the point of there being a triad of tribes in conflict of one of them that is heavily engaged in slavery; it occurs to me that a lack of administrative uniformity means that there isn’t really much to stop Raksi’s vassals from fighting amongst one another, so long as it doesn’t escalate to a point that disrupts Mahalanka. The example of what happened to Jaciral of the Endless Branch sounds like another good instance of how Raksi’s random acts of kindness can effect profound transformations. I like the way she keeps the nature of her powers close to her chest to enhance her mystique, and that even in the Total Control Zone, a grateful beneficiary encouraging worship of Raksi can cause tensions with local gods.

                        Zanzannec has a lot of interesting connotations; being another elaborate city-state as something of a peer to Mahalanka, another instance of internal struggles being allowed to play out without central oversight, and the manner in which Raksi’s self-sustaining sorceries might shape local politics. Like, the idea of one of her favoured apemen overthrowing the government, and then being under threat from one of the deposed royalty having overcome a trial to become transformed, that’s really cool. Mind, I imagine his petition to Raksi would find that she favours a bit of a Michael Scott approach to management: “I don’t care, everybody reports to me; the rest, just sort out amongst yourselves”.

                        Heh, interesting to see how the Zone is not quite Totally Controlled, although if you have to flee to a Wyld Zone for refuge… The best thing I’m getting from this is the manner in which the place’s name informs the manner of it being part of the Wyld, how the mutations are diverse, but not merely random, they’re constrained within a theme defined by the place that has become contaminated. It’s a good example to draw from for any other Wyld Zones… also makes me really want to actually watch Annihilation properly.

                        By the way, dummy that I am, it’s taken me about this long to fully grok that Raksi’s Dominion is vastly beyond the scope of any other thus far. Hence why, apart from her three chief ministers, it’s just too big to do something like give examples of important personages. Really good for setting it apart.

                        Okay, last of all are the important neighbours. Let’s see, an appropriately ominous spirit court to give a bit of local colour in the form of the dangers of deep jungle, and emphasize the significance of Raksi’s reign by her having brokered protection deals for caravans. And giving them a sense of contact with the neighbouring empire of Ixcoatli through a buffering tributary, who have differing opinions on the sensibility of pissing off a mighty Lunar empress.


                        Mahalanka has advanced considerably, feeling like a more vibrant, living place in its own right rather than just the backdrop for Raksi’s depravities. There’s a whole surrounding world for any of her adherents to have activity in while being slightly apart from her (and indeed, lends a bit to the question of what might draw them in the first place), and I find it gives a lot to Raksi’s credibility as one of the mightiest Exalted of the Second Age to have her maintaining this outside the reach of the Realm. I also think it does a good job of setting itself apart from other imperial structures, both in terms of a form of unification and administration fitting to the environment, and the manner in which being governed by a sorcerer would be experienced by its people. I think this should really do a lot to help the credibility of the Lunar Exalted in the Time of Tumult.



                        Okay, nice to finally see some daiklaves. What might Phaessa and Deinon be capable of?


                        Another nice example of mysticism underlying the creation of an Artifact, particularly entailed by something therapeutic in the face of losses to the Usurpation. It’s interesting to see the idea of beings bound into daiklaves consensually, and the idea that a Lunar might involve themselves in what have to be the especially esoteric politics of spirit courts closest to Luna; it touches off of things that I really liked in Glories, some elements I enjoy in Werewolf: the Forsaken, and kind of makes me want them to hit the stretch goal that would expand upon that side of things. The short daiklaves themselves sound very good looking, with their crescent moon shape and being distinguished by the light they emit.

                        It'll also be nice to see something utilising the imagery of lunar cycles with a bit more sophistication than the core book’s Moonlit Huntress. Yes, has one of those great internal systems, this time based on the blades being waxing and waning (and the state between either). Hmm, an interplay between dazzling light and confounding shadow, I might be able to make use of some of this for my own purposes… Considering the conditions for shifting the cycle of the blades, I appreciate how resonant wielders get to start them off in a chosen state if they win Join Battle.

                        Oh, the Evocations themselves will partially include ways to shift that state, okay. I think that would make the process more dynamic in an actual fight, and it feels like a good way to incentivize style a bit; also seems like a good way of shedding some pesky anima levels. Right, so the general theme seems to be one in which the waxing warrior pursues the foe relentlessly and with an ecstatic terrifying presence, and the waning fighter falls back and into shadow, so it’s also a good complement to general Silver Pact emphasis on hit-and-run.

                        Non-Charm bonuses to Resolve or Guile are nice, as is the idea of being able to use those powers repeatedly for waxing or waning, although the real thing that stands out for that power is the unique element of the persisting consciousness within the swords being capable of communicating with and offering advice to resonant wielders. It adds a very distinguishing element to them, particularly from the sense that they’re more cooperative with the wielder, compared to the more servile Adorei and some more malefic Artifacts that might require some give and take. Oh, and it’s also only Essence 1, so characters can get in on that fun from the get go, nice choice.

                        Ohh, a really like how the blades provide a means of shooting off weird moon fire, with its own special properties, largely because I like Lunars to get a few energy attacks. Nothing much to say about the capstone other than liking how it expands upon that imagery, and the way that it gets awakened by earning the respect of the bound gods.

                        Yes, a nice little set of swords. I do like when paired weapons are given distinct and complementary powers, and this one touches off of a few things I think would be really nice to equip Lunars with. The powers themselves aren’t exactly moving, but I find some of the basis in their origin to be.

                                    Okay… I’m going to get out one more Dominion now, get a proper sleep, be up properly tomorrow, get the other three done early, and then blast through the Stamina Charms while my brother is playing Resident Evil 2. After a couple of revivals for old familiars, it will be nice to see something new (as far as I know) in the Eskari.


                                    Oh, we’ve been shifted down to the South. A group of savannah inhabiting, camel-riding nomads, descended from a destroyed city that rebelled against the Realm. I wonder if these ones will actually stick with that, rather than being attached to a new city. A bit of description of the logistics of their travel between water sources, including the limits in size of groups that can effectively move like that. Their marriage and household structure… matrilineal and polyandrous, permissive of same-sex marriage (but make a big deal out of deciding who’ll count as the matron). Ah, I was wondering how that might work in a nomadic society, but it makes sense for them if they have an attitude that women run the household (such as it is) and men tend to the animals, so it’s useful to have two husbands who can alternate their time between home and herd. I think the idea that the family of a woman who dies without suitable daughter to take over becomes absorbed into the family of the mama’s sister could make for some very distinct and possibly a bit fraught family situations (even if I would expect their families to not exactly be huge). I wonder what the attitude of men is to the children of the other co-husband.

                                    Wait a moment… I thought the Eskari sounded familiar before, but are they just straight up… no, that group from What Fire Has Wrought lived with a persistent fear of the Shogunate, not the Realm. This just happens to be a group in a very similar boat. Gonna put that to the side for the moment… I like how this is a nomadic society divided into multiple distinct groups, some of whom are semi-settled, it just helps to make them feel more real. Elevating the desire to rebuild their lost homeland into a holy mission, that’s a very intense societal premise, I think. The description of their cuisine resolves a question I had had from reading a summary about traditional Mongolian food just a few days ago and wondering where they got the ingredients for the dumplings from, and I feel rather silly for not having considered that they might buy it from farming neighbours. It generally sounds like they eat fairly well, some nice rich dairy foods.

                                    I was wondering where the Lunar might show up, and the description of the storytelling including “Zamisha stories” in which they’re pursued by a punishing devil hints to me that this is going to be a very different kind of Dominion, albeit of a kind I’ve speculated upon in the past; where the Lunar is less of a leader and more of a disaster that drives the nomad people to chase the Germanic tribes into Rome, so to speak. Also particularly like the idea that the more settled ones dedicate themselves to convert their oral histories into tapestries, just for the depth it gives to any of their decoration that might be seen around there.

                                    Hmm, I feel as though I’m missing a little something in the description of their religion… but I think the basic distinction is supposed to be that these gods are closer than is usual for a society, whether nomad or settled. I guess the idea is meant to be that both having originated in a relatively recent exile created rites structured around that closeness of their gods being exiles as well, and newly adopted spirits are drawn into that right. I especially like the description of how some of those gods have changed in nature to fit their new situation, although some of the descriptions of their original natures sound very cool; I like the idea of a marketplace god having lots of hands in particular, and I guess a feline god patronising granaries is just a thing now (it’s really clever, and it makes me wonder if any real society has ever drawn the connection).

                                    Okay, tell me more about this Smiling Zamisha and what she’s doing to these unfortunate people.

                                    Oof, she’s had a very rough time of it. Might just have had one of the worst early experiences of any Lunar who didn’t come from the Shogunate. Can’t exactly fault her for being hard-hearted verging on cruelty following all of that. Also makes the name ironic, and not in the fun way…

                                    Right, so the purpose of her torments is in trying to harden the Eskari in a similar manner to herself, hoping to turn their pastoral lifestyle to raiding and their fear into a martial hatred that will have them turning back upon the Realm. It’s a horrible sounding, if understandable, strategy, although I will note that the description suggests that she can wield her power to rely more upon rumour and innuendo that grows in the retelling, without needing to actually do half of the horrors attributed to her. As I thought, this method allows her to be more flexible in relating to this Dominion; she can pop in occasionally to keep her myth alive and continue refining and cultivating it to her purpose, and freely depart to engage in other business elsewhere. I imagine that such a thing can be fairly common as Dominions go (come to think of it, True Voice was rather similar).

                                    What can I say about the system of government… the gyula, the overall leader of the people, doesn’t sound like a very powerful, which I think is good, I think it’s worthwhile to have societies in which the… local equivalent to a head of state does not have extensive powers while functioning as normal, and not because there’s a lot of resistance to their authority. Like, they’re a widely diffuse people, of course they won’t have a lot of power in a centralised leader. It mostly just reminds me of things I’ve read about old Irish kings. But still not a merely ceremonial leader, since their attitude concerning the Realm (and perhaps other enemies) gives a unifying force something to do.

                                    But what’s really interesting about the system are the husband-advisors, the kundu; utilising their polyamory as a basis for creating an intertwining network of familial connection and diplomatic communication, which might ultimately knit them together more closely than their decentralised structure might imply. I think navigating that system of overlapping marriages would make for some especially engaging and unique scenes for Socialize. I also like how the gyulas tending to favour kundus with priest, shaman and thaumaturge skills gives Zamisha a strong avenue to influence the society, as she can present herself in the manner of a divinity to them in particular, and they get to transmit their insights up and down the nominal chain of command. Plus the way that she can distribute her Moon Marks to sometimes influence the selection of the gyula, even contrary to traditional norms.

                                    Like the sidebar describing the evolution of the institution, both for giving a sense on how the Lunar’s influence has shaped the society to fit her purposes (and might continue to do so in the future), as well as their internal trends. It’s just good to have anything that keeps these societies from feeling too static across centuries.

                                    Good, lay out some of the influences in the clans… I think it’s really fun to give the Dzhenifa clan a custom that traces back to association with the Varang states, but they would refuse to acknowledge such a kinship over hatred of the Varangians siding with the Realm. Still, I think it’s a missed opportunity to not at least say what the omen behind matriarch Olanka’s name might be; do like her more spiritually driven agenda, though. The tutelary god, Glass Razor, is just okay. While the Leila military raids and history of blood feud with the Realm sounds like a good form for some first contact with the Eskari to take. I like how those ones have an emphasis on youthful vigour and a devil-may care attitude, too many fantasy societies place too much emphasis on the older. I think that’s embodied well in the idea that Gyula Zita is a younger woman than the norm, and considering Zamisha’s usual relationship with the Eskari, find some fascinating implications to the idea of her having acted as a mentor to the warrior chieftain. That the Leila matriarch schemes to replace the current gyula with her own daughter calls some of the Harkonnen intrigues from Dune to my mind; I just love those dynastic schemes based on advancing the position of your heirs rather than yourself. Ohh, and First Flint is grand in being a Bloody Hand who has advanced in the world by having a higher position through the esteem of an associated tribe. I want more instances like that in the game.

                                    For the Razhiin, it would be good enough to have a clan that acknowledges and is tolerant of intermarriage with other societies, but I think that making it a practice intended to extend their own power by assimilating groups on the whole takes them a step beyond. It does an especially huge part to keep the Eskari from feeling homogenous with that idea that many of their own customs have changed as a result of that assimilation, even if the result is that they’re derided as lacking the proper identity and pride from a lot of their fellows. With the gyulas having a larger number of husbands than the norm, I was wondering how they would relate to one another, so I’m glad to see the idea that the Razhiin kundu is engaged in a relationship with one of his fellow husbands, suggesting the possibility that their poly marriages could become even more crossed over. I’m amused by the irony of their own god having encouraged their intermarriage with other people, but being unhappy with the more diverse worship that brings with it.

                                    Just taking a peek ahead… normally I would object to detailing all seven clans, but in this case I’m giving it a pass on two grounds. One is that, since the setup of the Eskari is that they have relatively little, it seems to me that such writing would be necessary to fill them out, and saves on elaboration on minute details. The other is that I’m really liking the flexibility in these Dominion write-ups, how they’re not strictly bound by a consistent template, both because it makes them more engaging to read when you’re going through a lot of them, and because it emphasizes the diversity that they represent, both in their own right and how Lunars might interact with and utilize them.

                                    So, next ones, the Szonia, a more isolationist and puritanical group. Ohh, the idea that they’ve ranged far enough away that their Realm enmity has become transferred to Prasad is very clever. And then it goes and turns some of the assumptions of that on its head with the idea that matriarch Idoska is engaged in peace negotiations with a Prasadi tributary because they’ve come off a bit worse for wear in their conflict, helping to keep the clans from just being a stereotype, emphasizing that they can be adaptive and pragmatic in response to circumstances. Ah, I was wondering how their traditionalism would play off of Zamisha’s influence, and think that some really good conflict could come from the fact that she’s less of an apparent presence to them and they’re not happy with how she’s influencing the other six.

                                    I think there’s something very fun in the idea that the Tezelyke preserve knowledge of a bunch of crafts and practices for the time in which they might reclaim a metropolitan homeland. I think the idea of maintaining rites for dead gods is an especially compelling picture of an exiled people, and sand paintings of the lost city feel like they would be a really great application for craft projects and something to make a big deal out of unveilings. Does logically sound like a group in which revanchism (i.e. revenge as an ideology) would surge up. Heh, I love the minute (but probably meaningfully felt) distinctions between traditionalists supporting a gyula trying to conquer a new Eskaridam, and revanchists pushing for reclamation of an old one. But… ohohoho, that’s great, that’s inspired, a young firebrand passing herself off as being driven by visions from the lost patron god of their city, who is in actual fact the Exigent of a self-sacrificed mirage god who empowered somebody for vengeance against the Realm for destroying their cult. That’s a detail I tip my hat to. And oh dear, even their poor tutelary god believes that she’s getting real visions, and keeps trying to find a trace of the dead city father. This could either be a basis for the clan’s ascendancy or a recipe for absolute disaster, and I am here for it.

                                    You’ve got the Varoniikh, whose nomadism just consists of them moving back and forth between high- and lowland with the seasons, remain in fortified camps otherwise, and are close enough to the Varang for their to be consistent conflict between them. They maintain raiding distance to a major pass through the Summer Mountains and all. I think there’s a good format for their internal tensions regarding the question of whether or not they’re letting themselves become too aggressive against an enemy that outguns them (Zamisha’s influence is probably not helping), and it feels like a clan that is going to need some major changes to survive in the long term. I like how the granary god Damet is adapted to this situation, particularly drawing wildcats in to assist in raids north.

                                    And finally, the Vevyehn, largely settled down in riverside villages and with a willingness to accommodate foreigners. Just for a brief aside, I’ve not fully considered how some of the others might extend nomadism to temporary habitation in other people’s towns, but that seems like it would fit for some of them (especially the Razhiin; it seems to me that a lot of those marriages would occur while living in a town, and when they leave their spouses go off with them, especially if children are involved). But yeah, the inverse of that is a group who are themselves setting up the towns in which others might start settling down. Let’s see, they’ve got a claim to strength and wealth with the benefit of iron deposits… yeah, considering the significance the people place in reclaiming Eskaridam, it makes sense to include one of them who’ll say “look guys, we found it, let’s all just settle down here and build a new life”. I’m glad how the basis for rejecting that isn’t purely from other clans having contrary ideas of what Eskaridam entails (although I do love the paradox inherent to the stance that recovering the homeland from which they were exiled into nomadism is incompatible with ceasing to be nomadic), but there are also practical concerns about the vulnerability of the new towns to other raiders, and competition from the Guild. The gyula might not be a ceremonial leader, but I find it fitting that the matriarch of the clan that has started settling into townships would be, even if she is presented as having brought the town governments around to the idea that they need to oppose the gyula’s agenda as a threat to their own stability. Ahh, and of course the former marketplace god would be the patron of people that would take up this position.

                                    Okay, the neighbours. There’s already so much to the Eskari, spread over multiple fronts, that it seems there’s less need for a dedicated section here, so I think it’s the best call to focus on two of their most iconic satellites. First is giving a name to the Varang state that betrayed them, Haqad, with their basis as a source of Eskari animosity solidified with the point that they were enriched by possessions of the razed city being handed over to them. That they’re protected by sizable military resources really feels like something that would fill the exiled people with disgust at their comfort and wealth, and a sense of hateful impotence in the face of them. It really sounds like any Exalt or other power who could open the city to Eskari vengeance would effectively win the loyalty of the clans as a whole (if not all of the interest groups within them).

                                    The Kazhur have been mentioned a lot, especially as intermarrying the Razhiin (to the point of introducing a new major god to worship) and being subject to a lot of raids from the Szonia, so it’s a good choice to elaborate on them a bit. I wouldn’t have expected them to also be a raiding culture (the idea of raiding nomadic raiders makes sense, and is a thing I’ve often read about, but doesn’t quite feel intuitive to me, which feels like a very strange hang-up). Mind, considering how they have a custom of providing recompense when confronted by people that they have stolen from, it actually sounds a lot more like a kind of trading ritual that may have evolved out of a purer raiding practice or is an adaptation to their neighbours to match commercialism with shows of military strength. Either way, it’s a very unique kind of practice, but one I could believe having ever existed in some form in reality, so I’m glad that it’s been included here.

                                    And finally, the Sands Where No Man Walks, a kind of haunted wasteland which feels very reminiscent to me personally of times when I imagined adapting the Gerudo from Legend of Zelda (as they were in Ocarina of Time, as I didn’t yet have an inkling of their great portrayal in Breath of the Wild) to the setting. It’s a more interesting sounding shadowland than the norm, with a very compelling icon in the form of that smoke tree and the idea of it unleashing all kind of elaborate horrors of the dead into the world. I particularly like the idea of a people responding to the threat of proximity to such a thing by meeting it head on; the funeral practices of sending in the remains of the dead to add the ghosts of their people to help combat the horrors would be grand enough, but the idea that the living would sometimes brave the wastes to save their own tormented ancestors is a really great image, really lends a lot of esteem to the people.

                                    I think nomadic cultures have often been underserved in Exalted, so it’s wonderful to see one given such carefully designed detail here. I think the best part about the Eskari is their diversity; they still feel like a consistent people with a common background, while also feeling like a very real people from how they’re covering lots of different angles and have many different ideas. I find their backstory with the Varang and the Realm to be compelling, and really enjoy them showing one of the darker sides to the Silver Pact and the concept of Lunar Dominions, even while those things don’t completely overwhelm the portrayal.



                                    All right, up to the four-dot Artifacts, starting off with the slayer khatars given the not in the least bit ominous name of Ichneumon Blades.


                                    Yeesh, these things were born from an ill-fated encounter with Balor himself. It would be bad enough for the weapons to have their presumed nature in their own right, but I love the sense of transgression inherent to the idea that they’re successors to the spirit of a weapon held in a higher esteem; that’s especially fun for transgression as a theme of Lunars and their material, here turned against the will and ideal of the Lunar who put them together trying to salvage something more noble. That they bear a resemblance to ovipositors is a good look for them, and it of course makes sense that there would be a lot of Lunars less reserved (to say the least) to harness their dreadful nature.

                                    Speciality for piercing attacks, annnd yes, right off the bat, the expected quality of them partially shattering to leave fragments that burrow within the bodies of enemies. The capacity to partially ignore Hardness sounds like a good way to start the battle out with this, and then capitalise on the results. Also like how those embedded splinters require surgical removal, rather than being sustained by Essence. And the Resonant effect seems to overall say that the wielder should use them for piercing without reservation.

                                    Wouldn’t have expected a power for the splinters to give things like the position or intentions of the victim away. Ah jeez, I thought it was going to be focused on messing up the body, but the idea that those larval fragments can also assault the mind… and with a fairly diverse application of mental illnesses. The Icheneumon Blades don’t seem to want their wielders to fight to the death, but to the pain; stab enough to leave lots of fragments, and then leave to let them work their horrific magic, that will also call the wielder back to see and further their handiwork. I’ve been wanting a few dread weapons, and this one is living up to that beautifully.

                                    But that’s all just at Essence 1, it gets a lot more overt the next step up when the damn things start erupting as jagged masses that further maim and hook themselves in to become more difficult to extract. And then drag the body around as a meat puppet if it should die while fragments are embedded in it. Yes, I think I prefer this Artifact as a Lunar method for creating zombies than some of the Ink Monkeys Charms were. And the description of Ravening Swarm Consumption is genuinely uncomfortable to read, particularly imagining those erupted splinters scything through a person’s flesh as they make their way to the vital organs. Yes, this Artifact is recalling the memory of having ticks embedded and removed, in all of the best ways.


                                    So the Ichneumon Blades get the level 4s off to a good start, conveying… not so much a fighting style as a process for assault and torment, feeling effectively cruel and grim. I think it has room for a bit of expansion seeing as it only goes to Essence 3, but I’m not critical of that, and actually think there are some merits to an avenue for its greater powers to fall to the imagination of players; it certainly sets a good example for itself.
