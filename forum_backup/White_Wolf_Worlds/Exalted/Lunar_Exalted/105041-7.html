                Originally posted by TheSarcasticSage View Post
                Look... I get that you're upset that the Yozi invented sorcery.
                I'm upset that 2e retconned the Yozi into inventing Sorcery. There's an extremely huge difference.

                (Again, it's the difference between saying "I'm annoyed that this writer retconned Doctor Strange into having his powers derived from Galactus" and "I'm annoyed that the Silver Surfer uses the power of Galactus.")

                it doesn't matter where Sorcery comes from. The Yozi have absolutely no ability to influence what you can learn, or do, with sorcery. They cannot determine how powerful of a sorcerer you can become, they cannot stop you from casting any spells, nor can they influence or taint the results. Your complaint is like bitching about the fact that you just found out that the Chinese invented gun powder before the Europeans. It's an interesting bit of trivia, but ultimately that fact is unimportant to our modern day understanding of firearms.
                It absolutely does matter, for the same reason it matters that Solars were once kings in a vanished age, or for the same reason it matters that the Sidereals held a great prophecy and changed the course of Creation based on what it said. These things define the character you're playing, even if they don't have direct mechanical impact.

                The Usurpation fundamentally defines what it means to be a Sidereal or a Dragon-Blooded, even if it took place 1500 years ago. I mean, you can ignore it, personally -- I'm sure plenty of Sidereals do; they have a lot of things to worry about -- but it's still a huge part of the setting's definition of them, and if you play a game it's likely to come up eventually. The First Age defines what it means to be a Solar -- again, you can totally have your Solar ignore it, but it fundamentally shapes the place of Solars in the setting.

                The origins of Sorcery (if the game is going to specify its origins) fundamentally define what it means to be a Sorcerer in a similar sense. It defines what Sorcery is, and through that, it defines a huge part of how the game approaches a certain kind of magic (and an entire genre of magic.) While I think that the story of Brigid was unnecessary, 1e's origin story for Sorcery was still basically broad enough and mysterious enough to support what I wanted. 2e's retconned Yozi-centric version of it was not. It makes the statement that all the vastly weird mystery of the supernatural that Sorcery gives you access to comes down to "lol secrets of the Yozis." It says that when you initiate into Sorcery and begin exploring the cosmic sorcerous truths of the universe, you're just sorting through regurgitated Yozi-dreck. It says that, fundamentally -- speaking in the abstract here, in a sort of zoomed-out view of what Sorcery means --that the supernatural truth of Exalted is the Yozis, that if you boil down the deep secret understanding of the universe you get Malfeas' brainfarts.

                Exalted deserves better than that. Sorcery deserves better than that. The Yozis are vast and terrible, but the secret revealed supernatural truth of the universe deserves to be grander and broader and deeper than them. The Sorcerer needs to stand on its own as an archetype, and the fragments of knowledge a sorcerer stitches together into their spells should hint at a deeper, more holistic truth than just being the secrets of some mere has-been titans they defeated thousands of years ago.

                Demon-summoning should always be tempting, mind; the powers of Hell are totally an important part part of the vast supernatural tapestry that makes up Exalted, and therefore one of the many sources Sorcery should draw on. But it should just be one of many, and when you specify the lords of Hell as the ultimate origin by which Exalted learned the secrets of Sorcery, you're fundamentally changing the definition of what it means to be a sorcerer. If you don't see it, you might compare it to Necromancy -- unlike Sorcery, Necromancy did have an origin in 1e that explicitly said that it was the secrets of fallen titans. That was written in for a specific purpose -- to cast a specific sort of shadow over Necromancy, to give players an unsettling thrill when they draw on its spells, knowing that they're using forbidden powers ripped from the minds of dead gods. I don't know whether that's necessary for Necromancy in 3e, but it was distinct and served a particular purpose for Necromancy; Sorcery's origin, by comparison, was enigmatic, with only an allegorical myth of a supernatural journey, deliberately designed to be open to many interpretations.

                (Some people have pointed out that Brigid's story seems implausible in some respects; on reflection, I think that this was somewhat the point. Many real-life mystical or mythological traditions have that sort of allegorical journey in them -- I don't think it was meant to be read as literal in any respect.)

                Again, I don't think there's any danger that 3e will repeat that, because the authors have clearly indicated that they intend to tone down the Yozi-centric stuff that happened in late 2e, and 2e's retcon to the origin of Sorcery was one of the stupidest examples of that. I'm just arguing that it deserves to be tossed down the memory hole of dumb 2e shit that seemed like a good idea at the time but which we now blissfully pretend never happened. If you don't care, that's fine; I'm sure lots of people would say "why do you care so much if some writer declared that Doctor Strange's powers are all derived from Galactus?" I mean, yeah, it's a kinda geeky thing to care about.

                It's still stupid, though. To me, at least, it cuts at the core of what I consider to be cool about being a sorcerer, removing a huge part of the mysticism that makes them what they are and replacing it with fucking Galactus.
