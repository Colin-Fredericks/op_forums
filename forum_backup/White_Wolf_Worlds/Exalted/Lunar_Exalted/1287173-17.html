    Okay, finally up bright-eyed and bushy tailed and eager to try and push through a whole lot of this. The Bronze Tide is a more unusual name for a Dominion, so it has my immediate attention.



    Heh, first impression I got from reading the introduction was that those bells being referred to with the city of Steep were made of bronze, and there was going to be something magical about them that would ward off the Wyld. But nah, this is introducing the Dominion by way of first bringing up a city that is under an apparently unstoppable assault, which is quite a tone to set. I think the detail here most intriguing for me to find a follow up on is… the polished armour being worn by the Bronze Tide raiders.

    Yes, as I suspected from the introduction being set at a coastal scene, this is a subject of the setting’s far Southwest, another more newly developed part of the world. In particular the Cinder Isles, which puts it very far from the usual sphere of the Realm. The description of the Isles being home to scores of nations makes me want to look back at them on the map and get a sense of what those nations might entail, their possible scope and relations, but. The big thing I take from it, though, is the point of a force originating in its eastern quarter and moving west, which makes me wonder if the Bronze Tide is necessarily concerned with the Realm at all.

    Okay, it’s setting the scene of the historical background to the Isles; kind of sounds like another instance of a place beyond the Realm’s hegemony being subject to continuous tumults, albeit with seemingly more turnover and scattered diversity than what might be found by the Dreaming Sea. It feels like a very different environment, both in terms of climate and geography and the local power dynamics (kind of reminiscent of the Indian Ocean or South China Sea, things that the Western Archipelagos never quite covered) in which to set games, and even if it seems to be driving at the rise of the Bronze Tide putting a lot of these events in the past, it can give its own sense of background and legacy, a basis for old feuds and alliances and lost treasures that might be sought out and utilised in a Time of Tumult game.

    So that’s a lot to get from the picture of the region that the Dominion is crashing into (I’m particularly liking the idea that a lot of the dominant coastal cities are being affected by migrations that the new growing power is pushing up towards them), but still need to get to the subject in focus… which still seems to be being held off for a bit, as it’s also giving us a picture of what the mainland further south has been doing with greater proximity to the Wyld. I’m not complaining, but this write-up is giving me the impression of something that would have been written very differently had the book order been something else, and the new Southwest had been introduced elsewhere, but that’s just a stray observation. What matters to me here is that the question of people living very close to the edge of the world is something I think has never been given much attention before, seeming to be primarily conveyed and conceived on in terms of low population density, low infrastructure people in a hunter-gatherer lifestyle, relating to the Wyld largely in terms of being preyed upon, subjugated or using it in shamanistic rites. So it’s interesting to get a more in-depth look presuming a greater variety of social formats, of what Southeast Asian maritime societies would be like if they were situated at the literal edge of the world from which strange beauties and horrors emerge.

    Oh, actually jumped the gun a bit; it’s more like being referenced before getting to our subject, one Spear-Empress Bhadri, whom I’m guessing to be the Lunar in this scenario (unless she’s a raksha queen that the Dominion is going to be raised against). Was this character mentioned earlier in the book? There are some judgements I’ll defer until I have more of the facts, but for the time being I think it’s an effectively striking picture of people facing the threat of a major force from the Wyld, which feels distinct from other instances in this and other books of a regional status quo being overturned by more mundane conquerors. The descriptions of what slaves are used for and how the advance is being aided by strange vessels and crafts gives it that added texture of the Wyld.

    Ah, okay, finally comes to the point; that the Bronze Tide is the poetic name for the manner in which the coastal states have managed to unify in the face of mass migrations and an oncoming Wyld assault and are being driven out for their own conquest of the Cinder Isles. As an aside, I’ll note the concept of Yu-Shan ending up as a refuge for Terrestrial gods driven from their home sanctums, which isn’t something I’ve imagined before but now find to have strong possibilities in characterising the divine city.

    Okay, now the whole thing is coming together; Bhadri is a raksha empress of unusual power who is driving a new conquest in her quarter, and this Lukha Palash is a relatively new Lunar, Chosen as a result of his actions as a charismatic leader in this crisis, whose focus is on resistance to her rather than anything to do with the Realm, with the Bronze Tide being his per project to save the people from the Wyld. The relative shortness of his introductory section is giving me a sense of what sets this Dominion apart; one in which the Exalt is an active driving force, visible and commanding, while remaining only a singular element of a much bigger picture (emphasized by the fact that he can’t govern alone and needs the assistance of all the newly united princes). That’s good for two major reasons, both to provide a contrasting example to the likes of Mahalanka or Luthe where the openly commanding Lunar is somebody ancient and nigh unassailable, and it gives a nice example of how characters of the type that players could be running might conduct themselves in such a scenario.

    I would have liked a description of the culture focused on an element of disparate elements being pushed together for the first time, but the idea of a common standard owed to a shared history as provinces of a greater empire has its own charms (and conveniences). That one of the commonalities of fashion is widely wearing caps helps add to the sense of the area’s distinctiveness, and I do like the details of local variations. Likewise, the idea that even utilitarian metalwork is embellished with culturally significant imagery furthers the area having a unique aesthetic, something that I think could have an advantage to future fiction or games in which one could establish the location just by describing elements of everyday life. There are some nicely thought out economic implications to the forced migration, such as the significance of being able to keep large herds of higher value animals growing as a status symbol, and I think it ends up having enough of a reference to the people still being highly fractitious, even in the face of their shared crisis and an Exalted leader, to give the sense that this is all still something very new and unstable. That further distinguishes it as a Dominion; none of the others have existed in this state of crisis, with the challenge of holding together a diverse coalition, and I think it hints a bit at some of the limits of the format; I can’t shake the feeling that Lukha Palash is in for a bit of a tragedy (barring getting some extra muscle from some player characters wanting to go help out).
    Mind, even his success constitutes a major tragedy for some neighbouring peoples, but I suspect he cares less about them.

    All right, being given a breakdown of what the Bronze Tide is up to. So basically, their intention is to generate an atmosphere of terror, alternating between grand displays of martial fury as their ships approach island cities in the hopes of cowing them into surrender, and if made to fight destroying infrastructure that can’t be taken with them (i.e. basically everything but ships) and slaughtering fighters and leaders, enslaving people with valuable skills, while forcing inhabitants to flee into neighbouring islands to keep building up their own legend, in the hopes of ultimately allowing them to conquer without the need to fight. It strikes me as a strategy that would make a lot of sense to the people involved, driven by their desperation and perhaps a need to keep a constant forward momentum lest their coalition fall apart, and is absolutely terrible in the long run, both for the enduring traumas it’s likely to inflict and probably wasteful in a manner that presents even the conquerors with problems. And I like that, I like that sense of fallibility and people being driven by powerful emotions and immediate exigences, as well as a picture of growing crisis that really feels like it needs some interventions, or at least powerful added voices (one could always play other Exalted arising from within it).

    Ah, okay; where the culture section was giving some of the aesthetic background, the section titled Life Among the Tides is getting into the meat of tackling the subject of how all of these newly connected disparate peoples are struggling to function together. I think it does a very good job of presenting this not as an empire or even a confederation; it’s an alliance of convenience created in the most thoroughly ad hoc circumstances, and there’s not only been insufficient time to formalise a lot of the components through diplomacy, the manner in which they’ve been pushed together likely precludes effectively doing so in the future. That Lukha Palash is trying to keep a sense of unity through moving between the numerous cultures and equally celebrating their customs, to generate a sense that none is supreme over the others so they can remain cooperative, that is a detail I really like as making his leadership feel not just very distinctly Lunar, but distinctly Changing Moon, and having seen the Charms for that Caste gives it a very firm sense of credibility. That’s really good social and personal characterisation there. Finally I like the descriptions of how the people have prioritised adopting new religious practices, such as the offerings of sweet and pure liquids in hopes of making good impressions on new gods they have to ingratiate themselves to, as well as how those gods themselves are exploiting the opportunities (the idea of gods appropriating cults by taking on the names of deposed, or possibly killed, gods is absolutely wonderful and a manner for bringing in that element of real life cult ascendancy that I’ve never considered before but is just so elegant; hell, it makes me picture an Exalt doing the same, something that feels particularly fitting for Lunars who can outright make themselves resemble, in form and in power, the gods that they slay). Combine it all with references to how they’re handling cultural elements acquired from subjugated isle peoples, and I think this section validates the entirety of the Bronze Tide on its own.

    Okay, I was ready to think the sidebar describing a few of the prominent component cultures of the Tide was a good idea just to give a deeper sense of some of the parts, but giving them all some particular hangup with their new situation was really inspired. The particulars of each are pretty great and all; you’ve got the Lunar’s native culture, whose princes are annoyed at a sense that their authority has been usurped by an Exalt, a group of people that are being forced into an entirely unfamiliar geographic environment and have had their traditional pantheon completely destroyed, forcing them to try and rebuild a new one from scratch, a society that is not only resistant to external authority on principle but has an old grudge against beastfolk and feels an anxiety about being governed by a Lunar, and the historical rivals to Lukha’s people, who for all of his attempts at maintaining togetherness and a sense of egality, are still bothered that a member of that culture had to be the one Chosen. All of that is delightful stuff for how, with limited wordcount, it gets across the sense of these being real people, that the plights of the parts are distinct from the plight of the whole and may prove to be major dealbreakers, and that even somebody Exalted can have a lot of hassle to deal with from various people’s cultural baggage. If there’s any mercy in Heaven, somewhere out there Lukha will have an Eclipse Caste mate waiting for him, with whom he might be able to get some extra oomph to prevent these people from falling apart. I especially like the points about how being Exalted does not make you transcend native culture, and can actually prove a major liability even within your own culture.

    And finally, in place of the usual sections on prominent individuals and neighbours (the latter of which is a bit redundant when the whole point is that basically all of the neighbours have been invited into the house), there’s a description of the council of leaders that Lukha has surrounded himself with to try and govern this mess. First off I just want to say that I like the details given to what functions the council actually performs, since I like anything that helps me to envision the actual functions of a government.

    Ah, of course there’d be another Lunar here; somehow, despite him having been described as a Changing Moon from the very start and my own frequent references to him, imagining Lukha’s isolate has kept me thinking of him as somebody without tattoos, so it helps to solidify that by saying that he’s got one other Lunar sent as a Silver Pact emissary to back him up. I like how, even while Jural Three-Trees has been sufficiently won over by Lukha’s charms that he’s opted to remain and help him out, he maintains a bit of his own agenda concerning service to his shahan-ya (and even one that might incidentally help out the Bronze Tide). Plus the general fun of a Lunar venturing out with the intention of dealing with some big bad, and on the threshold of its lair worrying that it might be more than he can handle.

    It makes sense that there might be a Dragon Blood somewhere in that leadership, but Leja’s story sounds like an effective microcosm of the whole human tragedy underlying this situation. I think her trauma is a compelling element to reinforce the basic humanity of the Exalted, and the description of her nightmares is not only ominous in its own right, but gives an intriguing sense of what the powers of the Fair Folk might be; more than the possibilities of them actually being able to create such changeling creatures, or even assault somebody with such twisting of their dreams, is the idea that when it comes to the raksha, it might be hard to tell which one they’ve done. I’m suddenly struck with a hope that one of their major themes will be uncertainty about what is the reality of a situation; it makes me think of the comic where a raksha confronts that Solar from Whitewall with a prophecy from some of his manikins, and while the Solar dismisses the prophecy as a product of the fae’s narcissism, the noble assures his creatures that the point is that, having heard it, the Solar will be haunted by the possibility that it contains truths.

    But that’s mostly getting off track; let’s see if any of these councillors are mortal. Yes, Sufek the bard looks like a very useful addition; somebody more stable and native to help Lukha navigate unfamiliar matters of state, as well as being a propagandist who can work on the subject of keeping the people together and countermanding threats to Lukha’s actions and authority at the end of a pen rather than a spear. I know I keep harping on the book’s portrayal of soft power, but it’s a subject that became of interest to me in the game’s interim period, and I’ve been really glad with how this one has been emphasizing it.

    And an elderly strategist Parav, to round out youthful fervour with some practiced caution, to help keep the Tide from overstretching itself, while also benefiting from a lifetime of contacts across the membership if this new coalition.

    Ah, I had actually misread my little peak ahead, there is still a reference to some of the Bronze Tide’s neighbours. There’s a description of one of the city-states of the Cinder Isle, Argidos, that is in the path for conquest, desperately seeking allies and finding none going because it has a history of rapacious warfare itself and using mercenaries hired from one of the former states of the Tide against its neighbours, and they’re happy to leave it to their mercies. It’s a short description, but I find something very sad about it, just from the picture I’m getting of a once proud and confident edifice being left in absolute terror for its future, something that’s hard not to at least get a twinge from my sympathies. Mind, it also seems like ripe territory for a desperation breeding willingness to treat with some darker powers.

    There’s a pair of literally connected islands, Melanthes and Ipera, presenting a further complication for the policies of the new coalition, as they’ve historically had cordial and dynastic ties to one of the nations, who may prove unable to protect the old allies from the pressures and needs of the new one.

    Okay, yeah, I should have expected that the neighbours section would provide some of the details on the Cinder Isles that are dealing with the Bronze Tide. Next is Tenai, one that managed to effectively fight off the Tide’s assault, albeit with great human cost and the creation of a shadowland, left in the unenviable position of trying to get their lives back in order following that ordeal, remaining terrified that the invaders might come back for them, and if I’m reading correctly, distracted from the fact that an invasion force of Fair Folk is still on the move, and might be now closer to them than the Bronze Tide is.

    Then there’s the island Sayfar, home to the capital of an empire among the Isle that has alternately been able to stand against the Bronze Tide, and been fractured a fair bit by some of their possessions using the opportunity to rebel, only for the new invader to sweep them up. They also have an interesting local mythology and ecstatic religious practices in an affinity for the sea. I like these ones for presenting the idea that the Bronze Tide aren’t the only game in town, even if they’re currently acting at a bit of a disadvantage.

    And finally, a sidebar covering some of the nature of Spear-Empress Bhadri herself, and the court that accompanies her. Bhadri is relayed as a creature of violence and an unrelenting hunt, given such iconography as the power to summon legions from the earth, having a pet bird that mutilates the souls of fallen enemies, and a spear of ash and sapphire to cause those it’s cast at to wither. She’s referred to as having twelve companions, of which we get descriptions for two; Zalak of the Wave-Cutter Chariot, who rides a mare of living fog wields a triple-bladed daiklave of ivory and pearl that summons sea monsters, and is inherently fickle, capricious and given to bouts of wanderlust that can even distract her from pursuing the Bronze Tide, held to the course by a bound oath of service from Bhadri having beaten her in a duel; and Gelyb the Song of Fire, a poet-blacksmith driven by a wish to burn away ugliness and impurity, a creature of envy and vainglory paradoxically matched with a meticulousness in pursuit of his actions (which to him are a craft), who creates the camps for his fellows at rest by singing up great buildings from cold fire and fragrant smoke, and incinerates ships with fists wreathed in crimson fire.

    Okay, so this picture of the raksha nobles is super exciting, on a few fronts. For one thing, the pattern in the descriptions; that a noble consists of an iconic pursuit in its existence, around which it has a characteristic power and panoply, utilizing metaphorical or broadly applied aesthetics, so you can give them visually distinctive and very unusual expressions while still being tied to a coherent idea. Of the three, I think Gelyb’s is the most expressive of this, while Zalak’s has the most flair. Then there’s the implications of how they relate to one another, which sounds like it’s going to consolidate and streamline a lot of the old shaping combat functions down to the basic idea of “raksha can have interpersonal conflicts from which they wring binding oaths to determine their hierarchy”. Both of those together feel like they’re being presented as things the raksha would have in place of a genuine personality or real inner life. And finally the sense that the force assaulting the states of the Bronze Tide is not really an army as such, but just twelve nobles (albeit possibly among the stronger of their kind); armies are employed, but they seem likely to mostly be the conjurations of several of the nobles involved. If they can pursue this idea to a full expression, with some mechanics that might live up to it (reminding that I need to look back at the raksha in Adversaries), I think it might make the Fair Folk the most interesting and threatening that they’ve ever been, and ticking a lot of the boxes that I’ve always wanted from and struggled to fit even their best mechanics to.



    The Bronze Tide has kind of shot up there for being one of the most interesting Dominions yet. On the front concerning the Lunars specifically, it hits a number of good marks; showing an activity taking place in a sphere far removed from and unconcerned with the Realm, showing the big endeavours of a much younger Lunar so that Dominions don’t feel proprietary to ones at least a few centuries old, and really feeling like it’s being managed by the Lunar in a way that plays to their strengths besides subterfuge and manipulation. As a society in its own right, the Bronze Tide is immensely compelling as this huge, messy bloc of human activity being forced to respond to a crisis really unique to the setting, which is effectively balancing an understandable drive with some really horrific methods. It does a very good job of making the group feel genuinely multi-faceted and struggling to deal with the problems of that, which also helps to make the element of simple human society and interaction feel like a challenge worthy of the Exalted, even while it’s in the shadow of a major supernatural threat. And there’s real pathos to the way that, on the face of it, success and victory for the Bronze Tide comes at the expense of the peoples of the Cinder Isles.




    Now, next Artifact up is Penumbra Gleam, and it’s nice to see another Thunderbolt Shield, and I’ll be interested to see a four-dot one that doesn’t have a world inside of it.


    Heh, it’s deliciously fitting that, after my encounter with the praiseworthy raksha facing the Bronze Tide, the story behind this Artifact should open with another description within the same pattern; Maja of the Starling Song, driven by a pursuit of martial perfection, in possession of a magic looking-glass in which to refine her technique, and whose precise method in combat resembles a dance, with the added benefit of hubris in giving herself a title challenging the Sun.

    Or rather, she was, before a group of young Lunars cleverly stole her glass away and fashioned it into a shield that could reflect all her failings and mistakes, in the face of which her ego was broken and she submitted. Setting aside the mystical implications of it, that’s just really cool as a story for how the Exalted might deal with supernatural enemies, particularly the raksha.

    So the end result is a mirrored shield in which the flaws of those it reflects are revealed, not discounting the wielder, leading to a practice of granting it to younger Lunars who need to be taught a lesson. I’ve not yet had an opportunity to comment on the manner in which several of the Artifacts have been referred to as serving a cause in the community of the Silver Pact, but it’s been a through line I’ve enjoyed, both for the sense of methods for them influencing and instructing one another, and for a bit more detail for the processes and benefits of their intercourse.

    Huh, interesting that the shield’s first power is not based on it being used as a shield, but something to train in front of. The mechanical expression of that method for correcting one’s own flaws is very interesting; use it to make Instil actions against oneself to erode Intimacies of pride or bravado or create and strengthen ones representing humility or frustration with oneself, to generate a resource called Reflection, which can be spent to reroll failures on dice for Melee attacks or to make full defence actions, with resonant wielders getting a higher pool of Reflection to hold. This is making me excited to see the scope of this Artifact, as I really like the idea of such a flavourful powerset that is also extremely subtle in its methods and purposes.

    Ah, of course there would be powers based on using the corrective reflections to undermine or confuse enemies while wielding it as a shield. I really like the resonant effects in these, for making the severity of various effects depending on how far below Parry an enemy’s hit roll was, just to drive home the point about them being assailed with their own failings. Heh, I find it particularly in the spirit of Penumbra Gleam’s raksha roots for it to be not entirely honest about the flaws it reflect back, in the power to weaken even successful hits based on flaws in the initial attack.

    Still, it’s a four-dot Artifact, the occasional overt manifestation wouldn’t go amiss. As this level goes, being able to create a kind of distorted image effect of various defensive manoeuvres is still fairly low-key, and I like how it allows Reflections to be used as a fuel for improving defence. That an enemy can spend Willpower to overcome the effect seems reasonable as a balancing factor for what is technically a die adder is dependent largely on spending something other than motes that could be stockpiled before a fight, although with that in mind I actually feel as though Silver Phantom Phalanx’s actual mote cost may be a bit too high. Unless I’m misremembering something about the dice cap rules for static values, the fact that the bonuses to Parry for using this aren’t non-Charm makes me feel as though the five motes it costs would be better spent on just your regular Excellency, for a bonus that can’t be dispelled by spending Willpower, and saving on a resource that can have valuable use elsewhere.

    Yeah, the more I think about this, the more I feel as though you’d be a lot better served by reserving that Reflection for the power that can remove damage dice, rather than use it for a Parry die adder that is still fairly mote expensive and requires you to be Resonant and take a full defence action for enemies to not be able to ignore. But like I’ve said elsewhere, I’m not the best to assess these things.

    And finally the expected power to respond to an enemy’s attack with a reflection of them doing it, which can turn damage back on themselves. I quite like some of the mechanics in this one, such as being able to use an enemy’s own Initiative as the basis of the decisive clash attack, the capacity to use Reflection to add dice as if to say that the mirror is not merely reflecting the attack, but correcting its shortcomings, and what I think is a not too expensive Reflection cost to copy the enemy’s attack-enhancing magic.

    You know, it’s super weird that several of the three-dot Artifacts went to Essence 4 and both of the four-dots go only to 3.


    This is another very enjoyable Artifact, from a nice origin story over to some very evocative powers that don’t need to be all gimmicky visual flair. It’s always nice to see Thunderbolt Shields find interesting ways to elaborate on defensive applications as well.

    I have approximate knowledge of many things.

                        03-10-2019, 07:48 PM
                        All right, finally up to the last of the full Dominion write-ups, Sunken Luthe. The description of Leviathan already did a lot to improve my interest in this place, so I’m excited to see what a dedicated description of its nature and people can bring.


                        That opening description is nice and focused in its brevity, not spending too much time elaborating on the concept on it having been a city that sank, and making clear that the place has mostly been drowned out. The fact that it hangs on the edge of a precipice adds a nice bit of native tension, to keep the city from feeling like a place that could comfortably opt to sit out Creation’s conflicts entirely.

                        Luthe’s population is going to be one of the oddest of any Dominion, so it’s a good idea to dive right in to the nature and history of the people. I have a vague sense that the past depictions made the city more habitable to people lacking mutations, so when there’s supposed to be a lot less available real estate for them, and intermarriage has left only a few dedicated holdouts, I’ve got to wonder at the psychological state of those people. Just think about it; numerous generations without access to the sun. They’ve got to be kind of weird. On the beastfolk side, I’m hoping it will drop some of the caste distinctions that were around previously, they always felt a bit much. The fact that the city has become a destination for immigrants provides a bit of insight into a vibrant undersea social network.

                        There’s a description of how Leviathan has been around, but having always been a very distant figure the immigrant population needed to develop their own process for government, the Luthian Council; what was originally an ad hoc body to allow multiple clans to leave together in peace that evolved into a formal legislative parliament. By no means a democratic system, mind, with councillors choosing their own successors; the closest thing they get is when a very large majority of the citizens vote for the removal of a member, and even then it’s the other councillors who get to select and vote on their replacement, so it sounds like a very concentrated form of power. It mentions how that was the means for otherwise outside candidate Blood-Dimmed Deep to get in, although the biggest thing I’m wondering is how and why Leviathan found the time to sire a daughter, considering he spends virtually all of his time swimming around as a big dolphin, unless she’s supposed to be really old, or… a shapeshifting orca.

                        Ah, but in contrast to a few of the other Lunars who try to maintain mutual respect with surrounding governing bodies (and even with Raksi’s outright autocracy), that Leviathan is back in the game and looking to use the city as a war Dominion presents problems for the council, as despite his nominal respect his stature is sufficient that he can merely issue commands and the people and authorities feel obligated to comply (that his daughter is on the council to curtail their complaints and resistance can’t help). Given how Leviathan was described before, I’ve got to imagine that a lot of this is a simple matter of his lack of social grace.

                        Luthe’s condition creates some unique bases for its cultural development that are good to explore. Their cosmopolitan nature and safety relative to their surroundings is said to allow them to form a very elaborate artistic oeuvre, to the point that the majority of people engage in at least some level of creative output, of which apparently one of the most popular themes is coming up with elaborate ways to depict the city having sunk (leading me to picture a trend in which people try to outdo one another with how overwrought and extreme they can make it while still keeping in good taste). Religion is an element that is influenced by the nature of Luthe from before it fell; it was earlier given the title “City of a Thousand Gods”, which is elaborated on here by the point that, in addition to bringing and cross-pollinating their ancestral gods and the guardian deity of Leviathan, the people have taken to the gods depicted in the native sunken temples, and I’ve got to wonder what any of those gods think about receiving such an extra source of prayer.

                        There’s a description of their cuisine favouring raw fish (as well as aquatic plants), and I’m suddenly imagining Luthe and its people as having a resemblance to Zora’s Domain in Breath of the Wild, just because that game gave them a very good aesthetic which I think would be a good place to start from. Then there are a few points about Luthian value and craft materials: how they have little care for silver or gems as units of exchange, instead mostly using a system of credit and debit referencing a defunct ancient currency called the deben, and that they place great value in trade for materials hard to manufacture underwater, with glass being the favoured substance in weapons, corrodible metals disregarded entirely, and jewellery consisting of shell, sea ivory and precious stones, with the addition of gold for the wealthiest. There are notes on some particular concessions in everyday life for their underwater existence, such as how writing is used by carving into plates of gold and lead, as well as seashell writing and whale bone scrimshaw; I’m guessing that the metals are for more official documentation, and the more mundane, readily available stuff are for personal notes and references, and maybe more transitory documents like receipts. The people can’t get much use out of clothing, so they basically go by Barsoom rules by sticking to bags and belts from which to hang personal items. It’s said that the new war footing has led to a transformation of the economy to focus heavily on creation of things like weapons and armour, but I’m curious about the description of the funding coming from a major deben infusion by Leviathan; considering the deben being described as a largely fiat currency and that Leviathan has been in virtual isolation for a thousand years, it kind of sounds like he’s basically devaluing the currency be decree. Either that or he’s got some source of trade goods he’s been holding out on for centuries that he’s suddenly made available for liquidation, but I feel as though even that would dramatically affect the deben’s value. I don’t know, on the face of it, it sounds like a rather crude method for financing armament supply, but something in the wording of “vast outpourings” leaves me wondering if that’s actually supposed to be the intent, that Leviathan’s fixation is harmful to the city in the long run.

                        Okay, with those points about how the undersea culture works out of the way, it gets into a description of the actual layout of the city, starting with a point on how Leviathan personally lacks the talents or interests to restore its shattered form (having no sense of value for its more cultural treasures in the face of warring with the Realm), and hence much of its current nature is the result of embellishments provided by the newer aquatic inhabitants.

                        First off is the Drowned Quarter, starting off on the amusing note that, by the standards of the aquatic inhabitants, intact buildings are held in lower esteem, with features such as doorways and corridors being restrictive to swimming (further recalling some Zora aesthetics, although their city is not located underwater) and staircases derided as worthless, leaving them to be relegated as slum areas, while others get to luxuriate in spaces that provide privacy and security while being more accommodating to their natural lifestyles. It’s a nice way of making the environment and its inhabitants a bit more alien. Added to this is the description of their own building materials and methods, using local varieties of what sound like particularly large varieties of kelp and coral to bind breaking walls together to form versatile pods, from which necessary extensions are simply grown. There’s a nice point about how the choice between the building materials is largely aesthetic, with kelp regarded as cosier and coral as sturdier and more colourful, which helps get one into the mindset of the inhabitants and feel their basic humanity. There’s some helpful elaboration on their infrastructure, not just on how some indoor underwater illumination is provided by phosphorescent algae, but of the parameters for coral tunnels connecting structures that does double duty in giving a greater sense of the population; spaces that need to be made large enough to accommodate whalefolk, as well as some that are so narrow that only flexible cephalopod people and the diminutive “tetrafolk” (who have a major municipal function as the general maintainers of their constructions) can generally get through them. I’m not quite sure what to make of the tetrafolk, so I’ll need to see if there’s anything in the suffix to make sense of it later. We’re finally told about the central structure of the Palace of the Luthian Council, a huge structure of red coral that also functions as the home of the city’s main police force and standing army, the Siaka Guard (although I’m guessing they’re not literally all sharkfolk) commanded by Blood-Dimmed Deep.

                        Ah, now here’s something particularly relevant to my interests; about a year ago, I asked the developers if living underwater would be presented as imposing an impediment to speech, inspired as I was by one of the Flat Earth books in which such people are depicted as needing to using magical ear pieces to hear one another, so I’m very happy to see the point that the common language of Luthe is a form of sign language (another thing I considered in that query, and which I also really like as an image), in addition to more exotic forms such as people derived from cetaceans having a forms of speech, a few using controlled bioluminescence, and I would personally imagine that some with cephalopod features would be able to use controlled skin colouring for it. It’s a really nice way to make the people feel more adapted to their environment, both in the sense of it shaping them into something distinct, and in the use of ingenuity to overcome its limitations. And some of the implications to it… it’s got to really have an impact on oratory, considering that sign language doesn’t quite project the way that a voice would be able to. There are probably a few interesting workarounds one could come up with for that.

                        Next is the Sunken Hall, the remains of the meeting hall of the old city’s priest-kings found in the centre of the city, which has been appropriated as a meeting place for Lunars of the Silver Pact. We’re told one of the assets in the possession of the Western Lunars, a rather valuable sounding Artifact map of the Western ocean that can track the movement of fleets (even if it depicts them across outdated features), and to offset some of his more withdrawn qualities we’re informed of how Leviathan personally attends every meeting of his adherents and councils of his fellow shahan-yas (presumably needing to compress from his giant orca form), although still generally of an attitude of domineering pronouncements. It also says that members of the Luthian Council occasionally get invitations to meet with Leviathan or adherents for discussion of matters of city politics (I guess that, on occasions where the citizenry vote out members, he might have thoughts on who the council should nominate as replacements) and the grand strategy against the Realm, with him favouring Blood-Dimmed Deep in this function. I like the point about how part of the basis for that is her being his daughter, since it gives him some extra-warmth, and that the relationship between the two is complicated by her wish to start making war openly faced with growing frustration at his reservation; that seems to be a bit of a recurring theme with Leviathan, either his reputation or familial ties drawing a lot of people into his orbit, where they find his underlying personality very difficult to deal with.

                        Okay, going to try and compress the next few of these locales. Ooh, the description of the temple district having been constructed to resemble a map of Creation sounds really cool as an endeavour of artistry, spirituality and civil engineering; it sounds like there’s the mildest callback to some of Leviathan’s Second Edition personality in the note that he defiled the temples to the Elemental Dragons in his rage at the Usurpation. I’m always glad for descriptions of the people’s religious rites, and I’m happy to see another instance of the recurring intriguing notion of people maintaining rites for gods that have died off, it’s just a really nice personable sense of Creation’s growing diminishment. Likewise towards another depiction of Lunars viewing their patron in religious terms, and I like how there’s a unique tension to the priesthood that attends the temple to Luna actually disliking Leviathan owing to his treatment of their order’s founder. If any part of the city was most appropriate to sit on the edge of that abyss, this would be it. While it might be that on other occasions I would gripe about the idea that characters believing the slow collapse was due to natural causes is a misconception, and it’s actually a result of the Wyld is at the bottom of the fissure, on this occasion I’ve got enough general good will, a basis for seeing it as a logical necessity, and the general love of the new raksha that plays nicely off of the idea that they’re pulling temples in to feed off of the echoes of prayer within them, to be willing to set such enmity aside.

                        Heh, I like the reference to Mesoamerican sacred ball games in the original function of the Poet’s Court, and I’m really happy to see even a brief exploration of how their distinct language affects their cultural expressions. I particularly like the idea that they would supplement the experience of watching a form of poetry based in sign language and body movements with drugs to induce synaesthesia; it gives me a sense that the only time people are liable to ever “hear” anything is when they’re watching a poet’s hands flash through verse, and their brains are translating that into a sensation of sound. It makes me think it would be interesting to have an exchange between a Luthe native and a surface dweller (including some Lunar immigrants adjusting to life in the city) on the superior art form, as I’m imagining dedicated underwater poets decrying speech as a limited and inferior expression to the combination of their own language with an appropriately high audience.

                        And there’s finally a reference to how the inhabitants who still need air (whether to live or, in the case of some Lunar crafters, practice their works) get by. It’s mostly just a point on how even some of the aquatic inhabitants, such as those derived from mammals, need to stop in for air sometimes, that there are luxury quarters for those Lunars who’d prefer to stay in their true forms (which I have a sense belies a typical Luthian derision for such people), and some of the variety in methods for actually providing and purifying such air, from sophisticated artifice to stale old filters for the poorer inhabitants. Come to think of it, I actually wonder if remaining separate from the beastfolk in Luthe conveys wealth, for such people to be capable of funding the necessary infrastructure of their survival; it’s not elaborated here, so I guess it depends on whether or not one would assume that such people are maintained by government subsidy. Yes, I can see two ways to go about that; one in which municipal obligation devotes resources to maintain their survival, provoking griping from the general citizenry on how their tax debens go to preserve folks of limited productivity who refuse to integrate, or else that between old money status and being able to engage in some industries limited in the rest of the populace, they’re distinctly wealthy and able to maintain their own necessities. I wouldn’t imagine the air-breathing population is large enough to incorporate both.

                        Last of the described districts are the Outposts, which are more properly Luthe’s surrounding townlands to help provide it with necessary resources, as well as homes for the poor and iconoclastic. We’re given a picture of the integrity of the Siaka Guard by the fact that their patrol priorities are determined by the availability of bribes. Mind, it may be that their system of finance is not exactly formally developed, so it might be that bribery is just an integral function of how they’re kept in the black.

                        All right, the Prominent Figures list is giving us one last picture of Blood-Dimmed Deep… who I had not been cognisant enough to realise that she would be an orcawoman; between that and the point about how he’ll meet with his adherents in person, I guess that solves the mystery of where she came from. But there are more important things than that, she’s a power player! Oh hey, that desire to not be defined by her father is actually a theme of her write-up, makes sense. I like the idea, for herself and elsewhere, that having such authority in a key location of the Silver Pact and being the Moon-Touched of a very powerful member makes her basically a member of the Pact, and the fact that she actually isn’t means the Lunars don’t exactly listen to her. I’d almost want to play a Lunar who would share her viewpoints just to get some things going. Or hey, give her a connection to Sha’a Oka, that should create some drama.

                        Nice to see a genderfluid character included in the mix. The description of their anglerfish features sounds familiar to me, I wonder if we’d had beastfolk like that Inanja before, or if it’s just similar to one of the Pole mutant peoples of Autochthonia. Still, they sound good, and I imagine the luminescent hair makes for very good displays at poetry jams. I also like the idea that the best time and place to meet Leviathan is when he turns up to listen to somebody’s poetry.

                        Ah good, final nail in the coffin for Leviathan the Dragon Blood eater by having one active in Luthe’s society… oh hey, quite the contrary from him actively defending Gevan Batahul from some more overzealous adherents. I actually find something adorable in the idea that he (and by extension, much of Luthe’s population) doesn’t… wait, how can he never have heard of the Realm, who does he think that the city is being put on a war footing for? Okay, setting that aside, I think it makes for an interesting character to have a kind of amoral businessman and lawyer in this environment (particularly to help along the industry for arming the city), makes me think of him as a kind of underwater Morland Holmes.

                        Oooh, a First Age ghost hanging around. She’s described as having been one of the priest-kings, so I really want Lady Forever-Drowning to have not been Exalted when alive. I thought the idea of the people worshipping at the alters and idols of forgotten gods was really cool as it was, so the idea that those rites have been enabled by the ghost of one of their original administrators (for which she herself is venerated) is just a nice cherry on top.

                        Okay, finish off with the neighbours, starting with an allied spirit court, the Hollow Court. Hmm, the leader has the title of Storm Father (Halid) and is referred to as a storm mother, so I wonder what the implications of that are. Still, that aside, seems quite sensible that Leviathan would associate with some gods to assault Realm shipping with storms, and the payment in sacrificed sailors founds very Death’s Master. Oh what, now you choose to be a censor Fakharu? Yeah, I’m sure you’ll be wrathful right up until the point when they give you a cut. What about the ghosts of Galbadan? The overall character of that settlement sounds fairly standard, but the image of an underwater haunted city consisting of a ship graveyard is a pretty cool addition to underwater society.

                        With Luthe’s background, it sounds like a very interesting point of contact to have a cousin society consisting of land-dwelling refugees from the city. Heh, considering the history of the Realm’s expansion, it must have been fun times to come across this island and discover that their mythology despises the Dragon Blooded; makes me think that Dolaron was subjugated more on principle than because it had much of value to offer.

                        Aaaand a little point to touch off the history of the Niobraran League, and a first hint at the nature of the Spoken; apparently they were big. Obsidian sounds like a good counterweight to Luthe’s place in undersea society, and I enjoy how there’s a big source of weird lore for people to form religious beliefs around that is unrelated to the Old Realm.



                        And that’s it. Luthe is another one that I think has updated well, effectively coming across as alien. Even if it’s not a place in which to set whole stories (should people not be able to manage the difficulties of the underwater setting), I’d say it at least makes for a very compelling backdrop for any stories in which Lunars or other potential allies against the Realm come to petition Leviathan or seek to attend council.

                        Right, good, finished, aaaaand there’s still the Caul left to do. I miscounted the men Liz! I’ll get to it a bit later.



                        Back up with another suit of armour, this time a big ol’ suit of full plate by the name of Seven Furies Caged.


                        The origin story of this one sounds very reminiscent of one of the armours in the core, but being forged in the middle of the heedless battle out of moonsilver to encase an angry Lunar will convey some very different lessons. I often wonder at the origins for malevolent Artifacts, so it’s interesting to see one that was made with the best of intentions but under circumstances that inclined it towards something horrific. Heh, of course the thing would be spiky, although the main thing I like in the appearance is the idea of it having broken bits of sword and bone embedded from enemies in the course of being forged. Nice to see an instance of interchange between wildly different factions of Lunars.

                        Oooh, where would it go from increasing its protection based on how badly injured the wearer is? Let’s see, using wound penalties as bonus dice to go into battle… an enhancement for the as yet unseen Relentless Lunar Fury (makes complete sense for the theme)… extra power for attacking people that have managed to hurt you…at Blood Moon Ascendant, I feel as though I’m not going to be able to fully appreciate this without having seen Relentless Lunar Fury, but I’m getting the general idea of stoking rage from growing injury and the offence of successful foes.


                        Yeah, this looks good overall, but it’s not got anything that really jumps out at me, and I think I need to look at it again when I’ve seen the Stamina Charms.
