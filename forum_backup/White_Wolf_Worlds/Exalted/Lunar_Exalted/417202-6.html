    Originally posted by Kyeudo View Post

    You claim that "barbarian werewolves" is too narrow and restricting an image for Lunars and that they need more variety. What I see is their dilution away from a powerful, evocative image to one of "we are weird and glow silver because the moon". Adding too much to their themes just tears down the primal connotations they could so easily embrace. That lack of focus is what earned them the "Silver Solars" moniker.
    I don't quite agree. All exalts should have some theme, but for PCs - especially in an Exalted game - this theme should not dominate your character options. For example, what would be the Solar image if you also tried to describe it in two words? I imagine "Golden Heroes" would be a typical image, but there are many Solars that are subtle rather than radiant and cunning or wise instead of traditionally heroic as per the ancient epic tales. They still have a powerful narrative because of their story - the ancient kings and masters, the corruption, betrayal, etc, etc. You can play the Radiant Kings Reborn game with Solars, but you don't have to - their previous edition skillset gave them plenty of other tools, and the new one will likely also give them many other options..Dragon-blooded are even more varied, and they are still both quite evocative and at least imo have plenty of good hooks. Has anyone ever complained how Dragon-blooded, who were created to serve the Solars and act as their intermediaries, have charms that allow them to rule? Yet there were probably no few cases in the First Age where a socially apt Lunar acted as the "face" of a reclusive Solar savant, or as the sage and occult advisor to their warlord mate.

    It is not about adding tons of themes, it is about having a theme sufficiently evocable to draw people in, yet wide enough to appeal to many niches. Lunar barbarians are definitely an iconic image, but that does not mean Lunar courtiers, poets, explorers, merchants, priests or generals should not exist or they can't come from urban or otherwise "civilized" cultures. Straight out of the box, Lunar shapeshifting is an awesome tool for infiltrators or masters of intrigue - just think of what shapeshifters in other settings have done without additional supernatural powers - and it's not like Luna or several generations of Lunars were too dumb come with the idea of blending in. In the First Age, they could try their hand in pretty much any area, even if the Solars had a guaranteed top spot. Lunars in the Age of Sorrows are often at the edges and affiliated with barbarian societies, because the Dragon-blooded and their Sidereal allies had been spending a lot of time and effort to keep them away up until very recently. That shouldn't mean Lunars want to be and have to be barbarians all the time, forever, period. They were nobles in the First Age, outcasts in the next one, and have the potential to be something else in the future. It would be quite weird for a splat that has transformation as a "signature move" if they had to be conceptually typecast, would it not?

    I wasn't here when the "silver Solars" nickname came up, but from what I hear Lunar fans were overall pretty happy at what the 2E Lunars were compared to the 1E ones, and most would much rather have the "silver Solars" than what came before. I would argue that it is the execution rather than the concept that was the problem.

                            You know what I liked about TAW? It made barbarians cool. I know that's shallow and subjective and silly, but that's my feels. It gave a niche to Lunars that they didn't have before, it gave them a reason to stand outside society beyond simply being contrary, and it held up a mirror to the all the ills and failings of what the current keepers of power called civilization.

                            Forcing them to step side-ways also made the original idea of Solar wed to Lunar for political reasons a justifiable to fairly interesting thing, rather than something to shrug at or build weird mind control charms off of.

                            Creation ruled by the Solar looks like the 1st Age (maybe, I could never decide if the First Age was a logical extension of giving 700 people waaaay too much power, or a super cynical look at a magic utopia, anyway!) Creation ruled by the Sidereal looks like the one that exists now, tragic and bearable and full of necessary compromise.

                            We haven't seen what a world ruled by the Lunar looks like, and I think that's largely because the Lunar mindset doesn't preclude itself towards the building up of kingdoms. This is not to say they're not any good at it! (I admit I haven't thoroughly read or examined TAW, only skimmed it, if this is something it purported then I happily ignored it) Anyway, this is to say that the Lunar as a group have decided that they favor independence, that they think Creation does not need them to tell them what to do. It's an interesting story when all the other factions are seeking power in one way or another.

                            So in my Creation/the one inspired by TAW, a lot more of the prodigies and incredible magic relics dotting the landscape of Creation are Lunar sourced. These just tend not to change history so much, because the Lunar make them or fight their battles or seduce their princes or princesses or whatever for their own reasons. Their power and legacy isn't as easily shared as that of Solar.

                            And of course the Realm is going to be at odds with this, because any gigantic empire is going to be at odds with 300ish demigods going around doing whatever they want and flaunting their laws. Delicious conflict ensues.

                            - -

                            All this said, I hope 3rd Edition Lunars makes being a barbarian cool. Not to say that being a barbarian is the only thing a Lunar can do, but if they can make it interesting I think they'll have found an appropriate niche for the Lunar.

                                            I got poked and told there was a thread that might involve me on the OP front page.

                                            I checked. Oh, it's a Lunar thread.

                                            Then I went and made a sandwich, walked the dog, had a nap, and tidied my desk, because Lunar thread. Successive poking has convinced to at least post something.

                                            To answer the most basic questions from the opening post - between TAW Lunars and 2e Lunars, TAW are probably the "better" shifters. They've got more Charms that interact with their shapeshifting, no form lock, no Tell, and more practical effects stemming from each form, and it's easier to homebrew new forms for them to take. On the other hand, 2e Lunars get their totem form for free, and have much more room for abusing shapeshifting at the extremes - mainly by taking a form with absurd Strength and giggling. Similarly, TAW probably have "better" Charms. They've got a wider variety of tricks, they were written at the end of the edition rather than its start, and many of their personal-scale Charms use the same sort of numbers as Solars. The main "advantage" 2e Lunars have in that contest is their overpowered soak and regen Charms, plus a few social tricks copypasted from Solars.

                                            The trickier question is the one about their differences in theme and focus. The two main issues are that 2e Lunars barely had any themes or focuses, while for most of its short history TAW was a grab-bag of whatever bit of eccentric homebrew a handful of fans had decided to do that week. A lot of its early missteps can be seen where "unnerving outsider" scrapes over the line into "outright horror", which is more of the Abyssal or Infernal bailiwick - Lunar shapeshifting isn't, fundamentally, a biological process - the twisting of flesh and reconfiguring of bone is presumably something that happens, but it deliberately doesn't receive much focus. Pan and Lovecraft's Wilbur Whateley were both goat-legged chimeras, but their presentation was very different. This is what people tend to be talking about when they say "Alex Mercer isn't a Lunar" - his bag of tricks is a one-for-one match with what Lunars can do, but he's fundamentally a character of biological horror, which isn't what Lunars are for.

                                            So both 2e Lunars and TAW were nebulous clouds, separated mainly by vicious vicissitudes of the White Wolf forum shouting at itself, and even now it's difficult to know which version or interpretation of each is being referred to at any given time.

                                            Thankfully, there are paths to giving each a bit more definition. TAW is easy - it was trimmed into a final version, which cuts down on the verbiage and trims content unsuited to Lunar themes. It's much more coherent - and would have to be, as it's a single document put together with intent and purpose, rather than a drawing board covered in the graffiti and blueprints of a few people without so much as a Skype chat room to link them.

                                            2e Lunars is harder, but still possible. It's best to approach it by looking at its history. 1e Lunars had a troubled development, essentially stemming from a clear (albeit last-minute) vision of barbarian adventures wrapped up in a werebeast package being handed over to writers who hated or didn't understand what they were asked to write. This resulted in a horrendous failure on almost every level. 2e Lunars, rather than trying to capture the original concept behind its predecessor, was written as a response to the execution of 1e Lunars, patching up or flinching away from the points where it had most obviously failed. To its credit, it was well-received on release - and remains one of the most playable early 2e splats, out of the box. Unfortunately, the cracks became more obvious as the edition progressed, especially when a few more mechanically interesting splats arrived. 1e Lunars had been a dreadful approach to a good idea, but in flinching away from its approach, 2e Lunars had also managed to put distance between itself and the good idea, and been left with little but "animals and not like 1e" to go on.

                                            I will acknowledge the obvious potential for bias on my part, but there's literally no character you can make as a 2e Lunar that you can't do better with a TAW Lunar. The opposite isn't true.

                                            A more interesting contrast, then, is between TAW and 3e Lunars.

                                            To put it in simple terms, as I see it:
                                            TAW was a response to 2e Lunars, just as 2e Lunars was a response to 1e Lunars.
                                            3e Lunars are - or will be - an attempt to return to that original design pitch.

                                            In many places, they've hit on the same "answers" to Lunar problems in the setting. Both focus on "Lunars vs The Empire" as a major conflict, both try to make Lunar shapeshifting an interesting tool in itself, most of the previewed 3e Lunar effects work perfectly well in TAW (and, one suspects, vice-versa). It's no coincidence that the last time Holden and I disagreed about how Lunars should be portrayed, our disagreement came down to "Lunars should be monsters, a category that includes witches" vs "Lunars should be witches, a category that includes monsters".

                                            In others, they've taken completely different tracks. As part of a whole new edition, 3e Lunars takes advantage of and introduces new setting conceits, while TAW was intended as a plug-in, and so mostly reinterpreted isolated chunks of setting rather than outright re-configuring the face of Lunars in Creation. I expect a greater degree of influence from Werewolf, which (for all that some fans seem allergic to it) isn't a bad thing - it's a question of properly incorporating it. 3e is embracing that point of inspiration, while TAW largely eschewed it (lumping less specific "werewolf" themes under the general "skinwalker witch" idea). 3e obviously works off very maximalist design precepts, which means it can make Lunar shapeshifting interesting the same way it tackled artifacts - unique and exciting powers for each animal, while TAW had to be self-contained and so produced a selection of general, adaptable "animal traits" that could be slapped onto a given form. Though both 3e and TAW are being written with a better understanding of what it means to be "barbarian" than previous editions, they'll probably have different approaches to this aspect of Lunars. TAW approached "barbarian" as a practical, in-setting result of the greater theme of "outsider", while 3e Lunars will likely be tackling it directly, as a facet of primal Lunar power.

                                            At some point that shifted toward a general ramble, but hopefully you found it somewhat useful.
