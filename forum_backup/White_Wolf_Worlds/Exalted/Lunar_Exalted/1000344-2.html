                Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                ​I wonder what is precisely meant by marginalization here.

                ​Lord knows I went the way of considering Lunars in terms of representatives of the generally oppressed before, but by now I'm thinking that Lunars as individuals are outcast not so much for their origins or associations as the fact that Lunars of the Second Age tend to be scary monsters, which isn't entirely fair, but is also something I can't quite fault.
                There's a world-spanning empire and about half of Heaven itself backing a sophisticated and omnipresent apparatus for hunting them down and murdering them? And unlike Solars, they don't hold the literal Mandate of Heaven, a spiritual blank check to make of the world what they wish, with concrete metaphysical backing? They don't have mystical powers to sway the minds of the masses in the face of overwhelming propaganda, and they can't demand access to the body politic except by eating its heart and impersonating it. They aren't the rightful heirs to the First Age, with all of its wonders and glory - they are, in fact, unique in this: Abyssals and Infernals are part of the same legacy as the Solars, warped or not, while Dragon-Blooded can claim right of conquest and back it up with a nigh-invincible military apparatus and the Sidereals back them up. In the event that a Lunar DOES build something worthwhile, something genuinely theirs, it's likely going to be immediately rolled by the Empire, annexed, and turned against them. Many of the suggestions I've seen (more on this forum than by the devs themselves, mind) for what to do when the Realm comes calling amount to "turn into a bird and flee." The Silver Pact's main strategy to counter this is a long, gruesome war of attrition; we'll have to wait for the DB and Lunar hardbacks to see if it's actually accomplished anything except for making Creation measurably worse.

                Originally posted by Elfive View Post
                I've seen this argument a lot, and I've never really understood a) how you got this impression from reading the book, or b) how this is worse than actually eating people.
                The examples are very much there, and pretending they aren't is disingenuous. As for how it's worse, the problem is twofold: First, Vampire is meant as a metaphor for a variety of dysfunctional or abusive relationships, and the blood-drinking (which, importantly, need not be fatal) exists as a filter of the fantastical so that players aren't necessarily engaging directly with the full ugliness of the real-life situations for which it is an allegory. Nobody has to worry about triggering another player's PTSD with Ghouls or Frenzy mechanics or the Presence Discipline, even though these are all meant to reference controlling abuse and domestic violence. Yes, Elizabeth Bathory was real, but such a freakish one-off that it's staggeringly-unlikely any player at your table will break down sobbing because of Clan Ventrue.

                Beast doesn't have this filter. Beasts, more often than not, feed by abusing people, and they abuse people by actually abusing them. Vandalism, threatening messages, gaslighting, leveraging a position of authority to terrorize one's subordinates, and murder. Even their murders are more fucked-up than a vampire's killings - more often than not, a vampire lost control of their thirst and is rightly-horrified of what they've done, and if they're not, it's a sign that they're slipping toward BAD END. It evokes the horror and shame of having done something terrible and wrong in the heat of passion, and the fundamental tragedy of vampiric existence. When a Beast kills, it's to satisfy a deep, psychological need by reinforcing an internal narrative and asserting their primacy over the bulk of humanity. Their idealized relationship with Heroes isn't one of deadly antagonism, but of the Beast asserting the Hero's fundamental irrelevance by putting the Hero in their place. That necessary curtain of metaphor collapses here because the Beast's violence doesn't feel very metaphorical at all. I'm not exaggerating when I say that it reads like a serial killer's manifesto.

                This segues into the second big issue with Beast that the rest of the WoD/CoD gamelines don't have: It tries very hard to justify its protagonists' monstrosity. The Sabbat belief that humans should be cattle for their vampire overlords is why the Sabbat are the bad guys, even to other vampires. Werewolves are fundamentally-inhuman in mindset, and large portions of the book are given over to characters who REMEMBER what it was to feel human coming to terms with an unavoidable need to hunt and kill other sapient beings (they also have the Ghost Wolves to act as an audience surrogate, taking a look at Werewolf 'society' and asking "what even the sweet crispy fuck?") Slashers are slashers, meant first and foremost as an antagonist splat, and when they aren't they're for playing games in the vein of Suicide Squad. Beast asks the Hunters and Heroes, "What gives you the right to hunt me?" and then turns a deaf ear to the answer.

                        So here's my main issue with Beast:

                        Beasts, by their natures, hurt people. They are dangerous to be around, feed on others for sustenance and can't stop doing that. And as note,d the original write-up, their presence encouraged Heroism. So in the end, a beast is a parasite that hurts people. Like a vampire in a sense.

                        It's just that a vampire doesn't go and then claims it's the victim here. I think to me that's the fundemental disconnect I have with Beast. I think to me that's the big issue I feel of it. It has this weird situation-blindness where that the world hates you for being a monstrous predator is somehow the world's fault. And it's done I think with a goal to be contrarian to other CoD games (which feels almost ham-fisted since they feel more honest), with a crossover angle to them somehow (which I fail to see hwo it fits for Changelings who try to escape absue, magi who generally are just human not monsters and Created with lines that seem to be encouraging going Centimanus).

                        I also feel Heroes didn't help this. In the orignal write-up, a Hero while usually someone prone to it, is made that by the Beast. Yet it's somehwo the Hero's fault for playing into what the Beast made them.

                        And this is all in a context of trying to sell Beasts as a representative of LGBTQ people through metaphor. But I think it does it without first questioning what a beast is first. Since it in a way to me feels like it's just by accident stumbling into being exactly what folks say about LGBTQ people (that they are harmful to themselves and others and don't care). To me at least, it is both odd in its messaging and through that, undermines and actively harms its own message.

                        It's a game that wants to be about lashing-out agasint abusers. But to me reads about abusers blaming their victims. And while some supernaturals kind of do that (some Kindred more or less go this route), the game doesn't really praise them for it but often narratively feels liek it more calls them out on that.
