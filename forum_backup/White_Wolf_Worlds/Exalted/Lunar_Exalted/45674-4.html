                    Well, better late than never.

                    Incidentally, this reminds me of a discussion pertaining to postmodern analysis of the matter of disease killing a Ramses.
                    This statement of Latour's is really on par with Baudrillard's quip that the Gulf War never happened in that it's not meant to describe what happened to physical bodies, but the way they are captured by language. Latour is saying that a scientific understanding of what killed Ramses is constructed one way, but this is not the social reality of how he died, which was constructed another way. According to Latour's thought, neither is a simple description of reality, but a social construction based on interpreting observations. This doesn't mean the bacillus isn't real.

                    If we were to take this to a thought experiment and travel in time to show an ancient Egyptian the microrganisms that killed his king, he might construct a different story with the same data. For example, he might not divide the organisms and the mechanism of its infection into separate entities. This holistic description would be very strange to us, and it's hard for us to get into that guy's sandals. Taken further, if the time-displaced Egyptian used these categories of thought as the basis for scientific inquiries with the help of all of our modern toys and data, he might discover things that we can't, because of the way in which we build scientific facts. In no case do the physical phenomena actually cease the exist or even change.
