            Originally posted by Satori View Post
            Clearly the OP has never played D&D. You don't need a cleric. You don't even need a healer. In fact, healing in combat is a terrible thing to do to your allies, because there were at least a half-dozen more useful things you could have used that action for, and they need you to support the team, not waste time. [/tangent]
            There are some encounters where healing is useless. There are some encounters where healing is necessary. There are some encounters where healing helps, but isn't necessary.

            If you have a DM who designs small range of encounters, then you may have never been in a situation where healing is important.

            For example: Enemies have been alerted to your group's presence. They wait in room with a ready action of 'open fire at anyone entering.' Your fighter opens the door and half-a-dozen archers and two wizards immediately attack with arrows and color sprays, so said fighter is now down to half health and is dazed*, and it's not even the first round of combat.

            A healer can restore that fighter's HP, give him a buff to defense for one round, and grant him a free saving throw against being dazed with a +3 bonus to the roll, all in one turn.

            This is easily the difference between 'the fighter dies in the first round of combat' and 'the fighter immediately recovers and charges to the group of archers.'

            * Being dazed means your enemies get +2 to attack you, and you can only take one action a turn.

                                Originally posted by Satori View Post
                                If we could get back to exalted, I am still wondering if anyone had any thoughts about whether niches and niche-protection are actually a desirable thing. My gaming group doesn't really think so.
                                I don't think so, generally. The purpose of niches is (usually) to ensure that every member of a group has something to contribute to the group's efforts, because they have important, non-overlapping skillsets. Niches really only matter in party-based games (which D&D is intended to be), and niche-oriented challenges can be overcome with a little creativity, even in those. I think it's better to build a system around the assumption that every character can, at least to some extent, play solo, and trust that players are intelligent enough to cooperate without a forced mechanical reason for relying on each other.

                                                            I really have to agree with Satori here. If you only played 4E, I could get that you've been spoiled by a game that is a little harder to break. But 3.5 has just about every problem that Exalted 2E has: much longer turns for some PCs, large disparities in power from one PC to the next, numerous abilities that preemptively end encounters, and player-versus-DM/ST optimization arms races. I'm not saying this to cast doubt or to disparage your complaints though. What have you done, in terms of house rules or agreements (possibly implicit) to make 3.5 work out for you?                                                             Regarding Satori's question about niches and niche protection go, I agree that scene-by-scene spotlight balance isn't great. As important as it is for the rules to let us, you know, make our characters as we imagine them, we shouldn't easily become "social-encounter-only guy" or "combat-or-texting gal." One of the things I like about Ex3 is the promise of easier entry into a type of play: if one or two Charms suffice to make you competent at a task, then there's less incentive to hyper-specialize, dominate encounters of your chosen type, and look for something else to do when it's another player's turn to take 17 actions or mind control a city or single-handedly manage an empire via abstract subsystems. Viable dabbling is really important, and it generally makes the game better for everyone. Winning an encounter alone is cool maybe once in a great while, but collaborating effectively - even without perfectly balanced contributions - is for me the best thing about RPGs.
