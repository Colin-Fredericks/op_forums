            2.x revised things for the better in a lot of ways. But look: the point I'm trying to make is that pre-2.5 2e was a minefield, that things you thought were going to be trivial fights wound up being murderous.

            Five guys with sledges is, to judge from the fiction, a trivial fight for an Exalt. Except, oops, the fifth guy gets an auto-surprise attack, hits your DV 0 for stupid damage, and now you're dead.

            Or maybe you just had one attacker throwing an ambush, to be at all clever and different - a sneaky rogue type, who poisoned his knives. Which hit, because DV 0 vs. surprise attacks - and a PC is dead.

            Okay, so no poison, just a couple of ninjas throwing daggers - whoops, still dead.

            Okay, so no stealth, just a couple of guys - but less than five! And they're a team, so they'll coordinate attacks. Except, shoot, the Zenith didn't bother to buy a penalty negator, figuring he could rely on his Excellency. Which he could, right up until he took eight attacks with a -4 coordination penalty, and now he's dead.

            Okay, no more people! T-rexes. We're going to fight t-rexes for a while - heck, that was even the meme for a bit, wasn't it? So we throw in a tyrant lizard, and the players annihilate it. Next time, we try two big similar monsters - except, shoot, that's all it takes for one of them to get a surprise attack when they're that big. That... doesn't make any sense, but that's what the book says - aaaaaand, yep, dead.

            We haven't even added anything that can spend Essence yet! We haven't thrown in a Dragon-Blood, who can kill you by standing near you, or a low-Essence god who happens to have Essence Bite and Principle of Motion, or heaven forbid a Sidereal with any SMA at all out of Scroll of the Monk. We've just been fighting mortals. We shouldn't need to be worried yet.

            And yet we keep dying.

            And then the players get smart. They get Reflex Sidestep Technique. They get Seven Shadows Evasion. They get Dipping Swallow Defense, and Fivefold Bulwark Stance, and Leaping Dodge Method, and Iron Skin Concentration, and Infinite (Whatever) Mastery, and they run a really solid "rely on your DV, soak it if that fails," build. And they fight as close to mote-neutral as it's possible to get, with their decision plan sufficiently automated that you comfortably summarize it on a post-it note -

            - and that's every fight for the rest of the game.

            You talk about fights to first blood and yielding and so on, but when I pick up the book, I read about Wyld Hunts that want to kill me and normal people that think Solars are demons, and then I get fights that allow trivial mortals to murder my glorious god-king unless I take a precise combination of Charms. Not everyone who played 2e played in a style that ran into this problem, but lots of people did. This isn't just a "People were running the game with the wrong style" thing - if you ran 2.0 out of the box, as it described itself, with no prior instruction, you had a high probability of a no-warning failure state.

                                            2e is the superior game for a group that's not used to Exalted and hasn't put a lot of time into learning the rules. While certainly many methods exist to 'break' the game, they aren't discovered early on by a group that isn't interested in poking around for that sort of flaw.

                                            It adapts better to a new group because you don't need to understand the system to understand what you are buying, to such a degree. 3e has a complicated initiative system which is difficult to explain and even more difficult to fully anticipate in terms of mechanical interactions. In 3e, players who are totally new to the game and have no idea what they are doing will have a hard time choosing charms because they can't really understand the value of those charms.

                                            In 2e, even though the system may be less 'tight', charms are more or less far less complicated and easy to understand. They interact with a system that the ST needs to understand but the player doesn't. They can be explained in 'plain' terms once the basic system is understood. The utility of most of the charms in the 2e system is fairly obvious and straightforward with a few notable examples.

                                            Thus, the ST will spend far less time trying to clarify the value of abilities in 2e and also far less time trying to clarify the value of available-to-be-purchased abilities. Though the game will eventually break if players are interested in breaking it, 2e holds together reasonably well to a casual group and can run for years without issues.

                                            Therefore, I would without hesitation recommend 2e above 3e when starting from scratch with new players.

                                            3e is a system for when people really get into 2e, stress test the fuck out of it, and then want a mechanically robust alternative. Those kind of players are willing to invest the time and energy into understanding the complicated mechanics themselves, so they can fully enjoy the 3e product and not feel confused or frustrated by it.

                                            Note, also, those are the people who post most often on the forums, so you may see a bit of selection bias in these replies.
