        Originally posted by Robert Vance View Post
        Hun and po are real concepts in Chinese culture. Some of the translations on that page suggest the idea that the po remains in the corpse while the hun goes on to the afterlife, although I don't know how reliable they are.
        Interestingly, the Bible likewise makes a distinction between "soul" and "spirit" that generations of pastors have allowed to be forgotten.

        According to scripture, God formed man's body from dust, breathed spirit into the body, and the man became a soul.

        A person has a body and a spirit, but does not have a soul; a person is a soul. The soul is the consciousness and identity that arises from the combination of body and spirit. This is why the word "soul" is synonymous with "person"; "five souls were lost at sea today".

        When a person dies, their spirit returns to God, their body returns to dust, and the soul (person) falls into Sheol (the metaphorical Grave; conceptual underworld) where there is no work, no pain, no understanding, no learning, and no worship. That is to say that death is a state of oblivion; a form of unconsciousness beyond mere sleep. God needs to rebuild the body and breath new life (spirit) into it before the soul can reawaken and experience continuity of consciousness and identity.


        One can see the Po soul which animates the body as roughly analogous to the animating breath of God, save that the Po soul remains with the body upon death instead of departing. The soul (consciousness/identity) of the person is analogus to the Hun soul, which figuratively descends into the Grave, though obviously the Underworld is very different from Sheol, which is more like a less permanent form of Oblivion.

            Originally posted by Erinys View Post
            In Hinduism for example, continuity of selfhood happens through the reincarnating atman and its karma, even though memories are generally lost with each reincarnation. Buddhism denies that selfhood has any reality or genuine continuity even from one day to the next while we're still alive, no matter how we feel about it.
            Finally, somebody provides an answer to that question.

            I mean, I haven't asked it yet in this thread, but that was mostly out of a sense of futility.

            The point about a reincarnating atman and karma is particularly interesting to me, even if Exalted doesn't quite have the same concepts (I would find my notion of continuing cognitive patterns to be similar).

            Particularly considering that even if a ghost or living person or whatever thought strictly in terms of their continuing consciousness, is that the only thing to be concerned about leaving behind?

            There are people who do not believe anything of them will continue to exist when they die; does that mean that all states following the moment of their death are equivalent to them? Does that person not care about their legacy, the distribution of their possessions, the conditions of their loved ones, past the point where they think they will cease to exist?

            A person who thinks their own type of personality, their conditioned responses, all that jazz, contribute something positive to Creation might think in terms analogous to their reincarnated soul being something they bequeath back unto the world; something of themselves that they leave behind to do good again. Hell, not even the world; a person with particular self-esteem can think of it simply down to the person they'll be reborn as, that that person might get something out of inheriting them that they wouldn't from anybody else, or given a brand new soul.

            When you think about it, that's kind of what goes on behind the Immaculate Philosophies views on reincarnation; upright living continuously perfects you until you can be reborn Exalted, and make some really significant contributions.

            That's something beyond just "there need to be enough souls available".
