    Finishing reading The Realm

    09-18-2019, 07:21 PM
    Okay, I’m trying to rededicate myself to some of my lapsed projects, and that’s going to start with finishing this read through The Realm. It feels a bit silly to go back to a discussion thread that has finished completely, so I’ll just go with a dedicated thread for this final… 44% of the book (Jesus Christ).

    Right, actually reading Dejis prefecture now.

    Something that I think is rather clever in the premise behind the prefecture being located in a rugged territory is not only how that lends itself to yielding construction materials, but is established as requiring a lot of the actual work; of course people living in an area with a lot of gorges and mountains would require a significant amount of infrastructure such as tunnels, bridges and decent roads, and there’s a logical backstory to why somebody with a lot of pull would be situated there to place it. One might get an impression that Mnemon preferred a home base where she could more easily manage entry, and that means the area has a basis for being heavily built up in a manner that I think stands in contrast to most instances of hill lands located within larger polities, often marginalized. I also think it constitutes an understated instance of some of the anachronisms that can exist in the setting, as I’d say that kind of regionally specialized and exported labour force would not be common in a lot of pre-modern economies. It’s a mix of elements that nicely complements the newly enriched picture of House Mnemon, particularly with how their citizens being uncommonly well-travelled contrasts with expectations that might arise from Mnemon’s haughty image.

    Having House Mnemon willing to do temple construction work for free or close enough seems like it would be a significant contributor to the Order’s wealth, although I would imagine that far more of such work is required than the House’s firms can manage, and so there would be a long waiting list for the pro bono services that might necessitate going for a more expensive bid. I like the way that Mnemon’s closeness to the Order can be presented as having a militaristic element, not only in creating a zone that others would be hard put to attack (I imagine not just from concerns about the martial prowess of the monks but of the bad image it might give to another House to attack a concentration of them), but in furthering the portrayal of the Imperial Mountain as a source of unruly spirits and strange monsters. I think both of those things are a nice way to make the heartland of one of the strongest Houses (especially in the probable traditional mindset of players) into a dynamic frontier territory.

    For the city of Mnemon-Darjilis, I first of all like the artwork depicting it for the inclusion of a bunch of Egyptian-style pyramids among the architecture; it’s a very vivid reminder that the Blessed Isle isn’t all analogies to imperial China, and it feels particularly fitting the House Mnemon with how it’s been described as a group that encourages its members to elaborate their faith and artistic style with foreign influences. It’s also a nice realisation of the obvious image of a group of stern looking Mnemon Dynasts standing in dominance over toiling peasants, flanked by grandiose buildings, and with the Imperial Mountain dominating their own background. I don’t think that I had noticed before how the House description in What Fire Has Wrought was one of the few (if not only) to not detail their home manse; I suppose it didn’t feel like an absence when most of the rest referred back to where and how the founder was interred. So it’s nice to have a description of Mnemon’s residence; another pyramid is not what I would have expected, but I think it could be used to very effectively set a scene of somebody journeying to meet with her, of being brought up to this particularly imposing structure. I appreciate the breakdown of the components of the city’s construction industry to provide a firm sense of their specific services and income streams (or precisely what scions might be placed in charge of, if coming at it from that end). I also rather like the manner in which the heavy presence of the Immaculate Order is given an economic component in the realm of purely devotional work; it feels like something that would be very inventive on the parts of the inhabitants, and I could see it corresponding with how the Order in residence likely has greatly reduced expenses (when the ruling House does most of your construction and maintenance for free, and probably makes frequent generous donatives besides, paying people to participate in rituals full time probably seems like a good pursuit when you’re so flush with cash). I find it true to life of religious organisation that might not be familiar to a western background, and I think it helps to vary the Order up a bit.

    I particularly like what that last paragraph does for emphasizing the point that being the core of one of the most powerful Houses does not make it secure and static; that Mnemon’s power invites subtle challenge by way of scheming and intrigue by its very nature, and that her strong ties with the Order are a very clear target for enemies that would want to undermine that power in a time before war breaks out. Indeed, the degree to which her security is intertwined with, even dependent upon, a religious organisation could be seen as more of a liability, especially from that line about causing some kind of embarrassment; there could be actions undertaken in the prefecture, things the House is associated with or provoked into doing, that might not repel a strictly pragmatic ally but are unacceptable for one with a moral and spiritual emphasis. I’m particularly thinking of how that might be connected to instances of scions bringing back foreign heterodoxies, such as that sample armourer who designs based on inspirations from opium dreams. I could see a compelling story in Mnemon being placed in an awkward position of having to choose between House members viewed as too unorthodox and maintaining the good graces of the Immaculate Order.

    Hmm, how to handle the sidebars… I’ll just take them as they come up, and process them as little chunks. At the outset, I’ll say that it helps for the Blessed Isle’s status as a setting and narrative generator to show that it can have locations with much more narrow qualities, as things like short steps in a more episodic story or quirky places to have come from (and maybe occasionally the seed of something that an enterprising player could build upon in the course of a game).

    For Hawk Prefecture, I’m interested in that impression of a place that cultivated a reputation that could serve as the basis for official grants to give their community a specialized function, and their background of turning out serious-minded professional civil servants sounds like a fitting setting for a Dead Poets Society style story. While I’m often critical of the idea of the Exalted being willing and capable of using physical force to root themselves in a situation, it’s not a story that never has a place, and I find the novelty of this Tepet survivor going against the grain in teaching carpe diem to the students to be compelling in its own right, and a clever way of presenting that capacity for the Exalted to override social norms in a manner that plays off of the Realm’s mores specifically. It helps that the implied trajectory of the narrative is one in which the local authorities are seeking other powers to help their cause against this unacceptable teacher.

    Hmm, actually, looking at Mavinyos, some of these places feel a bit less like settings with narrow applications and more like First Edition or Chronicles of Darkness style plot hooks, just ones that are the jumping off points for whole settings in their own right rather than being attached at the periphery of a larger description. For the city itself, I’ve always got a soft-spot for stories of communities thriving off of salvage from coastal wrecks, so I like seeing an instance of that in the western Isle (I note that it doesn’t actually attribute the wreckage to luring, as features in a lot of folklore), and the whole thing feels like it logically holds together as a den of thieves. I don’t think I’ve ever considered before how a lone wolf thief might upend an established crime syndicate by refusing to give a cut and being able to evade the consequences, and the intertwining of that with local politics and general rising imperial tensions feels like it could give a smaller story larger implications. It’s also a clever use of limited information; it’s not just that we’re not given a clear indication of who the Magpie is, but that there are neighbours that the aggrieved crime lord might blame is not merely a suggested identity (or at least benefactor), but an element that might make the thief’s identity ultimately irrelevant (if tensions boiling over are not the point of the targeted burglary in the first place).

    Winter Blossom Prefecture packs quite a bit into a small space. The brief description of the cliffs and illicit cave shrines makes for a very lovely sounding area, it’s got an intriguing backstory that adds to indigenous conflicts and potential ongoing projects on the Isle as well as a basis for local idiosyncrasies, and it’s got a distinct and unnerving basis for fearing the return of open war to the Isle in the form of fighting to control a major strategic pass providing openings for the dead. I especially like that sense of a probably rather old Exalt who has been around long enough to have participated in a major act of heroism in the far past and then moved on to other pursuits that jeopardize old bonds; it’s not an uncommon narrative, but I’m getting a sense of scale from it that plays nicely into the lifespans of the Exalted and how those can be emphasized by the length of history.

    All right, finish off for now with Chanos prefecture. It might seem a bit redundant to have too rugged, hilly regions in a row, but really they’re quite different; Dejis is scenic inland hill territory, where Chanos is blasted, grey coastal hill territory. That’s some western Ireland landscape in there, and it speaks to me. I like how Sesus takes that in to make a symbolic statement about being fires sheltered through harsh winters, and it makes for a very vivid image of maintaining lines of communication at times of the year where the climate makes being outside a real danger. I’m not sure if the coast across from the Isle of Voices was ever given much detail, but it helps to emphasize the mystique of the place to have the locals distrustful of anything that washes ashore, particularly with the contrast provided by Mavinyos above in terms of the usual relationship seaside communities have with the sea’s bounty (also makes the prefecture seem like an ideal locale for at least one Innsworth-esque community). Miner’s Gambit sounds like a neat little local entertainment and conveyance of how people adapt to being stuck indoors (something I think Exalted has often come up with very interesting things for), although the most interesting thing about it is an example in which Dynasts are influenced by peasant culture, as well as a point of dispute across generations in a society that is usually so conforming.

    Chanos reminds me a lot of the depiction of cities from the Haslanti League, with the manufacturing focus and emphasis on stark economic disparity. Seeing it in these terms, I don’t know, suddenly I’m feeling as though I was far too harsh on that person who once insisted that Haslanti cities had a distinctly Victorian character… AdamantineRapier, that was the one. The settlement in a crescent shape with the districts progressing sequentially in affluence also reminds me of the capital of Valua from Skies of Arcadia. The city might not entirely stand out on its own characteristics, but it at least has some vivid sources of inspiration to me for its look and tone; I do think that makes its lavish and colourful Lapis Pillar Temple stand out (not least because I think that’s a very distinct structure for an Immaculate temple to take). The name of the Palace of Burning Wind also gives me a nice image of its fire raining power, playing off of disturbing recent trends in major fire disasters. The Hall of Excellence Untested makes for a very stark instance of a particularly harsh primary school, but I find something strangely endearing in the idea of the Sesus legions participating in their drills, and how such a thing is a spectacle for the local commoners; those House legions have always come across as one of the more brutish elements of the imperial military, so I like this idea that at their home base there’s something to give them a bit of local popularity. It’s another thing that feels very Victorian industrial port town. And the spacious sewers and storm drains plus the plausible excuse for going about in concealing cloaks is a fun little touch for making the substance of the town feel like an extension of the character of the ruling House. As for the Chanos Flicker, well, I’ll come back to that.

    I’ve got to say, while it says that Sesus needs to contend with the uncommon experience of their home prefecture being under the authority of an appointee from another House, I don’t quite feel as though the scenario is used to its fullest. Oh sure, the story of Ragara Nova tricking them into thinking that he’s less capable and more pliant than he is fun enough, and I do quite like the idea of somebody handling the espionage House by turning their extreme proclivity for spying against them by presenting the façade of a sieve that they trip over themselves flocking to, but it feels like a scenario that is ripe for the tension of conflicting authorities that is not followed through on by Sesus feeling like they’re in control.

    As far as I can find, the Isle of Voices has never received very much detail compared to the sorcerous academy it houses, and the Heptagram certainly looms over the entirety of its write-up here. Yet, I find that this level of detailing gives a bit more character to the island and the institution; the Heptagram feels a bit more lively and ominous when we’re told about the travails that surround it, the kinds of unnerving challenges that need to be navigated for those who traffic with it and the places that the students of the occult might venture for education, experimentation or recreation. With things like the monsters that lurk on the cliffs and coastal waters, the phantoms that surround those who approach and the prodigies hidden amidst the geography, it feels very Earthsea’s Roke by way of Tanith Lee (with maybe a hint of Dagobah and Ahch-To); I like the elder tree that provides visions of one’s death particularly.

    Not much to say about the Dragon’s Blanket sidebar, other than it signposting the northern Isle as a place where bandits and the dispossessed might find refuge.

    Okay, so the Chanos Flicker. On the one hand, I do like the description of how the city’s environment lends atmosphere to the actions of such a thief, and it plays nicely with the name to create a very tactile element to such a figure. However, it does feel a touch repetitive coming just after the Magpie; I’m not saying that there’s only room on the Blessed Isle for one mysterious thief defying the system and evading authorities, but it would help if they weren’t right next to each other and harassing the same House. A sidebar elaborating the mystery with a selection of optional identities is decidedly not to my taste, but I’ll forgive it here when the book has otherwise been so good about that kind of thing. As for those options, a probable Solar Exalted is fine, if a bit obligatory (although the description of being compelled by seeing Sesus treasures from afar elevates it a bit), and the ancient First Age servitor that can’t tell the difference between its own charges and other mystical valuables is rather neat (particularly for the description of its composition). The real standout there would be Chanos Tsunbal, with the interplay between what is and is not said; the manner in which we’re introduced to a figure from a fallen Great House and just hit with the bombshell that this person inexplicably finds himself in the Time of Tumult, with language even suggesting the possibility of alternate realities rather than temporal shenanigans, nicely topped off with the point that Tsunbal has unidentified powers and is being communicated with by an unknown agent. There’s enough there for one to tailor an explanation, while the basic feel of the originating mystery is vivid enough that one could very well get by without one. I also like how it’s wrapped in a bit of personal and political intrigue; that Tsunbal has been plopped down in an unfamiliar world and could go looking for an answer as to why, but his own priority is placed more on taking control of a city that he sees as his by right. Heck, even the point about promises of vengeance interplays with the matter of how he doesn’t even have a sense of what it would be vengeance for, and is not immediately interested in finding out. Honestly, including this as one of the possible options kind of redeems the whole sidebar for me.

    And I’ll take a break there. I feel as though I’m getting back into the flow of things; part of why I lapsed was from a sense that a chapter of such catalogued setting information would be a bit overwhelming, but I’m being reminded of how it can all serve as a very engaging read.

          Next up is Pangu Prefecture, which introduces itself well with an illustration of bustle around a canal leading out to a port. The activity, architecture and colour scheme (lots of warm browns) reminds me a lot of the town from that episode of The Last Airbender where the group ran into those pirates. I like the variety of wares and modes of peddling them on display, particularly the drover hauling a cart of lush “herbs”, and the junks in the background stand out nicely with their wine colour. Nice way to show that focus is shifting somewhere warmer and more verdant.

          I liked how Cynis was given a new emphasis on agricultural produce, so I’m glad to see that depicted in this expansion on their home prefecture, both as a source of luxurious plenty and an advantage they might hold over legions that could be suddenly starved. That the home of this House would have an emphasis on artful landscaping is to be expected, but I like the reminder of how people across social strata contribute to it, with the reference to couturiers and perfumers even giving me a sense that there’s a certain higher level of economic prosperity here to support such business. I’m amused by the matter of how Immaculate grievances with Cynis might include making places that are too nice, drawing spirits to idleness and open revelry in a manner that needs to be reprimanded. All of that is starkly contrasted with the human cost, which I find to be conveyed with tasteful brevity and a few named locales to focus attention on.

          Pangu city makes for an distinct take on a Canton System, what with the Realm not restricting foreign trade in general but limiting and concentrating the traffic of the Guild specifically, and I think it’s interesting that it’s a monopoly that Cynis has to agitate to actively maintain. I like the description of how the wealth brought in by foreign trade results in the city being continuously built over, as I find that reminiscent of the history of Belgian city Gent, it makes for a very vivid punctuation of how such port cities can be so dynamic. And they’ve still got that cosmopolitan vibe I’ve been attached to with the city possessing foreign architectural and fashion styles.

          Yes, that impression of prosperity across the board is enhanced by the description of the city’s various fine amenities. It does help to round out the portrayal of House Cynis to suggest that they’re not the only ones enjoying the benefits of their value for beauty and luxury, and it’s nice how their affinity for plant life is strewn throughout those attractions. I like the idea of the estuary being so heavy with boating and that it would require some skill to weave the vessels between one another, and lots of islets covered in buildings gives it a nice Adriatic look, so it all makes for a very strong backdrop for a lively drug marketplace. I think it’s fun to have instances of mortal families in specialized service to the Dynasty, particularly when they compete viciously with one another as the manufacturers of anagathics do. And while I think that some of the reminders about how organized crime on the Blessed Isle falls under Dynast supervision is a bit redundant with information in the setting chapter, I enjoy the point about how a city that brings in such a mass of goods and is so steeped in business provides an opportunity for a few syndicates that are big enough to operate independently.

          Numinous Rolling Wave Dominion, I’d been wondering about this addition to the Blessed Isle, especially its distinctly long and thin boundaries stretching from Imperial Mountain to City. Makes sense that the answer would be about maintaining a special administrative zone to monitor and preserve the conduit of Essence flowing from the Elemental Pole to the manse controlling the Sword of Creation. If the quadrants of the Blessed Isle are microcosms for the Directions of Creation, this Dominion feels like it serves as a kind of essential microcosm of the Isle itself, giving me a pristine, even Arcadian image of finely managed communities and low density of construction and the spiritually affirming beauty of the landscape. The description of how the social layers relate to one another in particular feels as though it could come from some imperial agrarian propaganda, especially that sense of the farmers selling directly to Dynastic estates. It also sounds like a very nice aspiration for outcastes in service to the Realm for their retirements and possible social advancement, although I imagine that being the prefect here has a very narrow margin of error if peasant villages can be dispossessed just for threatening the local geomancy with errant barns. This wouldn’t be the first place where the Great Houses display a willingness to plunder the resources and overturn traditional policies of the Realm for short term gain, but it’s especially going against the grain to jeopardize the sanctity of the Realm Defence Grid. A little description of the Imperial River Basin also appeals to me, since information about how such places accumulate populations has been a recent interest of mine, and the kind of legendary status they tend to acquire complements being placed alongside Numinous Rolling Waves literal power nicely.

          Now, this Valley of the Ancients… the Egyptian Valley of Kings is so iconic that it makes a lot of waves in popular culture, and it wouldn’t even be the first time it pops up in Exalted since it’s probably a major influence of the Scavenger Lands. And yet, this is more than just placing such a thing in the heart of the Realm, it’s something to capture some of the interesting contradictions inherent to the relationship between the Scarlet Dynasty and the First Age. Even if they’re appropriating them (although I’d suspect more than a few Dynasts have a better idea of the identities of the interred), it still speaks to how the artistic and technical achievements of the ancient Solars can remain a source of awe and fascination to the modern Dragon Blooded. At the same time, a willingness to build their homes there seems more than just a wish to reside amid the monuments to bygone glory, but as another symbolic gesture of dominance and usurpation. And yet, they’re still kind of subjecting themselves to the memory of the Solars, not just in things like admiration for constructions from their ethos and digging for the products of their artifice, but the degree to which retirement here takes over their lives. On that note, I’m quite tickled by the notion of this divine hero retirement community that really ultimately serves as a kind of gilded cage for its inhabitants; the idea that a place really built up to seek residence in having an ulterior motive is nothing new, but I think it plays off nicely with the personality of the Scarlet Empress to make the catch not something overtly sinister but just the fact that the cost of living here is prohibitively high, and you’ve got some of the worst neighbours. It hits a lot of the same notes to me as that Lunar Shangri-la kind of place, where it’s an isolated community but isn’t static and boring, with things like changes to one house requiring adjustments to all of them, tomb delving, handling some of the strongest hungry ghosts and general small town high society intrigue. I want to think that murder has some huge implications here, because it seems to me that rendering any one dwelling uninhabited has dire implications for everybody, since adjustments likely have to be made across the board even if a building is demolished entirely (which is no small cost on its own). Heh, the Festival of Remembrance even more obviously makes that point about how they’re inadvertently tying themselves ever deeper into a kind of symbolic service to the Celestial Exalted; I never would have expected any part of the Realm to engage in ritual of celebrating and venerating the Solars and Lunars, with pride of place in taking up their roles in narrative, even if they do save pride by filing the serial numbers off, and it’s all delightful. The illustration is a little bit sparse, but that in itself kind of makes the point about what life in the valley is like, and the manses themselves are very attractive in their little models. And for the sidebar on the Ancients, this is a case where I don’t mind it being direct about them actually being fallen Solars (although I’m often a little bit iffy about books directly addressing readers in a colloquial manner), but I like the descriptions of the little tall tales that have accumulated around some Solars, that might invite players to figure out what the truth behind those feats and how they were achieved was; I think that’s a very good way to handle portrayal of Celestial and other Exalted as figures of ancient and somewhat embellished legend, allowing such stories to not limit themselves to strict capabilities from the mechanics and providing some encouragement to come up with novel powers or applications of the familiar ones.

          One last thing I’ll say is that I don’t think it would have been amiss to have been given a few profiles for some of the local authorities... although I suppose that such has been absent thus far anyway.

          Hmm, not too much, but I’ll leave it there for the moment; if the Scarlet Prefecture is next, I want to give it my full attention. For now I’ll just say that I’m liking this approach of starting at the centre and spiralling around, I think it should make for easy referencing in future, and conveys a nice sense of following a gradually transitioning vignette around the Blessed Isle.

              Rather busy weekend.


              The Scarlet Prefecture has definitely gotten a bit of a revamp, although I find it to be in an understated way that’s a bit hard to articulate. First and Second Edition each portrayed it a bit differently between them, but the impression I get here is that it’s being rendered a bit more naturally formed and densely populated, less of the pristine backyard for Dynasts living near the Imperial City. Indeed, with the description of settlements having accumulated around Exalted estates and service industries placed along the roads, it gives me an impression of being a model of Edo period Japan nestling in the heart of the Blessed Isle, and I also like how it calls back to the origin story of the Scarlet Empire. I recall times in the past where there’s been a comparison between Nexus and the Imperial City in which they possessed the same wealth but the latter had it much more static and concentrated at the top, but I think it makes a more inviting picture to give the place tension arising from how it provides opportunities for commoners to make their fortune.

              That also plays off nicely with the new life breathed into the patricians here, building off of their increased prominence and independence in the ministries and that plot hook about wanting to establish more autonomy for themselves in What Fire Has Wrought (was it anywhere besides Kingfisher Swift’s chapter fiction, I can’t recall). I can see a lot of narrative potential in the point about how the perceived need to band together is grating against a long history of internecine conflict, especially for Dynast players; a character could probably make a name for themselves by securing a staff of skilled administrators by effectively pressing on bad blood to prevent them from banding together, an area that a lot of House elites might not be so focused on. Alternatively, an outside might secure a major power bloc on the Isle for helping to smooth over differences and win over clans with a more longstanding loyalty to a Great House to ensure that the patricians of the Imperial City are a force to be reckoned with. In any case, I like the inventiveness of how they’re smuggling their own troops into their estates.

              Nice to see a dedicated description of wilderness reserves for upper class hunting, that kind of thing stands out to me as a way in which the wealthy can shape landscapes throughout history. It seems as though the Scarlet Prefecture is big enough to allow that kind of thing without many farmers feeling as though they’re being crowded out, but holding dangerous beasts for the sake of providing challenging hunts for the Exalted might be a source of contention. There are times when I’ve felt as though the Scarlet Empress is depicted as hardly ever leaving her palace, so I’m happy to see her given a personal reserve, and it lends a bit more to her sorceress character for it to be stocked with some particularly strange sounding entities (I guess that keeping such things so close to the capital is a privilege of being the absolute monarch). It’s come up before without me mentioning it, but with three of them here I’ll say that I’ve been liking these manses being given distinct names as a way of conveying powers and unique characteristics. That they’re prominent in this prefecture lends a bit to me for that idea of their focus being on something other than the patricians, as well as a possible asset that they might make use of to safeguard their interests from the Great Houses if they can snake them out via friendly outcastes, even if they obviously have utility as a means of the Dynasts overwhelming the prefecture or turning it into one of the most fiercely contested battlegrounds. Between this and Numinous Rolling Waves, it would appear that they’re laying down an idea that Mnemon, Ragara and Sesus are prominent in the manse game, I wonder if that might be developed in future. In any case, I like this being yet another front in which young new House V’neef has to figure out how to manage its assets on the fly.

              Aaaand we at least get one prefect with a return of good old Mnemon Bata. I’ve always had a fondness for him, or at least for interesting things he illustrated about his mother and her relationship with unExalted Dynasts. Mnemon has a bit more going for her now, but he’s still got that fun element from having gone from possibly the most boring job in the empire to somebody having fun making his relatives beg him to do things he was already on board for. I even see shades of Mnemon’s sentiment that her son is respectable but might need to be killed if he keeps pushing his look in the line about how he doesn’t know the risks he’s taking, although it’s probably just about how he’s highly placed in what is going to be hotly contested territory.

              Now, the Imperial City itself. First of all there’s the architectural quality, which I think consolidates a number of things I’ve liked in previous descriptions of the city. For instance, I’ve often thought that the First Edition core gave it one of the strongest senses of being constructed from unusual materials at the behest of demigods, and the whole premise that they need to continuously build upwards and over streets is both a fun stylized rendering of how crowded cities have historically been as well as a logical extrapolation of how a city might be confined by something such as dependence on a mystical resource or location. I think the latter is a bit less extreme here, makes the place feel a bit more navigable (more room for parades and festivals at least), and the latter has been expanded considerably from just buildings of marble and obsidian with things like containment of First Age devices and the public lighting. I think that the Imperial City has often captured the sense of being the centre of the strongest empire in the world in its scope but been somewhat bare in its monumental features, and that’s more than made up for here with things like huge statues flanking the entry gates. The thing I really love is the addition of stolen treasures and monuments from the corners of the empire and general conquest, to really emphasize the kind of power that they’ve abused and give the place some authoritarian flair; I like the mosaic made from ground up thrones particularly. I think it’s clever how the Palace of the Deliberative is made more multicoloured to reflect the greater variety provided for the garb of its members (and I’m glad how the structure is tied back to House Mnemon specifically), while the range of services surrounding them strike me as useful as a means of clandestinely approaching the place or supplies that can be manipulated around it. I get why the detail of the Deliberative having once been purged en masse by the Scarlet Empress was removed, but it was something I was fond of, so I appreciate at least having the memory of it preserved in the garrison at the Strix Tower standing as a tacit threat by her against the senators; it allows for the possibility that the reason there’s never been such a massacre is that no Deliberative has ever felt secure enough to chance their arm with repeatedly overturning the veto.

              I suppose that if the prior sections gave the City some more open spaces for monument, ceremony and the housing of the elite, it needs to be balanced out by making the commoner sections sound more cramped and segregated than ever. The same goes for the descriptions of residences set aside for others who were personally close to her, which I find to reinforce the sense I got before that her forming intimate relations was given more emphasis in a way that I found a bit more humanising; I like that her having had such is prevented from falling away as a distant abstract by giving her a still living ex-husband and actually referencing a mortal child for once. Its overall literal and narrative position hasn’t changed, and I find that it doesn’t belabour the point about them. I notice that the garden in which the Manse resides has been changed from an open field of red flowers to a more enclosed space of tall trees, and I wonder if that holds any deeper significance than just a change of scenery; I also take the implication that, while squat, the Manse has been made somewhat larger and more imposing than its previous description of standing one storey off the ground. The new life injected into the Imperial Palace especially.

I think that, even in-character, the question of what becomes of gods in response to major environmental changes is a bit of an elephant in the room. Yu-Shan at least has the convenience of presenting sections of the divine city as degenerating into slums, but Creation lacks an obvious place for them to end up, and I like how that is made a central focus here; that a lot of gods find themselves without anything to care for and are stuck between trying to dodge around Immaculates (who in this case might be seen to have a valid point in preventing disruption and competition among established hierarchies of gods and relationships with their communities) or stuck playing out defunct roles. It’s a situation that makes the appearance of a politically significant literal dragon pop; it’s one thing for such a dramatic and iconic monster to swoop down and demand tribute from the locals in the style of Tanith Lee’s Flat Earth, it’s another for them to serve as a focal point for disenfranchised spirits. I find it to be an effective use of how elementals grow in power over time as compared to gods, that an elemental dragon can be something new and powerful to shake things up, while still part of the spiritual ecology (I do think the last line of that paragraph is a bit awkwardly written though, it sounds like setting up different sides of a point that are actually just the same point). You’d expect it to be a focus of ghosts, but I think it both compounds the pathos of the gradual environmental breakdown by having a lot of the ghosts wanting to move on but not knowing how to, and complements the capacity for being an area where traditions break down by them openly offering services to the living and seeking alliance with the dragon. And I’ve got a soft spot for deserts being homes to dangerous ghosts and spirits that has some kind of fortress or monastery resting at its border dedicated to keeping them in check, nicely connected to the current political concerns by having a lot of those stationed there going off for a more attractive service. The approach to manses leaves some interesting ruins to dot the landscape as well as what I find one of the stronger instances of House Mnemon talents being put to use (being able to save a manse under threat feels like a more demanding feat even than building an entirely new one), and the Well of Holy Fire intrigues me with its collection of ambiguous features in a state of decay and the implication that some sinister entity is operating around it. The Well actually feels like something that the Blessed Isle could use a bit more of, things that feel as though they’d be ancient beyond human history and emphasize that it’s more than just the big continent in the middle of the world, it was the original homeland of the gods and possible focus of other powers.

                                  As for Hualli and Grip, I think it’s always nice to include a few odder Exalted and especially among the Dragon Blooded given how they’re such a larger group that can cover a lot of outliers, and they’re well matched to such a tumultuous corner of the map as this. I like how Hualli is more than just a beastperson with an especially vivid appearance (I would still picture him resembling Kass the Rito from Breath of the Wild) but that he’s certainly got some stories to tell from being possibly more well-travelled than a lot of Celestial Exalted, which might also really emphasize commitment to his mission on the Blessed Isle that could put a lot of local peasants at ease with him (I’m suddenly thinking that might have been part of why he was received as a celebrity rather than a figure of scorn, although I guess even in a society that would generally reject beastfolk some of them are just way too pretty or exotic looking). Grip unfortunately can’t help but feel a little bit dull beside that, but I think she plays off well with my feeling from the Legion of Silence write-up that it gave the members more agency and humanity lying behind their inscrutable façade, to have her feel empowered by Exaltation to reject the life she was brought up for and apply her gifts to one more focused on peace, contemplation and beauty.

                                  I’ve been seeing glimpses of this “A Memory of Empire” sidebar the last few times I’ve looked around here and it intrigues me, so I’ll go straight for that.

                                  Hmm… honestly, my feelings are a bit mixed. On the one hand, I like the idea of an ethnic group asserting that the Blessed Isle was once home to an empire functioning separate from the Shogunate until their terrible downfall as a result of Exaltation rate having dramatically dropped off, who survive as pastoral nomads. It’s a nice quirk in the setting’s history to suggest that the major government of the world hasn’t always reigned from the Blessed Isle, I like an inclusion of a state’s failure coming from the potential for Terrestrial bloodlines to fail (possibly the kind of thing that haunts the Great Houses), and that kind of enduring national resentment of a group given a raw deal feels fitting as something that could be found even in the Realm’s heartlands. However, the manner in which it phrases these things in terms of heresy and blasphemy takes me a bit out of it when I don’t feel as though there’s a clear sense of the nature of those things here. I’m all for ambiguity and leaving gaps to be filled in, but this feels too much to me like an omission (when it’s described as a basis for active persecution, I feel as though I need to know what the Immaculates are targeting), and placed alongside the more clearly stated elements it weakens the subject of the sidebar to me.

                                  Okay, Justiciar Prefecture. My, the actual place itself is short. I’m feeling some more mixed feelings about this one; I get that some prefectures would be fairly placid and not distinguish themselves from their neighbours, and don’t think that’s inherently unworthy of focus, but it does create a bit of an uphill battle to make it interesting. Fair enough that it’s described as being distinguished by a lower Immaculate presence that makes it happen to be a region with a higher than usual capacity for heresy, but I don’t really get why. I can even accept the idea that there’s no deeper underlying reason behind it, if it was just a situation of “logistics happens to be playing out in a manner that leaves this place overlooked and an emergent property of that allows it to foster deviance”, but I can’t help but feel as though such a conceit would feel stronger if it was text.

                                  The city of Justiciar has a lot more going for it. If this is supposed to be a portion of the Isle that is a bit more uniform and uninteresting with lower development, I think it’s a neat idea that one of the prefectures would specialize in providing administrative services for its neighbours. It touches on elements of specialization that I’m liking in this rendition of the Realm, as well as appealing to familiar matters of rural areas sometimes having problems getting much government service. I also like how this shapes the urban culture towards being very crepuscular with the secondary industry being service for the bureaucrats on their off-hours, as well as how their demanding schedules forms the basis for a thriving dining scene. It uses the way that business would grow around a primary industry of paper pushing to make a distinct city in an otherwise unremarkable prefecture.

                                  Yuktata confuses me a bit, however. We’re suddenly introduced to the idea that various forms of administrative malfeasance here are so common that they stir the wrath of a powerful goddess of justice to the point of her physically lashing out. The official response to both of these things is for the monks to throw major festivals to her on a weekly basis? Some of this feels at odds with how the institutions of the Realm are generally portrayed as dealing with problem officers and especially with the gods.

                                  Well, confused may be a bit of an extreme term; it’s not hard for me to put together a functional explanation for all of this. I’ve already presupposed that the western quadrant of the Blessed Isle is somewhat more impoverished, less infrastructurally developed and with less accommodating geography (I privately refer to it as the Connacht of the Blessed Isle). So with that combined with some other things said about the prefecture and the city, there’s a basis for thinking that we’re out in the boonies where they need to make their own rules a bit; hence a number of prefectures outsourcing their administration to a consolidated centre. That much concentration might be a natural breeding ground for a lot of graft and corruption, enough to provoke a local deity offended by those things, and be a bit difficult to effectively root out with the limited oversight they’re given. And if the local Immaculates are simply underfunded, they might be forced to cope with such a thing in the best way that they can even if it might invite censure from their superiors. I can imagine such an explanation, and I think it covers most of the bases, but I wouldn’t mind such a thing being more overt in the text, it would make the section read a bit cleaner to me. Although I’m now wondering if I initially underestimated Yuktata, and her being referred to as the regional goddess of justice is meant to imply a spirit more on the level with somebody like Ahlat, tricky for even the Realm to restrain; in fairness, the optics might be bad when the spirit that you’re opting to wrestle into submission is one who was acting so provocatively because so much injustice was rampant within your borders. For that matter, if her purview had that scope, I suppose she would be being pushed by more than just what’s going on in this city.

                                  Anyway, moving on from that we’ve got a prefect who would seem to further evince it being a bit of a wild west out here, both with feeling a need to have such a protective detail and with the fact that such private troops ruffle so many feathers with the local official police; I think those kinds of stories of jurisdictional friction crossed with outright physical violence (ala Tammany Hall New York) are often a lark. And if Iselsi felt distinctly absent from their home prefecture, I’m very gratified to see them display a presence in a former holding so that they’re in a prime position to strike unawares. I especially like a bit of a nod to previous depictions making them very prominent in the Order with one living under the cover of being a monk and using sand garden drawings to pass coded information and instructions to her network that can easily and unobtrusively be destroyed.

                                  For the artwork, it’s a gorgeous depiction of that kind of rocky landscape for a start. I think the building has a mix of architectural influences that makes it look familiar but not quite like any one real building, and I like how the overall picture is very brightly coloured but the scheme on the building makes it stand out distinctly from the surroundings. There’s also some very nice lighting effects, from glows reflecting off the surface of the streams and river and the look of a slight mist rising up and creating a distorting effect. The man on the raft gives a sense of scale for just how large the construction is, and the impression I get of the scene is of an idyllic, out of the way part of the Realm that nevertheless features a monumental reminder of the majesty of its rulers; it’s not just here as an expression of piety, but for when people drift by on this river or turn up to do their laundry they’ll witness yet another representation of the strength and glory of the Exalted.

                                  Gonna finish up for now with the next places of interest sidebar. With Brilliant Autumn Shades Prefecture, I’m liking how often we’re getting prefectures that are home to creepy ghost stories or similar dark secrets. It’s nice to see a settled outcaste be the origin of such this time, as well as just a bit of representation for their impact in general. Murex Blaze has got a nice mix of establishing herself with the local gentry through generosity and favourable marriages (it’s a fun turning on the head of a typical dynamic, where the nouveau riche might want to build credibility by marrying into the older families, where here it’s an Exalt showing up and the old money are super juiced to welcome her children) with copious applications of evil magic. The idea of the big fish in the small backwater building up and maintaining their prominence with black sorcery is another fond motif to me, the whole blood magic and cannibalism angle invokes a lot of freaky folklore and some compelling stories based off such (for me that includes some nice Mage: the Awakening stuff), and while the narrative of the foreigner who comes in with exotic weirdness that they use to distort and bend the local natural order has some problematic connotations, it’s still got a lot of merits and pedigree. I find something gratifying in how the thorough destruction of the family was spread out over several stages, and as elsewhere I’m really feeling this approach of presenting hooks surrounding lingering ghosts and their chicanery in terms of the local rumour and hearsay (with just a dash of another resident sorcerer who has his own agendas). It’s more sophisticated than just “this may or may not be true”; there’s definitely something going on, but recognition of what it is is distorted through the lens of observers who may be ignorant and a sinister legacy that people will naturally attribute it to. You combine that with descriptions of other phenomena on the Isle and this could be anything from “things are exactly as people describe them” to “it’s still ghosts, but sharing stories has distorted the facts” all the way over to “there’s an unrelated root to it all, but people are going to reason it emerges from the dark local history”. It’s a very interesting way to put oddities and mysteries such as this in the Realm.

                                  You go from that to something much more grounded and direct in Damson Prefecture. I like seeing House Tepet apply their modus operandi for buffer groups in the Threshold to at least one of their problem holdings on the Blessed Isle in settling experienced soldiers in an area plagued with banditry. I also think it gives the much beleaguered House a much needed little victory to say that, even if they had to sell their stakes in the territory to House Ragara, they were sure to take their devoted former soldiers with them to both shore up their own forces for the future and thumb their noses at the new lease holders. It’s fun to see Ragara wrong-footed, and I like the mixed reaction they have to the event in the form of being annoyed at the loss of people who were integral to keeping the peace but also being glad to see the back of ones so loyal to a rival and nervous around the ones that remained. It seems like it would be fun to play a Ragara having dealings with such freeholders while uncertain of their priorities.

                                  And Nine Envies Prefecture would appear to be one in which Cynis concentrates a lot of its agricultural operation, which I feel as though makes it a very unusual prefecture in terms of its economics and demographics while being unremarkable in its environment, a little island of dramatically distinct agrarian practices lying unobtrusively among the rushes (at least barring the occasional exodus or revolt of the disproportionately large slave populace). That’s a solid plot hook with them dealing with the aftermath of a recent uprising that has one leader held captive and a lot of power with the free ones who might push things into a major insurgency. Gavenne of Grieve being locked up looks like a nice focus for stories of devotees plotting to release… them (I’m just now noticing that none of the leaders are explicitly gendered, and as far as I know the names aren’t clear indicators), or fallout of Cynis putting on some very public reprimand. While Lark seems to present the interesting dilemma of what to do with the Exalted slave in the process of defiance; official protocol probably holds that the priority should be placed on bringing them on for imperial service while the local Cynis would probably prefer quickly and quietly eliminating somebody who is kicking up such a fuss, and it seems ripe for representatives of the Arbiters to be present to insist that Lark be taken in alive (a tension that can be compounded if the reps are monks with their traditional antipathy for the ways of Cynis). It’s a nice little thorn to stick in the side of their organisation.
