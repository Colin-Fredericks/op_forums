    Originally posted by JohnDoe244 View Post
    But... well unless a Dev is going to chime in and say "The Unconquered Sun is now objectively immoral", then the immorality of demon summoning remains less than self-evident, in my opinion.
    As a dev, I am going to chime in and say that "objectively immoral" is a meaningless expression in terms of Exalted-the-published-setting. The game explicitly and overtly rejects objective morality. Players may bring their views of objective morality to the table; they may construct characters who believe in one form or another of objective morality; these things are desirable! The setting will also attempt to present the harms done by people and institutions in a clear and unobstructed way, to avoid apologia for such harm in the real world. Much of the interest of Exalted play comes from the clash of ideologies that comes forward when PCs have the power to impose their ideologies on the world around them. But the game is not going to decide for you what's right and what's wrong, nor how to resolve moral complexities and ethical dilemmas.

    So, no, I am not going to say as a dev that the Unconquered Sun is objectively moral or objectively immoral.

    Originally posted by JohnDoe244 View Post

    Kant's categorical imperative is all very well as a personal ethical lodestar, but it is fallacious to assume that desiring that your moral stance would be a universal law means that it is a universal law. You argue that if everyone says no to the Deathlords' bargain, then the Deathlord will be forever stymied; but desiring for that to be the case will not make it so, because the people of Creation are written to be like people in the real world. People in the real world make all manner of selfish and destructive bargains for any number of reasons less dire than "or else you unavoidably die this very moment."

                    It seems to me like we should distinguish a couple different questions:

                    1. Is it morally right to stand up to the Deathlords?
                    2. Is almost everyone selected to be an Abyssal going to stand up to the Deathlords?
                    3. What is the most effective way to stand up to the Deathlords, if you are selected to be an Abyssal?

                    1 and 2 are not very interesting questions.

                    As Eric pointed out, the Exalted setting does not contain an objective morality, but in the point of view of most people who played the game and most humans within Creation, the answer to question 1 is yes.

                    The answer to question 2 is pretty clearly no. It's possible that a majority might stand up, but probably not even that, especially if the Deathlords show some skill in selecting their targets.

                    I think question 3 is the only one that people here are interested in debating.

                    One proposal is to refuse the Abyssal Exaltation and die. Another would be to accept, serve the Deathlord, and try to do as little damage as possible. A third would be to accept, flee the Deathlord's service as soon as possible, and then run as far away as possible.

                    It obviously depends on how exactly Abyssals and Deathlords are described in this edition, but the third option seems by far the most effective to me. You deprive the Deathlord of a useful servant for as long as you survive, which if they find you and kill you might not be very long (but still probably more time than it takes to find a willing servant) but if you avoid their reach, might be decades, centuries, or, effectively, forever.

                    As long as escape is possible (and I understand from an unrelated source that even in 2e it was) the choice to accept or reject Abyssal Exaltation can't be seen exactly as a choice to collaborate with oppression or reject it.

                                    WIth regards to the other topic, my understanding is that summoning is not necessarily worse for demons than imprisonment in Malfeas is normally. Of course this isn't an ethical justification for slavery, but rather a criticism of Malfeas.
