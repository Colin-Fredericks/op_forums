    I guess broadly speaking I don't think the greater cosmological, metaphysical, or political powers of Creation subscribe to philosophies of introspection, informed consent, or a commitment to moral integrity defined by rigorous self-criticism in the name of consistency no matter how uncomfortable it is, though I absolutely do think the audience should examine Creation through those lenses.

    Creation is the sort of place where most of the people in power, all the way up to the highest god, think that authority confers the right to maintain that authority through violence applied to those who'd defy it, and that things like "Mercy" and "Full disclosure" are, in the words of Kid Spock in the J.J. Abrams Star Trek reboot, "Morally praiseworthy but not morally obligatory." Which means that if you're someone's boss, let's say because their great-great-grandfather promised you fealty in exchange for being spared execution after you beat them in a war, or because you tricked them into saying they agree you're their boss, and this person whose boss you are decides they don't want you to be their boss anymore and absolutely will not let you boss them around anymore even to the point of you beating them to death, then showing mercy in the face of their defiance by stopping short of beating them to death probably makes you a good person, but not stopping short of beating them to death doesn't make you a bad person. Because you're the boss; what were you supposed to do, accept their theft of your authority without standing your ground?

    And this informs everything from "Do Deathlords tell their Abyssals what the Black Exaltation entails?" to "How okay do people in the setting think it is to use demon slave labor?"

    Shit's fucked. For the purposes of the genre that Exalted occupies, shit is supposed to be fucked.

                I'm a tad concerned that this is overly focused on the introspection of the people with power without regards to the outlook of people beneath them.

                I mean, I guess it matters for how one approaches and characterises those figures who employ power in such a manner to not totally write them off, but that it bears considering that other people will still observe and pass judgement on them.

                My mind goes to Roman politics at the end of the Republican era. Caesar has popularity with the people when he has things like the victories of an upstanding Roman and disseminates war booty among the populace, but they still raise eyebrows when he's doing things like commemorating victory in battle against other Romans, on the verge of executing a teenage princess from a highly prestigious lineage, and pretty openly having an extramarital affair with a foreign queen with alien customs and putting a statue of her in his family shrine. Caesar's lieutenant ceremonially offers him a crown, and some accounts suggest that this actually is intended as the pretext for assuming kingship but for the fact that the crowds respond pretty angrily to the spectacle. But conversely, when Caesar is ganged up on and stabbed in the Senate hall, his popularity helps bolster up the public opinion that such a thing is dreadfully done despite the assassins' arguments that the action was both moral and legal, and the riot at Caesar's funeral sends those conspirators to flight.

                I guess what I'm saying is that a discussion such as this should not exclude the input of society at large, and that we do indeed get historical precedent for society at large indeed condemning the unmerciful liege in a manner that creates further difficulties.

                I think that Exalted already has enough problems with people enthusiastically pursuing the angle of "the power of these individuals means that they can effectively tell society at large to go fuck themselves, Idowatiwan"
