    With the game I do now, I wouldn't say gender roles are particularly important; because, tbh, I've somewhat played them down deliberately compared to the historical influences. I don't really want Kings to refuse to let the Changing Moon fight because she's a girl, or everyone to laugh at the No Moon because he does magic (and everyone knows magic is unmanly).

    But as my first story arc was based on L'Morte D'Arthur, it came up a bit, from the fact that everyone's furious with the Queen for cheating on the King, but most people seemed less bothered by the fact that the King had a bastard (possibly by his half-sister). Except the Queen who pointed this out when the PCs confronted her, of course.
    However, the No Moon did call the King on this, saying that he's an oath-breaker too, so just as bad as his wife.
    I felt this was ultimately fitting, as one of the main themes that Mallory is trying to get across in L'Morte D'Arthur is that actually Arthur and his male friends are corrupt, impious, adulterous, murderous, sometimes incestuous, and occasionally rapists, and thus the fall of Camelot is the result of their own sins, it's not just because of Guinevere (who comes out a bit better than previous portrayals with her adultery in). A deconstruction of the previous century's mores and values, to some extent. So I was quite happy for the PCs (mainly the No Moon) to point out that the Arthur-expy was just as bad, if not worse, as his wife.

    Of course, you could argue this is less about gender values and more about people letting off the regnant more than the consort, but I'm pretty sure it was originally about gender values (where senior male aristocrats can much more easily get away with lovers than female aristocrats can).

        Something else to mention regarding gender roles and how I sometimes benefit from them as a Storyteller/GM.

        In my experience when I see a PCs backstory they might have a strong sense of who their father is/was as an aspirational figure or cautionary tale but their mother is frequently a non-entity either absent from the text or briefly presented in a way that suggests the player considers them boring.

        I don't directly challenge this but it can be fun to approach the backstory from a different angle to what the player anticipated and do something with the mother that fleshes out their role or challenges perceptions of them.

                            Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                            I once had an idea of a society where, for the military caste, gender roles differed depending on your age. Older men and younger women were the home guard and trained the soldiers for the campaigning forces, which would thus consist of younger men and older women.
                            How often did that arrangement result in dalliances among the homeguard, between the older men and younger women?

                            Or is the assumption already that the old men are married to the young women, and the old women who enter the campaigning forces are widows of the previous generation of old men? And that the young men are bachelors trying to become distinguished old men whom future young women will want to marry?


                            Biologically, the risk of babies being stillborn or born with defects like Down Syndrome increase after the mother reaches her 30s, and risks are pronounced at 40. Nevermind that Creation doesn't have modern health standards for most people to REACH the age of 40, let alone prenatal health care for dealing with birth complications that become more common after the mother is 25.

                            So it's no wonder that historically, men tried to marry younger women, and young women consented to marry young, because everyone wanted pregnancies to succeed and babies to be born healthy. Indeed, this might support the idea that men are attracted to younger women as a matter of reproductive instinct.

                            Meanwhile, male fertility lasts practically forever, especially in a world where few men reach 50. A woman's ovum are all made when she's born, so even though they hibernate they all grow equally old together, but men are constantly producing new, fresh sperm.

                            An argument for the existence of a hypergamy reproductive instinct is that prehistoric women wanted to have babies as soon as possible, before they reached the peak of their physical strength and athleticism. So these women wanted a mate that could immediately provide those things in abundance, for the women themselves and especially for a whole family.

                            Rather than settle for young men their own age, young women would desire those few men who had proven they could survive the harsh world by growing into their prime. Men in their prime also tended to have the most resources, gathered by virtue of their experience and peak strength. Men would thus compete for women by proving themselves the strongest, the most cunning, the most capable of accumulating the resources necessary to support a family and of protecting both resources and family.

                            Younger men could thus rarely compete with older men. This, and the fact that a society could lose a majority of its male population and still survive as long as the majority of women survived, would have meant that young men had less societal value than young women. Which of course made it easier to allow (or send) the young men to go off and die in hunting and warfare as the young men struggled to grow stronger and richer to build their value to future wives.

                            Combine this with testosterone, which supports the growth of muscle and bone mass, induces higher metabolism for more energy production, and encourages aggression, competition, and risk-taking.

                            Suddenly, the typical shounen-genre manga story makes a lot of biological sense. The stories usually feature a young man with virtually no societal value, struggling and struggling and struggling to become stronger, smarter, more skilled, with more resources, and with other male allies who have also proven their strength, all of them striving to reach adulthood as the most valuable male members of society. The most eligible bachelors.

                            Meanwhile, many times, these stories feature a love interest of a similar age to the male protagonist, and this young women already has much more societal value than the young man. Kurosaki Ichigo and Kuichi Rukia from Bleach, as an example, though there are plenty.

Cultural bias is also ubiquitous in human observations, including studies, so trying to separate the nature from the nurture is not readily or easily accomplished.
