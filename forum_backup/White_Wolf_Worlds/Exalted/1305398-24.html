        Most Wyld Hunts that I've played and played against don't just rush in and attack, they only get engaged like that if they get the anathema sprung on them. In my experience Wyld Hunts put together a huge amount of setup and planning to execute properly.

        In one memorable case the PCs were the Hunt, hunting a Solar near a town which was half hanging above a waterfall on an ancient spar of some First Age architecture. First they lead the Solar through their army, sending a large wave of heavy infantry and its exalted commander to wear him down. Then they lured him onto the spar, detonated explosives that completely knocked out the ground under his feet sending him tumbling hundreds of feet down onto jagged rocks, and then immediately detonating the other set which brought the entire spar crashing down on him. Then he leaped back up, but before he could engage them their friend in a Warstrider on a nearby hill started pelting him with arrows the size of ballista bolts. So he jumped into close quarters with the warstrider and destroyed it. Then, at last, he leaped back into the town to finish off the PCs, where they had another ambush of their allies waiting to take him down.

        They actually had more fallback positions and plans in place, assuming that ambush would fail and they'd have to either chase him down through the woods that they'd seeded with more surprises, or lure him down into the catacombs beneath the rest of the town, which they'd filled with deathtraps and soldiers. Thankfully it was not necessary.

                                        When a character is at close range, he is in an opponent’s face, close enough to easily attack with a hand-to-hand weapon such as a sword, spear, or his fists. This doesn’t mean that the characters are necessarily within arm’s reach of each other at all times, but rather that they are able to close such a distance in moments. If an opponent is within close range of a character, that character is embattled, and must use a disengage action if he wishes to move away from the opponent.

                                        When a character is at short range, he is outside of the immediate reach of an opponent wielding a hand-to-hand weapon, but close enough that the opponent could reach him with a quick sprint
                                        As I see it, close range is within leaping distance, so no more than ~10ft. After all, you need to be able to Defend Other from anywhere within Close range, which is not gonna happen if you're 20ft away from your charge.

                                                It's going to massively depend on the circumstances.

                                                Reflexive movement doesn't help this -- what is free movement as part of being at Close, what is "a short sprint".

                                                I'd say the maximum distance for Close can be anywhere from 2' to 20' depending on the circumstances.

                                                Flame weapons used to have a range of 45' and are now Short ranged. If we say Medium is ~50', that leaves a lot of leeway for how big Close and Short might be.

                                                Generally, I'd peg Close at ~10' (because D&D), and Short being 10' to 50': but we're taught the average person can clear the average room in one second -- which squares with the 21' knife thing. You can close a twenty foot gap in a second (a Bolt is clearing 30' in a second) and an attack is three seconds.

                                                I certainly don't think it's unreasonable to say two people within 20' are Close, especially in the scenario described. I wouldn't be comfortable making Close range any bigger than that, but someone sitting on the back of a 15' Tyrant Lizard is still at Close range from foes on the ground. So it can't be MUCH smaller than 20'.

                                                But then, if the Deebs were PCs and wanted to have some at Short and some at Close, I'd allow that too. It's all about the narrative: and this is telling one heck of a story. The point is to be dynamic, not nickel-and-dime palace sizes.
