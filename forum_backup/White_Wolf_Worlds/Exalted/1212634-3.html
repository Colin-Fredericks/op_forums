    Originally posted by Lioness View Post
    My problem with Game of Thrones is less the source material and more the smug faux historical explanations I see for its world building decisions.
    Absolutely - that is quite infuriating. Like when a Black person asked George R. R. Martin why European-based fantasy is so white and monocultural and he said, "Because it's mostly been written by white men". Yes, but Europe did have quite a few PoC back in the related eras (or how more recently a Black fan asked him for more diversity and he said there will be some PoC characters in Winds of Winter... but not too important) and even then, why, since it's all fantasy, why not making it a more diverse setting?

    Anyways, yes - it looks superficially like Medieval Europe, but for instance in the Middle Ages the lowest thrall could bring their lord before the Justices if they didn't fulfill their obligations. The king had to be good to his dukes or they would depose him, and the earls could do the same with them. You couldn't shoot your crossbow at random servants like Joffrey does without being immediately deposed, or flay people alive as for that, and killing whole cities of people was a surefire way to have a depression. If you were a noble you had many important duties and god forgive you if you didn't attend to them. Of course this all varies in different centuries and nations, but that's the gist of it.

    The treatment of women? I could write a book about this... Martin likes to say "history is written in blood" but rape could be punished with whipping, castration, or death. Noblewomen, for starters, could and did handle farms, manors, castles while their husbands were at war - they needed to heed the men's advice, but also be somewhat decent military commanders if the lands were attacked, manage and supervise hundreds of workers, and also be able to keep their husband's business afloat and possibly prospering - most of all in case they were widowed (Sansa shows some enterprise skill in Season 7, which is good).

    Nuns - nuns could study and work, and in convents they handled everything, from farming, to the treasury, to church services, to teaching young girls. Later on they even acquired a lot of servants. They didn't just walk after you chanting "Shame, shame, shame". Okay, different religion, sure.

    Middle class women, similarly to noblewomen, had to handle their family's townhouse and help with their husband's business, or practice their own different job. Usually no job was forbidden to them. They became apprentices and learned the jobs they wanted to practice - barbers, apothecaries, armorers, shipwrights, masons, carpenters, brewers... they were fewer than men, and weren't allowed to become masters, but they existed. The silk industry was practically all women, even in positions of authority, and they had a lot of power as an organization. Not to mention midwives - forget maesters - they were well-paid, swore oaths, were compensated by the city in the case of a mother too poor to afford their services, had apprentices, distributed public welfare, and acted as backup medical assistants whenever needed. In some cities women could actually study medicine.

    Women without particular skills as craftsfolks could sell food in the streets (like fish - because it was easy to catch, plentiful, and good for days of religious fasting - , or even, admittedly, clams with lemon juice, like Arya does in Braavos). They often became servants, and sometimes nurses or prostitutes. They actually didn't have a lot of nice prospects, that's true.

    Peasant women helped men in all the labor at the farm. They probably worked more than their husbands, considering they had to take care of the kids and spin, spin, always spin textiles for the lord. Some did not marry - they stayed with their families, or they became servants at another farm, or at the manor, becoming maids, sheperds, anything, doing work exactly like any man - or they could travel from town to town and if they managed to stay away from serfdom for a year and a day they were free.

    It's okay if women in GoT are universally oppressed, it's unpleasant, but it can propel some interesting narratives... but let's not say the reason lies in any interest in realism or verisimilitude.

    People also had soap and a sort of toothpaste, so their teeth weren't all rotten (that's okay) and they didn't walk around caked with filth (why are people in fantasy always caked with filth?).

    Also, Arabic culture was a central and very important presence in Europe - at the court of kings, in universities, in the way it influenced poetry, philosophy, trade, science... PoC weren't confined to another continent an ocean away, all slavers and slaves and assassins and exotic eunuch soldiers.

    Also, where are the wall decorations and colors, where are the graffiti? Why is everything so brown and grey and dead, is Dorne the only decent place to live in? Since at least Classical Greece times, people have always painted their architecture - the Middle Ages were no exception.

    So yeah, I too am okay with the narrative in GoT (up to a certain point - really, too many gratuitous rapes and too few PoC... to start with) - but no, it doesn't resemble the Middle Ages much. It just doesn't. If Martin, or the producers and writers, want to find any trace of legitimacy in their work, they will have to look elsewhere - this is Hollywood Middle Ages, or a mix of tropes and clichès from a disparate amount of different cultures and epochs. You can't build a setting like this and claim you're doing it to make it feel realistic, to ground it in real world history - go study real world history first, and study it well. Or, alternately, don't go with that explanation - just say you wanted a gritty setting and you only took the worst aspects from various actual sociopolitical realities, at most.

    Jeez.

                                        Hmm... George R R Martin. Well, he's is not a historical or pseudo-historical novelist. He's no Guy Gavriel Kay. That is, he lacks a huge historical background (more so than Exalted strives for and Geoff Grabowski had, on the whole) and has a taste for the lurid (again more so than Exalted, though there's perhaps more of that kind of thing in the dna of how the Realm is written and so on than a complete contrast would imply). He's kind of doing the best he could.

                                        He also doesn't write pseudo-historical novels (ala GGK) - it's sort of hard to fault Westeros for not replicating European interactions with the rest of the world or their norms when it's a very differentiated place with a different history (Westeros is really governed as some kind of continent sized mega empire that possibly has more parallels to a medieval China, which experienced few interstate conflicts and much more extreme civil wars in absolute scale.)

                                        He was writing back in '91, and regards himself as a Progressive, so I'd guess if he'd written today, and lacked any burden to make his writing consistent with what went before, he'd have rebalanced with some aspects that made Better Stuff - Essos would not be perhaps the most Orientalist thing ever committed to fantasy fiction. (Some could have for the worse, with various 21st century Progressive myths that no one would have believed back in the Good Old Days incorporated, but I suspect that you need a degree of ignorance and bias to really believe some of those that he just wouldn't have.)
