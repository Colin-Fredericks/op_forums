    ​The case for Destiny, Heaven and the Sidereal Exalted

    05-15-2016, 09:25 PM
    In modern Western society, a great deal of emphasis is placed on the importance of the self; self-reliance, self-improvement, self-determination, etc. In general, stories where one side seeks to restrict or dictate behaviors of individuals to associate freely and pursue their individual desires almost always portray the restrictive side in the role of the antagonists. Stories in which prophecy and predestination figure prominently often have to do with the protagonists seeking to break out of the prophecy, to determine their own future and not be bound by unseen strings.

    Additionally, the side in the story seeking to limit individual choice is almost always in the superior position at the beginning of the story, allowing the hero to be the scrappy underdog that only wants to be free. Everyone cheers for the Rebels. The protagonist is an agent of change- both change of themselves and change of the status quo.

    This traditional protagonist-freedom-disruption / antagonist-stasis-status quo mirrors the Solar-centric game of Exalted quite well. Solars forge their own destiny with their excellence and arete. They are the champions of the Sun, bringing righteousness to the world as best they know how. They are Nietzche's creative ubermensch, unconstrained by convention, taboo or old morality to forge a new and better world. This is obviously a problematic tradition when it comes to the Sidereal Exalted, Chosen of the Maidens of Destiny; seers, prophets and puppet-masters.

    To an extent, many of these problems also arise when considering how to approach Dragonblooded stories. However, the Dragonblooded stand halfway between the Solar's ubermensch and the Sidereal's otherworldliness. They seek to control the world and maintain the status quo, but for reasons that the audience can be immediately sympathetic towards- they want things to stay the same because they *benefit* from things staying the same. In addition to that, with such a large number and variance amongst the host of Ten Thousand Heroes, it's easy to tell dynamic political stories where the protagonists are seeking for their personal freedom against the tyranny of an elder family member, political superior, or other antagonist figure- even as they don't seek to generalize this story to the rest of the world as Solars will tend to do.

    Sidereals don't have that benefit. Fate and destiny, as they are traditionally understood in western culture, are the very most stringent definition of the status quo. Stories that explore the consequences for violating such forces tend to veer in one of two directions; either, it's metaphysically destructive in some fashion (such as the events of Terry Prachet's Mort, where the titular Mort's sparing of a young queen nearly results in the destruction of the world from cascading causlity errors), or it's revealed to be part of a deeper fate, which is fulfilled due to the protagonist attempt at rebelling (perhaps most famously with the story of Oedipus).

    Exalted has traditionally gone with the first approach; without the Loom of Fate, Creation would becomes a vastly less stable, more chaotic place. Natural law would no longer be consistent or dependable; the Wyld would rise and Creation would fall back into the primordial chaos from whence it arose.

    The problem with this approach is two-fold. First, it's unsatisfying. It tends to be very tell-don't-show, because the consequence of showing are that the world ends. Even moving away from such dramatic all-or-nothing consequences, you still end up with the least interesting form of utilitarianism, forced to defend the status quo because the alternative is worse. A story in which the protagonist neither makes choices nor changes is not generally a very interesting story.

    Second, it doesn't address one of the fundamental question that defending Fate raises- whence comes evil? If the future is determined, why do bad things happen to good people? Why do the wicked prosper? When Fate is something strictly external to you, this can be swept away- but if you are going to be defending and upholding the status quo, this question has to be addressed, because you are complicit in that evil. (And that's not getting into the very common trope of a character being forced to take some relatively despicable act in order to forestall a worse consequence)

    The alternate option, however- that Fate is bigger than the choices of the characters -is functionally impossible to pull off in a roleplaying game. Put aside it's narrative satisfactoriness or lack thereof; it basically can't be done without constant and significant retcons.

    So, if the first option is unsatisfying and the second option is impossible- where does that leave the Sidereals?

    --------------

    All glory to the Heavenly City of Yu-Shan! All glory and triumph to the Jade Pleasure Dome at her heart, where the Incarnae take their leisure while playing the Games of Divinity! All glory and praise to the Commission on Abstract Matters and Celestial Concerns, the Bureau of Heaven, wherein the proper order of Yu-Shan is decided! All glory and honor to the tireless servants of the Celestial Monitors of the Seasons and Weather, and the Superintendency of Nature Grand and Humble, the Bureaus of Weather and Nature! All glory and dignity to the Divine Witnesses of Human Works and Deeds, the Bureau of Humanity!

    And all glory to the Most Excellent Designers of Destiny and Sidereal Conjunctions! All glory to the Bureau of Destiny, wherein the desires of the Gods may be projected onto Creation's stars. All glory to the Five Divisions and the Twenty Five Stations! All glory to the Loom of Fate, which may assume these projections and weave the strands of That Which Is, holding Creation stable and maintaining the Creation-Ruling Mandate of the Exalted!

    --------------

    * The Gods of Creation are constrained in action by the Loom of Fate. Although possessed of free will, the Gods may only materially intervene in Creation under limited circumstances. Where Destiny proceeds much as designed, their powers are restricted. Where Destiny has become askew, they are at greater liberty. The unchained appetites of the Gods are a terrible thing, and would push Creation from an Age of Sorrow into an Age of Savagery unknown since the dawn of her earliest days.

    * The Gods are not evil, but neither are they good. Many are wise and restrained, but many are also foolish or gluttonous.

    * The constraints upon the Gods are a mix of legal and metaphysical; when fate is strong, they are truly less capable of interceding. As the weave loosens, they may intervene more and more directly- influencing events, possessing animals or people, and finally incarnating in the flesh. While the gods are capable of rebelling and violating this law regardless of the strength of fate, few due for fear of the consequences- both legal and metaphysical.

    * The Loom of Fate weaves the substance known as Asher, or That Which Is, according to the designs of the Gods. However, That Which Is is a force which describes, not demands. It is the flow of a great river, carrying all forward- but with determination, the river can be damned or the course changed. When the weaves of Asher describes a world other than the one Creation has become, strangeness can begin to occur- errors in observed causality, spirits acting out, elementals spawning from overburdened dragon lines. Creation is not contingent on the Loom of Fate, however; Creation predates the Loom and could survive her loss. But without the Loom's guidance, mere chaos and mad divinities would be loose upon the world and there would be no indication when incursions from Creation's enemies occur; the doom of the world would be hastend, even if it would not be immediate.

    * No one in Creation, save possibly the Maidens of Destiny, can have certain knowledge of events yet to be.

    * The Sidereal Exalted, as the Chosen of the Maidens of Destiny, are the agents of the Bureau of Destiny. When the skein of the Asher becomes tangled, when a god wishes their will be done but they are constrained by the Loom, it is the proper role of the Sidereal Exalted to enter Creation and act as they best see fit to straighten the weave, to enact or deny the will of the god, to realize as best they can, with the secret wisdom of their Exaltations and the insight from service in the divine, the righteous future as they understand.

    * In Ages past, the Solar Exalted could also walk the streets of Heaven, and the Lunar Exalted were welcome guests in many divine villas. Many Gods circumvented the prohibitions of fate and the Bureau of Destiny in this time by persuading Solar and Lunar Exalted to intercede for their favored causes personally- and these unofficial and unplanned wrecked havoc upon the weave of destiny. One of the causes of the Great Prophecy was to divine a means by which the increasing disjoint between the weave and the world could be reconciled.

    * Nothing must be, save that which is.

    * Rakan Thulio can make a compelling ethical argument- that Creation should not be governed by uncaring gods, that a machine which can cause a man to drown on dry land or to fall in love with another is a monstrous and evil engine, and that the Sidereal Exalted are complicit in the death of every innocent child and the prosperity of every wicked man by supporting the Heavens. His desire would not lead to Creation dissolving; while many of the Sidereal Exalted believe that the dangers of unchecked divinities are greater, it is a moral question rather than an existential one.

    * The Sidereal Exalted can make a compelling ethical argument- as Creation has become more wild and the Gods grown less restrained, chaos has gripped the land. More starve, more suffer, more die. The Sleepwalker is driven by a mad vision, and cares nothing for the consequences of his war on Heaven, only that he avenge an imagined slight from thousands of years ago. While most of the Getimian Exalted scoff at this, having themselves suffered as a consequence of the monstrous and evil engine- but some look at the present state of Creation and wonder what blood will stain their hands if they succeed; it is a moral question rather than an existential one.

    --------------

    Thoughts?

              The idea of Destiny being Heaven's Five Year Plan is an awesome subversion, and still lives at the heart of this musing.

              The tricky thing is- by the end of 2e (and really the idea is present in 1e; the roots runs deep) the idea of the Loom of Fate as being something upon which Creation is contingent had become fanon. Discussions of 'What would happen if the Loom of Fate were destroyed' basically ran the gamut from 'Everything immediately dissolves into primordial chaos' to 'The laws of physics just completely shit the bed'. It was a symptom of the crapsackification of Creation; the choices were between being deterministic robots and passing dreamstuff. And some of the consequences of that can be seen in folk's responding to Rakan Thulio; 'Hell yeah, tear down Heaven! Hell yeah, down with fate! Sign me up!'

              For Rakan Thulio to be a compelling villain, he needs to present a case that doesn't end with 'And then Creation is destroyed'. We already have thirteen villains for that slot, it's full, thanks. Ergo, he wants to establish a new status quo, one without Heaven's Five Year Plan but which also doesn't destroy the world.

              For the Sidereals to be compelling heroes, they need a reason to oppose Rakan Thulio apart from 'Because our position in his new status quo will be shitty'. We already have an entire splat dedicated to maitaining the status quo for their personal enrichment, and the Dragonblooded do that very well thanks. Ergo, while it can't be an existential threat, Rakan Thulio's desires should be something a reasonable person could want to stop even after understanding them.

                                  I hate to sort of just react to the first paragraph, but I do think you underrate the degree to which Western audiences cheer when figures like Obi Wan or Gandalf (presumably there is some equivalent in the Harry Potter books I never read?) appear to put destiny right, reveal the true destiny and awaken the true potential of "our hero" to be in his place. There's more compatibility with Fate with the popular stories than I think you're admitting. Dark forces may play their hand, to obscure the *true* way that great prophecies must unfold, yet the Sidereal Exalted stand to put that right. (For another point, you mention Mort; however the popularity of the Discworld's Death, as a "righteous" psychopomp figure, suggests that there's an audience for that.) However, YMMV on this, of course (we could have different impressions of culture without either of us being right or wrong).

                                  Reading the next few paragraphs. Separately, one thing that I would note in the Traveller in Black is that his role really, is making sure "Fate" (well, Order really, but let's say Fate) tends to happen to primal, elemental magical forces that would enact their whims on humanity. He turns aside their caprice to allow man to self rule through the cultivation of wisdom and common sense. To a degree, the equivalents in Exalted are demons, sorcerors, scavenger lords, godlings, ghosts (although Exalted is obviously much more compatible with a degree of magic in the world). This is opposing the antagonist's freedom to disrupt, for the protection and betterment of mankind. I think you're quite right that this *isn't* often the status quo in the typical Western narrative about guardians of fate, but I certainly think the Sidereals stand to gain from embracing this rather than trying to circumvent. This is a lot like the metaphysical path you describe, however it is personalised, which I think makes it more satisfying. (For other strands of similar personalisation of metaphysical disruption, you could think of Cobb in The Farthest Shore or even the Otherhood who are partially a Sidereal inspiration).

                                  Certainly, there should be Sidereal judgement involved in these, though! When and where do they choose to intervene.

                                  So, hmmm, I guess that actually brings close to your conclusions about the gods and Fate.

                                  The biggest block to Sidereal sympathy, and to sympathy for Heaven and destiny, I find, is that at least Gandalf and his ilk tend to be fairly open, while the Sidereals are not. Yes, they are by nature mysterious. But not purposefully occulted. While the Sidereal establishment are fairly explicitly often people who are "for secret, dark debates" and who probably tend to believe "when it becomes serious, you have to lie", the worst kind of bureaucrats who justify any kind of high handed authoritarianism in the name of preventing war and strife. However, they are supposed to represent a fairly questionable hidden hands of Fate, after all. (Exalted has always posed becoming that as a dubious choice the Sidereals made, than one they had to make.)
