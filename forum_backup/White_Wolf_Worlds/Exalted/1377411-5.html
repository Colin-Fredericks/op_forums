                        Originally posted by JohnDoe244 View Post
                        I disagree, not least because the Realm is dominated by the Wan.
                        To say that the Realm is not informed by examples and outlooks arising from the modern history of Western Europe because they don't resemble them physically is pretty superficial, not least because the Wan/the Realm has still largely been written by white people of Western European cultural descent.

                        Like, not just the imperialism; the Immaculate Order's system of religious supremacy, establishment and absolutism and the manner in which it is used to provide a moral justification for imperialism has a lot more in common with English Protestantism than it does with any Asian religion.

                        Originally posted by JohnDoe244
                        Nor do I consider a white man's burden narrative inherently problematic for a story, especially if placed within a culturally sensitive arc about growth and understanding.
                        That arc is more difficult to accomplish when presented with an antagonist that is reduced down to a horrific monster people who lack apparent semblance of a sophisticated inner life.

                        Originally posted by JohnDoe244
                        I'd go further to say, that some cultures do practice cannibalism: yes, baseless accusations of cannibalism have been used by imperialistic conquerors to dehumanize and other "barbarians", but people do eat people.
                        So what?

                        I'll point out that there's a distinct difference between the historical example of "consumption of flesh is an aspect of already existing practices of warfare" and the fictional trope of depicting a culture that sets out to make war on people for the specific purpose of eating them.

                        And sure, there can be a certain cultural weight to a marauding army engaging in cannibalism or alternative desecrations of the bodies of the dead for purposes of humiliation, although it has its most deviant character when the ones doing the humiliation are the aggressors who possess a disproportionate amount of power and have undertaken war for the sake of profit or indulgence of some base violent need. I think there are key differences between the Brussians going in to roll over the Krukanians at the behest of their authoritarian president and cannibalising enemy soldiers as a tactic of terror and humiliation than if the resisting and less powerful force engaged in such a desecration as part of a message of "fuck off and leave us alone".

                        If the Brussians then turn around and have the state press try to both sides it, or worse retroactively state "look, the violent extremes they engage in while resisting us serve as a post hoc justification for our annexation in the first place", they're disingenuously trying to control the narrative by presenting key elements out of context, and it lacks a certain sophistication to take that narrative at face value.

                        That being said, there's a stronger degree of narrative and historical value to the point that empires or other forms of centralised authority have used the actual fact of border communities being at risk from raiders to justify greater degrees of power, and there are ways to portray that in terms of "complex circumstances can make people accept bad systems with the best of intentions" that manage to dance below the threshold of "external raiders justify imperialism". Not least by having a mind for the fact that the raiders have historically been actual people with material concerns that strongly inform their raiding culture (while also not forming the entirety of it; within such a society, there's always a point at which people within such a society go beyond raiding as material necessity and into the level of personal aggrandizement).

                        While keeping in mind that we've got historical examples of those forms of border raiding giving way to mutually beneficial arrangements driven by interpersonal familiarity, cultural exchange and diplomatic overtures, something that is only possible when both parties recognise one another's inherent humanity (that they also have to possess).

                        That's a very shaky dance to engage in, I expect even the best writers to fumble a few of the steps. Good faith artistic discourse can point out those fumbles while recognising the good intent and not condemning the work as a whole, as well as being able to acknowledge those imperfections without feeling attacked or otherwise losing one's mind. Or even like... yeah, sometimes people will react to it strongly enough to actually attack somebody, and there's a maturity to being able to weather some of those attacks, even point out their mistakes, without doing something like angrily doubling down and refusing to make any shifts.

                        And yes, there are fantastical stories that do want to sometimes just utilise the compelling aesthetic elements of something like a militaristic society or grandiose empire, or even just... war in general without wanting to grapple with the problematic moral dimensions of such, and so create an opponent that is unrelentingly violent and cannot be reasoned with. That kind of fiction can even be enjoyed, although it ought to be mindful of the degree to which it resembles propaganda narratives, because otherwise it still functions as a form of propaganda.

                        That's the thing about propaganda, it's not really about making a rational argument, it's about forming psychological habits. Most people are not so compartmentalized that they can readily separate the emotional centres that process fiction from the world view that engages with reality, and that does mean that the mental processes of "the enemy can be something sufficiently inhuman that the only recourse is to destroy it" is something that can more readily be applied to the real world.

                        Originally posted by JohnDoe244
                        More than that, we're talking about fantastic people mutated by magic powers, not exactly an obvious stand-in for a real life ethinic group facing persecution.
                        This is a Thermian Argument. The fantastic people don't exist as a result of independent processes, they were created by a writer, and artistic critique interrogates both why they opted to write that thing and what kinds of reactions it creates in people. All art is political.

                        Originally posted by JohnDoe244
                        And, again, there are protagonistic aspects to the Varajtul, who are heroic in their default presentation but presented in a different light in an opposing splat book. I would not want to play Exalted if every faction had to be unambiguously less evil than the Realm.
                        The question of whether any given community or organisation is more or less "evil" than the Realm is a complicated one, particularly considering that the Realm has no opponent that is in possession of greater reach or resources than them, but it's still possible to thread the needle of portraying local instances of the Realm fighting an enemy that engages in genuinely grotesque actions without lending anything to the framing of "and the existence of such greater threats ultimately justifies the Realm's existence and provides allowances for its excesses".

                        It's possible to present that scenario and avoid that framing even while depicting the people under Realm rule as believing that their protection justifies their oppression.

                        Feedback from the audience helps calibrate that portrayal.

                        Originally posted by JohnDoe244
                        I still think, however, that "Exalted against the Wyld" is an appropriate narrative space for a Dragon-Blooded hero in general, and that Tepet Elena's narrative is a lot less heroic if she fighting against normal mortals, specifically. I'm explicitly fine with the Realm fighting against worse evils than itself, because that adds a layer of moral complication and consequence for the PCs to engage with. In order for the Realm to fight against such evils, these evils must exist.
                        I kind of... half agree with this. How do I put it...

                        Alexander the Great is a figure who, in his personality and audacity and accomplishments, is truly impressive and compelling... while also being an absolute prick at best and a genuine monster at worst. Like, as a warrior and general he was truly brave and intelligent and lucky and charismatic and exceptionally strong and hardy, who not only commanded intricate campaigns and elaborate battles but personally threw himself into the thick of fighting and took wounds of a kind you would expect to be life ending today repeatedly. While also being somebody who undertook great campaigns of conquest that resulted in the deaths of huge numbers of people out of a belief in himself as a man of destiny and general desire to elevate himself and indulge in that thrill of battle, was paranoid and impulsive and violent in a manner that resulted in the horrible and arbitrary deaths of people in his close personal circle even when they'd displayed incredible loyalty to him, and there's a strong case to be made for him having deliberately led his army back through a hellish desert march resulting in much suffering and death after they refused to continue campaigning into India.

                        Taken as a personage, even the things that make him a terrible person make him a compelling figure to look at the life story of.

                        And there's an emotional need that can be engaged through the safe medium of fiction in which a person like and scenarios such as that, which would realistically be... let's say exhausting to deal with, can have interaction from a position of safety and freedom from consequence. In those terms, I argue that it is possible to engage with what is compelling in somebody like a Realm political leader or soldier or general without giving them a greater evil in comparison. I quite like this Edition's portrayal of Mnemon exactly as she is, warts and all. She requires no (objective, external) justification. Indeed, I would find leaning too heavily on a point of "if Mnemon doesn't take the throne and beat all competing factions into line, then the Realm is almost certainly going to fall and leave the world exposed to even greater evils" to actually diminish what is compelling about her.

                        It takes a certain kind of writing and a certain kind of audience to engage with the narrative of what was compelling about the life of Alexander the Great without falling into the trap of being enamoured with him in a manner that does have real life consequences. Which do exist; now more than any other period in my lifetime, we have examples of people who want to appeal to the base urge of what makes Alexander compelling to accumulate their own power while also illustrating how so many people giving into that urge and provide that power is more likely to lead to disaster. Statistically, we're so much more likely to get somebody who sells the paper mask of a strongman that is incompetent (and likely malicious) in the face of genuine crisis.

                        And as I said above, that kind of fiction writing, and even engagement, will probably never be 100%. It takes participation in conversations such as this (and preferably a bit of reading into some actual history books or political and philosophical treatises and a decent amount of journalism) to make up the difference. Talking to people with a broad array of experiences and perspectives and learning more about the world is the kind of thing that, say, prevents a person from falling into the trap of thinking that China's models of state capitalism and international agenda are actually kind of a good thing (a mask being set up a bit by film media that gratifyingly depicts Chinese authorities as reasonable, intelligent and diligent), and can be taken as separated from the severe political and ethnic repression and political culture of secretive elitism.

                        However, my final word on things as Exalted goes is... the foundations of Exalted and the manner in which it initially portrays itself are ones in which it wants to ultimately challenge the kinds of power narrative that rationalize the existence of the Realm. The cannibals, the demons, the Wyld, those all come later, and...

                        Shit, I was ready to say that presenting them in those terms of "indulge in the fantasy of the enemy that cannot be reasoned with" is incongruous with the core elements, and I still kind of think that's the case to a strong degree, but I'm also suddenly struck by the notion that part of the value of a large, multifaceted setting and premise conveyed with multiple source books is that it can support a variety of narrative approaches in different people's games, as determined by which books they give the greatest focus to. Okay, I have to admit, I was outmanoeuvred on that one.

                                Isator Levi, I don't really know that the Immaculate Philosophy has many parallels to Protestantism, or more specifically English Protestantism, particularly. It seems:

                                ​Hindu/Buddhist - hierarchical reincarnation towards more perfect and more enlightened forms.

                                Lamaist/Theravada/Catholic - in its overarching religious structure, parallel to a secular structure and not exactly co-terminous with it, and serving as a source of moral authority for secular structure and check on behaviour of the nobility (and ofen allies with the people in opposition to the state to do so).

                                Confucian - in the emphasis on a precisely ordered and hierarchical set of "proper" behaviours for persons of different social status, mandated ultimately by Heaven, and ultimately in service of preserving social order (the proper order of relationships) and the rule of the sovereign as a high good.

                                The status of the Immaculate Dragons also seems rather generally Buddhist and to a lesser extent generically Christian (perfected, incarnated individuals in the past who set teachings for people today and were involved in some sort of ritual self-sacrifice against sin and evil, for the greater good of humanity).

                                It's a kind of fake engineered religion in setting, so what it does is not really like a real religion - there's no such religion that actually goes "By unholding the orders, stability and social standing of the state and your social superiors, you gain merit for your next incarnation", which is a kind of too much of an "evil fantasy state church" thing to be quite true. But I think it's built most strongly on a base of engaging with most Asian religious traditions and trying to use those to shape its setting, and is "about" them, in a sense (it's not a Eurocentric rehearsing of European theological disputes dressed up with an Asian gloss, but is genuinely engaging with those ideas).
