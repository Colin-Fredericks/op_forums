            I have found it helpful during my games to downplay official "factions." I portray it more along the lines of the Christian/atheist divide, where it's more like there's groups of sympathizers but there are still differing philosophies within each faction. Each head is only as powerful as he or she can convince, extort, or bargain his or her members to consent. For instance, Richard Dawkins is seen as a thought-leader for many atheists, but there are still some who disagree with him and no one would really say he's "in charge" of atheists. Likewise, Martin Luther was a revolutionary Protestant, but he didn't seek to become an Anti-Pope; he was just a loud, convincing voice. A simpler (and clumsier) comparison would be the same structure most high schools have with jocks and nerds. No one is in charge of either group, but there is definitely a top jock and a top nerd.

            Similarly, most of the Bronze Faction believes Chejop has the right idea: oppose Anathema. Some are willing to take it to the length of forecasting Solar Exaltations and murdering their new hosts. Others seek to guide the Wyld Hunt to harry and harass the surviving Solars to the edges of Creation. Some still seek a way to strengthen the Scarlet Empire to be able to withstand all threats and views the "hunting" of Solars to be distasteful.

            The Gold Faction has some members that assassinate the most prominent and zealous of the Terrestrial Exalted; others seek to train the Solars and unleash them back upon Creation to remake the grand society of the First Age. Still others seek to establish academies or mentor-student relationships that will permit their wise dominance over their Solar brethren.

            Something both factions have in common is that it's a good idea to coordinate efforts with their own fellow believers, but they aren't beholden by any sort of pact or unified organization. There's not even a voting system in place; it's more along the lines of those who believe Utopia is achievable vs. those who believe man is inherently evil and untrustworthy as individuals.

            At least that's been what's fun for my game. It gives individual Sidereals a lot more free agency and permits me to come up with more nuanced NPCs.

                        Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                        Something I like from the opening comic in Manual: Sidereals is how we're given a group of Sidereals who are explicitly members of either faction, evidently fairly low down on their respective totem poles, and it thus amounts to some light, though cutting, ribbing at each others' expense, without really affecting their jobs.                         This.

                        Also on this subject... when it comes to the factions being at each others throats, it doesn't help that one of the most prominent Bronze Faction functionaries we've ever seen fleshed out in an explicitly "this is a faction partisan" way is Ahn-Aru.

                        Now, I love Sad Ivory. Love her. But, well... she's somewhat zealous, isn't she? She murdered another Sidereal to forward her faction goals, which is something that's pretty extreme. That's fine and all, there should be faction partisans who are willing to go that far, and part of the structure of the setting is that Sidereals are now in a position where the faction goals go from "theory" to "deadly," and the question of "you may need to murder your friends and co-workers in order to make your vision for Creation come true; will you?" is quite salient.

                        But she's the only one who has gotten that sort of play. The other Sidereals we've seen have either been fleshed out in non-faction ways, or fleshed out in ways that involve faction politics but don't actually involve directly going at other Sidereals. When Crimson Banner Executioner showed up to save Elias' bacon, he didn't need to kill his co-workers to do it. The only example we've seen of them actually facing each other down is... well, Ahn-Aru. There ought to be less stabby examples.

                        As for parallels of the Sidereals to political parties or cliques of elected representatives... I'm not sure that's quite right, especially ones in western democracies. Political parties of that nature are often alliances of near-equals with independent power bases with limited ability to directly compel each other. A sitting U.S Senator, for example, doesn't really effectively answer to anyone but the voters of his state; he can tell all 99 other Senators to go fuck themselves sideways, and he still gets his vote in the Senate and can actually probably still keep getting elected; Senators often have fund-raising and political machines in place that are completely divorced from the formal party structure. This sort of weak control means that the way the people in the institution self-organize in order to solve collective action problems is different than it would be in a strict hierarchy, where, say, the Majority Leader could actually issue orders to other Senators.

                        The Sidereals do exist in a hierarchical, authoritarian organization. They have a great deal of freedom because of the nature of their work but they have jobs and superiors and are expected to obey orders and answer for their actions. They can be promoted, demoted, have their pay cut, assigned shitty tasks or desireable ones. The faction politics are theoretically things that happen "off the clock" but they intersect with their day job heavily and the factions will often expect them to do things that aren't necessarily in line with Bureau policy.

                        The correct parallel, I feel, isn't political parties, but internal corporate politics. Or the internal politics of career (as opposed to appointed) civil service officials in one of the more hierarchically inclined ones. Office politics.

                                            That... does that happen a lot? Is Zombie Lincoln sitting in the Senate gallery working the pols? Because I might start paying more attention if that's the case.
                                            It does in China. A lot. Though, now the important figures are gone (like Mao, Zhou Enlai, and latterly Deng Xiao Ping), the current leader Xi Jin Ping is happy to ignore the previous leaders, and in fact is happily purging and jailing their cronies, while thumbing his nose at said previous leaders.

                                            It used to happen in Britain: John Major (PM after Thatcher) had terrible problems with, at one point, Thatcher interviewing every single government MP one-by-one and demanding they vote against their own leader (it was about a European treaty).
                                            But things have changed since then, since nowadays defeated leaders tend to fade into obscurity (in the game of Thrones, you win or you die), and the other leader who "voluntarily" stepped down, Tony Blair, is detested by large parts of his own party. So it doesn't really happen anymore.
                                            It's not th​e 1800s, where William Gladstone could leave the Conservative party, found the Liberal party, become a Chancellor under someone else who largely followed his lead, then become Prime Minister, lose, become Prime Minister again, lose, become Prime Minister again for 2 terms and then die at 82. Or Harold Wilson who did something similar in the 1960s.

                                            More seriously, I will admit the analogy isn't perfect, especially since the Westminster countries have radically more hierarchical political parties with more formal control over their members than the U.S does.
                                            Their control isn't, officially, massively more than America, but in Britain the party provides the funding in a way that's not such a thing in America.
                                            And then there's continental countries that often have some variation of PR, which means the MPs have no independent power bases at all.

                                            Anyhow. I think your point about office politics is probably a better comparison than US politics.

                                            I'd probably compare Sidereals to the modern Chinese Communist Party though. Everyone agrees they should, and indeed must, stay in charge. Everyone agrees who they do or don't worship (for the Sidereals, the Maidens, for the CCP, no-one). Everyone publically praises the important leaders and ideology. But behind the scenes, there are multiple oblique factions. Ideological factions (ie what used to be called the Blues, Reds and Browns) crisscross with patronage groups (such as those under the protection of former leaders), and regional groups (such as the Shanghai faction). When one faction defeats another, it's not a matter of anyone being killed, it's just a matter of missing out on top jobs, being reassigned somewhere rubbish, or being told its time to retire.
                                            Until recently, when the gloves have come off, and now being on the losing side means suddenly no longer being protected from prosecution, and being sent to jail for your corruption and murders, like Bo Xilai (one of the last prominent Reds). Okay, sure, everyone's corrupt (how do you think Xi Jin Ping's family is so loaded?) but when you lose, it gets outed to the public and you go down. That never used to happen in the 90s.

                                                I think the precise amount of internecine warfare/intrigue in the Bureau is left somewhat undefined so the ST can dial it up or down to suit the story. If a group is mostly made up of Solar building a name for themselves, then perhaps the Bureau is more like the current incarnation of the US Congress, where both sides have just enough power to block each other, and nothing happens. Similarly, if the players are Sidereals, and the ST wants to build the story around their group finally breaking the factionalism and leading the Fellowship to do something big (like stopping a Deathlord), then there needs to be enough factionalism to warrant breaking it.

                                                On the flip side, if the ST is telling a story about Sids "doing their day job," then he might pull the factionalism way back, to the level of Yankees and Red Sox fans working in the same office (okay, maybe not that bad). An incident like Sad Ivory murdering another Sid is considered Just Plain Wrong by everyone, Chejop included. PCs are a combination of the factions, and they mostly focus on things like stopping Getimians and fixing Loom snarls.

                                                There is also considerable room in the middle, of course. To me personally, Sids and the Bureau most resemble an intelligence apparatus. They do very important work, that often involves morally questionable, or downright loathsome things. They are all schooled to some degree in deception, intel gathering, etc. They have personal and factional agendas, oh and they live for centuries. That is a recipe for a complex web of deceit, double dealing, and schemes that operate on the scale of millennia..
