            Merely a reference, and certainly not a moral judgment.

            On that note:

            I went back to look at the Sidereal Scriptures, and it seems that only two of them are hostile to children. They just stuck out most in my memory since they were so horrible.


            The Scripture of the Drowning Maiden features a drowning maiden whose every attempt to breathe kills "a human child". If her breathing killed anyone regardless of age or character, it could be taken as a statement of Exalted's universe's indifference. It's not a statement about her morality because there's no clue whether she's doing this intentionally or specifically targeting children for her own gain.

            What's additionally confusing is the thesis that "Survival is acceptance". Who is supposed to be accepting anything? Is she accepting that she must kill to survive, or must the children accept that they must die? The scripture doesn't present the idea that killing others is a thing she rejected and had to learn to accept, since the bit about killing children only comes up in the penultimate line.

            The final confusing bit is that this is the scripture for Brawl, which has no Charm for survival by putting your tribulation on someone else. That's Someone Else's Destiny, a Charm under Resistance, whose scripture involves nothing about killing others for survival.


            The Scripture of the Maiden on the Shelf is the other one, and it belongs to the Melee Ability.

            The scripture is of a doll that watches the world from a shelf, until it disappears along with a child's severed head. Most of the poem is about what the doll looks like and that it does nothing but watch, so I'm not sure how it leads to a thesis that "Survival is control", or what that has to do with the Melee Ability.


            Dishonorable mention goes to the Scripture of the Expectant Maiden, for Martial Arts, where the maiden wishes to kill "the boy" who put her "there", but at least in that case it's clearly a case of specific revenge, with the boy representing anything or anyone.

            It's tempting to draw a connection with the Scripture of the Maiden on the Shelf, but it would seem to be the only time two different scriptures are linked, so it's a dubious theory.

                    The Maiden is powerless. She's trapped in a blinding, freezing descent without purchase or mobility. There's not even escape through death, and it instead puts that loss on others to preserve her life. In such a circumstance as that, there is no recourse but acceptance.

                    Originally posted by Sunder the Gold
                    The final confusing bit is that this is the scripture for Brawl, which has no Charm for survival by putting your tribulation on someone else. That's Someone Else's Destiny, a Charm under Resistance, whose scripture involves nothing about killing others for survival.
                    Looking at the fluff and mechanics of the Charms, the Sidereal Brawler is not the one who must accept; the Sidereal Brawler is the inevitability that must be accepted.

                    Looking at it now, I strongly suspect that the associations there arise from the common idiom use of the term "gauntlet" to refer to a circumstance of suffering and using it as the literal basis for fist fighting.

                    I guess that's another thing to see in how Sidereals express their themes, making those metaphorical connotations the logic behind their Abilities. Most parts of Sidereal Brawl before Easily-Accepted Proposition Stance are visually straightforward as punching people, but it's punching that can hardly be escape or undermined as an expression of the thesis of that constellation and scripture.

                    Originally posted by Sunder the Gold
                    The Scripture of the Maiden on the Shelf is the other one, and it belongs to the Melee Ability.

                    The scripture is of a doll that watches the world from a shelf, until it disappears along with a child's severed head.
                    Well, within the scope of poetry and archetype, a doll isn't going to be in the possession of an adult. I think it being a child is incidental, the point is that when the doll elects to leave, it takes the head of its owner with it.

                    Originally posted by Sunder the Gold
                    Most of the poem is about what the doll looks like
                    More than just that; it's a focused description on the form of the eyes and an expression that is fixed and artificial.

                    Originally posted by Sunder the Gold
                    that it does nothing but watch, so I'm not sure how it leads to a thesis that "Survival is control"
                    The doll is patient in the extreme, totally aware while wasting nothing in extraneous movement or feeling, until the ordained moment comes and it acts with severity and no hesitation. It possesses both self-control and control over its circumstances.

                    Originally posted by Sunder the Gold
                    or what that has to do with the Melee Ability.
                    The particular Sidereal interpretation of the Melee Ability seems to be focused on observation and precision, which I think often comes up in real life fencing philosophies anyway. It makes me think a lot about the outlook and fighting style of the character of Ashina Isshin from Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice, a man whose very name means "single-mindedness" and "no hesitation".

                    I propose that the Spear, the sign of discipline and precision, is represented by a doll rather than a Maiden because a doll would appear to be a static and immobile thing, unburdened by flesh or needs or morality.

                    Originally posted by Sunder the Gold
                    Dishonorable mention goes to the Scripture of the Expectant Maiden, for Martial Arts, where the maiden wishes to kill "the boy" who put her "there", but at least in that case it's clearly a case of specific revenge, with the boy representing anything or anyone.
                    Contextually, it also reads to me as not using the term "boy" to refer to a literal child, and more in the colloquial manner of referring to an adult of some intimacy.

                    Besides, if there's anything to be disturbed by in the Scripture of the Expectant Maiden, I would say that it's not in plotting the death of somebody, child or otherwise, but in representing the perspective of somebody who keeps putting fulfilment off to the future and unwilling to accept that it's never coming, before their life ends and their hopes amounted to nothing. That's certainly the part that's touching me right now.

                    So yeah, Accelerator, this is the thing about the power of Sidereals (and a bit of what Dex Davican was saying earlier); what power they have over fate isn't so much in "we can just make whatever happen as we please" so much as it is being the enforcers of "you cannot escape your destiny, you can only choose how to meet it". They know the underlying principles of Destiny and embody them through their capabilities.

                        I personally read it as her own acceptance that her life comes at the cost of other lives. I'd say that the sutra raises the question of whether there's a moral difference between killing to survive and surviving at the cost of someone else's life. Easily Accepted Proposition Stance (which, in retrospect, is Charmspeak for "an offer you can't refuse") puts that moral choice on the Sidereal's most powerful opponent. Will that person choose survival by accepting the Sidereal's dictum, or reject an unjust fate at the cost of their own life (or at least their health).

                        Note that the most effective way to use this as a killing weapon is to declare a destiny that your opponent will find too abhorrent to accept, a cost they won't pay to survive.

                        I read this sutra as an examination of the very Sidereal philosophy that "apparently-harmless or even beloved observer" is a role from which a canny person can exert significant control. It merely requires a willingness to discard love and the appearance of agency.

                                    Originally posted by Sunder the Gold View Post
                                    But the target of Crimson Palm Counterstrike is an opponent trying to do a Sidereal harm, and the children in the scripture have nothing to do with the Maiden's tribulation.
                                    Note that "Children" in this context could refer to "Children of Earth" or a similar concept, i.e. mortals. Lots of sobriquets also refer to character types of presumably adult characters as "Children of ..." or the like, so it doesn't need to refer to actual juveniles. Also, most of these people have nothing to do with a Sidereal's missions and life, but she still crosses in-between them nonetheless, and that causes anything from complications to mortality along her line of work preserving destiny.

                                    The Expectant Maiden does specify a boy, though. I said that "a boy" can be "a child".
                                    Any references to a state of youth in an Endings scripture - as a rule, always about transitions and changes of state - are probably fleeting and in the past. This is setting aside other colloquial uses of the word "boy", like "old boy" of a school, referring to a graduate and hence probably not a child, or an address of confrontation toward a man, potentially one younger and less experienced, or abhorrent "boys will be boys". It tends to flavor the scripture with a tone of familiarity with the individual responsible for the focus character's predicament, rather than referring to a juvenile, male-gendered individual. Literal readings seem to be the last thing in mind for most Sidereal scriptures.

                                                    Sidereals are the favored servants/pawns/children/slaves of a set of goddesses who claim to be more or less omniscient and claim to be working for the Greater Good without every revealing what that Greater Good is, and who may or may not be sub-sapient machines carrying out their programming without any real goals/soulless chessmasters who move everyone and everything about as puppets on a stage in the service of goals that only they can see and only they care about.

                                                    Sidereals are the Chosen of Destiny, noble defenders of the causality who have to make hard choices in the service of what they believe is the best future, who are reassured by the Maidens that, yes, indeed, it is for a good future and even if it wasn't its still better than every other alternative.
