                    Originally posted by The Wizard of Oz View Post
                    I'm not really bothered by the cannibalism (I like to see cultures with unusual cultural practices), but I do wonder, in the desert, how many people there actually are for them to eat. Seems like it'd be more just a food for special occasions.
                    See, that's why I added the qualifier of being a way of life.

                    As far as I know, in any real life culture that engaged in cannibalism, it's something that has very specific connotations and purpose. Perhaps not always a thing done to foes as a process of honouring their strength, but that one features prominently. A lot of times, cannibalism has been reserved for one's own fallen, with implications of things such as recycling their power and spirit into the community, or otherwise making something very intimate of something like a feast to honour them.

                    It's not something I've read much on in a while, and there are probably gaps in my understanding, but that's where I'm coming from.

                    When you get examples such as the Dune People and those tribes on that Northern island who were featured in the opening comic of Manual: Dragon Blooded (but not with the Lintha, interestingly*), you get the much more fictional instance of a culture that is all about cannibalism, to the point of attacking people specifically for the sake of eating them. It's not always on a premise that doing so is their primary source of sustenance (and it's particularly absurd when it is), but it's often exaggerated in a manner that makes it a basic shorthand for a community of people that are basically monsters. To be sure, I don't recall offhand any instance in which you got a depiction of somebody from a society that was super into eating people who was really pleasant and friendly and admirable in their own context.

                    There are a few issues with it. One of the most prominent to my mind is that it has a really ugly legacy of being used to other and marginalize people indigenous to parts of the world that have been traditionally colonised and exploited by European powers, so it kind of raises a red flag to me, if only for uncritically invoking something with a stain on its history.

                    As the Dune People go overall, I feel as though they exist in a somewhat odd place where the game has kind of flirted with the idea of wanting them to be well-rounded and acceptable and sympathetic, but kind of messes up the landing with wrapping them up in so much that is coded as feral and alien. I feel as though having both in the same place doesn't work the best, and that one is more worthy of keeping than the other.

                    Never mind the other issues with using albinism as a shorthand for somebody alien and sinister. Sure, it's a better treatment than most in that it remembers that albinism comes with increased risk of skin cancer, emphasized by putting them in an extremely sunny environment, but like... you could do that without them also being scary cannibal people.

                    I've yet to read Exalted: the Lunars personally, so I can't yet say if the book's overall perspective on people coded to indigenous groups necessarily validates the manner in which it uses the "cannibalism as a way of life" trope. Still, from second hand accounts, I have gathered that the book evinces a rather odd blend of depicting such groups as actually being marginalized by powerful, centralized states against which they are in a constant struggle for survival, and taking at face value the narrative of the barbarian at the gates of civilization, constantly waiting to just roll over and send everybody into the Dark Ages (even if it's from an angle of being the barbarians at the gates). I guess it depends on whereabouts the Dune People fall.

                    * People seem to tend to forget that, as depicted in Blood & Salt, the Lintha only really ever cannibalised one another; they considered other humans as unworthy of the attention, that doing so risked contaminating their Lintha blood, and would get super mad if an outsider was ever to eat the flesh of a Lintha.

                        I remember watching a program once about an explorer who went and lived with remote tribes in the jungle. He joined this Indonesian tribe of cannibals. Except, of course, they didn't seem to eat anyone.

                        Eventually, after becoming close enough that he felt he could raise it, he asked them about their well-known cannibalism. His friend explained that they don't just eat anyone, because that's screwed-up. They only eat witches. Because it's the only way to get rid of their curses.

                        The guy admitted he once ate a witch, and it was awful, traumatising, he felt like he was going to vomit. But he didn't feel as he had any choice, because, well, the other guy was a witch.

                        So, my point is that essentially I think Isator is right; cannibilism is generally part of specific cultural or religious rituals, not something done any time. This tribe, which was infamous amongst locals as being cannibals, actually ate people so rarely that this particular cannibal had only done it once in his life.

                        Of course, there are cases where people starving will commit cannibalism. But this tends to be something they stop once they acquire more food, it's not something that becomes part of the culture.

                            I'll actually say that my own read on the Shogunate is also that it was a time in which government and business interests went in for the mass enslavements of entire extant cultures (as distinct from the possibility that the First Age had magically created cultures to serve a kind of slave function) for the purpose of providing labour to shore up failing infrastructure and providing an alternative to the kind of efficiency produced by Solar directive or ancient sorcery. Heck, I would even read the Sidereals as institutionally promoting and enabling such processes, in their function of trying to keep Creation up and running; of all of the things I would imagine them seeing as a downturn in Creation, mass exploitation of human labour as an alternative to powerful and useful magic that is no longer available seems like one of the more obvious.

                            Sure, I would imagine that a significant part of many Sidereals coming around on "the costs are too high, we need to go for the riskier option" was witnessing the difference between the idea in the abstract and the actual human costs of it in action, and I would certain think that there would be economic, social and political processes going on with and complicating the process as it developed, but yeah, I'm all for the idea that human exploitation took a big upswing after the Usurpation, and that the Sidereals were more than merely silent observers in that.

                            I personally have no interest in condemning the fictional characters over that, or of seeing it primarily in terms of them being basically bad people. That's not to say that I'd categorise those Sidereals as good people that are tragically required to do bad things for the sake of a greater good, just that they're people with varying characters, priorities and perspectives trying to deal with things as they crop up, and dealing with complex forces that they're not fully in control of.

                                The major thing about tribal cannibalism is that, as a practical matter, tribal societies don't live in areas with the population density to maintain it as a regular thing anyway. Mathematics of population and food consumption are such that the only people who could be regular cannibals are members of small elite classes in settled agriculture-based civilizations. Cannibal tribes are never going to do it more than rarely and (as a side effect of the rarity as much as anything else) ritualistically.

                                I mean, theoretically, sure, you could have a tribal society that lived next to a high-density settled population and raided it regularly for people to eat. Sociologically that's unstable; either the tribe will have the ability to take over and become a small elite leadership class in the settled society, or the settled society will raise an army and crush the tribe.

                                And it's only going to happen if the settled society doesn't have access to large domesticated herbivores, which are cheaper and safer sources of meat. Cattle (horse, sheep, whatever) raids are going to be preferred to cannibal raids by tribes, and elites eating beef are less likely to provoke peasant revolts than eating people.

                                    I'm not so sure as to whether the Shogunate or even Usurpation would represent a step up in mass enslavement. (I don't quite share the model of slavery as being likely to be driven by economic substitution; it's not how it happened IRL at all, ever, and I'd prefer to hew to those causal factors in the interests of my preferred version of verisimilitude).

                                    But I don't have any problem so much with it hashing out that the Sidereals were often on the side of accepting "more slavery". (They're not "my guys" who I want to have clean hands!).

                                    It's more that I don't see this as motivated from any Bronze Faction specific factor of a preference for higher populations whatever the quality of life, or "expediency", that can be drawn back to a decision that, in my view, was mostly about how to resolve Solar tyranny alone, rather than a general philosophical perspective on quality v quantity of life, or on potential v security.

                                    And it's not a decision which begins or ends at the desks of the Bronze Faction (still less their inner circle alone); Gold Faction, Lunars, Dragonblooded, gods, mortals, etc. have all made decisions that have led to the current state of things, and with similar motivations of maintaining real power. (It's not the case that the Gold Faction or the Lunars are or ever have been generally "anti" slavery, while the Bronze Faction is generally "pro"; both those parties are as equally likely to support it or use it in the interests of their clients and their own power politics). The influence of the Bronze Faction is only a sliver among those vast array of factors beyond their control.
