    I'm curious what level of predictive accuracy you think should be accorded to Sidereals? The basis of everything I was saying in the post quoted is predicated on Sidereal optics being excellent.         With regard to slavery: I wasn't talking about altering the practice once the Realm was a "globe spanning empire." I was talking about when the Realm hardly existed as yet. There weren't complex multi-Directional trade influences at this time. There weren't powerful external economic drivers. At the time of the Empress's rise, there were basically three drivers of the Blessed Isle, the Empress, the several hundred Dragon Blooded who bowed their heads to her, and what was becoming the Immaculate Order. There is ZERO canon influence representing Chejop Kejak in ANY role as anything but a driven Utilitarian, whose overwhelming concern was making Creation's Fate into a) Something sustainable, and b) Something controllable.

        I've said before, I truly believe EX3 needs to distance the Bronze Faction from this "Creation's Survival at all Costs" mindset. While ancient individuals like Chejop Kejak, and Anys Syn might be obsessed with the binary "Creation Lives/Creation Dies" because of their pre and mid-Usurpation experiences, that isn't a mindset likely to be shared by Sidereals who weren't even alive at a time when Creation as a whole was, or recently had been, threatened. Individuals like Sad Ivory would, for example, likely share a little of that (as someone born in the immediate aftermath of the Great Contagion), but anyone born say, a hundred years post-Crusade wouldn't have the same sensibilities.                                                             Hmm... Serfdom in general, very approximately, some kind of legal restrictions binding a person to service on the land. Legal bondage to the land.

                                                            Rather than the situation for most agrarian peasants where you *could* go to the city... but:

                                                            You'll leave the only wealth you have (in the form of a smallholding) behind and if you have any running debts (and agrarian life is full of mutual obligation and communalism and "credit") it won't be good for your remaining family for you to walk out on them. You probably don't have a lot of urban artisan skills, life as a unskilled wage laborer is tough, and even the cheapest calories (likely some form of porridge) are more expensive in the city than the country and you're more likely to starve to death than in the country, even as a skilled worker. And let's talk about the cities' greater tendencies to high murder rates and disease as well...

                                                            I mean, in reality, for most pre-modern societies there were societies that restrained by law the freedom for agrarian workers to leave the land they were born on, or migrate to cities or towns. But even those that did not, faced pretty major constraints in how possible it even was for significant amounts of the population to move or become a non-agrarian worker, so had an enduring character of a large peasantry.

                                                            (Not sure which of these characterises the Realm best! As best I can remember, the Immaculates frown somewhat on too much social mobility, but I can't recall much in the way of legal bondage to land.)

                                                            Societies that overcame these limitations often did so by combinations of good fortune and clever engineering (e.g. watching a great documentary on Angkor recently) that boosted agricultural productivity or trade (so local surpluses when achieved could be traded). Even powerful rulers who ruled huge empires, without those factors could only centralise smaller towns and cities into grand capitals, not really achieve a lasting reduction in the agrarian share of the population and an expansion in the urban share and non-agrarian share (the Persian Empires probably had vaster capitals than any city-state of classical Greece... but the degree to which the % non-agrarian of the population was greater is doubtful). And usually when these factors did work, when people did produce enough food to outrun the limitations, there was a time limit on how long they boosted non-agrarian share for, and a return to the old days.

                                                            I think as moderns, we can overestimate the universality of the tendency to which historically people were kept in agrarian situations because of oppresive legal forces. After all, city life is great, and there's plenty of opportunity and extra wealth,right? So why wouldn't you move? But this is not the case through much of human history; the technologies and economic and institutional changes to allow this to happen were huge (and arguably remain precarious to a degree today).
