                  Jaja - I mentioned them in my non-Human races thread. Started off thinking of D&D races (which are trope-codifiers and can cross-breed with humans).... moved to Elder Scrolls (same as D&D but with a more unified origin story)..... ended up on real-world ethnic groups (so much real-world diversity).

                  I'll note that Pelago means "deep sea" and the Pelagothropes had to go deep enough to escape Exalted death-squads so I think the Pelagothropes are an expy of Mermaids or even the Dreugh (if you google, there are different breeds - with the "land" version having crustacean legs - and the "sea" version, tentacles.

                  The Bajau Laut only live off coasts, so they're more like the Maormer of Elder Scrolls (who also have been living off coasts / isles for centuries).

                  re: Wyld Exposure
                  Currently, I think the Wyld is kind of useless. Most of Creation's inhabitants never live that far out, so any OOC mechanics reserved for it are wasted - and IF you do live that far out, you're so far removed as to be irrelevant to Creation.

                  So one idea I'm toying with - especially with new players - is to create various Human "races" / ethnicities. Elder Scrolls & several real-life examples serve.

                  For Example:
                  Maybe give each ethnic group 1-3 default abilities (like specialities - it doesn't matter what the character individually is, these would represent the effect of culture). And then maybe 1-3 subtle mutations (again, nature doesn't care what you want to be - you're born with the bonuses you have).

                  So a sea-coast ethnicitiy (like the Bajau Laut) might be given some Athletics or Survival bonuses for swimming - maybe a mutation to help them live longer. And although OOC they'd be considered human, IC the non-Bajau "races" might make-up some mythical origin story for them.

                  The Marukani in the Scavenger Lands? Give them bonuses to Ride (again, it's irrelevant what an individual character wants to do with their life - even a Baker or Tailor among the Marukani must have learned how to ride a horse when they were little), etc.

                  Sijan? Shadowland/Death tainted cities might have some tweaks - to not make them full Ghost-Blooded - but perhaps a resistance to disease (explaining why cities full of so much death haven't died off of a plague) or an Occult specialty (Elder Scrolls' Dunmer are a good example - in Morrowind, their ancestral cults are so strong, they have the "racial" ability to call on ancestor ghosts for help.......it's "racial", not because of something silly (ie, "they're the only people with ancestors!") but because "their cultural veneration of the dead is so strong, ghosts simply know to repay them".

                  It's really an extension of Lookshy (who also has built-in requirements for citizens) - and of the An-Teng snakemen (who aren't considered Beastmen IC - but are confused for a semi-divine race). It was this example that really re-established (for me) the divorce between IC and OOC knowledge (since so much of Exalted has an OOC perspective).

                  Sooooo.... that's the gist of the idea.

                  Less "let me use these cool mechanics and references on obscure mer-people".

                  More "let me modify all humans into regional trope-codifiers - so everyone is human but with slight tweaks - and then IC, let rumor and urban legend convince each other they're unrelated". So OOC, new-players get more hard-coded ideas of each place ("Lookshy? The war-people, like Spartans, bonuses to War" - Sijan? The death-people - Marukani - the Ride people - the Bajau? the Sea people - the Nilotic? the fast people - etc).

                  Someone mentions that the Lintha are probably going to be Human+ in E3 (instead of a completely different species). Maybe those are the Bajau Laut? Purely human - but their specific mutations are for living at sea.

                                  I imagine a very large amount of mutants in Creation got their mutations from... their parents. Whose ancestors may have got it from a Lunar, the Wyld, First Age sorcery, etc, etc.
                                  But also, maybe they're just humans who were made like that originally. Most likely, after all, the original human stock was created in a variety of forms (including stuff like Djala).

                                  Plus, there's not just Wyld Mutation, but the influence of the Elemental Poles. Why do Northerners sometimes have blue hair, and southerners sometimes red hair? Coincidence? I think it's because the elemental pole subtly affects them.
                                  Maybe the elemental pole of water subtly affects people born there, so over the last 10,000 years they've slowly mutated to be able to hold their breath longer.


                                  One of the PCs' puppet kings in the game I ran was a sea person; gills, webbed fingers, bluish skin, no hair. He was just from an ethnic group that were all like that, and lived under the sea. Maybe they were descended from mutants, or fish people, or maybe it was just the effect of living near the Elemental Pole of Water for uncounted millenia. Maybe the Primordials just made them like that in the first place.


                                  Creation's residents generally just think any mutation is a result of the Wyld, sorcery, or spirits screwing with you.
                                  That, or those foul goat-****ing moon-witches.

                                  But while that's true, I think a lot of small mutations might just be assumed to be ethnic variation. Like those ancient travelogues you get where people claim they've come across people with one giant ear, or who can eat rocks, or are 10 foot tall, or mermaids. They don't assume these people have been changed by magic; they just assume they're weird people who were made that way by God/the Gods/whatever. If you've not seen most of humanity, you don't really know what's strange; if you've lived all your life in a peasant village where everyone has brown skin and black hair, you have no particular reason to find foreigners being 10 foot tall much stranger than foreigners having white skin and blonde hair.

                                  So if someone in the east meets a western woman with gills, he might just assume that people in the strange exotic west often have gills, just like they have weird-shaped faces and weird colourations, rather than leaf-green hair and bark-brown hair like normal people do.

                                  Obviously the more mutations someone has, and the weirder they are, the more likely they are to be recognised as a mutant.

                                  And conversely, someone from a never-seen-before ethnic group might be assumed to be a mutant by the ignorant.

                                  Probably a lot of this depends on the socio-cultural context in which the viewer sees mutation, as well as the variety of people they've met.
