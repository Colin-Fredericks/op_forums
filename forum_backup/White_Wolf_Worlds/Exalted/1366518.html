    The Great Curse : How do you make great use of it ?

    02-09-2020, 07:06 AM
    The title says it all.

    When first reading Exalted, it was The Great Curse that sold me on Exalted 3e. After discovering some games that used similar elements (Monsterheart), the opportunity for me to play some sort of epic mythological fantasy, with passioned heroes that sometimes hardly control their passion (whatever form it may take) was the moment I realised Exalted was for me. I already envisioned Heracles, killing his family in a moment of crazyness, in play with my players. And, to my uttermost surprise, it usually delivered, and it delivered even beyond my expectations, which is something not so common in my experience.

    However, I happened to notice 2 trends in my manner of handling limit breaks (Solar, I didn't get the opportunity to play other Exalted enough) :

    (1) Agreement with the player to perform a specific act that would be very meaningful for him, and then find a thematically coherent Limit Break that will fall upon the character. This was usually the most satisfactory outcome for me, the drama was on point, the changes were radical, I don't recall having a single bad experience.

    (2) Find a thematically appropriate Limit Break, and then plays it out naturally. It's where I usually had the most "meh" experience. Sometimes it was very bad (the character just do nothing important and follows the group in the appropriate mood of the Limit Break), sometimes it's interesting (a character does an out of ordinary action that changes some elements of the game, but usually it's not major) but I found it very lacking in intensity. I also noticed this trend with another ST, so it gave me the idea it wasn't only me.

    So it's clear I prefer (1) rather than (2). But I had players tell me they preferred (2) over (1) because it felt less driven to them, which I totally understand, even though I ususally find it lacking.

    I'd also like to open the discussions to the following element :

    (3) I usually use the Limit Break as a way to change the dynamic of power/relationship in the setting. It was so damn awesome, because through Limit Break, I had a PC go from the highest social status to a simple Immaculate Monk, and then to a hero, then to murder his pupil, and then to be the infamy of his people. I loved every second of it, and it was just one character ! I've almost never seen a character progression in a game which is not linear, and it was so refreshing to have this.

    (4) The Great Curse is a taboo. I explicitly forbid players to let their characters think about The Great Curse, other than thematically or metaphorically (like I accept a character saying he was "driven mad" by the presence of the Fair Folk nearby and entered a Berserk Rage). If they do not follow this rule, and somehow decide to try and explain and conquer the phenomenon, the campaign end. I can imagine sometimes deciding to do a campaign about vainquishing The Great Curse, but honestly it would be an exception.

    So here it is. What do you do with The Great Curse ? Do you like it ? Do you ignore it ? What do your players and ST do with it ? What would you like to change about it ? Did it bring an enjoyable experience in your games ?

      Frenzy is a difficult mechanic.

      People are attached to their characters and want their character to do what they tell them to do.

      That's part of the reason I like 3E Dragon-Blooded so much. No limit track, you enter Limit Break when you want extra dragon experience points.

      If there's something that a player has in mind (they want to kill their family in a beserk rage) then I think (1) is a great way of bringing that about in play. But it has to be player driven.

      (2) is how most of my Frenzies have played out across every WW game which uses the dynamic (granted Exalted gives you a bit more leeway than, say, Werewolf: The Apocalypse). Like you, I've found Limit Breaks to be lack-luster when played this way -- they don't add a lot to the drama, but they feel a bit less forced.

      With your (3) experience, I'm interested in what led to that: did you agree to become a monk before you RPed the scene (a kind of a (1) scenario) or was it something that just happened (more of a (2) thing)? It sounds like these were unplanned changes that just kind of "happened" -- which is Limit working as intended. Killing your family and going on a great quest of atonement is SUPPOSED to be a life-changing thing, this is what Limit (in the ideal) is supposed to do. It's just a bit lackluster Rules-As-Written.

      (4) seems a little heavy-handed. There's no in-character reason why the PCs should know that the Great Curse exists. People shouldn't be trying to "game" it. But ending a campaign because the characters noticed their passions were running high and wondered whether there was more to it... in a world where supernatural mind control exists... seems heavy-handed. People like to deny responsibility for their actions, and the Immaculate Philosophy specifically teaches that the Anathema will lose control of themselves... it seems unreasonable to rule that the PCs can't ever look into this aspect of the game, and just have to turn a blind-eye to it.

      Personally? I say run Limit Break with the Dragon-Blooded mechanics. Make it a tool for players to use for mechanical benefit, and use the reward of Dragon XP to drive the drama. Make it a thing the players can do to change the narrative, not something that is done to their characters which changes their character.

      Hi, I'm JohnDoe244. My posts represent my opinions, not facts.

          JohnDoe244

          (1) Is usually player driven with me. I won't do this to someone if they don't agree with it, or if they don't trust me when I tell them it's a secret I want to setup.

          (2) I find Frenzies to be the most "effective" of the (2) kind : except if the character get totally and immediately rekt by other players, it's usually the start of a great change in the dynamic of the game

          (3) I would say it was one of the (1) thing, but it came from the player. The characters and their Pharaoh were in political conflict with the local Immaculate Temple, who was about to declare them anathema. Finding an opportunity to 1) Find an interesting resolution for the scene, 2) Allow for interesting character development, and 3) have a coherent explanation because his character just killed innocent people in order to set a trap on the Immaculate Order, the player proposed to make a "Vow of Piety" Limit Break for his character, where he would bow down on the ground before the Immaculate Order in exchange for salvation, and the salvation of his king. He then was trialed to spend a whole week in this position, in the middle of the throne room, unable to communicate with his pupil and best friend the Pharaoh, before being admitted as an immaculate apprentice. I truly believe that the core element in this is that he first found an interesting act/consequence to take, and then found a limit break that allowed him to play along.

          I have a complicated relationship with (4). As said, I'm ok with players thinking about their morality, stability, their sense of self, of control, etc ... But at the same time, as quite clearly said in this post, Limit Break is a core experience of Exalted 3e to me. I literally think I would not have played this game if this mechanic was not it in. Not because the mechanic in itself is extraordinary (roll dices accumulate a counter break free) but because it is core (to me) to the fantasy Exalted is depicting, of tragic heroes doomed to fail when they are at their best and blessed to rise when they are at their worst. Having a psychological approach of "Oh actually I'm becoming crazy because of a specific curse placed upon my soul, not because these events are taking too much on my psyche when I dramatically deal with them" would kill the mystic of this approach to me, the same way "he has a lot of medichlorians" demystified the Force in Star Wars. I get it, but I don't buy it.

          I really like the Limit Points of the Solar (and I guess Lunar as well), because it allows players to go all in. I've done a short campaign of like 5 games of DB and no one did a Limit Break. I think it's fine, because DB has a different experience, but I think the Limits Points speak differently to the experience of the players and what they can do.

                      I think the curse is first and foremost a meta tool the game uses to teach players that when your character is an unstoppable demigod, making the character fallible and shooting yourself in the foot now and then actually makes the story more interesting.

                      If players are completely unfamiliar with the concept, then using the rules provided gives an okay if somewhat uneven way to try it out.

                      Once players understand the idea of fallible heroes and decide they like it, they can start roleplaying their character's failings themselves rather than rely on predetermined triggers and limit breaks, as this usually leads to better stories.

                      And if the players try to play Limit by the rules and decide they hate it, then they can just throw the idea away, safe in the knowledge that they tried it out and it wasn't for them.
