    Agreed.

    ​Might I also recommend reading the Book of Judges? There's a pretty Exalted-esque depiction in there where a man is such a badass on the battlefield that even God can't kill him - he sends down the stars from the sky to end the guy... and he sweeps them into the water and drowns them. But then a woman's able to do the job, seducing him and then stoving in his temple with a mallet and tent-peg while he sleeps.
    Ah, Sisera.
    Actually, in the "historical" bit, Sisera (general of the Canaanites, ie the lowlanders) takes 900 chariots and an unspecific number of infantry, Barak (who Deborah appointed general of the Israelites, ie the highlanders) takes 10000 warriors.* In the battle, Sisera's army panics. He runs away and goes to hide in a woman called Jael's tent, but she staves his head in with a tent-peg while he's asleep. She doesn't really seduce him though, she just gives him food.

    *Like most pre-modern reports of battles, take the numbers with a pinch of salt.

    The victory song the prophet Deborah sings (which is, incidentally, one of two songs that are by far the oldest parts of the Bible), about how great God and Deborah is (and naming and shaming the tribes who didn't help), does feature earthquakes, rain, stars fighting Sisera, and the torrent Kishon that sweeps "them" away, but I'm not sure what Kishon is or whether "them" is the stars.

    Anyway, nice to see a middle-aged woman famed for her wisdom and intelligence (rather than sexiness or badassery) get to be the leader and hero.

    (Judges has a bit of a post-apocalyptic vibe too, as it's set about 100 years after the Bronze Age Collapse, which they occasionally mention off-hand. Anyway, lots of inspiration for Zeniths. Hmmm... is there a charm for siccing bears on people for mocking your baldness?)

    Moving on.
    I quite enjoyed Romance of the Three Kingdoms, because as it's semi-historical, it has a lot more unexpected twists than fictional works do! It's loooong though, and sometimes difficult to keep track of all the characters, especially as some have similar names.


    Hmmm, what else.
    So, ages ago I read a book called Spellwright, by Blake Charlton. To cut a long story short; magic works by glowing runes that you pull out of the air, or people's heads, or something. It's all language based, you literally write the spells. The main character goes to magic school, except that he's got magical dyslexia, and mispells (literally) everything, causing all sorts of chaos. But in the end, while he gets expelled, he learns that his magical dyslexia is essential to the world's magic... for some reason I forget. It's kind of a fun, original-though-it-doesn't-seem-original, okay-not-amazing book. (IIRC, he's dyslexic and his kid is dyslexic so he decided to write dyslexic Harry Potter to inspire his kid, or something like that. Oh, and apparently the author is really nice at conventions.)

    A while later, at the library, I picked up a book called Spellbreaker, which turns out to be the third in a trilogy. It was, again, decent but not amazing.
    But anyway, the interesting thing was that it was about a culture chock-full of gods. The main character is the granddaughter of the character from the first book, and her boyfriend is a shark-god from a small group of islands (I think maybe they break up because he can't stop himself knocking island girls up, because the islanders believe he's destined to father a great island hero). She also travels around with a wrestling god as a bodyguard. And they interview a beggar god, do diplomacy with the head god of the island empire, etc, etc.
    Previously, I'd always been unsatisfied with gods in Exalted. But this made me realise I should stop using them as dematerialised spirits who hang out in sanctums and their priests do most of the interactions. Instead, I should use them as an excuse to have a wacky character who's weaker than or equal to an Exalt but stronger than a mortal, and I can mostly make up their powers.
    So my current game's original sandbox area featured Volundr the strong dwarven volcano-god who the Full Moon competed with in strength (who's also Jarl of the city on that volcano), Orm the irascible snake-god of the harsh river, Orma the art-loving courtier snake-goddess of the pleasant river, Dhrun the falcon-headed god of a mountain who leads a warrior lodge of wrestlers, White Bull the dopy forest god who leads a warrior lodge of minotaurs, and the bay goddess Sig-Yanna the Hungry Maw who dwells in her court of water elementals and mermaids and fish-people in an ancient sunken city. I found that much more satisfying. I think one of them dematerialised, once (after a PC beat him in a menhir-lobbing contest).




    I have one more book (series) I'd be tempted to recommend. It contains at least 5 really enjoyable books!
    It's about a young man who is the reincarnation of an ancient hero from a lost age of advanced magic and magitek. He wields immense power, but this power is tainted with madness. As his power's grow, he must contend with the invasive memories of his past life, a great hero who went steadily insane.
    The protagonist's best friends are a guy who gains the memories of history's greatest generals, and a dream-walking wolf-eyed commander of wolves with amazing senses and reflexes like a wolf.
    However, our protagonist must contend with two enemies;
    1) the continent's main religion, whose magic nuns believe that his madness-tainted power cannot be allowed to threaten the world and so he must be killed or have his magic taken away;
    2) 13 ancient beings, the Dreadlords, who are also heroes of the ancient lost age but ones who swore themselves to an ancient bound evil in return for great power.

    But, well... as they say, friends don't let friends read the Wheel of Time.

                        Originally posted by KymmetheSeventh View Post
                        I'm gonna toss out a recommendation for Debt: The First 5000 Years. It's definitely not a narrative text, but it's incredibly informative and is packed with the history and culture of civilizations all throughout earth's history. The book's presentation and style are also extremely polished. I found it a really fun read.
                        Reminds me, some good non-fiction for Exalted:

                        Debt: the First 5,000 Years by David Graeber, as mentioned. A lot of Exalted 3e's stuff on economics and how the Resources Merit even like, work, take inspiration from this one. It has good examples of how different cultures did things, how different societies handled debt and usery, and so on. Lots of good dieas to yoink from for your own games.

                        1491: New Revelations of the World Before Columbus by Charles C. Mann is interesting for explorations of the pre-Columbian New World, the kinds of socieites there, notable societies, and the impacts of the European conquest in the realms of disease and war. I think the stuff on the post-disease years is actually pretty good idea also of the way Creation probably felt like a lot. And also there's some interesting bits on the way that colonial powers at times view conquered peoples, which has its own interesting elements when considering the Realm.

                        1177 BC: the Year Civilization Collapsed by Eric H. Cline is really cool. Creation is often called Bronze Age, but I think that folks actually 1) Assume that's more primative htan it is and 2) Miss out on a lot of the stuff that is later. But still, it is a great book on giving a snapshot of the world of the Bronze Age, how interconnected it was, the way war was conducted, and so on. The causes of its collapse and such are also probably good inspriations for the current Time of Tumult and what the Realm Civil War might do it things.

                        Seeing Like a State by James C. Scott is kind of a different on. It's about mostly the 20th Century, but the previous devs at least have noted that a lot of how it talks about Modernist thought, and the human costs of some of how it was implemented were really itneresting. I think it's a good example to me on what kinds of things to consider when building "large scale" stories, and how often drama isn't about whether or not you succeed, but whether you had unforseen consequences when you get your policies implemented. Exalted rulers have often a lot of these same conditions of the things the book talks about, and I htink it's ag ood book for both STs and players to consider on building such stories.

                        Against the Grain also by James C. Scott is insteada bout how agriculture came about, what led to folks building cities, and even some interesting points on how for a lot of human history, non-settled peoples were the majority. I think it's especially interesting in context of how the Realm and Threshold probably see "barbarians" and how a lot of the space between settlements have vibrant, healthy cultures. Plus the kind sof means states do to create themselves, exert power, and maintain power over their populations.

                        King Leopold's Ghost by Adam Hochschild is an account of how brutal extractional colonialism and empire can get. Kind of wroth remembering that a chunk of the Realm's inspiration is not just be The Empire in a fantasy sense. It's The Empire running on real politik. And that means the Realm evil doesn't have to ham things up. Real life empire was brutal as it was.

                        Zealot: the Life and Times of Jesus of Nazareth by Raza Aslan is pretty good at painting a picture of occupied Judeah in the 1st Century. I like how it discusses things like the Roman views of the province, the people there's views of the Romans, and what a historical Jesus might have been like at the time, how he rose to power an dhow some accidents of history led to the version of the Christian faith we hae today. I think that it's good for imagining the Realm's occupaiton of a place, how quickly religions can build up, and the impact people have over time, and how quickly faiths can develop. (This last one is important to me in understnading how fast the Immaculate Philosophy spread, and any modern groups like the Cult of the Illumianted might operate.)

                        And stuff.

                                The reincarnation-related stuff, and the First Age, is quite Exalted. The magic system isn't. Other than the Dreadlords, the bad guys are more like Warhammer: beastmen from the Chaos Wastes to the north.

                                If you got stuck at 11, I'd recommend reading the last 3... ie the ones written by Sanderson instead of Jordan. They're much faster-paced, as you generally get from Sanderson. 5-11 are the really slog-fest ones.

                                Which reminds me.
                                The Stormlight Archive, by Brandon Sanderson, is set in an exotic world of rock and crustaceans and massive storms. A long time ago, magical reincarnating warriors armed with huge (but light) armour and weapons, empowered by their principles and ideals, fought to save the world. But they abandoned their duties, turned from good, and vanished.
                                In the modern day, aristocrats use the ancient magical arms and armour (the giant swords can be summoned and dismissed into elsewhere). But now, the ancient warriors are being reborn, bringing back secret magic from long ago.
                                The fights in that feel much more Exalted. Search "chasmfiend", "shardblade" or "shardplate" on Google images, you'll see what I mean.
