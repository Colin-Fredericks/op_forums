        Originally posted by AnubisXy View Post

        In my own games Realm's dysfunction and lack of a succession system was very much intentional on the part of the Empress. I mean, she didn't plan on dying any time soon and imagine if she had gone, "Hey Mnemon, you're my heir, but since I plan to live forever, unless foul play occurs and someone murders me, you'll probably be dead long before I ever am." That probably wouldn't be a great idea.

        Further, she just plain didn't trust Chejop any further than she could throw the Sword of Creation, and quite a bit of her paranoia (well deserved considering there were at least 300 shapeshifting Exalted who wanted her dead) was directed at him. The two were allies of convenience and nothing more. Their goals happened to be in alignment for the moment - she wanted to stay alive and remain in power. He wanted to ensure Creation's stability and the best way to do that was to work with the Scarlet Empress, but that could easily change in the future. So she wouldn't want to make it too tempting or easy for Chejop to decide that it would be best for Creation if the Scarlet Empress were replaced with a more pliable successor.

        Knowing that the Realm would collapse into civil war if she vanished simply made it less likely that Chejop would decide to try and get rid of her.
        Well, by the way that it was written in the original book, the Thousand Hazy Paths was started as a somewhat extreme response to a random assassination attempt that came distressingly close to succeeding.

        Whatever one opted to make of the Empress in precise details, I found the general inclination of the backstory to be one in which she did have a genuine concern for the fate of the world, at least at the beginning, and was terrified at the possibility of what would happen if they immediately resumed the old kind of fighting. She would almost certainly prefer not to die, but I could see her at least having an attitude towards Chejop in which there's enough respect to presume he would install somebody competent (or perhaps rather, she would respect him in a manner as to assume that she shares her perspective that nobody else is competent). I think that she was far more worried about being killed by some random opportunist, who wouldn't know what they were in for.

        Hell, she may have even opted to develop a system of succession were it not for the fact that one of her earliest children was Ragara. Across all Editions, he's just been... the worst.

            Are the Dragonblooded good? Some of them are, yes, Is The Realm good? Thaaat's thornier.

            It's important to realize that most, if not all of Exalted's major powers are, by our standards, pretty bad. It's 100% legal to own your fellow human beings as property in most of Creation, and most people think that's just how the world shakes out. If you took the exploitation, greed and human misery out of Nexus, Gem, Harborhead or Paragon, just to pick four major cities out of a hat, they'd probably collapse into screaming anarchy in a week, and take a large chunk of an entire Direction's economy with them.

            So..Good by whose standard? If I'm using mine, nearly every society in Creation is varying degrees of evil, and The Realm can only be called worse due to scale. You're not going to find a Good nation unless you make one.

                I know, but my point is that a subject like this becomes complicated by the fact that, when portrayed realistically, a genuinely wronged group still has a capacity to respond in a manner that is liable to have a lot of collateral damage (or very deliberate damage towards targets that might be dubious), which can generate a genuine fear in the other side.

                I'm reminded of Star Trek: Deep Space Nine and its portrayal of the Bajoran Resistance: the show was very clear on how the occupation of their home world entailed a number of atrocities, but it didn't shy away from the idea that they had no compunctions about attacks that would kill civilians or even occasionally their own people, and we'd thus see Cardassians of various ranks whose stated motivation would be primarily defending their own lives or the lives of their people.

                        Come to think of it, one might take a few worthwhile things in how to portray the Realm with complexity and nuance with the presentation of the Cardassians in Deep Space Nine.

                        Gul Dukat in particular, one of the all time great villains; an all around terrible person, but multi-faceted in his being so, quite compelling even at his worst and with a certain charm that can occasionally make you forget the worst (a thing that happens to characters dealing with him every now and then), and with a few... it would be inaccurate to call them redeeming characteristics, but things that he does that are not terrible, and even laudable in their own right. His activities a combination of internalized values that he expresses uncritically from his imperialistic, militaristic and authoritarian society*, and his own unpleasant personality. In particular, many of his rationalizations of his activities are highly self-serving, and it's likely that a lot of his reprisals for repeated attempts on his life were driven by a wounded vanity.

                        * One of the compelling and somewhat laudable things that he does is, when his homeland is being invaded by Klingons and he manages to acquire valuable intelligence, he is enraged at how the government is unwilling to use that intelligence to fight back and turns down the opportunity to resume a high-ranking office that he'd been forced out of, opting to instead go on a one-ship guerrilla campaign against the Klingons. But he frames this in terms of his people having become weak and cowardly, and that his willingness to fight means that he's the only real Cardassian left in existence.

                            The questions around them, where there's room for "more harm than good" should you choose to approach that angle, often revolve less around whether they are directly malevolent or purely self interested when Exalted (though such people are among their number), as whether

                            a) loose cannons with aspirations to improve society or visions of grandeur will ultimately have a positive effect on the system, or whether events will become larger and are larger than the talents and assumptions can manage, and their revolutions and campaigns will get out of control - the arguments against revolution and against vigilante action

                            b) whether their decision making will be twisted by the Great Curse at the worst moments, ruining what they try to accomplish, and whether it will gradually harden and coarsen them to be worse than they are - the idea that the Great Curse will make tragic heroes of them

                            c) Solar rule, since they are so individually excellent and irreplaceable, won't tend towards the kind of fragile centralization of authority into a limited number of persons, along the lines of what we've discussed ITT for the Scarlet Empress.

                            But generally this is not the main assumption that is the default.

                                                            In assessment of the Realm, at least in terms of its origins, I'd note that it's unlike any real life impetus for imperialism in that it formed in the aftermath of the world having very nearly ended. That defined both the environment that it existed in, and the motivations underlying its policies (which would have also been informed by the memory of the Shogunate). It's been my general read on the initial imperialism that it was compelled both by a sense that order needed to be imposed upon a chaotic Threshold (that would have also been vulnerable to Anathema), and that there needed to be a national grand strategy to give the Dragon Blooded something to do besides constantly fighting each other over territories under their direct control and access to the highest office in the land.

                                                            So one could read the beginning of the Realm as founded in good intentions executed with ruthlessness. One can even play that as the motive that persisted throughout; that the Empress remained constantly alert for keeping the house of cards from collapsing, to the detriment of her personal peace of mind and self-interest (possibly to the point that she wound up largely desensitizing herself to the lives of others), and that was an attitude that she prioritized in her most highly placed ministers and personal agents; the likes of Bal Keraz and Tepet Eshana.

                                                            But at the same time, the Realm itself runs into structural issues; you get Great Houses that become successful enough to raise flags for the Empress, ones that are still inclined for something well-intentioned but ruthless, with the ruthlessness to the point of entertaining bad decisions, ones whose appetites are growing past the point of sustainability, and some who still wound up having their own secret agendas of dubious quality.

                                                            It can be an open question to the individual group as to whether the political landscape presented in What Fire Has Wrought is relatively unexceptional in the history of the Realm, the sort of thing that comes up every couple hundred years, the kinds of plates that the Empress had become well-practiced at spinning (possibly having started with an aptitude that would have been why Chejop supported her in the first place)… or if it's the kind of thing that the Realm had been building up to across the entire length of its existence, accumulating more and more issues until it becomes hard for it to not fall apart. One can develop different games from whether the absence of the Scarlet Empress is a cause or symptom of the current crisis.

                                                            But as the place of the Realm in the setting goes, whether or not it's good or bad might matter less than the fact that it is in a position of desperation, both in whole and in parts. In particular, Houses Ledaal, Nellens, Tepet and V'neef have the demeanour of wounded animals, and that can make them dangerous and unpredictable.

                                                            I've always liked the quote about the game of thrones being one that you either win or die, because I think it captures the mindset of a lot of dynastic or similar power struggles across history; where assessing a given figure's moral character becomes difficult when, for a number of reasons (not all of which are necessarily in their control), the question of who winds up in charge is literally one of life and death.

                                                            Even if, in practical terms, winning does not always need to mean killing everybody else; just the fear that losing has a high enough chance of meaning death that it's unacceptable.
