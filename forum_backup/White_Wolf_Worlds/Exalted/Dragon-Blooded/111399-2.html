                                I think powers that fail against magic are fine in a few specific situations -- generally, abilities with broad applicability where the things they're likely to go up against are correspondingly narrow, such as disguise or tracking charms.

                                Here's why: Broad applicability allows those charms to still be useful even against people who can counter them, because they can be applied indirectly - eg. you can simply avoid the guy who can see through your disguise, which I think is a valid part of playing a master of disguise; and your talent at disguise will help you avoid them by letting you take other routes.

                                This means you can define a useful charm entirely based around those weaknesses, as long as you keep them in mind when considering the charm's intended use. The Jedi mind-trick, for instance, is basically described as "influence the weak of will." This probably means that any sort of firm resistance is going to stop it -- it won't work on Jabba the Hutt, and it certainly won't work on Darth Vader. But it's still an incredibly valuable power to have -- even if you can't use it against Dace personally, you can use it to slip past his guards and wait in ambush in his tent.

                                The problem is that previous editions have sometimes just slapped fails-vs-magic on charms carelessly or in places where it just felt like a gaping weakness (rather than a Jedi Mind Trick-style "this charm isn't intended to be used directly against your main opponent anyway" sort of thematic.) Having that limitation on Force Push or something else that feels like it's supposed to be a central part of your arsenal is not going to be fun at all.

                                                            Originally posted by Aquillion View Post
                                                            I think powers that fail against magic are fine in a few specific situations -- generally, abilities with broad applicability where the things they're likely to go up against are correspondingly narrow, such as disguise or tracking charms.

                                                            Here's why: Broad applicability allows those charms to still be useful even against people who can counter them, because they can be applied indirectly - eg. you can simply avoid the guy who can see through your disguise, which I think is a valid part of playing a master of disguise; and your talent at disguise will help you avoid them by letting you take other routes.
                                                            I think it's better if the master of disguise avoids the guy because it's risky, not because it's a guaranteed failure. That gives you more tension: Do you attempt to move about indirectly, or do you just cross your fingers, roll the dice, and see if the disguise holds up? Plus, the "My powers are useless here!" scenario means that it's entirely possible your powers will end up being useless. If your disguise powers fail against, say, royalty, and you want to fool the Queen of England, then your "master of disguise" won't be able to apply his skills at all. If you're disguised as some foreign dignitary, and suddenly the Queen shows up and wants to meet you, you're going to be in trouble. If the Queen is simply really good at seeing through disguises, then you can say "I may be a master of disguise, but can I really fool the Queen?", weigh the risks, and choose whether you want to abandon the scheme or take the chance. That's far different from saying "Whelp, I can't fool the Queen; might as well give up right now."

                                                            The Jedi Mind Trick is a poor example to use because it's a binary pass/fail that instantly ends the encounter if successful. If Luke could simply wave his hand and say "You don't want to be on the Dark Side anymore; you want to let go of your hate," there wouldn't be any story. Hopefully, 3e won't have as many of those "save-or-die" type effects.

                                                            Note that I'm not going into my other objection to "fails against magic," which is that magic in Creation is integrated into the very fabric of existence, and therefore should be treated as part of that world's physics rather than as an alien set of rules imposed on mundane reality.
