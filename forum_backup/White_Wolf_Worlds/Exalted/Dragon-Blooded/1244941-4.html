        So Primordial War. Since we've gone there as is inevitable in these sorts of threads. It was pretty tenable in First Edition, at least for most of the edition, that the Primordial War could be argued to be a Bad Idea or at least unjustified. It was not well defined how much the Primordials of the age were actively malevolent towards life in the world, or how much the kind of chaotic, fecund magic they brought into the world could be mitigated, or how much the rebellion by the Incarna was a power grab, or how much the Primordials were changed into much worse beings by death and suffering. The First Edition writers and developers mostly didn't want to get into it - I can't remember if Geoff Grabowski ever commented on the subject.

        From late 1e to 2e, you mostly had Neph and then Holden, who did hold, in contrast, strong public views that the Primordial War should and must be justified and the Incarna should to a degree be in the right, with their thumb on the scale of how sadistic and malevolent and dangerous the Primordials would be. And so it would become increasingly difficult to argue any alternative.

        3e, which was again initially at least under Holden's direction, walked away somewhat from the idea that it's something that the books should get into, rather than a distraction from the human focused drama of the Age of Sorrows. (Though I think he still would've answered "Fuck the Primordials" if you ever asked him!).

        So it's one of those things I would have been up for arguing about in the (increasingly distant) past, but kind of not anymore because either you come to the conclusion of 1) if you use most of the pre-Holden+Neph material, we really don't and can't know, or 2) no, if you use most of the canon, the Primordials were clearly in the wrong... but that's doesn't seem very interesting either because it mostly just reflects Ascended Fans solving arguments they got involved in on the internet in favour of their preference, once they were in control of the canon. (Like, if we were having this thread' discussion about whether the Dragonblooded could be "the good guys" and then a fan who felt very vehemently that they shouldn't ever be, got into control of the canon and wrote it such that they couldn't be, you'd have to admit that the argument was "solved"... but I don't think you'd feel very enthusiastic about it.)

        (This is admittedly totally a kind of third wall breaking, mostly useless for people who just want to get into the heads of the setting kind of argument, and probably kind of repellent for new people who are not interested in all these shifts and view clashes over time, which are now, what ten years dead. Just kind of hard for me to look at through another perspective I guess.)

                    Isator, hey, you could not start a new thread and perhaps some of us'll dip our toes?

                    Though I did want to comment on your point from #45 about how the Realm formed in a response to an impetus that is unparalleled in real world history. I'd agree with it quite a bit, but also challenge that a little, that there may be more RW common ground - there are certainly shades of the unification of China.

                    The Realm is put together with a conscious idea of historical cultural unity, the Old Realm, after a period of "Warring States" nominally under unity under the Shogun, with some justification of being in order to oppose an external foe (the Fae in the case of the Realm), and preserve the unified ideal of civilization. So potentially parallels with Chinese unification here (the Shang and Zhou are the idealized Old Realm, powerful dangerous Mongolic steppe competitors are like the Fae, at least as experienced by the frontier Qin state). Though not Roman unification of the Mediterranean (which is not any sense a "re-unification" even ideologically to its founders).

                    (Of course, I'm being a bit loose in applying some factors here, like the degree to which they'd actually experienced a massive invasion).

                    At the other end in terms of how the Realm operates in the Threshold and much of the world, the better available analogy for the Realm *is* probably Rome around the Mediterranean. There you have a clear central power which does extract by tax from the periphery of its empire to fund spending by its governors, military and in its core. Rome does hold these territories because they are launching pads for barbarian invasion and competitor states (e.g. analogously, anathema led assaults for the Realm) , yet are not integrated under a shared memory of cultural unity. Rebellions are frequent. At the same time, despite all this extraction, the effect of unified governance and the "Pax Romana" (despite significant warfare from rebellions) maybe really does boost trade and productivity and how people (at least some) live all around the Mediterranean and not just in Italy.

                    (What doesn't apply so well to my mind is mostly European colonialism in the "New World", or in its two phases (Early Modern and 19th century) in Asia and Africa. Or the recent world system and international global capitalism. These all being characterized by economic systems, technologies, circumstances, histories, social changes and balances of power that mostly don't exist in the Realm's encounter with the rest of the world. There are bits that apply, and in some places more applies, but the enabling and spurring factors and practices mostly seem more different. But that's a topic and tangent probably for another thread.)

                    On your wider post there, I have less to comment, I'm think maybe less sympathetic to the idea that the Realm is under anything like a rational strategy ("national grand strategy") to constrain and channel the Dragon Blooded to useful ends, rather than formed through exigency and to maintain power in the short term (across a lot of successive short terms!), and to manage recurrent crises.

                    All that said, the Realm would seem to face many more constraints than even the analogous real world empires in selecting a new elite that is chosen for loyalty to the state and the nation beyond aristocratic loyalties. Chinese dynasties are able to slowly over time select talented people from gentry classes to replace dependence on elite aristocracy. The Realm probably can't really do anything like this so effectively, both because of the realities that the most talented individuals will be Dragonblooded Dynasts and because its religious system completely moves in the opposite direction.

                            Originally posted by Ghosthead View Post

                            The Realm is put together with a conscious idea of historical cultural unity, the Old Realm, after a period of "Warring States" nominally under unity under the Shogun, with some justification of being in order to oppose an external foe (the Fae in the case of the Realm), and preserve the unified ideal of civilization. So potentially parallels with Chinese unification here (the Shang and Zhou are the idealized Old Realm, powerful dangerous Mongolic steppe competitors are like the Fae, at least as experienced by the frontier Qin state).
                            That can be kind of analogous, but I'd still say falls far short.

                            There have been events in history that have entailed some kind of major death and destruction that, to the people experiencing them, probably would have been severely traumatic or had something known and familiar torn away forever, or made it feel (literally or figuratively) like the world was ending. The Bronze Age collapse, the Black Death, 9/11... those can give a starting point for what the experience of the world in the days following the end of the Scarlet Empress using the Sword of Creation was like, but I would still say that something needs to expand beyond them. It all needs to come from the idea that in the Great Contagion and Fair Folk invasion, the majority of people died, and large parts of the world stopped existing.

                            Set aside the likelihood that many people were in a state where, just days previously, they had been resigned to the probability that nothing could save them; think about what it's like for the known and the familiar to be so utterly torn away like that, the sheer enormity of the idea that most people who existed just a few years ago were now gone. The loss of land alone has enormous implications, cultural and economic; that's not a country being gone in the sense of a political transition; that's entire places, whole landscapes, that simply don't exist anymore. Even if they're not places that you ever personally visited before, or expected to ever visit, think about what it would be like to know that they don't exist anymore; nothing that came from there will ever come again, whole identities just dissolved in a flash.

                            The game has never been wholly consistent on how much of Creation was lost to the Wyld (as opposed to its remains being greatly depopulated), but I honestly don't think that it would need to be very much to severely mess people up for quite a while.

                            That's the world in which the Realm was formed; one in which there really had been, undeniably, unambiguously, devoid of all political rhetoric, a crisis of all-encompassing scale that had threatened not only "civilization", but the existence of everything.

                            If nothing else, one cannot reasonably assume that any of the actors involved would have had anything like objectivity.

                            Originally posted by Ghosthead
                            I'm think maybe less sympathetic to the idea that the Realm is under anything like a rational strategy ("national grand strategy")
                            Grand strategy is just the technical term to refer to how a certain body (often a state) employs all of the resources at its command to achieve a defined long-term goal. It doesn't necessarily represent a value judgement on the plan or goal in question, nor does it imply one single plan that is rigidly adhered to at all stages.

                            Originally posted by Ghosthead
                            to constrain and channel the Dragon Blooded to useful ends, rather than formed through exigency and to maintain power in the short term (across a lot of successive short terms!), and to manage recurrent crises.
                            Well, how long is the short term in this case, and if it was only short term, what was the intention for afterwards?

                            Again, this isn't about talking about a singular master plan, so much as a primary objective and the continuous development of secondary objectives in support of that, and plans in pursuit of the secondary objectives.

                            In the case of the Realm, the idea that the Dragon Blooded have to be cultivated in accordance with policies that will keep them from disastrously fighting one another while still being available to contribute to an overall well-being is... something that I would imagine as just being a secondary objective, but one that takes a lot of priority.

                            But it is something that I would see as a unifying thread in how the Scarlet Dynasty came into existence and how it functions, even if I would never frame it as something that adheres wholly to the vision of the Empress (itself an already fallible thing); it still noticeably consists of compromises, patches and ad hoc solutions. And whether or not it could have lasted much longer is an up-in-the-air question to explore in game.

                            But I find the presentation of it as a system that would keep the Exalted from being like they were written as during the Shogunate to be plausible and compelling. More than that, I would find settling upon an underlying motivation of keeping the Dragon Blooded both satisfied and occupied, in pursuits that they can't openly fight one another over, to be a good basis for developing consistent backstory that can be used for many games involving the Dynasts and the Realm.

                            It's at least something that I would find to resonate with several historical instances, such as Louis XIV being agreed upon to have developed the Versailles court culture as a way of occupying the highest aristocracy, or Toyotomi Hideyoshi's invasions of Korea being driven at least partially by a desire to direct the military ruling class of a newly unified nation collectively against an external foe.

                                So often, these posts follow the pattern:
                                OP: "Is this group at fault for this action?"
                                Response: "Collective action is a myth. There are no groups, only individuals. And, are these individuals really at fault for their actions, when prior actors/destiny forced their hands? What is causality anyway?"

                                I think this is disingenuous. Yes, collective action is not only real, but evidenced (repeatedly) in the Exalted lore. Being good at collective action is arguably one of the Dragon-Blooded's shticks. Escaping destiny is also possible, which means you own the consequences of those actions (even if 'you' is a prior Exaltation that sided with the Unconquered Sun's revolt and brought the Great Curse upon your collective heads - the setting has a deliberate blur on Celestial identity).

                                What we shouldn't jump to is collective punishment. Yes, the Dragon-Blooded may be responsible for a rapacious empire, and strongman dictatorships in the Threshold. Yes, perhaps that empire and those strongmen should be reformed or resisted, perhaps even violently. But you should fight the war with an eye on the peace. The surviving Dragon-Blooded will need a place in the new world order, and for that they must be forgiven for the crimes of their families, or even their own crimes. Be magnanimous in victory. The Unconquered Sun would expect nothing less of you.

                                In this view, the Wyld Hunt is unjust cowardice. Unable to evaluate Anathema on their individual merits (even Fae. Even ghosts. Even demons.) the Sidereals have maneuvered the Dragon-Blooded into a kill on sight regime. While I respect the writers including it as a way to drive conflict in the setting, I don't respect it as an upright and moral way to engage your fellow sentient being. It is a policy for viruses or bacteria, not those capable of reason.
