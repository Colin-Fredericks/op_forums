        Originally posted by Mewtarthio View Post
        I think it's better if the master of disguise avoids the guy because it's risky, not because it's a guaranteed failure. That gives you more tension: Do you attempt to move about indirectly, or do you just cross your fingers, roll the dice, and see if the disguise holds up? Plus, the "My powers are useless here!" scenario means that it's entirely possible your powers will end up being useless. If your disguise powers fail against, say, royalty, and you want to fool the Queen of England, then your "master of disguise" won't be able to apply his skills at all. If you're disguised as some foreign dignitary, and suddenly the Queen shows up and wants to meet you, you're going to be in trouble. If the Queen is simply really good at seeing through disguises, then you can say "I may be a master of disguise, but can I really fool the Queen?", weigh the risks, and choose whether you want to abandon the scheme or take the chance. That's far different from saying "Whelp, I can't fool the Queen; might as well give up right now."
        That's not true. Your disguise powers are still useful to have even if you can't use them to fool the queen -- for example, you can use them to sneak past her guards and get close to her.

        If your response to a limitation your power is "well I can't use this power as a club to brute-force past absolutely everything by using it directly on everyone, so it's sometimes useless and I suck!", the issue is just in your head. Your disguses are absolutely still useful -- even against royalty -- even if every royal on the planet can see through them instantly with no roll. It is a weakness you have to plan around, which makes for interesting storytelling. Sure, it does mean that your powers will not be 100% applicable in every situation... but no power is. Your disguise-that-fails-vs-royalty charm won't help you directly sneak past a royal's line of vision, but it won't help you make a salad, either; neither of those are part of its function. It is still broadly useful, even against royalty, if you plan ahead and take its limitations into account.

        That's my point. Some powers are designed in a way that makes it both thematically and mechanically useful to say that it doesn't work on everyone.

        The Jedi Mind Trick is a poor example to use because it's a binary pass/fail that instantly ends the encounter if successful. If Luke could simply wave his hand and say "You don't want to be on the Dark Side anymore; you want to let go of your hate," there wouldn't be any story. Hopefully, 3e won't have as many of those "save-or-die" type effects.
        Disguises are often also a binary pass-fail that instantly ends (or bypasses) an encounter if successful. That's why (sometimes, when you have a situation where you don't feel the disguise should be applicable) you can design it to say "your disguise flatly does not function against this guy"; it can be preferable to force players to use their powers in a specific way (working around their limitations and therefore playing out interesting plans) rather than to have it just boil down to a roll of the dice.

        But more generally, consider the problem with "save-or-die"; they turn things into just one die roll. A die roll, on its own divorced of any other context, is often a boring way to handle things. That's why, if you're going to have Jedi mind trick in the setting, it's often better to say "it just doesn't work on these people" rather than allowing a roll -- you don't want people trying to target it at Vader at all, because nothing good is going to come of that. Sometimes it is better to say, upfront, "this charm will not work on rival Jedi" rather than allowing a roll.

        Not always! It has to be done carefully. But the sense I'm getting from your objections is that you don't like the idea of your charms having failure states built into them because it makes you feel weak, rather than because of any mechanical consideration. I don't feel that that's valid -- some charms just aren't meant for certain usages; if you have a power that lets you easily sneak past or command commoners because you are the Exigent of Royalty, it will fail against royals and that's that, and that's a totally cool way for it to work. It has to be considered carefully in the context of the charm's intended use, but I don't see why every charm you have has to give you a chance of successfully using it on Darth Vader in order to be useful.

        Also, as an aside, note that this ties into the themes of different sorts of characters. Solar charms tend to have few failure states because, in general, Solars are about brute force -- Hypnotic Tongue Technique does work on Vader, because as a Solar who focuses on Presence, your default mode of operation is to apply overwhelming Presence force against your opponent until they crack, whatever that means or requires. (Conversely, though, Solar charms tend to not be as specifically focused on "punching downwards" as other Exalted; Hypnotic Tongue Technique works on mortals, but is priced as a charm that works on anyone.)

        Not everyone else works the same way. Jedis are (in this respect) a bit more like Sidereal or Infernal trees -- you start out with your Jedi Mind Trick intended for reliable use against the weak-willed, then instead of developing into super-presence, the same tree might lead to Force Push or lightsaber combat. In that context it's fine for Jedi Mind Trick to lack the overwhelming force needed to give it a shot against a powerful opponent, because the structure of your charmset is more based around eg. using it to sneak past the guards and then using your lightsaber skills, acrobatics, and telekinesis to murder their boss, rather than using mind control as a universal sledgehammer for absolutely everything the way the focused Solar would.

        Also, important! You said that you felt that you'd prefer if the "master of disguise" had a chance to sneak past anyone, no matter how powerful they are. But that's not how everyone's powers work -- different charmsets have different themes. Master of disguise is the charm-thematic behind Solar Larceny, say, so again, it makes sense for their disguise charm to work against everyone. But if you're playing that Exigent of Royalty with a charm that says "mere commoners cannot obstruct me", it makes much more sense for your charm to fail against the queen.

                Why? Not every charmset is going to support every playstyle. Some charmsets (like the Solar one) are going to have few inherent failure states in their charms, so if you prefer a playstyle where your charms are broadly-applicable and capable of brute-forcing almost any situation where they can be remotely construed as applicable, play a type of Exalted like that.

                But there are other playstyles which require different sorts of designs, including charms that will have unusual built-in failure states to fit unusual thematics, or charms which aren't designed to be used directly against hard targets. To me, it seems like you are the one who is being dismissive of other playstyles -- you're saying "I don't want charms like this to exist, because they make me feel weak and therefore should not exist anywhere."

                Nobody's forcing you to play a Dragon-Blooded! But within the DB paradigm (as well as several other Exalted), it's entirely valid to have a charm that is designed for utility support against weaker targets and not intended to be used against "hard" opposition. The fact that this has sometimes been done badly doesn't mean that it's always invalid.

                If you don't like that kind of charm... don't take it. Play a kind of Exalted with fewer or more "natural"-feeling limitations, or focus on other charms instead.

                                                Originally posted by Arteliex View Post
                                                The effects of Dragon-Blooded vs Celestial Magic... or Dragon-Blooded vs higher-Essence magic... I thought was pretty lame. I don't find it interesting inside the setting, and as a player I don't find the extra challenge fun when presented in that manner.
                                                The second type i'm a little less bothered with, but either way i have replaced both types of weakness with a Iron Skin Concentration-esque "Roll (his Skill + Essence) against a difficulty equal to the opponent’s Essence. On a success, the Charm works normally but becomes Obvious (if not so already)" or something along those lines. Makes things a bit more convoluted, but can be fun.


                                                Edit: i use a little personal system, based in the logic of wyld resistance (page 284 of 2e core) to balance between tweaked/made up charms while maintaning the power level gap. Essence is the core of all charms divine, demonic or elemental, but the exalts' essence reflect the might of something greater than themselves. Dragon-Blooded add one die to their pool, Sidereal & Lunars add two dice, and Solars and their twisted variants add three.*

                                                So it will mean, for example, that an Ess 2 solar charm (like many of their perfects) will be somewhat equivalent to an Ess 3 sidereal or lunar charm (ditto), Ess 4 terrestrial charms (where one may find some perfect-esque effects) and a Ess 5 spirit charm (where one finds their generic perfect too). This sort of frame of reference has been most useful when it comes to balancing charms inspired by systems from other exalts, balancing NPCs or better customizing a spirit and its charms (or balancing the MA charms from Scroll of the Monk).

                                                To this i have added a little caveat: Essence 8+ deities add one to their Ess when defining charm power (like terrestrials), Essence 10 deities add 2 (like celestials). These bonus reflect on any charms that emanate of their power, such as those of any descendants or hypothetical chosen.

                                                And yes, i admit such "hypothetical chosen" and their making were the actual motive behind the caveat - and whole little system - all along.

                                                The whole idea of such a system came from Infernals & the Glories series and an understanding of their charmsets/panoplies as the ultimate expression/customization/extrapolation of spirit charms.

                                                In case you are asking themselves how solars +3 fits into this, it reflects the UCS keyword/purview/specialty of perfection/surpassing others bumping up an Essence 10 deity's +2. We use that kind of things to fudge the system balance dynamic here and there and to explain/reflect some spirits having charms 1-2 levels higher than their Essence.


                                                *Me and my group are so far undecided about where in the scale we should put alchemicals (what will certainly change fast the moment we actually play them).
