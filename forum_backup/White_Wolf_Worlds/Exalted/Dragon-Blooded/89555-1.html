    Originally posted by BvBPL View Post

    The focus on just one combat ability is an aggravating factor to the problems I'm discussing. When you focus on one combat ability to the exclusion of others you naturally wind further down the charm tree. That means more and more complicated charms and combos which in turn mean more table time for resolution of the fighter. Indeed, this is more of a problem for Solars because their charms are so much more powerful, both natively and how they grow in power relative to DB charms.
    Focusing on multiple combat abilities is a bit of a trap. Athletics charms give Melee and MA characters the ability to close with enemies, and they also give Thrown and Archery characters the ability to stay far away, so there's no need to do something like Archery + Melee. Doubling up means your character is either mediocre at two combat abilities or is only good at combat, since you've thrown dots and charms and XP at two combat trees instead of just one. Athletics, on the other hand, lets you participate more outside of combat.

    Combos are cool on paper, since you get to come up with clever names, special effects, and so on, but they can also feel like an XP sink that gets in the way of healthy character growth. A character with, say, 10 charms for an ability probably has no use for at least a few of them, making those charms a waste of XP. Then she has to buy a combo to really use them, which could mean another 2-3 charms worth of XP. Even better, she has to decide when to buy the combo. Buying a combo early means having fun with her charms sooner and having a better chance to survive, but it also means paying for a new combo - possibly more than once - after learning more charms. After all that cost, you can bet she'll use her best combo (with a 2-die stunt for WP) every turn.

    The combo system also discourages lower-end investors in combat charms. If a thinker or crafter or social character decides to learn 3 or 4 combat charms eventually, he has to pay more XP to use them decently. After all, he may not have Dex 5, Melee 5 and a Grand Daiklave, so he needs every edge he can get. Also, he's possibly inclined to spread those charm purchases out (after all, combat isn't his focus), making it more likely that he'll need to buy a second combo later to meaningfully use the charms he's just picked up. If you're hoping just to contribute meaningfully in combat, investing so much XP is not a lot of fun.

    If you haven't already, I strongly encourage you to check out the 2.5 errata. The errata don't solve every problem with 2E (combat still takes forever) but they do fix some under- and over-powered charms on top of eliminating combos. At the very least, this means players get to use their XP to grow their characters instead of to buy weak prerequisites or to buy charms they can't use for a few more months until they can afford a new combo. Exalted 2E kind of reminds me of D&D 3.5: if you don't have the time and inclination to learn the whole system and plan your character really well, it's easy to get frustrated and feel like you're overshadowed and progressing more slowly than other people. Both games can be fun for a while, but it gets really tiresome to deal with the large disparities in character power and spotlight time.

        Originally posted by Gayo View Post
        One of the things that makes DBs good is that their charm trees tend to have some combat applicability stuck in there regardless of what they are. This was an intentional choice to emphasize their martial bent, but it's a good principle in general for a game where combat and combat ability are taken for granted. I am curious to see if 3e dips its toe in those waters in the Solar charmset. D&D is actually also a good example of this methodology, particularly 4e.
        I'd say DB's elemental thematics (paired with their origin as troops of the Exalted army) give much more creative leeway here than Solar's perfection.

        Still, it's worth noting that even in 2e, every Solar Caste has something in it's Ability spread that can be utilized for combat:
        Zenith: Resistance, Integrity, Holy attack Charm in Presence
        Twilight: Spirit-Cutting, extra temporary HLs in Medicine, Sorcery
        Night: Dodge (duh), Athletics (mobility and even a damage enhancer), Stealth (properly utilized it's completely devastating in 2e/2.5 combat)
        Eclipse: Ride and Sail both have their combat uses, though admittedly, they're clearly the least militant caste.

        There isn't much support for using those Abilities as combat enhancers in their Charms, but the premise is there and can be expanded upon.

                        As to the OP:
                        I actually find Solars work better as a party together than Dragonblood, most of the time. Not for reasons of power or skillset, but for fluff reasons. The Solars have to work together because of the Wyld Hunt, plot, etc, and also because they're so rare they're the only ones who have things in common with each other, and often end up making friends with each other. There's generally no particular reason why they should not like each other, unless they come from diametrically opposed cultures.
                        Whereas in most Dragonblood games (though not all) the PCs are Dynasts from different houses. And those houses often spy on each other, compete, clash, and if you're running the Civil War (which I imagine is in a fair few DB games) they could end up on opposite sides, which makes the PCs clash. I've never had a Solar or Lunar killed or driven from the party by another party member, I have had DBs killed, or driven out of the party, by other party members.
                        And more generally, most of the Celestial parties I've seen, while not everyone always has got on, have been a lot better at working together than the DB parties.

                                                            This is why in my Dragonblooded games I've tried to encourage a view of the Great Houses as overwhelmingly interconnected, every Dynast PC having close relatives in other houses, tried to push push political factions into cross-house conspiracies, and tried to suggest that due to the often vast differences in age between members of a family with Dragon-Blooded members, the most revered kinship in Realm society isn't that between parents and offspring, between siblings or even between spouses, it's between members of a Sworn Brotherhood. You can't choose your ancestors, and your mate may be a political necessity out of your control, but your Brotherhood is your truest family.

                                                            This is harder when a faction leader is also a house elder like Mnemon, and pretty much demands that Dynastic Charms be flushed. But it has let me encourage PCs to treat Brotherhood goals as higher priorities than House goals, and to let inter-party conflicts reach a sharper more familial pitch without spilling over to PC deathmatches. In my Creation, the most tragic figure in Realm literature is the last faithful soldier who continues to cherish the ideals of a Sworn Brotherhood that split up over some conflict too monumental to endure (and the most common cause of such conflicts is the reason there's a subtle taboo against Brotherhood members becoming lovers). On the other hand, the last living soldier in a Brotherhood that died fighting is treated as a paragon of Dragon-Blooded virtue, with a duty to educate the rising terrestrial generation. Joining or forming a new Brotherhood is certainly an option for a sole survivor, but it's one that comes with a lot of complicated baggage. While there's a lot of social pressure on young Dragon-Blooded to join into Brotherhoods, a lone survivor is generally permitted to endure in stoic solitude if they prefer.
