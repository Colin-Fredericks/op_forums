            Originally posted by BrilliantRain View Post
            Though, I didn't like the intro fiction for the Core Book, but you piqued my interest and I'd enjoy reading your analysis of it, in the hopes that, regardless how I end up thinking about it, I understand it better.

            Ask, and ye shall receive. In excruciating detail.



            So, the opening fiction – there are three important functions it has to serve. It has to introduce people new to Exalted to some of the concepts within the game, it has to showcase some of the changes between editions, and it should be an enjoyable enough read to not make the reader reflexively want to skip it.


            Now, one of the things I find striking about it is the sheer efficiency of the text. Let’s take a look:

            “There in that place the sea was still; its waters were stopped up, its currents were broken. The sluice-streets down in the bottom-most layers did not run wet and then dry three times a day, like in the better districts, but simply sat there pooling, seeping, gathering bugs and gunk and plague. They who lived there were scarcely recorded by the men and women who kept the books in Heaven. “Some number of little people reside below,” they’d say, or write, “—and bugs,” and nod their heads.”

            Immediately in the second paragraph, we are introduced to the concept of Heaven, and get a strong impression of how it is different from, say, the Upper Planes of D&D – its bureaucracy, its fallibility, its arrogance. (Note that the text says „the men and women who kept the books in Heaven” – not angels, not gods, just men and women, prone to writing off those living in the slums as insignificant.)

            It also sets up the origin of our protagonist, which is further elaborated on in the next paragraph:

            „When Suzu, the younger of the two, was four, she went out into the sluice-streets to play; only, instead of dying to the hungry dead, or falling into the hands of some flesh-taker, scavenger, or priest, she found a white pig (that her father said was likely sacred), with an earring that was a bell. She’d led it home, she’d loved to keep it, she’d ridden on it and confided in it and drawn great swirling patterns of black ink upon its flesh. She’d tugged on Sabriye’s sleeve—that was the older girl—and told her all about it, and it was a precious pig to little Suzu, and come the winter when they cut it open they found oracles in silvered letters on its bones.”

            Again, note the economy of words – we’re only three paragraphs in, but now we have a very concrete image of the kind of place our protagonist grew up in (the slums of Wu-Jian – stinking, unsanitary, ravaged by plague, slavers and the hungry dead), introduced to the person she came back to see, given a taste of the strangeness that suffuses Creation (you can sometimes run into sacred pigs that are born with silver inscriptions on their bones, and apparently nobody finds this incredibly noteworthy), and shown by implication the mindset of the people who live in the slums. The text reveals just as much by what it omits than it does by what it says out loud: we’re informed in great detail about how much Suzu loved that damn pig, but it is never mentioned that she was particularly saddened when they ate it. Need and practicality rule over emotional attachment, even for a four-year old. This speaks volumes about the circumstances these people live in, and the writer accomplishes this purely by choosing what not to focus on.


            The next paragraph introduces the reader to the concept of Solar Exaltation – what it is („Sabriye had joined the ranks of legends; had done some great unrecorded deed and won Exaltation: drawn down into her body a portion of the essence of the divine Unconquered Sun”), the way previous incarnations work [I](„t was a legacy and a power that had been Liam Island-Tamer’s before her, and Red Dove’s before him, on back to the beginning of the world”), and the tremendous in-setting impact of it („[t]here was all manner of consternation among the bookkeepers in Heaven at that change; it provoked great flurries of paperwork, anguished tugging of the beards, and Heavenly commotion”). This is the part aimed at newcomers, for whom this might be the first thing they read about Solars.


            The next part sets up the central conflict of the story, and boy, do I love it:

            „She understood only that she had changed. That her steps had lightened, her eyes gone clearer, and a sun-mark glittered on her brow. If she were to try to explain it— ...there was no explaining it. Words would fail her. Was it some sun-borne curse? The blessing of some small god of river, grass, or tree? Was she, as the Immaculate Faith would surely tell her, shamed and shameful beyond all measuring: indwelt, possessed, inhabited by some Anathematic demon-god?
            If she knew the truth of it, deep in her soul, then to her mind it yet remained a mystery: a secret that lived beneath her tongue and in her throat. That stuck there, that weighed her down, that pooled in her like stagnant water.
            The words would not come out.
            And so, like anyone who has words they cannot speak, she sought out a kindred spirit to not say them to. She returned to Wu-Jian, hunting for the nameless house on the nameless street that her cousin had used to live in—rehearsing as she searched for Suzu all the words she would not say.”

            On a technical level, the opening fiction already manages to distinguish itself from the industry average purely on the basis that it actually has a central conflict, and it is resolved by the end. (This is kind of sad.) But the thing that I really like about it is the nature of this central conflict– it’s not a foe that threatens the protagonist, or some deadly situation she has to extricate herself from: it’s simply the issue of communicating the experience of the change that befell her. This accomplishes two things at the same time: in the mind of the reader, it anchors the Exaltation as a momentuous, transcendent miracle that defies words (an important message for newcomers, but an even more important one for veterans – much of the discussion around Exaltation still basically treats it as an autonomous missile in some corners of the fan community), but just as important is the fact that it firmly roots the Chosen as having fundamentally human concerns. Not being able to put a feeling or idea into exact words is relatable.


            Then all of a sudden, Sabriye’s journey of introspection is abandoned with a silky smooth transition, as we go from the difficulties of putting words on the indescribable to a Dragon-Blooded mocking the cuisine of Wu-Jian:

            „In this, she was not alone.
            Ten floors above her, at that time, and three wards left, Jin chewed. Jin swallowed. “I don’t even know how to describe this,” Jin said. He put down his meat bun. He made an ancient finger sign against evil—most specifically, against Anathematic demon-gods—in its general direction. His partner Toad Rat snorted a laugh. “I want to describe it,” said Jin, “but—there are no words.””

            I think it’s a fantastic gag.


            …I’m not going to go over the entire text in this level of detail, or we’ll never get back to What Fire Has Wrought. So, just the highlights…


            „Let us speak of deeps and gutters; of starlight hidden in the darkness; of city piled on city: Of Wu-Jian.” – the book says, and in a rare moment of synergy between text and layout, we are greeted by the absolutely gorgeous two-page spread of the city upon turning the page. This is technically not a strength of the opening fiction, but I still found it worth mentioning.


            The story of the outcast islands is conveyed with appropriately mythic imagery, and I also like the in-setting lack of certainty that is conveyed here. The purpose of this section is, again, to introduce newcomers to the ancient deeds of the Solar Exalted, and I think it does a fine enough job of it.

            Another thing I find worth noting here is the effortless way the writer uses language to convey scale.

            „…in that moment of its defenselessness he replaced his pipe, seized a shaft of sunlight from the sky, made it gold, and plunged it like a pillar, like a needle, like a spear, through the body of the island, through the stone and through the sea beneath the island, and made it anchored to the ocean floor.”

            Drawing out the resolution of the sentence like that creates the impression of the scale of an island-spanning nail in a much more tangible way than just writing „yo, these things were really big” in however flowery a manner would. Then the same tool is employed again to convey the sprawling nature of the city itself:

            „Atop that dusting of pinned-down land amidst the endless waters they built a crisscrossing maze of bridges, an iridescent web and stronghold, rising, falling, slanting, sloping, reminiscent in its many angles of a broken-legged spider crouching, injured, on the sea.”

            I’m not saying this is high art, mind – just that the tools the writer uses are employed much more confidently and with a much greater level of conscious intent to reach a specific goal than they are in the chapter fiction of WFHW.


            Next, we learn something about the Dragon-Blooded – again, this is for the benefit of newcomers.

            The transition from the expository flashback into the immediate present is pretty good here.

            This entire fight sequence is fantastic and much more dynamic than the one we’ll talk about shortly. The way it shifts into a discussion is also much better executed, not to mention the contents of the discussion – the characters start to argue about meat buns. This sort of levity serves as an important shout-out to the realities of the gaming table, which I find valuable in gaming fiction.


            I will never stop adoring the sentence „Toad Rat (…) never wanted anything more from life than to have his name well-regarded in society, and, never to have to hunt down and engage in battle with an invincible demon-goddess of war”. The humanization of these throwaway characters is more effective here than the attempt is in the Empress fiction – the writer only allows the Empress to show weakness in a very narrow and societally approved way even in the privacy of her own home (a point could be made about that in the text, actually, but isn’t), and because of that, the moment feels contrived, rehearsed, theatrical – a shorthand meant to evoke the idea of humanity instead of the real thing in the messy and undignified way it truly exists. By contrast, Toad Rat is allowed to be slightly cowardly and motivated by the approval of his peers, and Eastern Star is allowed to be a person who constructs elaborate justifications for antagonistic feelings that stem from jealousy and pride, and they are stronger, more memorable characters for that.


            A powerful point is made a bit later, with the way the text calls back to the expository flashback earlier. I’ll quote it:

            „Sabriye had loved this tale best.
            “We’d dug down,” Sabriye’s aunt had said. “They came in and they were wreathed in animas of wood and fire, earth and water, wind—they had armor forged in the ancient days and weapons that could shatter buildings in one go—but we dug down, we hid ourselves; we gnawed and nested at the roots of their great grand house like rats. Hated and despised we were, but we were the unconquerable gold; raised up our bosses, us, to extort from them, our thief-kings to steal their goods from them and made our priests and murderers our saints. And in the end they gave in to us. They didn’t have the steel in them to burn us out, to uproot Wu-Jian, and lose their great grand port in the western sea.”
            She’d grinned with the missing teeth of her, and glee.
            Years later, Sabriye would remember that grin; hesitating, uncertain, on a roof not far from Suzu’s one-time door, she would remember it, and match it with a smile of her own:
            “The demon-gods could tame the sea,” her aunt had said, “and the Dragon-Blooded cast them down—”

            Jin and his heavy spear crashed down; shattered her reverie, burst the roof that Sabriye crouched on, tore through it as she rolled aside.

            […fight scene goes on for two pages…]

            To speak of the positioning of her legs, her hips, her hands, would be inadequate to describe it; in that moment, her Essence surged, a radiance—an anima—of sunlight flared up around her, and the whole of her Solar prowess became more than its individual parts.
            She moved, and it was as if he were seized up by a giant hand. His stance shattered and his breath blew out; his armor seemed to slam tight around his chest; an absurd admiration welled up inside him as inexorably, impossibly, he was lifted up into the air, bent over double, and then slammed down straight.
            The demon-gods could tame the sea, Sabriye’s aunt had said, so many years ago; and the Dragon-Blooded cast them down—
            But they could none of them not stand one whit against the people of Wu-Jian.
            And Jin passed out.”

            The one who vanquishes Jin is Sabriye the gutter rat, the nobody from the slums of Wu-Jian, and not Sabriye the Solar Exalt.

            (Some would no doubt say that this is retarded, because in an alternate universe where Sabriye the gutter rat is not also Sabriye the Solar Exalt, the same confrontation would end up with her skewered on the point of a spear. To this, I can only respond with „you’re missing the point”.)

            (…To which a reasonably smart person could, of course, respond with „no, I got the point just fine, here is how it could have been conveyed in a manner that isn’t self-defeating…”, followed by a proposal of a version of the scene where Sabriye defeats Jin by drawing on her superior knowledge of the surroundings, demonstrating that it is indeed her status as a native of Wu-Jian that earns her the victory – and they would not necessarily be wrong. Just arguing against a different point than the one being made.)


            Then Eastern Star is introduced (but I already said what I wanted to say about her), followed by another conversation-turned-fight-scene. This one is fairly tense and desperate.

            Eventually, a massive pig god appears, calls Sabriye one of the Solar Exalted, and the core conflict of the story is resolved. Honestly, this part could’ve been written a bit more artfully. Still, it ends with another fantastic sentence:

            „And it seemed to her, in that golden moment, like she might understand the secret that lived beneath her tongue; what it might be that she could tell her cousin Suzu—if she still lived there, if they ever met—before the great wall burst, and she saw the ocean beyond it, and the dawn; and water came rushing in to the sluice-streets, swirling, pouring, flowing, sweeping, pounding, raising her and all that muck towards Heaven like some great paean to the sun.”

            It’s some pretty great imagery.

                                        Time to resume our WIR with some more complaining about chapter fiction, wherein I reveal that I’ve been cheating and actually already read the book once before starting to write down my impressions. Fair warning: this is going to be vicious.


                                        I genuinely hated this vignette. The nicest thing I can say about it is that it’s likely to annoy the kind of people I don’t like. When the biggest positive of the work is that it might make somebody’s day more unpleasant, you know that it’s… really not fantastic.

                                        (Still better than the worst of 2E chapter comics, though.)

                                        Okay, so why does it not work?


                                        Let’s first summarize what happens, for those who don’t have their own copy.

                                        We’re at a gala. Our protagonist, the renowned merchant-adventurer Nellens Leferi, gets insulted by some rando who – unlike her – doesn’t get his own entry under the description of his House. She responds in kind, and is attacked for her trouble. She proceeds to effortlessly whoop the ass of her opponent, then gloats about how this will earn her house new contracts for some reason.

                                        *Sigh.*


                                        Probably the most egregious problem is the weakness of the dialogue. The rest of the issues would be forgivable, if not for the fact that the writer apparently wants us to believe that lines such as „You’ve correctly identified my profession. Yet it seems your tutors were remiss in properly explaining how it works. Probably several tutors. Speaking slowly.” represent the height of wit.

                                        No, seriously, I can’t emphasize enough how bad the dialogue is. The way it seems utterly divorced from how people actually talk and behave like. The young man’s response to the line above? Shouting „you thin-blooded Nellens cur”, and a roundhouse kick. Because clearly, „you’re dim” is the kind of hot diss that leaves no other recourse but to escalate to physical violence. At a party. In a society where patience and discipline are considered to be so important virtues that the stereotype of masculine intemperance seriously undermines the chances of men to be deemed suitable for higher leadership roles.

                                        Another example:
                                        „You dishonor yourself and your house with your clowning. I won’t brook such insult to the name of Sesus’ father” – because apparently, launching into a tangent about the qualities a merchant must have is an insult to one’s ancestors. Bhu… what? In what possible world does that make sense? (Let’s, for a moment, ignore that a Sesus probably would not refer to Nellens as „Sesus’ father”. It’s an ugly, clunky way to deliver exposition and context, but it pales in the face of the fact that whatever is coming out of Daral’s mouth is just pure nonsense.)

                                        Yet another exchange:
                                        L: “The second thing a merchant learns is risk. You? Pretty, perhaps, but rash. Definitely low risk.”
                                        D: “For the honor of my house!”

                                        Daral’s lines are embarrassing to even read. In and of itself, this wouldn’t necessarily be a problem – he might just be the kind of person who is really bad at trash talk. That’s a valid characterization! But the scene is bad and broken on multiple levels simultaneously in ways that mutually amplify the effect of each other.


                                        One of the most important ways the writer fails at their intent? The framing of Sesus Daral. Giving him dialogue that befits a rash loudmouth who can’t handle the bantz and prefers to let his fists do the talking is at odds with how he is being portrayed when his mouth isn’t open.

                                        „Sesus Daral fixed her with a cruel smirk, all muscle and chiseled features and swooning entourage.”

                                        „The young talonlord punctuated his remark with a dazzling and textbook-perfect spinning roundhouse.”

                                        „Laughter came from the terrace above, though Daral silenced most of it with a glare.”

                                        He’s described as a person of some note (a military officer), surrounded by a „swooning” entourage, and apparently he has enough status among the guests that he can get most of them to stop laughing with a glare. The person that is being portrayed here is not somebody whose best idea of a comeback should be „imma kick them in the face”.


                                        Another problem is just how effortlessly he ends up being trounced. He heaves and strains and flares his anima, yet our protagonist vanquishes him without the slightest bit of exertion. The scene is devoid of any threat, and therefore tension; moreover, a hero is defined by the quality of her opposition, and successfully fighting a schoolyard bully who is a drooling dimwit and can’t pose a meaningful physical challenge either is not actually a good look. I don’t come away from reading the text with the impression that Leferi is awesome, I come away with the impression that Daral is really, really pathetic, and am left wondering why defeating such an unworthy foe would raise her status in the first place.


                                        So, the scene kind of fails at setting up expectations and creating tension. You know what else it fails at? Maintaining basic consistency with the rest of the information contained within the book.

                                        What do we learn about the average member of House Sesus? „By the time a child learns to walk, she’s started training in the games of Dynastic intrigue. Before entering primary school, she’s studied the fundamentals of subterfuge and tradecraft.”

                                        What do we learn about Nellens Leferi? „Nellens Leferi has carried out business transactions with petty Threshold despots, barbarian warlords, and the subterranean artificers of the Mountain Folk. When she’s not adventuring throughout the Threshold and beyond in search of profitable ventures and trading partners, she acts as a proxy for the Conclave’s commercial interests, putting both her business acumen and her formidable martial arts prowess to use bargaining with those too proud to palaver with mortals.”

                                        It is clear that Daral is out there to start some shit. We also know that wearing house colors is not fashionable („Every Great House also has a set of emblematic colors. Sometimes in vogue, their use is currently out of fashion. Today, wearing one’s house colors — especially on a regular basis — is a sign of a traditionalist, though it’s also a useful way to curry favor with elder Dynasts who might be a bit set in their ways.”). Yet he knows that the person he’s insulting is a Nellens, and a merchant at that – so it stands to reason to assume that he did some legwork before coming to the party, maybe looked at a guest list and asked around about the names on it. One would think maybe he could have learned that Leferi is known for her martial arts prowess, and looked for an easier target to bully. So, I guess we can add „spying” to the growing list of things Sesus Daral fails at.


                                        At the end of the day, the fundamental problem is that Daral is denied basic humanity. He is transparently not a person, but an obstacle to be thrown in the way of our hero and handily disposed of in a flash. We never learn why he thinks it’s a good idea to start shit at a party. We never learn why his ego is so fragile that he immediately jumps to fisticuffs instead of trying to respond with an insult of his own (because let’s face it, against the razor wit displayed by Leferi, even a „no u” would suffice). We never learn how he could fail so badly at the basic shtick of the House he is so proudly a part of.

                                        This would be forgivable if the scene was entertainingly written. It isn’t.



                                        …I think this is enough words about what I didn’t like. Next time, we’ll be back with the cool stuff! Maybe even look at Cathak. We’ll see.

                                                            One that I think underlines the point that game mechanics don’t necessarily correspond to in-setting reality is the following: „The house’s hearthstones and artifacts are likewise the matriarch’s to apportion. Once a loyal scion awakens an heirloom’s Evocations, the matriarch typically allows him to retain it for as long as it’s of use to him.” This is interesting to me, because a lot – if not the overwhelming majority – of artifacts in Arms of the Chosen immediately unlock their first Evocation for any non-dissonant wielder upon attunement.
