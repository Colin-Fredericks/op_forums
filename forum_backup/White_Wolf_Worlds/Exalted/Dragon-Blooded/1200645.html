    Oh look, a bandwagon, watch me jump on it, or: where I read Dragon-Blooded:WFHW

    03-28-2018, 08:16 PM
    This seems to be in vogue.

    Frankly, I'm not sure that the forum needs three different topics of various people working their way through the book, but hey, I may as well open a topic on the forums instead of spamming the Skype group of my Exalted players with my impressions as I go along.
    ***


    I'm not a big fan of chapter fiction in general (albeit I still find them preferable to 2E's comics which often made me want to disappear from the face of the earth with secondhand embarrassment). However, I found the one at the very beginning of the core really entertaining and worthwhile, so I'm going to make an exception to my usual policy and not skip them outright.


    Three survivors and a plethora of dead bodies, huh? Some people were rolled into a battle group, I see.

    Interesting to see how readily the remnants of the Realm's force accept the two newcomers. Albeit I suppose they're not in a position to be picky - and it's obvious that Mathar is not here as an official representative of Lookshy. Still, I can't help but think about how common this sort of thing is, random strangers showing up uninvited to Wyld Hunts, offering their assistance.

    Oh, it's because he was already tracking them. Nevermind, then.

    The rest of the chapter fiction is, for lack of a better term, uneventful, I guess? Our heroes swear the oath of kinship, then proceed to fight the two exhausted Anathema and win. Interesting that apparently Kinships can be made and and dismissed trivially.

    Dammit, now I can't help but wonder how they work. Can you be in multiple Kinships at once? I seem to recall fairly stringent requirements from an earlier Ask the Devs post, so probably not. Is it common for Dragon-Blooded who have fought side by side for decades to maintain this bond even after they part ways? What mores surround the practice? If a member has to go on an extended assignment somewhere far away, does the Kinship get temporarily disbanded and reassembled when she gets back (because doing so frees her to get the benefits of a Kinship with her local peers, whose status is probably more relevant to her at the moment)? Or is it seen as a more significant commitment? Do the Dragon-Blooded often cling to such bonds out of sentimentality, rejecting to disband or expand a Kinship after key members have fallen? When this happens, how does the wider Dynastic (or Lookshyan, or...) society view it? Inquiring minds wish to know.


    Introduction! We open with the Romance of the Three Kingdoms quote that has explicitly served as the inspiration behind the idea of a Sworn Kinship, fittingly enough.


    Based on the chapter summaries, it seems that next week we'll get Life in the Scarlet Dynasty and Beyond the Realm. I await these with great interest.


    Suggested resources! Let's see what we have here...


    Huh, they put the Mahabharata in here. Not the kind of book that would immediately jump to my mind when thinking about Dragon-Blooded.

    Interestingly enough, this is actually one of those books that were a formative read of my childhood, and it wasn't actually that long ago that I've re-read it. It was a strange experience, because a lot of the tales contained within are super misogynistic, which has not really... registered with me as a child, or at least not in the same way as Greek mythology did. It was easy to understand that Zeus chaining Hera to the sky by the wrists and tying anvils to her ankles was kind of a fucked up thing to do, but the fact that there are a bunch of women in these stories with no agency whatsoever, who literally only exist so that the protagonist can earn their hand in marriage, then almost immediately move on to a new adventure and get a new, functionally identical wife didn't really seem too strange or unusual to me at the age of 10.

    Oh, it also features a monkey who manages to get his testicles crushed by a log that was split halfway. That is literally the extent of what I can recall from this book: "more misogyny than I remembered from my childhood, and a monkey gets its testicles crushed".


    Codex Alera sounds really cool from the description we're being given. Yet this is literally the first time I hear about it. I wonder what's up with that. Is it a case of a good concept marred by subpar execution? Or am I just really out of the loop regarding recently published fantasy novels?


    And, of course, ATLA gets a mention, along with Legend of Korra. I can't help but feel that Legend of Korra is a bit unfairly maligned here - season 3 is actually my favorite of the entire franchise, and in general, people tend to forget how many mediocre-to-bad episodes the original series had.

    ...No, seriously, LoK is fine. It holds up even in comparison to ATLA. (Except for the second season. The second season was utter garbage. ATLA never sunk as far as LoK did at its worst, but I think it also never reached the same heights as it did at its best.)

    (...I always wanted to write a blog post about the way Legend of Korra teaches some really good, really important GMing principles, but my plans of doing so were invariably thwarted by the fact that I don't actually have a blog.)



    And I think this is a fine place to pause, for now.

                  With that out of the way, let's proceed with the next part of our WIR: wherein I analyze the contents of a single page in unreasonable detail!



                  In the next chapter fiction, we get into the head of the Empress herself.


                  …I have to admit, I have no idea what that stone is supposed to be. The hearthstone of the Imperial Manse, perhaps? My none-too-perfect encyclopedic knowledge of Exalted lore fails me.


                  I like the callback to Aspect Book: Air. This is really how callbacks should be done: unobtrusive enough that someone unfamiliar with earlier editions doesn’t lose out on anything, yet still capable of giving veterans a nice and fuzzy „I understood that reference” feeling. It also has the added benefit of firmly centering the scene in the game’s timeline in a way that simply writing down the date doesn’t really do (no, seriously, don’t tell me I’m supposed to know the canonical „present year” of Exalted off the top of my head).


                  I really like how the description of the murals being made from the five magical materials drives across the decadent opulence of the surroundings. The Empress has murals depicting centuries of her deeds, wrought from jade, moonsilver and orichalcum. Just imagining the scale of that, thinking about how many artifacts could’ve been forged from what’s basically decoration in her palace, really hammers the point home.


                  …Yeah, this is not the corebook’s opening fiction. I’m not entirely sure what is being communicated here (I sincerely hope that posting quotes is okay):

                  „Here, alone and naked before her deeds, a single sob escapes her lips. (…) Her Essence is stone and flame, and the fire is ever upon her within her depths. Yet embers can grow cold, she fears, and even mountains can fall to the inexorable sea.
                  It is a moment, though. The seas recede, salt and water vanishing into air.”

                  Why is the Empress crying? Or rather – why is she crying now? I feel like this passage is meant to humanize her, but it… doesn’t do a very good job. Is this meant to be a regular occurence? If yes, why? She has ruled the Scarlet Dynasty for centuries – you would think that she’d have gotten numbed to the day-to-day reality of that. Or is this meant to be a singular moment of weakness, the like of which only happens once in a few decades? But if it’s that, what prompted it? I can buy that she is largely inured to the pressures of… being who she is, yet every once in a while, things pile up, it becomes too much to bear, and the facade breaks for a brief, private moment – but why now? A few paragraphs ago, she was calmly thinking about making her current lover a magistrate. There is no real transition between her „focused on getting shit done” state of mind and what we see later. The fleeting moment of humanity feels unearned.

                  The imagery also feels confused. First we have this: „Her Essence is stone and flame, and the fire is ever upon her within her depths”, and I’m like, „oh, the writer has managed to work three of the five elements into a brief sentence describing her (with ’depths’ being an obvious allusion to water), that’s kinda clever”.

                  But then we continue with „Yet embers can grow cold, she fears, and even mountains can fall to the inexorable sea” – so apparently the sea is now something external (let’s go with „the slowly grinding pressure that comes with leading the greatest empire on Creation”), and in a single sentence, water suddenly turns into a threat instead of an inalienable portion of the Empress’ very being. I guess it still works as long as the reader is willing to go back and re-interpret the previous sentence – depths can allude to earth as well, but two out of five elements is considerably less clever than three.

                  So far so good, but the next sentence requires an internal re-framing again, with „The seas recede, salt and water vanishing into air”, which is just a flowery way to say that she regains her composure. It kinda works with the earlier „the sea is a metaphor for external pressure” interpretation, but at the same time, the obvious literal imagery is staring you in the face. Taken in isolation, this is not a bad thing! Art is created in the spaces between the layers of the literal and the metaphorical. The sentence is fine on its own – but at the same time, if the reader is required to re-evaluate what you’re trying to do with the concept and imagery of water three times in four sentences, maybe you’re overdoing it a bit.

                  (Again, this stands in stark contrast with the opening fiction in the core, where it is very clear what every stylistic flourish is meant to evoke or underline.)


                  I like the touch that attendants are meant to avert their eyes when she’s grasping the crown – it is described as heavy and she has to socket the stone into it, so my mind immediately jumps to „this rule was probably made because fiddling with the socketing mechanism while holding the big-ass metal thing with one hand must look somewhat undignified”. The unsaid implications of this throwaway half-sentence manage to humanize her far more effectively than the flowery paragraph we just went over in detail.


                  Aaand the rest of the fiction is pretty much what you’d expect – it turns out that she was preparing for an audience with the signature circle, who show up with a gift Anathema in tow and are rewarded accordingly. Cool.



                  Well this bit ran slightly longer than I originally planned, so we’ll take it up from here next time.

                      Next up is a brief history of the Dragon-Blooded.


                      I don’t actually have anything insightful to say about this that others haven’t already. Yes, it’s nice to see the Dragon-Blooded do things on their own – Celestials having to request their help in the First Age, with some of them saying „fuck no, I have better things to do” and starting their own kingdoms with blackjack and hookers; the Shogunate’s taming of unconquerable territories and the wide-scale destruction that resulted from their conflicts (they blew up Chiaroscuro! The bastards!).

                      I adore the Outcaste groups mentioned here. We get a clan of poisoner-assassins in the East, a rogue Imperial legion in the South, a sect of heretics in the West, and a cult of fae-touched Dragon-Blooded in the North. We’re probably going to hear more about them later.

                      There’s a census of Terrestrials in this section. I find it interesting that apparently there are nearly as many Outcastes in the Realm as there are outside of it in the entirety of Creation (4000 versus 5000). Also, all cadet houses taken together apparently can’t produce as many Dragon-Blooded as an average Dynastic House by itself (750 versus 1000). These are not the numbers I’d have expected. (Also, holy shit the average Dynastic House produces nearly as many Dragon-Blooded scions as an entire Direction outside the Realm. Just how big are these families?)


                      Huh, they explicitly went out of the way to hard cap the age of Dragon-Blooded Exaltation at 20. This kind of rules out the story possibilities of the failed scion, written off by his entire family, suddenly igniting his Essence in the midst of a midlife crisis – unless shenanigans are involved. (But then again, this is a very shenanigan-friendly edition.)

                      Interesting that there’s an entire paragraph about how much of Dynastic adolescence revolves around waiting for the magical moment of Exaltation, but no mention of the stupid and hazardous „HIS CLASSMATES HATE HIM! TRIGGER YOUR EXALTATION WITH THIS ONE WEIRD TRICK” methods that desperate scions experiment with (to no provable effect). I always liked that bit of 1E fluff.

                      So apparently the Immaculate Philosophy is not really invented wholecloth, just… pilfered from the works of First Age philosophers and metaphysicians, and subtly altered. Honestly, it doesn’t make much difference to me either way, but I’ve heard some people say that this makes it more respectable in their eyes, so… huzzah? Also, I have no idea why has this been put under the chapter about the Dragon-Blooded Exaltation itself.

                      An entire page details the subtleties of the moment of Exaltation. I found no groundbreaking insight within, but I like this sentence: „Deaths from the fallout of Terrestrial Exaltation are uncommon — most people have the sense to leave the area as soon as a child begins to glow with power — but property damage and injuries are almost guaranteed.”

                      A lot of words are spent on clarifying how the Elemental Aspect works – how it is inherited (the children of two people with the same Aspect are likely to be of the same Aspect as well), how it changes a developing person in body and mind, and what the limitations of its influence are (TLDR: it won’t override your entire personality overnight). There’s also a description of every individual Aspect, and their strengths and weaknesses. (This reminds me of an old dev quote, which basically said that the in-universe motivation behind the Immaculate Dragons’ portrayal was largely to steer impressionable Dragon-Blooded away from the negative extremes of behavior their Aspect naturally pulls them towards. Re-reading the core, some of this fits, others… not so much. On the other hand, I noticed for the first time that the symbols of the Immaculate Dragons on page 72 have little depressions in the direction that corresponds to the Pole of their respective element, which is a nice visual touch!)

                      There’s a quote in the section about Essence Fever that I really like:
                      „A young Terrestrial Exalt not only has to contend with the unfurling power within her, but also the mundane changes she’s undergoing. The Essence of the Dragons is volatile, capable of passionate love and tempestuous rage. A newly Exalted Dragon-Blooded is encouraged to spar and roughhouse to vent these passions. Her Aspect shapes not just her body, but her mind and emotions. She is a tempest, a bonfire, a tsunami, a force of nature given flesh and walking among mortals. Essence seethes and thrashes within her, driving her to be as aloof, passionate, or nurturing as her Aspect dictates.”
                      It feels like the book is explicitly calling out that this is meant to facilitate cheesy teenage drama in secondary school games. I approve.

                          So, the book has the same kind of „prestige spreads” about the Realm, Lookshy, Prasad, the Forest Witches and Outcastes that the core had about individual Exalt types, which is a pretty clever way to lay out player options.

                          The option to be a Dynast is kind of a given, and honestly, I don’t need much convincing to want to play a decadent asshole noble with a superiority complex that would rival Tony Stark’s – this is pretty much my default mode of play in every game anyway.

                          The part about Lookshy fails to convince me that they’re very interesting. They seem like generic soldier dudes without any of the things that make the Realm compelling, and not much of their own in return. „You get cool toys” is not much of a draw for me in Exalted, where pretty much every character starts out with all the cool toys they’ll ever need.

                          Prasad is, of course, fantastic. The thing that makes me feel really interested in it as a setting, however, is this part from the Realm preview: „[T]ainted souls, Wyld mutants, Anathema, and even inhuman monsters such as demons and Fair Folk may surrender to the shikari and seek the wisdom of the Pure Way. Through hard work, obedience, and purifying rituals, unclean creatures seek a cleaner death, and a place in the cycle of reincarnation.” When Lunars come out, one of my campaign pitches will certainly be „so, how would you guys feel about playing a triumvirate of Alucards in not!India ruled by heretical Dragon-Blooded?”.
                          (Is this idea straining against canon? Probably – it would be really stupid of the Wyld Hunt to keep around a bunch of Anathema for the specific purpose of dropping them on other Anathema and hoping that they won’t cut and run at the first opportunity. But sometimes, you just want to let your players be Alucard first, and worry about constructing elaborate justifications later. Which is kind of a betrayal against the principles behind Exalted, but... I find myself unable to give much of a fuck about that. A lot of the discourse that surrounds the game is focused on making sure that every tiny detail is in order, internal consistency is meticulously maintained, and tone is strictly controlled – but when all is said and done, I find the idea of getting too snobby about what amounts to a bunch of grownups sitting down at a table to play make-believe with glowing anime superheroes incredibly absurd.)
                          (Which is not to say that some amount of snobbery won’t improve your game – after all, what is snobbery to one person is maintaining basic standards to another. But sweating about the dubious canonicity of a campaign idea is generally not a very productive use of one’s time, I think.)

                          The Forest Witches are next, and at this point, I’m very tempted to use them to introduce one of my groups to Exalted. „You get to play elemental kung-fu demigods plugged into augmented reality… in a mythical Bronze Age world” is not only an immediately attention-grabbing premise, but it also comes with a strong inbuilt motivation for PCs to get out and interact with the world (the pool must be fed, after all).

                          Last but not least are the Outcastes. Much like the Dynasts, I don’t think much convincing is needed to sell people on them, but for the exact opposite reason – Dynasts are interesting because their place in the setting is concrete and well-defined, with a plethora of character concepts and story hooks a player can build upon, whereas Outcastes are interesting because they can be anyone, slot into anywhere, and have none of the baggage that being a Solar carries (the need for secrecy, the unshakable feeling that you’re playing a Mary Sue, and the trouble of having to justify why you were Chosen – which limits character concepts to an annoying degree). It’s also a nice touch that the text explicitly calls out that an Outcaste doesn’t actually need to be a rootless folk hero – deserters, exiles and disgraced Dynasts are perfectly fine concepts as well.



                          And that’s the end of the prestige spreads! Next time, we’ll pick up from here with another unnecessarily detailed sequence of complaining about chapter fiction.

                                                          It doesn't seem to be; after all, you've just cited the passage stating that they have a principle of allowing the Anathema to try and redeem themselves.

                                                          I think "hard work and obedience", in this context, means something more... spiritually edifying than punching people. A supernaturally strong Anathema might be put to till the fields, doing the work of ten men in a single day. A Craft-oriented one might be sent to build a dam for a village threatened by floods. And ones without such obvious uses would be doing the kind of activities you'd see monks being sent to do by the titular abbot in the Record of Linji.


                                                          Originally posted by BrilliantRain View Post
                                                          As regards Alucard and Hellsing and using that as a model for a Solar powered Wyld Hunt (heh), I think the main issue is that the Hellsing Organization has some unspecified control mechanisms that mean Alucard has to obey Integra that seem to be nigh infallible, while, in Creation I really doubt that most people would think that there are any sort of mind control or whatever that could constrain a Solar long term.

                                                          That said, if you WANT to play that game, just create a Sorcerous Oath Binding or an Artifact or whatever that forces them to obey their DB keepers and you should be fine. As long as the players are down with the concept (and you don't have their bosses tug on the leash too hard or too often) they shouldn't spend much of their time struggling against the bindings and you can just sort of place them in ST fiat level territory that no-one worries about, the same way spy games aren't usually about the players plotting to take over MI-6 and/or England.

                                                          I think the idea works better with Lunars, aesthetically - the more inhuman nature of their powers underlines how their handlers see them: dangerous monsters, to be employed against other monsters as shock troops or a weapon of last resort. It’s also much easier for the players to do this.

                                                          To me, the control mechanism mostly just seems like „Alucard has a Defining Tie of Loyalty towards any sufficiently ’pure’ human who manages to vanquish him”. It’s not externally imposed. Creating a sorcerous oath or an artifact to do this job, therefore, seems to me like a wrong approach to take. Honestly, I was mostly just thinking that they would be pumped full of all sorts of nasty and long-lasting poisons, and get the antidote as long as they remain obedient – small infractions provoke a temporary withdrawal, and going on the run poses… difficulties. The nice thing about this approach is that the core explicitly assumes PC exceptionalism, so nothing breaks setting-wise if we say that their handlers have spent months testing the limits of their tolerance and set the dosage based on that, then they’re in for a nasty surprise when it turns out that the party has collectively picked up some poison resistance Charms during gameplay. It not a reasonable assumption for the Wyld Hunt to make that they would develop such an ability in the sort of timeframe the game takes place in. This is important, because half the fun is letting the PCs decide whether they want to turn rogue and exact a terrible vengeance, or go on with the original premise!
