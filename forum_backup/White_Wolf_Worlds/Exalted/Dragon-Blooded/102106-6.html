                                                And yet, their by far most iconic war Charm is a torrent of pure elemental destruction. Not an expression of strategic genius, nor indomitable combat discipline. Pure, unadulterated elemental destruction harnessed for battlefield use.

                                                Frankly, I think the militant focus of their Charms is more of a cultural thing than a statement on their fundamental nature. Ever since the Ten Thousand Dragons marched against the armies of the Primordials, being soldiers and warriors defined them above everything else, and that cultural definition stuck with them through all the vicissitudes of the ages after. That's why even those DBs whose main forte lies outside the battlefield seek ways to weaponize their talents - something that comes rather easy to them, given the elemental associations of their Charms. But I'm sure there are many Wood Aspects who can give incredible, sensual, pheromone-enhanced performances as readily as they can rip someone apart with ballistic guitar riffs.

                                                In other words, the way I see it, they are elemental Exalted with solid cultural reasons to put their magical talents to militant use, not soldier Exalted who happen to hurl fireballs.

                                                        The main difference between the "elemental-ness" of 2e's charms and 1e's is that 2e had far more charms that evoked your Aspect rather than that of the charm's Ability, or that had a "module" for each element with you learning your own first. With a couple of exceptions, 1e Dragon-Blooded charms reflected the element of the Ability they were associated with. I think this was a good change, though probably worse than 3e's decision to just have charms of different elements in the same tree. But the 2e charmset only changed DB thematics in subtle and inadvertent ways, by messing up mechanical statements embedded in their design. In terms of broad aesthetics it's largely the same, since its chief problem was that it was naively copy-pasted.
