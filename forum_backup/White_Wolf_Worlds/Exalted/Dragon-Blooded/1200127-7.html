    Now, to break down what is sure to be one of the more dense subjects of the book.

    We get the Dynasty’s perspective on the bloodline that begats their Exaltation, a sacred trust that is valued more than anything, and for which they feel a deep obligation to pass it along. More pragmatic is how it forms the basis of connections between Exalted families, binding the Realm together. I think placing marriage up there with the Wyld Hunt really emphasizes and contextualizes the significance of it in this culture.

    Reiteration of the typical time between graduation and marriage, along with the priorities in negotiation; partners of a similar age if it can be managed, elevated station for one’s sons, a source of good blood for daughters. Good, keep reinforcing the matriarchal elements. That element of women getting the higher stipend is put into context now; it’s not about a sense that men can’t support themselves, it’s about how once your son is married, somebody else is paying for his stipend, and they’re doing it to compensate you for how all of his children belong to their House rather than yours. That’s a more interesting element of the matriarchy, and adds a fun additional layer of complication to their relationships and negotiations, particularly the element of wanting husbands of lesser status whose stipends will cost less. Really, that whole idea of how you’ll need to pay for everybody married into the House for the rest of their lives; among other things, there’s a shiny new motive for assassinations. To finish off, there’s the humorous image of a man pricing himself out of the marriage market, at which point they need to marry lower status women, who need to make a big one off payment to compensate.

    Suddenly I’m thinking that the reason that Sesus Eshuvar is married to a mortal is because having taken part in a successful Wyld Hunt (that followed up on a disastrous one, no less) when he was still very young greatly increased his stipend, making him more expensive than many Houses were willing to pay for. I’ve actually got a general image of there being a delicate balancing act in one’s stipend and how that relates to marriage; a prominent early achievement elevates it above one’s peers, but leaves potential spouses’ families asking “okay, but what else have you got that would justify us paying so much?”

    I like the image of how being able to choose your own spouse is either something that comes from some very canny interactions with your own House, and either the House of the one you want or the one you’re refusing (possibly both)… or just being low enough on the totem pole that your marriage wasn’t going to make much anyway, so you’re not worth the effort of bringing into line.

    There’s the note on the difficulty of getting a judge to officiate an unsanctioned marriage, including that tidbit I had hoped for about the necessities of marrying a peasant (with the implication that it’s actually a thing that happens sometimes). Aand I think the details of how marriages can end has been adjusted somewhat; as I recall, it used to be that if the union went without children after fifty years, it was automatically dissolved, in contrast to now when it is twenty years (a more readily achieved timeline), and with the agreement of both matriarchs or a judge. That implies to me that there can be infertile unions (including same sex ones) that get to continue existing indefinitely, if nobody in authority should object. Other than that and one of the partners being convicted of a major crime, there is no divorce.

    And here’s laying out the details of the newly added same-sex marriage; it faces stigma over the possibility of lacking children (that it possesses several means of getting around) and lacking House approval that any other marriage might, but they’re unconcerned with homosexuality itself. The difficulties are still sufficient that there are many gay Dynasts who’ll just accept an arranged marriage, and they’ve got a system worked out for which House gets the children. It’s not stated here, but I imagine that’s got to add another layer to possible objections; I imagine it can be a bit of a blow to have a daughter who falls in love with an older women, meaning that your grandchildren through her won’t belong to your House as you would have expected them to. Overall, I’d say this is an effective way of getting this into the setting.

    Now, getting into the details of the matrilineality: children belonging to the mother’s House and status, husbands being expected to show the same deference to their wives’ mothers as their own, uh-huh. It doesn’t specify a gender subject to the matter of peasants and outcastes who lack names of their own marrying a Dynast (or patrician), so I presume that applies to men and women. I guess it can still maintain a certain matrilineal element in the case of the latter, if a peasant woman is automatically considered a member of the House (even if the earlier sidebar stated that mortals assume the masculine role as a rule). One thing that’s different than what I would have expected is the social acceptability; I always imagined that marrying a peasant was the kind of thing that necessitated becoming a virtual pariah from Dynast society, but here’s the idea that it officially elevates their status in a manner that allows one to keep showing their face in respectable company. That really carries a whole range of unique scenarios into the game.

    Husbands who outlive their wives return to their own families (unless they should get permission to stay with the new House, which typically angers one’s birth House), and I’ve already seen how the children remain under the control of their mother’s family and get adopted in elsewhere. They occasionally game the system by marrying a useful Dragon Blood to a mortal so that they can come back within a few decades (which probably synergizes nicely with that element of higher ranking ones being above what Exalted wives will pay), or even just within the House (which is surely big enough to have many distant cousins) or people that are closely associated with the House.

    I’m curious about precisely what has been changed with pregnancy… not much, other than it’s no longer a year. It still has the elements of how long it takes to show prominently, and how little it debilitates even then. The biggest element to the childbirth itself is stating that it’s possible (even if still rare) for stillbirths and miscarriages to happen (with some prior characters having already demonstrated a capacity for defects). The main improvements on the process seem to favour the Exalt, while the still mortal foetus retains a few risks.

    I believe that this section on the transferred loyalty of husbands answers my question concerning whether husbands are ever trusted with a House’s secrets; if they’re raised with the idea that their loyalty goes to their wives House, then once they demonstrate necessary competence, I’d say they can probably be drawn in. It likely represents a complication if the wife should die, though; I can picture another scenario for assassinations coming from the occasion in which a freed husband might compromise a House’s secrets, and they can’t effectively get another marriage to bind him back in. I can also see that as a particular scenario in which a freed husband requests to stay with his wife’s family; it’s probably better to have your birth House angry with you then to have the likes of Ragara kill you to ensure your silence.

    In any case, it’s being complicated as hostility between the Houses rises. It’s interesting to me that the typical scenario still consists of a man trying to show loyalty to his wife’s House, or evade the matter altogether, rather than there being a huge prospect of men siding with their birth families… beyond the matter of how many Houses are starting to stonewall them out of fear. I also enjoy the note on how even if a man should serve with his family, there’s the risk that he’d need to fight his wife. There’s a whole minefield in there.

    And there’s a sidebar concerning the subject of marriage in play. I’ll admit, when I saw that heading, I expected something commenting on the subject of how a Dynast player might relate to a spouse that isn’t an active part of the story or play group, but this is mostly about reiterating the significance of not using in-character conceits to force marriages on players who don’t want them (as opposed to players who want to play characters who don’t want the marriage). I’ve certainly seen enough concerns about the subject to think it’s a valid and necessary piece of Storyteller advice, though.

            Now, for one of the bigger changes, the function of the bloodline. It opens off the bat with the idea that the Dragons’ blood doesn’t refer to something literally genetic, but the metaphysical capacity of the Terrestrial Essence to propagate itself, rendering it beyond any magic lesser than the Exaltation itself. It takes time and energy to pass on effectively, and is never guaranteed. Okay, so how does that interact with the notion of a bloodline’s strength?

            Ah, so it’s not a matter of eugenics and ancestry, but on how long your parents waited for their progenitive Essence to replenish. There’s a perception (that may be erroneous) that the so-called leftover children themselves have a lesser capacity to have Exalted children, so matchmaking in pursuit of stronger bloodlines isn’t about things like how many mortals are in there, but whether or not one is the product of a union with maximized Essence, and how many generations back that trend held. I was already on board with progenitive Essence, but still had some reservations with the idea of and references to bloodlines, not least because my own background in genetics kept intruding on my attempt to conceptualise this whole part of their society. This considerably simplifies things, removes some of the skeezier elements entirely, but we still get the fun of those elements of tracking genealogies and intricate matchmaking (speaking of, so glad that term is in now, as an alternative to “breeding programme”). Mortal children of the Exalted are still considered to inherit the progenitive Essence, which remains consistent regardless of their child-bearing patterns, although I imagine that it’s considered to lessen across generations.

            …unless… part of the idea of matchmaking mortals is that the potential does remain consistent across generations, so they’ll be looking for mortals descended from a union of Exalted who maximized their Essence. I’m going to put a pin in this.

            The Essence is passed on with conception (adding another dimension to needing proper contraception in one’s affairs), and the mystical quality of it means that the same rules go even if the child is formed by magic. I wonder if that’s something that can apply to another person generated whole cloth through the use of sorcery, or solely in cases where some of the parent’s actual tissue is used as a basis.

            Okay, the subject of extramarital children… about what I would expect for men, a sense of greater stigma for them having numerous affairs because of the higher potential to keep expending their progenitive Essence. I must admit, though, that I can’t entirely follow the logic for women… they’re considered above reproach for extramarital pregnancy because everybody can tell that they’ve done it? I’m missing something here. On the other hand, extramarital relations with the same sex are held in higher regard because there’s no potential for pregnancy and wasted Essence at all, although still the kind of thing where being really obvious about it invites hostility, and marrying the same sex is only an issue if one hasn’t gotten children within a couple of decades via any of the proscribed methods.

            Hmm, another error… there probably wasn’t intended to be something else in place of repeating the first paragraph. Instead there’s a sidebar covering the subject of transgender… right, so they’ve made Danaa’d into a transwomen in the mythology, and that society holds one’s gender to be what they say it is. I’m guessing this is in stark contrast to being agendered, which would generally be taboo but for examples such as a Tepet making it an application of their irreproachable warrior oath. Marriage follows the same convention, but is expected to still be fruitful. It’s an interesting place to also put details of the Dynastic attitude to employing sorcery in the endeavour; with the more mystical nature of the heredity, it’s solely a matter of how squirrely the Dynasty is about sorcery in general, rather than such methods interfering with one’s chances. Okay, that makes sense. Rawar of Arjuf has also been rewritten as a trans man (I’m guessing that there was no occasion of him making statements that the throne would be a burden too heavy for the Empress, assuming that was ever about gender rather than pre-Contagion status), and Mnemon and Ragara are now the results of sorcery (while still having a father). Heh, if anything could have added to the dynamic between those two, it would be making them full blood siblings. And if I’m reading correctly, it’s saying that the example of Rawar lets them lift the sorcery taboo more for couples involving a transgender person, more than they would if it’s same-sex.

            We’ve got a section using the premise of the leftover children concept to detail how long the Essence takes to regenerate, and how that relates to the usual demographics of family size. It doesn’t say directly, but I assume that it’s working according to the principle I speculated; if one carefully husbands their Essence, then the majority of one’s children can be expected to Exalt. In most cases of several children at once, if any Exalts it will generally only be the one because basically all of the Essence goes to them, while it’s possible for more to Exalt as well.

            Whatever my confusions about the social attitudes to extramarital relations, the legitimacy is clear; women always have full claim to their children, men have no claim whatsoever on children outside marriage (so even if more of them could be expected to Exalt, there would be no advantage to it). Women can choose to acknowledge the biological fathers of their children, but that seems to only really give them a cultural avenue to a relationship with them. Men can publically acknowledge that they’re in extramarital relationships with patricians and peasants, but is looked down upon and contributes to stereotypes. Aaaand while men don’t get a unilateral legal claim on their natural children, there are conventions for bringing the pregnancy into the fold of the Dynasty (probably no sense in letting even a minor potential Exaltation go to waste, especially if the basis of bloodlines is different now), whether it’s via a sister or cousin or whomever in the man’s original House, while occasionally doing so with their wife’s House, accompanied by some rough hazing. It seems pretty extreme, but I imagine that there’s a desire to make it a hard time to ensure that the Dragon blood doesn’t stray widely out of control.

            And the final part on the life of the Scarlet Dynast is the experience of parenthood, coming full circle… wait, no, obviously there are going to be sections on old age, retirement, and death… last part before going into details about outcastes. Anyway! We’ve already seen this from the child’s perspective, but here the parental one is that the manner in which they distantly manage the child’s upbringing is itself a strong expression of love, since it prepares them for life and leaves them with strength. They have a whole literary tradition to emphasize the nobility of placing that concern over a personal relationship, and stigmatize those who opt not to do so. In any case, the most basic legal obligation is to provide for their essential needs and education.

            And a useful little sidebar clarifying how the Dynasty is very bottom heavy, with the lifestyle killing most before they can reach a hundred (presumably only having managed two to four children of their own), resulting in a vastly disproportionate number around the same age, clustered around a smaller number of venerable middle age, and a stark minority of elders. Lea commented elsewhere that it makes the Dynasty’s demographics less fudgy, but still necessarily fudged a bit, but I’m satisfied with the principles of it; it’s credible enough, and removes some of the complication that would come from an extremely elaborate cross-section of ages.

                                (I'm trying to move back away from simply summarising the book}

                                Next up is the life progression of outcastes in the Realm. The lost egg term once again originated with the Scarlet Empress, a reference to an analogy concerning individuals who need to be returned to the nest. Never been entirely sure what the Dynast reference point for dragons having eggs is, though… So they’re legally designated as distantly related to the Empress as the basis for the state asserting authority over them, which grants status above the Realm’s mortals but below the true Dynasts.

                                I suppose that patrician families can still reliably produce Exalted because they’ll often be descended to greater or lesser extent from the Dynasty, and we’ve got some specific codification for the whys and wherefores of their being incorporated into the higher society, including the benefits of the groundwork provided by the shared primary education.

                                Sooo adoption seems like the option that yields greater benefits, becoming a full member of the House, but is harder to achieve because they’ll only go through with it if one manages to excel in secondary school. Even with that, they’re still only really considered half a Dynast. I wondered if they might cut loose somebody who can’t hack it, but the book is right, that would be a waste of a valuable asset. I do wonder what the work of a client Dragon Blood entails; possibly not much different from another young member, just without the same pay and benefits, and under a system of working towards eventual adoption. Mind, if there’s an associated stereotype of having no head for books, perhaps there’s a tendency to give clients more of the muscle work.

                                Oh jeez, needing to pay back what a House provided if the patrician is no good at all, that has to be ruinous I’m picturing some patrician, all pride and gratitude that one of their own children was blessed by the Dragons, and a few decades later they look on from the end of their life as it turns out to have been a curse bringing about the downfall of their family. I wonder if there are a few outcastes wandering around with the memory of having compassed that.

                                Fostering would be the system that gets the like of Bal Kerez through their education. If I’m reading it right, the difference is… the client system entails a patrician family being paid by a Great House to secure the rights to their ultimate adoption, becoming a part of the House’s businesses, while fostering has a patrician family go into debt with a House to secure their assistance in getting their Dragon Blood through schooling, at which point they take up a position with the patricians. The benefit of that is having an Exalt on their side, and the hope of eventually cultivating that into a bloodline strong enough to be incorporated wholesale into a House. It sounds like a system that entails a lot of risks, but if played effectively, can yield enormous rewards. Cadet houses can also take that option, and seem better suited to it, gaining the benefit of a member that will be viewed on more equal footing with members of the Dynasty.

                                Outcastes from a commoner background don’t have such lofty options, and they get the option of the razor or the coin. I have a vague recollection that before, the choice was something that was more immediate, and I think there being an extended grace period to give them at least an impression of what they’re in for so that they can make an informed choice helps make it a bit less haphazard, a bit more effective at ensuring that one gets personnel that are suited for (or at least all in to) the role they take up with. I can also see making a lavish ceremony out of making the choice something that helps to instil a sense of awe and loyalty into prospective servants of the Empress.

                                Errbepebrrr, taking the Razor gets you sent to the Cloister of Wisdom, dropping out or failing is not an option (although it least it’s something as benign as needing to repeat years as necessary), and after you’re directly initiated into the Order. I have no judgement about the Order developing a tendency to send outcastes directly for their training; it’s not just that their motives are fairly altruistic, but it’s got to be a bit of a cruel joke to have to endure Pasiap’s Stair only to find oneself set aside at the end of all that training.

                                Addressing the question of what happens to legion outcastes who finish their terms of service (extended or not) is good, that’s another area where one used to have to fill in the blanks a bit. I’m guessing that even ones who don’t opt to marry into the Dynasty can find good opportunities, things like freelance work for Houses or using their own resources and connections built up in their career to build up an independent business. For those that do marry, there’s something amusing in the idea of a degree of culture shock in taking on Dynastic expectations of marriage and gender roles. On the other hand, with the draws they can exert from marriage to patricians or the like, it might be that even an outcaste who retires with little to their name can very rapidly accrue what is needed to set themselves up alone. Hmm, between them and patrician families that go in for fostering, I’ve got an image of the future of the Realm in which a coalition of patricians and outcastes try to independently set up a similar deal to House Nellens, cultivating their own stronger bloodlines.

                                The Deliberative hasn’t yet been mentioned very much, but the description of how it functions in the matter of contemporary adoption of lost eggs to the Houses has me thinking that it’s being specified as an instrument of the struggle between the Houses, rather than having much potential to function as an independent body. For adoption itself, I suppose the benefit of going that route is that you get the outcaste under your umbrella, but don’t need to tie up somebody’s marriage in order to do so (that and when you’re a small and new House, and could do with having your ranks filled out quite a bit).

                                And a fun little extra note on how outcastes sometimes get snuck into a House by being given the identity of a dead scion. I’m picturing something of the scenario in that really bad Simpsons episode; an outcaste serving in the legions alongside a Dynast comes home when the Dynast does not, and their family decides to tell everybody that it was the other way around. I’m seeing montages in which the outcaste gets a crash course on the lost child’s personality and mannerisms, and all motivated by some critical arranged marriage or the like.

                                Oh, there actually is a description of what they do if they don’t marry into the existing power structure. Not being Dynasts, their marriages could serve as the foundation of new patrician families, and I’m picturing them as having a tendency to favour other outcaste patricians for further marriages; I’m seeing a whole subculture of Dynastic marriage intrigue in miniature going on among the patricians. This is where the outcastes drummed out of the legions are widely ending up, and I think it’s got some potential to transform the landscape of the Exalted of the Realm.

                                Finally there’s the foreign outcastes. Some prior references suggest to me that these occasionally intersect with the prior category (I suppose mostly in satrapies), but for the most part it’s about the perception of Dragon Blooded who come up wholly independently of the Realm. I find this to be another effective area to elaborate on; there is, after all, a stronger emphasis on unique cultures of Dragon Blooded beyond the Realm (and Lookshy), and it makes for a richer and more plausible picture than previous Editions where it felt like there was an expectation that the Arbiters actually could be expected to pick up the majority of lost eggs. And even when the existence of foreign, independent Dragon Blooded was acknowledged, it was up in the air about how they would be received in the Blessed Isle; I recall at least one person being of the opinion that going around openly there would basically get you jumped by the Arbiters, and shanghaied immediately into the legions or the Order.

                                No, this time there’s a distinct element of classism crossed over with exoticism, with the idea that a foreign Exalt might be subject to lavish entertainments, but not really treated with as a serious actor unless they’ve engaged in some service that suits the interests of the Realm or the Dynasty. Dragon Blooded from coherent societies like Lookshy occasionally defect, but will often be regarded as untrustworthy, and options to be tied into the Realm’s upper classes are sharply limited and tightly guarded for the individual Exalt. The impression I get is that the foreign Dragon Blood faces a strong insinuation that they’re not wanted; if they come to the Blessed Isle on their own business then they’re allowed to undertake it, and even liable to be invited to an event as a curiosity, but they can’t participate in the true life of the Exalted of the Realm, and are expected to leave fairly promptly.
