                                All right then. All right then.

                                My dear, sainted sister has loaned me her laptop for the moment, on which the document uploads in the manner that it was doing until just last night. I’m going to work off of that for the time being. I’m still a bit buzzed from how it was previously broken and… associated unpleasantness, but I’m going to try and power on through.

                                So, the additional Martial Arts that the book included. First is Golden Janissary Style: the description here has been made nice ad focused, describing how it’s been passed down through generations of devil-fighting sages, trained with katas, footwork and meditation on light and shadow, and favoured by, ascetics, people who live among or adjacent to monsters or might regularly take part in the Wyld Hunt. The unarmed attacks are described as sweeping, and it favours the spear and staff as weapons. It’s compatible with light armour, and complemented by both Occult, and Athletics for the improved mobility.

                                The first Charm is Where-Is-Doom Inquisition, with which one can add their Occult to rolls for Join Battle, as well as Awareness rolls to detect hidden threats while in combat. When creatures of darkness oppose the roll, 1s on their rolls can add non-Charm dice to the fighter’s rolls. The Charm doesn’t specify which foes are dark, but the fighter will know that somebody is.
                                Ah, I had almost forgotten that Mastery effects were a thing. Those who have it gain the benefit of being able to reflexively move one range band towards a creature of darkness if they win Join Battle against them and use the first turn to attack one. They can still take a normal movement action, and gain their Occult in dice of post-soak withering damage or one success on a decisive damage roll.

                                There’s a sidebar reiterating/clarifying the definition of creature of darkness (I can’t recall if the core had one), and then Rotten Leaf Arrested to give the stylist’s withering attacks a slightly increased Overwhelming and knock them prone if damaged, with enemies that are crashed being incapable of moving on their next turn altogether. When used against creatures of darkness, the Overwhelming bonus is half the stylist’s Occult, and the withering damage roll gets an automatic success, and if crashed can neither move nor attack on the next turn.

                                Cleansing Flame Strike adds a die of damage to decisive attacks and let them ignore two points of Hardness, with creatures of darkness giving the damage roll double 10s and taking aggravated damage, and then it’s the Form Charm. When the Golden Janissary Form is assumed, the martial artist gains half their Strength as a bonus to their soak and dice to rushes, and gains one Initiative when a rush is successful. Against creatures of darkness, they also get increased Parry and their Occult added to soak as well, and an extra point to base Initiative whenever they land a decisive attack. It can be reflexively entered when a creature of darkness is decisively attacked, automatically granting the extra Initiative.

                                Devil-Slaying Spear Dance can be used when a foe is incapacitated, to reflexively rush a different enemy with double 9s, without it counting as their movement action. If they rush a creature of darkness, 1s on the opposing roll grant the warrior Initiative. Mastery allows one to instead instantly move to close range of an enemy and reflexively make a decisive attack on them, and if that foe is incapacitated, the Charm can be applied again.

                                Paralyzing Combustion Imbuement is used to make a decisive attack that cause a foe to erupt in melodious golden flames, penalizing their Stealth and increasing their wound penalties. If the penalty is risen above the target’s Stamina, then the target becomes incapable of disengaging from close range to the martial artist so long as they remain lit. The light burns creatures of darkness, inflicting dice of aggravated damage that ignore Hardness every turn. The light lasts for the scene, or until the inflicted character crashes or incapacitates the one who did it to them. Masters are able to make their enemies incapable of disengaging from any range, while Terrestrials are limited to an effect that is broken simply by landing a decisive attack.

                                Light-on-Dark Shield is used for a withering clash attack. It can’t have more damage than the character’s Overwhelming value (which they don’t receive), but against creatures of darkness a successful clash inflict one die of Hardness-ignoring aggravated damage. For Terrestrials the clash counts as their attack for the round, and thus cannot be used if they’ve already attacked.

                                Finally there’s Lone Spark Lights the Conflagration. A decisive attack can be made against an enemy suffering from Paralyzing Combustion, allowing the attack to include threshold successes to the damage roll, with the total allowed increased against creatures of darkness. When a creature of darkness is damaged, the light that the attack naturally emanates flares out to strike any other creatures of darkness within medium range, giving them one die of aggravated damage, plus one for every 10 rolled on the initial damage roll. Damaged enemies will also acquire the effect of Paralyzing Combustion Imbuement. Masters get a very strong bonus to the Charm; when they incapacitate a creature of darkness and reset to base, they can use Devil-Slaying Spear Dance to rush against all enemies struck by the Charm’s aftereffect, making decisive attacks against them one after the other (I feel as though there’s an inconsistency in how this describes resetting to base, but I’d need to review some things). Terrestrials are instead limited to only being able to use the Charm once per scene, but can reset it by crashing enemies suffering from Paralyzing Combustion.

                                I’d say that Golden Janissary came out well. It’s got some flash, some straightforward internal synergies, and I think it’s sufficiently effective even against normal foes while having some nice boosts against creatures of darkness.

                                                Okay, get a Mantis Toboggan reference out of my system from the outset: Mantis Toboggan is a persona that the character of Frank Reynolds occasionally adopts when he’s impersonating a doctor in the hit sitcom It’s Always Sunny in Philadelphia. It’s funny because of the surreal absurdity of the name and its demonstration of the character’s unhinged depravity, particularly in the contexts in which he employs it.

                                                Good, now that’s out of everybody’s way.

                                                I believe Lea was the one who said that he insisted upon another animal Style being included, because he didn’t want them forgotten amidst all the flashy abstract Styles. Whomever was responsible, I appreciate the addition.

                                                Mantis Style is described as being based on grappling and rapid strikes (quite appropriate, considering the insect’s hunting method). It combines offense and defence, using disabling joint holds and redirecting blows, and is placed into the book’s context by being referred to as favoured by Immaculate monks as a means of subduing foes without killing. The unarmed strikes are a combination of strikes with hooked fingers, knees and elbows, as well as quite a variety of weapons: batons, seven-section staves, war fans, and the newly introduced nunchaku and kama. This is the first of the Styles in this book with no armour compatibility, and has no complementary Abilities.

                                                Iron-Arm Block starts the Style out with an application of its basic principle, granting additional Parry and inflicting onslaught on the one who attacked if they are successfully blocked. If the user is grappling, then a blocked blow doesn’t reduce rounds of control, and Mastery means that the stylist does not suffer onslaught penalties for successfully blocked attacks.
                                                With Crushing Claw Technique, the Mantis is able to add an automatic success to the attack roll for a grapple, or other decisive attack, and is able to make a Martial Arts roll with bonus dice to establish control of a grapple, or add a limited number of 10s rolled on the attack as extra dice for the damage roll.

                                                The Form Charm comes very early. Adopting the characteristic intimidation stance of the mantis, the user gains increased Parry, and is able to add their Parry value to their natural soak or half of their Parry in Hardness for attacks that they failed to block (a nice representation of the image of outstretched arms getting in the way). When attacking enemies with lower Initiative or that are being grappled by them, they double 10s on decisive damage rolls. Mantis Form can be activated reflexively upon winning control of a clinch.

                                                Leaping Mantis Technique allows the stylist to rush an enemy with bonus dice (with increased range under circumstances that I can’t fully parse until I reread the rush rules), and able to reflexively make a decisive attack or grapple if the rush brings them into close range of their target, with half of the threshold successes on the rush to the attack roll as bonus dice. If the rushed enemy doesn’t do… something, then the stylist can make a decisive attack or grapple that is unblockable, with all of the rush’s extra successes as bonus dice to the attack roll. Masters get a point of Willpower when they successfully land these attacks.

                                                Using Joint-Locking Technique, a grappled and restrained foe can have unsoakable withering damage equal to the attacker’s Strength rolled against the enemy, with no cost in rounds of control if the enemy was crashed (with Masters able to convert Initiative gained from the withering attack into more rounds of control, on a 2:1 exchange ratio). While Grasping Claw Method is used to immediately disarm a foe upon grappling them, tossing their arms out to short range (with Mastery allowing one to add threshold successes from the attack roll into bonus dice on the control one).

                                                Last up are three at Essence 3. Grasping Mantis Defense sounds like a fairly self-explanatory name, aaaaand actually I was mistaken. I was assuming some kind of improvement to defence while grappling, but it’s actually an ability to clash a close range attack with a grapple, with the successful clash adding extra non-Charm dice to the control roll. That makes more sense. Terrestrials are limited by the clash counting as their attack for the round*, and thus unusable if they’ve already attacked.

                                                The charmingly named Joint-Breaking Attack allows a stylist to inflict a special savaging attack on a clinched enemy, rolling a difficulty 6 gambit that requires no attack roll. Success inflicts a single lethal health level of damage (against which Hardness doesn’t apply), and breaks one of the target’s limbs for a crippling injury, either imposing a -3 penalty on any actions that require two arms or causing a broken-legged person to treat all ground as difficult terrain. It also increases wound penalties by two for the remainder of the scene. A metric is given for how long the broken limb can take to heal. Martial artists the crash a grappled enemy with Joint-Locking Technique can activate the Charm reflexively, while Terrestrials can only use the Charm once per scene unless they reset it by expending three rounds of control over a grapple to do so.

                                                Last of all is Unfolding Retribution Strike, a signature finishing move of a rapid barrage of blows, which I suppose is a kind of representation of the highly efficient shredding mouth parts of the mantis. A fighter that is controlling a grapple and has at least 15Initiative can release the clinch to launch an unblockable, undodgable decivie attack, with each round of control given up adding 3 dice to the damage roll, and increasing the onslaught penalty by one. It can only be used once per scene, unless reset by crashing a grappled foe whose Initiative was higher than the fighter’s. Mastery lets one add half of their expended rounds of control to base Initiative if their victim was incapacitated, while Terrestrials are limited in how many released rounds of control they give up by their Essence.

                                                Yes, having seen this one in full, I want to reiterate the value of it being here. It’s nice to get reminders that Exalted martial arts don’t always need to be all flash and special effects; that there’s plenty of room for a fighting style based on efficient and specialized ways of hitting people.

                                                * I’m suddenly struck by a bizarre image from how many times the Terrestrial keyword forces things like this to count as your action for the round, of Hermes Conrad telling off Dr. Zoidberg. Like, there was definitely at least one time that he told him that doing something counted as his lunch break or vacation or something. It’s probably going too far to call Terrestrial Exalted the Dr. Zoidberg of Martial Arts… although I did characterize the keyword as depicting people who are earnestly trying so very hard…

                                                    Now, White Veil Style. I’m going to be upfront; I don’t personally care for the whole schtick of talking around the White Veil Society existing, even if I like the society and its martial art. I get the significance of including it here, since it’s the first instance in a new Edition, and it’s going to be somebody’s introduction to the concept, but I am not personally going to participate.

                                                    With that out of the way, the White Veil Society is introduced as a loose-knit association of spies, assassins and martial artists spread through the Realm and elsewhere. Their motivation is not described, but I’ve personally preferred it as more of a Freemason’s kind of group, promoting certain values and mutually assisting one another, rather than being a conspiracy with a goal. They have their signature deadly martial art, that exists beyond the society due to students willing to pay vast sums for learning it and the occasional rival that has uncovered records of its techniques. It’s widely employed at social gatherings, and is described as alternately being quick and quiet, and torturous and stretched out over weeks. It uses swift, precise attacks to hit pressure points, as well as garrotes and needles, is incompatible with armour, and complemented by Socialize and Stealth for obvious reasons.

                                                    I said all of that while excising the endless negatives. I’m willing to give the benefit of the doubt in principle, but the difficulty of actually reading this wording and figure out what it’s saying would be grating under any circumstances, and right now is really wearing my patience thin. If this is continued throughout the Charms, I’m going to be quite nettled.

                                                    I like how garrotes can inflict either bashing or lethal damage, depending on the material.

                                                    Okay good, the Charms look as though they’re written sensibly. It starts with Bordsong Over Blades, allowing the stylist to conceal their attacks behind mundane motions. A successful decisive attack can be made to forego rolling damage (and thus, doesn’t need to reset to base), while still getting to inflict poison from weapons and the harmful effects of Charms besides their improvement of actual damage rolls. This means that even realising an attacks has happened requires an Awareness roll against a potentially quite high difficulty by all onlookers, including the victim. Using the attack from concealment doesn’t break stealth, and one can apply their Stealth successes as the difficulty of the Awareness roll if that would be better than the normal one. Mastery lets the Charm also inflict a penalty to the victim for resisting poison and other effects.

                                                    There’s a sidebar on how to deal with poison that drains Initiative outside of combat. I’d say that needing to roll Join Battle to create a pool just for the poison is sensible, allowing it to still be fast-acting and worthwhile, without being either kind of worthless or too much better outside of combat.

                                                    With Owl Clutches at the Night, the stylist gets to conceal failed attacks, forcing a similar kind of Awareness roll to the prior Charm, with attacks made from concealment maintaining stealth if nobody succeeds on the roll. Mastery gives the added benefit of receiving Willpower if nobody sees through you.

                                                    Using Alehouse Memory Stance, one is able to adopt an innocent demeanour, adding base Guile as bonus dice to a Stealth roll, ignoring the penalty for attempting that in combat if nobody has yet seen them make an attack.

                                                    The White Veil Form settles the fighter into a relaxed stance that improves their Evasion and Guile, removes the penalty for a flurry so long as it includes at least one Socialize action, and lets one substitute Manipulation for Dexterity in calculating Evasion or rolling to enter concealment, disengage or withdraw. Enemies that roll Join Battle in response to the martial artist need to roll with the lower of their Awareness or Socialize. The Form can be entered reflexively when a surprise attack is successful.

                                                    With Blithe Unruffled Plumage, the fighter is able to apply their Guile + 1 to their natural soak and reduce the Overwhelming value of attacks, or else gain their Guile in Hardness. It includes bonuses provided to Guile by other sources, and lets the user conceal the attacks of others when they fail to hit or damage. Mastery allows one to steal Initiative equal to Manipulation from an attacker whose blow was concealed from all onlookers.

                                                    The White Veil stylist can render onlookers Blinded by Laughter to attempt an ambush even while in plain view of a target, seeming completely innocent and rowdy until the blow lands. They get to roll Manipulation + Socialize with double 9s against the target’s Resolve to establish the ambush, and if successful uses the result of that roll in place of Join Battle. Otherwise, it’s a normal ambush or unexpected attack. It can only be tried once per scene, but is reset by landing a decisive attack on the same target without being noticed, although when reset, it can’t be used on the same person. With Mastery, a successful ambush doubles threshold successes on a withering attack, or adds up to Guile threshold successes on a decisive one, for purposes of rolling damage (which includes bonuses to the Guile).

                                                    Tickling the Dragon’s Throat constitutes an attack to cut off air supply, allowing every two threshold successes on a decisive attack roll to impose a penalty to the victim’s actions and Defence, up to a maximum of the user’s Essence, lasting until the onslaught inflicted by the inciting attack wears off. Mastery lets one use any threshold successes left over when the penalty reaches its limit to increase the number of turns that the onslaught penalty lasts for, at an exchange of one turn per two successes.

                                                    The Dragon Dies in Bed is a decisive attack to inflict a supernatural disease generated by the Martial Art (and also waiving the cost of Birdsong Over Blades if it’s put in a combo with this Charm). The disease, referred to as Subtle Mercy, has a virulence based on the user’s Manipulation, a high morbidity, and an interval of one day. In addition to the usual infliction of penalties or botches, it has magical effects at each intensity: minor causes weariness and lethargy, inflicting a slight fatigue penalty unless the victim sleeps for twelve hours each day, and greatly increases the difficulty to diagnose the disease; major causes the fatigue penalty to stack for every day that doesn’t get the full sleep, up to a maximum of five, and damage cannot be healed naturally if one remains active during convalescence, while the disease becomes a bit easier to diagnose; defining increases the required sleep to twenty hours, and is also necessary to recover Willpower, while the disease is at its easiest to diagnose.

                                                    Next up is The Dragon Dies Screaming, with which one’s decisive attack becomes inherently poisonous, with the inflicted toxin able to inflict 4 Initiative damage per round, and Aggravated when crashed. It has a fairly long duration based on Essence and Manipulation, and inflicts a penalty equal to Manipulation. It also waives the cost of Birdsong Over Blades. Diagnosing the poison has increased difficulty, and the attacker can elect to delay the onset of the poison for anywhere between an hour and a number of weeks equal to their Essence, with an even higher difficulty to diagnose when delayed. It can only be used once per scene, reset by incapacitating a character while no other witnesses can connect it to you, and Mastery enhances the poison to be incapable of having its duration reduced below one round.

                                                    Finally, there’s The Dragon Succumbs, making a decisive attack (that can again be combined with a free instance of Birdsong) against an enemy suffering from poison, disease or both. A struck victim must make a Resistance roll against the morbidity of every disease that they’re suffering from, with failure immediately advancing its level; if already Defining, it instead inflicts a level of aggravated damage that cannot be prevented. The attack also inflicts one interval of any poison automatically, as well as one for every threshold success on the attack roll, up to a maximum of the White Veil stylists’s Essence + 1. If suffering from multiple venoms, the attacker chooses which one to apply. The poison’s actual duration isn’t lowered by the inflicted interval, but the Charm still can’t be used to inflict more intervals than the poison actually has left. Mastery ensures that if a victim fails their roll against a disease’s morbidity at the Defining level, they’ll die after a number of turns equal to their own Stamina, unless somebody that has diagnosed the disease manages a Medicine roll against a rather hefty difficulty. While, in the Style’s one and only Terrestrial effect, it can only be used against a target through an ambush attack.

                                                    I find White Veil to benefit quite a bit from the consolidation of Martial Arts, giving it room for some especially impressive effects to really punctuate its place as an obscure but lethal method of attack. I especially like the pinnacle’s method for making somebody’s poison or disease fall upon them all at once.

                                                    Okay, my eyes are starting to get squishy, so I’m going to get in a few hours of sleep, before bombing through the rest.

                                                        All right, I’ve got just under six hours to go. It should be easier from herein.

                                                        Sorcery doesn’t waste any time with reiterated descriptions, and gets straight into new shaping rituals. I liked how the description of the Heptagram accommodates sorcerous initiation by virtually any path, but do think that it is a good idea to have a common method that represents the academy’s scholarly approach. So, they can acquire sorcerous motes through consultation of texts or an appropriate mentor, channelling it through arcane formulae and well-practiced mudras, or harnessing some geomancy through elemental manses. The last one in particular I like, setting the precedent of being able to extract the Essence from a certain object, but I also like the capacity to measure the reaped motes from a stunt rating in the second one for the encouragement to describe what kinds of formulae or mudra one is relying on.

                                                        Ah, of course, this is where the Geomantic Prodigy Merit that I saw mentioned in another thread would be. Fairly basic, one can add their Occult in dice to a roll for crafting regular architecture or geomancy every once in a while, that sounds helpful; even if the resulting structure is mundane and powerless, I like the idea of its form conforming to those standards as a means of giving verisimilitude to interesting buildings with holes through the middle of them or the like without needing to channel direct Essence. And the Merits for interacting with spirits play nicely into the old sense that Realm sorcery favours summoning.

                                                        Now, the greatly hyped up Wanasaan Exorcist path, acquired through drowning in a frozen spring to see the truth of the world at the brink of death, and come back with a splinter of ice in your heart. They can gain sorcerous motes through burnt offerings and grave goods offered to them while they still live, suspending themselves in salt water until the brink of drowning again, or getting a ghost to return to the cycle of reincarnation. Although the last seems like it has among the highest capacities of any shaping ritual to store motes, I find the first to have one of the more interesting logics behind it, with one’s soul having been made able to benefit from such sacrifices as though one was already dead.

                                                        Obviously Eyes in Both Worlds (see ghosts by default) is very effective for their basic purpose, and I like the idea of having a Merit to proof oneself against drowning for that uncanny sense of how sorcery can transform. I can see somebody looking upon an exorcist who ritually drowned themselves once and now cannot die from breathing water, and thinking that they’re pretty damned weird and wanting to give them a berth when possible.

                                                        Now, I’m hoping that since this is limited to Terrestrial Circle spells, it won’t be too long. Beckoning That Which Stirs the Sky seems like a good place to start, since weather shaping has often been described as among the most basic functions and luxuries of sorcery (all the way back to Moorcock), and I think the system of difficulties for how drastic the change is and the inability of other sorcery to override it comes out well. It also gives the spell a certain visual twist with the Lovecraftian (at least as might be interpreted by Guillermo del Toro in Hellboy) image of chitinous limbs emerging from nowhere to alter the course of the atmosphere. If those are visible, that has to really freak out the normals. Now, how can it be distorted and what’s the control benefit… ah, of course, for the latter, one can create weather that follows them, and for the former the altered weather in question is made into its opposite.

                                                        Hmm, Floral Ferry, a maritime version of the Cinderella method of transportation, without needing to bother with all of that messy fauna because the wind and currents will provide your propulsion… or rather, magical propulsion in place of wind, even. The ship can sail itself using the creator’s Occult to serve for necessary rolls, but its own magic is incapable of more complex functions such as chasing or fighting, requiring manual control to be taken of a ship not really made for it, and where its magical draw to intended destination becomes counterproductive. I can imagine an interesting scene of trying to conduct a naval battle by tacking not the wind, but the boat’s intended destination.

                                                        I had thought the highlighted words constituted different forms for the ship based on the seed material, but now, they’re extra powers that Essence can be spent upon. The hull can be hardened to give additional levels of damage that it can take (that again fall back onto the regular hull once the duration has elapsed), they can leave behind trails of pollen in the ship’s wake to gum up pursuer’s so that they suffer a penalty based on the sorcerer’s Intelligence (a nicely weird effect for an already odd spell), and being able to double the ship’s Speed by having the sorcerer at the helm. Having it as one’s control spell allows the ship to develop Evocations, and being distorted means that the interfering sorcerer can give it a new destination. Given how it isn’t strictly described as ending if its summoner is no longer upon it, I like to think that a port or two in the world has an old tale about the time a strange ship that was covered in peach blossoms rolled into port with nobody upon it.

                                                        Impervious Sphere of Water is fairly self-explanatory. I can see the balance requirements in making the full cover that it provides apply both ways, plus the difficulty of breaking the magically maintained surface tension in order to step out and perform an attack unimpeded. Mind, it’s still water, so I can imagine that it can be swum to the top of to get through the top and attack from there, for whatever that’s worth. And of course the sorcerer is limited to reflexive actions (with the benefit of moving if it’s the control spell). I like the distortion effect of rendering the water unbreathable and a complete impediment to those within while being open to without, and not ending if the sorcerer takes action. It really speaks to what makes distortion more interesting than the old counterspells; it approximates the literary idea of dealing with a magical effect by devising and applying a contrary one, compared to the boring game idea of flicking a spell’s off-switch.

                                                        Keel Cleaves the Clouds enchants a ship to be able to sail upon vapours such as mist or fog, which probably won’t generally carry you as high as the clouds, but can probably help to avoid a lot of traditional maritime dangers that one would strike within such a body of vapour. Oh wait, a reference is made to the potential to get as high as the clouds, fair enough. It’s offset a bit by losing Speed bonuses from being on the water, such as for oars and currents, and if the spell or vapour runs out the ship descends gradually and safely… ahh, of course being able to continue sailing over land would be another benefit that it could confer. Sorcerers with this as their control spell gain the stange feature of being constantly surrounded by mist (which they can disperse at will but returns if their emotions are wrought or they’re heavily focused), and have a natural capacity to walk themselves on mist or fog, with the traits of difficult terrain. Distortion makes the mist the ship is sailing on engulf it, obscuring the sight of those aboard, penalising sailing rolls and preventing foreseeing going over the edge of the bank.

                                                        Sculpted Seafoam Eidolen (my God but aren’t a lot of these related to water and sailing… unless there are going to be spells conforming to all five elements? So much for being shorter…) lets one construct an almost indistinguishable replica of a person or animal or object out of the foam and reflections of water. Its imitations can only be basic, with simple speech and not really doing actions, although it passes any basic sensory tests. If the sorcerer is present, it can be puppeteered directly, otherwise it reverts to an uncomplicated pattern that nevertheless comes across as realistic action; not running a race, but probably not walking into walls either. It’s possible to observe the simulacrum closely enough to figure out its illusory nature (against a difficulty of the successes rolled in creating it), especially if its being touched, and reading its intentions can also be used to realise that it’s a fake person. Having it as a control spell lets it create multiple illusions per casting, and being distorted allows control to be seized over it. I’ve often liked media depictions of magicians conjuring doubles of themselves from reflective surfaces, so I like the method of conveying (and expanding upon) that here.

                                                        Now for something different, the spell Spoke the Wooden Face. A sorcerer can carve their own face into the bark of a tree, and thereafter can cast a spell to see and speak through it at any distance, able to make (unenhanced) Perception or influence rolls through the face’s senses, although using it takes one’s awareness away from their own body and surroundings (unless the spell is ended prematurely or the sorcerer takes damage). The sorcerer’s presence can’t be detected naturally until they give themselves away, and the faces can be destroyed to render the spell unusable. I like this one for the manner in which it incentivizes odd and potentially creepy actions; walking into a wood surrounding a reclusive Dynast’s estate and seeing how every tree has been carved with one or more faces, or a person without context or comprehension coming across a sorcerer carving a face into a tree. Having it as the control spell lets one see and act through several faces at once, and distorting it cuts off the senses of a face, which also causes the sorcerer to lose a point of Willpower.

                                                        To represent Earth, there’s Stalwart Earth Guardian, a Ritual spell to draw, trace or carve a protective circle. From within, it provides a slight improvement to Defense or Resolve against attacks from without, but the real benefit is in what it does to those attackers; if within medium range of the ward’s centre, the earth itself attacks them with an environmental hazard of decent danger and difficulty, which can also make the terrain harder to move through, and interfering with attempts to approach stealthily by imposing a penalty on movement actions and being noisy enough to awaken sleepers. If it’s a control spell, apart from giving the sorcerer a strange earthy quality, their fingernails become hard enough to etch stone, functioning as an exceptional tool to Craft it, or write messages in Linguistics. Distortion turns the effects of the spell inward upon the ones hiding in the circle.

                                                        Air gets Thunder Wolf Howl. This one has the kind of description of a spell that I like, invoking ancient forgotten forces and monsters, as the basis for designating a spot within medium range that will be subjected to a powerful thunderclap. It forces characters to roll Stamina + Resistance against a difficulty of the sorcerer’s Essence or Intelligence (with battle groups penalised by their Size), with those that fall short suffering decisive bashing damage that ignores Hardness, equal to the sorcerer’s Essence plus whatever Initiative is divided evenly between targets. It deafens those that take damage, requiring medical intervention to fix, and gives them a crippling penalty equal to how many 10s were rolled in the damage against them (reduced as each turn passes). It can also shatter appropriate nearby materials, as the Storyteller deems suitable. Sorcerers with it as their control spell increase its difficulty and base damage, and their voice gains a booming quality when they’re overcome with strong emotion. I like this one for representing an unusual method, even by the mechanics of it, for using sorcery to attack.

                                                        Hmm, I wonder if Unslakable Thirst of the Devil-Maw is elemental… hmm, opening a disgusting mouth in one’s palm that leeches away moisture from air and flesh might be another one that falls under that element… Anyway, it lets Intelligence + Occult be rolled as an unblockable decisive attack against all characters in a cone out to medium range (also being undodgeable to battle groups), with every hit character taking dice of aggravated damage equal to the sorcerer’s Intelligence + extra successes on the attack roll, minus the target’s Stamina or Resistance, down to a minimum of one. It doesn’t reset to base Initiative, and doesn’t work on enemies who bodies would contain no moisture (while also not needing to subtract from the damage roll against victims with primarily liquid bodies). As a visual effect, it completely desiccates any normal plant life within its range. The stolen moisture gathers in a sphere above the sorcerer’s hand for a few minutes, that otherwise functions as completely normal water; it can be placed in a container, drank, or used for Charms like Elemental Sheathe. Hence, the spell also has a mundane utility in gathering water when in all but the very least humid environs. If it’s the control spell, then the spellbinder has a permanently open maw on one of their hands (probably best not their favoured one), which allows their unarmed attacks to have aggravated damage and the Piercing tag against enemies with bodily moisture, and dealing an additional die of withering or decisive damage against water elementals and similarly vulnerable foes. This is another one that is good for providing a sorcerous method of attack that is just plain damn weird (and presumably alters the actions of one with it as the control a little bit, assuming that they have to wear a glove most of the time).

                                                        Finally, to let Fire get a word in, there is Virtuous Guardian of Flame. A burning sword can be drawn from the heart of the sorcerer, that functions as a protector to watch over them. When the casting is completed, the sword coalesces into a small wispy flame that follows them around, and can be concealed by directing it to an already lit lantern or torch that the caster carries. If the caster is attacked, the wisp reforms into a sword to apply a defend other action with a Parry of the sorcerer’s Essence or Intelligence, +1. It makes its own rolls to notice possible threats, using a dice pool composed of its charge’s Essence, Perception and Occult, and destroys anything wooden that it blocks, as well as inflicting a die of Hardness-ignoring lethal damage when parrying an unarmed or other natural attack. If its defence is overcome, it slows attacks down enough to increase the target’s soak by their Essence, unless the attack was already unblockable.

                                                        The fire sword can be damaged in its own right, having soak, Hardness and -0 health levels all based on calculations from the caster’s Essence. It has equal Initiative to the sorcerer when they Join Battle, although purely for the benefit of interacting with withering attacks. If the sword is crashed, it dissipates for the length of a scene, but returns thereafter. If destroyed, it can’t be summoned again until its original duration elapses. If it’s a control spell, it can unlock Evocations, including for being wielded offensively, and distortion allows it to be stolen to provide protection for a scene to the usurper. One thing that I like in sorcery is weird little summonings, so this one plays in nicely.

                                                        There are good spells here, both for providing examples to fill out the elemental interactions with Dragon Blooded Charms, and generally broadening the scope of what this option can do.

                                                            Now, for the chapter on Heirloom Artifacts. After Arms of the Chosen, this one ought to be good.

                                                            First a fiction in which we finally get to see Left Hand Chalima in action. Huh, I was not expecting her to be the sort who would set themselves up as a ruler, so I’m already invested (particularly since she’s dealing with the boring mundanity of administrative paperwork). I particularly like the idea of her having a husband who offered to perform that work for her that particular evening, as well as the idea that she turned him down.

                                                            Heh, also the idea that, as the new boss, she’d redesign the prior dynasty’s mausoleum to something that she viewed as more tasteful and less ostentatious, while still constituting an impulse to actually be respectful of the ghosts; plus the way that they griped about it like troublesome relatives.

                                                            Oh, and she’s had a prior husband that she’s directing a prayer to. That makes sense; her current one is probably also mortal, and she’s definitely more than fifty years old, so she could have conceivably outlived at least one prior one. I like an Exalt being depicted as having a relationship with a mortal spouse such as this; where the bond and memory of one might endure even past their natural death (possibly literally if her husband did actually leave a ghost). Also the way that she makes a wry observation to comparisons between the ease of starting and stopping fires. Yes, she’s got a kind of Conan-vibe to her, with the distress over dealing with an enemy that can oppose without fighting, I like it.

                                                            Oh, her husband actually did leave a ghost, and he shows up for what is apparently the first time. I’m guessing that praying over the difficulties faced by the increase of Anathema activity is a big enough deal to prompt that.

                                                            Aww, and he prompts her to try and get her old band together.

                                                            This is a lovely little story. I already liked her design, but I think that Chalima might be my favourite out of the signature Kinship.

                                                            So, Heirlooms of Power. There’s a sidebar briefly reiterating what Resonance and Dissonance is, and then… straight into the write-up of an Artifact. Huh. Let me just check ahead a bit… I see, there isn’t a special description of a general system or approach for Artifacts passed down through a family, the chapter is just an assortment of Artifacts with their Evocations (which may still be described in a manner referring to family legacy). That’s not a bad thing, mind, since Artifacts are pretty great, but it does complicate my approach…

                                                            Okay, handle this in the same manner as Arms; don’t analyse each device down to its individual Evocations, just a broad overview of history, concept, and any standout powers. They each get about a paragraph. Nice and simple.

                                                            Calumny: a green jade wrackstaff, previously the weapon of a member of House Cynis that went around with the identity of an infuriating thief to hound the Great Houses. It’s made from bamboo from the Imperial Mountain, banded with brown-speckled green jade, and passed down through generations of the House’s blackmailers. It has a basic function of being collapsible into a short, easily concealed rod, and is powered by secrets, with intensity based on the kind of influence that would get somebody to reveal it publicly (which also means that its value to the staff can shift over time, as the secret becomes less fraught). It’s got powers to find secrets, weaponise them against opponents, improve defence against somebody with them… ohh, I like the image of the pinnacle to convert a person’s secret into an actual poison that can kill them, that’s a strong image. God I love Artifacts now.

                                                            Daring Venture: green jade powerbow, a birthday present to V’neef from her father, based on her tendency to sneak off into the woods for her own explorations and a brief escape from stifling normal life. She struck music from it to tame a giant monster wolf, and it became bonded to the weapon’s legend (that’s a very nice spin on the usual narrative as a basis for its power, as well as interesting insight into herself). It’s described as having a yearning for adventure and discovery in the wilderness (as well as summoning the wolf, Hundred Rivers), and the busy V’neef often lends it to children with a similar inclination to herself to chart the future of the House. It has powers to tame beasts (or enhance familiars), guiding one to new places, and gaining Hundred Rivers as a familiar that will immediately travel with all haste to their side (plus a description of the rather lovable monster), as well as enhancing the Charm to train the wolf and empowering his loyalty to his master.

                                                            Okay, I see the significance of heirlooms now; it’s not a special form for Evocations to take, it’s a context in which an Artifact’s legend develops, and the legacy conferred by it being something that can be passed from one Exalt to another for generations. Daring Venture is the matriarch’s powerbow, and the House’s connection to an icon of her legend (which might endure even after she herself dies, since Hundred Rivers already seems to be far longer lived than a normal canid). There’s a context of respect and honour to being entrusted with it, with commensurate disgrace if it is lost and motivation to get it back. That’s good; it roots these Artifacts to the Dragon Blooded, and gives the write-ups a distinct flavour from Arms, with its focus on a hoary past.

                                                            {checks back} Also, Evocations that unlock automatically for House scions.

                                                            Faith’s Pillar: white jade grand goremaul made for Mnemon’s son Beral, who established their standard for proselytization, by respectful Mountain Folk. Beral carried it into battle against the monstrous enemies of the Jadeborn, and when he fell in battle they returned it to his family, where it gets passed to family monks. I like the description of it being carved in emulation of the Imperial Mountain, with symbols of the four Directions on each face, and how it’s otherwise a stark, geometric shape; a nice sense of Immaculate minimalism still making for a stylish Artifact. Its power centres around a chosen Principle of religious belief or social order, either enhancing that belief or using it to enhance the wielder’s stolidness and force, or punishing those who violate it. Also, being able to coat the head with a clod of compacted earth or stone to beat on people with the strength of your conviction, as well as culminating in a power to seal a crashed enemy in a suffocating tomb (especially difficult to break out of if they’ve violated the Pillar’s guiding Principle).

                                                            Heavenly Typhoon: a much storied (and thus obscured) blue jade infinite chakram of House Ledaal, often said to have been made by an ancient hero inspired by the force of the hurricane to make a weapon to strike down Anathema, actually made in the First Age by a Solar for her spymaster Terrestrial to uncover plots and strike down the corrupt and the blasphemous, eventually used by the original owner’s granddaughter to kill the Solar who made it, and ultimately found by a distant descendent from Ledaal. Its blue jade design has a very pretty addition in an orichalcum inlay that alternately looks like a jagged lightning bolt and bared dragon fangs, depending on the angle. Its power is built around a notion of condemning enemies, which despite the name, doesn’t constitute a moral judgement, just an enemy struck by its first Evocation (which has no cost against creatures of darkness). The powers from this can assault or harry those condemned by it, with a pinnacle of being able to cast a storm of lightning-wreathed chakrams to hit numerous condemned characters at once with a special withering attack.
                                                            There’s also a short sidebar on the Realm’s disdain for mixed-material Artifacts, but not so extreme that they don’t use them.

                                                            Pyre of Legions: red jade grand daiklave from House Cathak, initially owned by a member of the House that was regarded as unaccomplished and unexceptional despite her determination and disdained for being anti-social, until giving her life in a spectacular display that cost an enemy greatly. I’m assuming that she possessed a mundane weapon that was used as the basis in forging the daiklave, which has the striking detail of its first wielder’s burning handprint on its hilt. It carries the memory of Cathak Falu’s last thoughts, conferring a Defining Principle of giving anything to protect comrades to those who attune it, that cannot be weakened or altered. Its powers are based on standing at the side of comrades against overwhelming odds, and protection of those comrades (which has a nice synergy with the character of Fire Aspected Melee), with the self-sacrifice angle coming from many having a cost in aggravated damage. I think there’s something particularly special in its pinnacle Charm; letting the wielder spend Willpower instead of health levels on the prior Evocations, and treating them as though they have the worst wound penalty for the sake of a power that accounts for that. The image that the spirit of somebody who sacrificed themselves might live on by offering an alternative to that for a future generation, that exemplifies what is so good about Artifacts now, not just for power, but for personality, and ideals, and (as especially fitting for the Dragon Blooded) legacy.

                                                            Sea’s Verdict: a black jade longfang of a Peleps judge, carved from the stone that was used in sacrificial rites to an elemental by a cabal of priests that attempted to murder (and then bribe) the judge. It has the form of a trident (which distinctly gives it the Disarming tag) carved engraved with silver serpents and moonsilver prongs, and typically wielded by the House’s poets and pirate-hunters to destroy criminals and other evildoers, although the Artifact itself is indifferent to such ideals or romanticism. It has its own anima banner that grows as motes are spent on powers (even besides its own Evocations) to enhance its attacks and parries, causing its form to grow wild as the engravings dissolve, building it to add the Smashing tag, and applying the indiscriminate pressure of the sea to batter and crush opponents. This is an interesting example of an Artifact contrary to the norm, where its legend is informed less by the nature of those who created it and more by the unfathomable and unfeeling quality of the force from which it was made.

                                                            Smiling Razors: a pair of red jade short daiklaves that were bestowed on a monk from House Sesus, who grew too enamoured of the violence of Wyld Hunts while disdaining monastic serenity, very publicly extricating herself from the Order by burning off her robes with her anima and profaning an alter, openly thumbing her nose at the Order with challenges to monks and drawing Dynasts to apostasy with such extreme audacity that she was actually considered to qualify as Anathema, and ironically subjected to the Wyld Hunt herself. Her own cousin led the Hunt that killed her, and claimed the weapons (contrary to his own temperament) to make a public show of cleansing their dark history, so they could be added to the arsenal of his House. The Razors have powers to attack in secrecy, adopting the demeanour of a shadow in firelight, as well as improving infiltration and bestowing painful, burning wounds.

                                                            Vengeant: a black jade set of razor claws in the possession of House Iselsi, forged from the remains of previous members of the House’s arsenal on orders from the Scarlet Empress as a show of force, magnanimity, and reminder of their new status, which they adopted as a reversal of weapons to create dynasties into one that would end them. Its powers grow from judging an enemy to be despised, one who is hated to a particular member of the House or has a personal or ancestral connection to having wronged them, to launch secretive and disabling attacks (with a nice emphasis in the attack names and effects of targeting the other elements specifically), building up towards inflicting greusome deaths that leave a mark on those that discover their aftermath.

                                                            Horizon Cleaver: blue jade grimcleaver (and first four-dot Artifact), another one dating to the very end of the First Age, made by a Sidereal inspired by the powerful storms created by battling Exalted in the Usurpation to be given to a Dragon Blooded lieutenant. It’s haft is banded in steel and black rubber, with curved twin blades inlaid with red jade poetry to extol the virtue of the Usurpation, and in battle is wreathed with blue lightning. It passed down through the Shogunate to be wielded by one of Tepet’s sisters in the siege of the Imperial City, and then in service to the Realm through the new House, to be lost track of after the campaign against Kaneko. Its power is based on unleashing powerful blasts of lightning that strike indiscriminately, that can become increasingly tamed as the wielder’s power over it grows.

                                                            Imprimatur: a white jade daiklave that the Scarlet Empress initially gifted to Nellens, and then bestowed upon the House elevated in his name (rather than the ones formed around his Exalted children by her), often carried when members of the House go into tense negotiation with other Houses, who view its existence as an insult. Imprimatur’s power emerges from the Renown that it bestows upon those Nellens scions that wield it, with powers based on elevating the stature of that wielder and breaking down and humiliating those that oppose her (including in certain social engagements), building up to a pinnacle that both improves the enhancement that Renown provides to them, and allows them to draw strength from special messages written on the blade by the Empress, such as strong powers to enhance allies or deprive foes.

                                                            Perdurant Vault: a white jade thunderbolt shield created by an occultist of Ragara in the early days of the House, made with the aid of a bargain with a queen of the Underworld. It’s described as a large shield strung with an obsidian chain, made to be ornate so that it would not be out of place in high-society fashions, with a polished surface occasionally revealing a hidden city in its depths. While used in occult experiments of the House’s high society, it’s primarily employed by socialites, bureaucrats and politicians to shield them in the intrigues of Dynast society. Hence, it provides various powers to aid one in social interactions, protect them from clandestine and corrupting attacks such as poison or disease, and preserve them against other influences. As one gets deeper into its powers, they can benefit from hiding or banishing parts of themselves in the City of Opal Unstained, which can ultimately be entered so that they can be interacted with for special insights, or confronted in order to excise them.

                                                            Not really much to say about the chapter in summary, other than it being another exceptional suite of magical weapons.
