            Moving on: Wyld Things! (Dum bum, dundun, dum bum, dundundun.)

            We open with a handful of raksha, who are… wow, those are healthy little suckers – something like 15 HL buys even a basic cataphract some time. This is also the highest base attack pools I’ve seen so far – 16 dice is substantially better than even Ahlat or Octavian were throwing around. Presumably that’s because it’s a more accurate weapon – again, it’s really starting to feel weird with these stronger enemies that there’s not a Decisive/Withering pool split, because that makes them a lot more dangerous.

            Iron is noted as dealing aggravated damage to the fae, but who cares? It’s not like there’s soak to bypass at that point. (Iron does seem to work better against some raksha magic, which is nice.) The raksha gets a power to sub your Resolve for your soak, which, that’s a game changer for 3m – and then we get this odd stand-in where appropriate Intimacies can pull the job that Valor used to do. This feels awkward – nothing in the Intimacy rules suggests that my brave hero will get in trouble without an actual Intimacy of bravery (instead of, say, love for the people and places he wants to protect). The big limitation here is that the fae have no standard die-adders – they’re going to need to significantly outnumber a Solar in order to wear through his defenses.

            Hm, maybe the raksha do still have enormous base pools – the lorelei has Appearance 7, at least. She also has a power that removes your ability to realize something is dangerous, making possible influence that would otherwise be blocked – that’s a fun, thematic power.

            Silverwights are probably the weakest creatures to get an effective excellency – a pack of four wights hunting together gets +3 successes to all of their attacks, which makes them substantially more dangerous than their stat line would show. Except… can you actually have a pack of four individual silverwights? They’re literally identical, fused into a gestalt hive-mind – by the rules for battle groups, more than two of them automatically glom together.

            Is this illustration meant to show a buck-ogre? Because it… kind of looks just like an ogre – no deer-heads, which is confusing in the context of the other two-headed thing that was just described. It’s also oddly lopsided – the second head looks like a cartoon doodled onto the shoulder of the original sketch.

            The manticore may be “that most terrible of creatures,” but it’s unlikely to ever successfully sting something with 8 dice. I think this is my problem in general, here: a lot of these creatures get detailed write-ups taking a page or more of special abilities, but they can’t actually hit a player with a decent starting statline – not unless a led battlegroup of manticores falls on the party, or something. And that’s before we get to actual Charms – realistically, even a single Solar with 4s in his stats should be able to hold off two or three of these creatures long enough to Crash one and bisect another just off his Excellencies. So, again, who are these detailed statlines for? What threat are they meant to offer, that justifies how much pagecount they’re eating?

            … Well, here’s a section where the answer should be obvious: The Exalted. We get Essence calculations: Solars win the mote-pool war, of course, though Sidereals… okay, what in Sam Hill? Sidereals lose personal motes between E1 and E2? That can’t be right. Lunars actually start out well ahead of Solars in terms of total motes, and then gradually fall behind, which is kind of an odd set-up, but sure.

            Dragon-Blooded lose their old half-cost Excellencies; now they pay the same rate as Solars, to a lower die-cap. But… huh. They gain the ability to spend 1wp for a late-step reroll. That’s huge – a DB who misses you by only one success can cash in to close the gap, while one who fell several short can cut his losses. The Dragon-Blooded anima flux is back, but any level of armor blocks damage from it – sorry, martial artists.

            The first Dragon-Blood example is… E1, really? That’s kind of sad, but I guess he’s starting character opposition. We get some sample Charms for each of the Exalts here – homebrewers, ahoy! – which is good, since this is the first real opportunity to calibrate how Solars stand out. And…

            … and that’s a power costing 6m and your full anima that only grants +1/2 success on a Decisive roll. What? Why would you – what? I guess it’s more useful on a Withering attack, but that Decisive effect passes “sad” straight on into “borderline useless.” There’s a 2m effect right afterwards with the same or better effect on your Decisive pool. This does not seem exceptionally well balanced, so far. Is the balance factor here a late-step activation? That’s not clear, if so.

            Ooh, we get chunks of the Elemental Dragon styles here – that’s a nice bonus. I wish these DB Charms included a “for characters of this Essence” note the way the spirit Charms do. Fire Dragon Form looks like the most vicious of these – slower enemies take -2 to their Parry as an Onslaught penalty. (Fire Dragon Form + Solar Brawl?)

            Critiques aside, it is really nice to have some Charm guidelines here, especially since we’re probably… what, a year and a half or more out from having any of the old Exalt types other than DBs?

            Huh. Lunar Excellencies are substantially changed; with a stunt, the Lunar die cap now becomes (Attribute + other Attribute). That’s Solar-level, despite a base that’s lower than Dragon-Blooded die-adders. Interesting.

            There is a Lunar dude tearing it up, here. Interestingly, he only has a very small, crescent-shaped tattoo pattern on his chest – is he casteless (probably not; it’s a crescent moon tattoo), or are the tattoos no longer full-body affairs, or…?

            It’s hard to get the flavor of most of these Lunar Charms, but there is one that lets the Lunar rush better against a wounded enemy. That’s a nice touch. There’s also a promise of at least temporary form-theft without murder, which, yes, please and thank you. (If you don’t already know about the “must eat people” thing, I suspect it’s easy to mistake “take the heart’s blood” for something more poetic than it actually is.

            Why is “THE SIDEREAL EXALTED” in a smaller, less obvious font than the sub-heading “ESSENCE” a few lines down?

            The Sidereal Excellency is… okay, so, let’s talk probability. In 2e, the Sids had a die cap of (Essence), which they could (like all Celestials) render free by using an Infinite Mastery. They could also buy down their TNs from 7 to 4, which meant each of those few dice was more efficient – but there was no discount on the buy-down, unless I’m misremembering. So at Essence 4, a 2e Solar could drop 23 dice, averaging 11.5 successes, for free; a 2e Sidereal could only muster 17 dice, for 8.5 successes, for free, but could do 13.6 dice if he dropped 3m on it. Since 3m was also the cost of a perfect, not such a big deal.

            So, here we are. Nobody gets Infinite Masteries anymore. At E4, a Solar drops 21 dice, for 11.5 successes, but it costs him 10m to do so. At E4, a Sidereal drops 15 dice, for 12 successes, but it only costs him 3m. At equivalent mote costs, with identical base pools, the Sidereal substantially beats the Solar for raw output at any Essence rating; from E4 onward, he also caps out higher. (If we’re looking at, say, Withering pools, this happens a lot earlier.) That’s kind of bothersome.

            I mean, obviously, we only have a tiny piece of the picture right now, but one way or another, this power is a much bigger deal than it was in 2e – currently, it makes the Sidereals king of the dicewhompers, in terms of sustainability at an absolute minimum. An E4 Sidereal who wants to hit you with his Decisive will do so a majority of the time, and he’ll pay less than half what it takes you just to cut his odds back that far.

            It bears watching, I guess is what I’m saying.

            Abyssal Charms still look like they have a number of Solar mirrors – I see Dipping Swallow and Ready in Eight Directions, at a glance. Which I’m cool with. The sample Abyssal in general is pretty low-accuracy; I think I’d rather fight him than the sample Sid.

            Oh, man, this Abyssal picture. Okay, first, it’s really well done: a mournful-looking deathknight standing atop a pile of corpses, his enormous daiklave buried a foot deep in one of them; around him, his army of the dead shuffles through the ruins of a city. In his hand, he holds a single, perfect red rose, which he contemplates mournfully.

            It’s darkly gorgeous, and I really wish this was the Abyssal pic used in the chargen chapter instead of that ugly brown one they actually have. It really seems to capture that mood of “the romanticism of the dead,” or whatever, that the earlier chapter referenced. The Abyssal also has this expression that suggests he hangs out in a lot of coffeeshops in between mass murders, talking about how tragic pop culture is and asking if he can read you his poetry, and I want to slap him more than I want to engage in awesome kung-fu duels with him. So, mixed emotions.

            We’re still not going to admit that Abyssals are corrupted Solar Exaltations? Hokay.

            Liminals! The Frankenstein themes are strong here, but I feel like most of these powers could be easily refluffed for Abyssals instead – I’m not catching the divide strongly. There’s a Charm here that explicitly ruins one of your arms to use, which is a fun mechanic.

            Sidebars are still misplaced – this Liminal sidebar is solidly into the section on Exigents. Speaking of, we do get one Exigent: the chosen of Wu Jian, whose powers are city themed (though that’s surprisingly broad – stealth in crowds, sex appeal like a prostitution district, the power to open any door in a city, the ability to analyze a building to learn what’s happening inside.) And… that’s actually it. One Abyssal, one Liminal, one Exigent. I kinda wish some of the earlier stuff was cut to make more room here.

            And finally, we have… animals? Guess I would have expected these to come earlier, back with the mortals. Animals have some innate powers, some latent powers that can be unlocked by training, and some magical gifts that Exalts can awaken – that’s a nice tiering. It’s a little funny that humans are the only thing in-setting not to get any edges… anyway. We get a nice spread of default powers – including, good grief, what’s basically Adamant Skin Technique. My familiar can get AST?

            Animals are, in general, big threats to mortals – a bear, as many have noted, will mess you up if it gets its paws on you. It’s the “If” that’s the key, there – again, Excellencies make for a big swing. My suspicion is, again, that most of these animals are going to rapidly become non-threats except in numbers that would ordinarily suggest battle-groups. (As an easy example, the death moa gets huge damage bonuses against low-Initiative or Crashed enemies, can gain bonus successes, and so on. But it’s biggest attack pool is, again, eight dice – what’s it supposed to do with that?)

            This observation isn’t original to me, but: it’s kind of a problem that we don’t have actual Attribute/Ability stats for animals, given how the training Charms interact with that.

            Ah ha ha, raptors – er, claw striders have a power that might as well be called “Clever Girl” – that get bonuses to distract on behalf of a raptor in stealth. Fabulous.

            Okay, this list is enormous – I’ve seen close to 20 creatures, and I’m only up to the Gs. Presumably this is for the sake of Lunar players? This almost feels like it might go better in their book – again, I’m not sure these are going to be meaningful antagonists to use in any other context.

            Yep, the quoll-lion is a drop bear. Don’t think I’d have caught that one.

            I had never realized until this edition that raitons are something like an archaeopteryx, not just ravens. That is immensely cooler.

            I feel like about half the creatures in this list are real megafauna, and unfortunately, I just don’t recognize most of them – the raptor, t-rex, stegosaur, ankylosaur, and triceratops aside. (Childhood dinosaur obsession, don’t fail me now.) The dinosaur art, though, is just not doing it for me; first, I’d rather have fewer crittters with more pictures, and second, the pictures that do exist are mostly wooden affairs, where the creatures themselves don’t quite seem to fit with their environment.

            Hooray, yeddim stats! We finally find out their size: Legendary. And aw, no simhata?

            And oof, that’s the end of chapter eight. The Exalt and demon stats are the best of the bunch, probably followed by the other spirits and the fair folk. There’s waaaaaay too much overkill here for my tastes, and too many lovingly-described enemies with unique powers… that are totally useless against anything that can generate a Defense over 5. Still, if you want a wide range of choices for your familiar or animal form, or if your players are sporting combat pools of about 7, there’s a lot here to choose from – it’s not bad so much as it is just not especially useful. The editing continues to have some gaps in it, though not as badly as the sorcery chapter. In the particularly good sections – especially the selection of demons – the book really shines, and its presented boss-monsters look like they fill the job very nicely.

            One more chapter to go: Chapter 10, The Grand Panoply.

                                    All right. One last push.

                                    Chapter Ten: The Grand Panoply.

                                    The size of this chapter is a bit deceiving; rather than the eightysome pages we have left, it only stretches for about another sixty (“only”) – the last 20 or so are backer names.

                                    New fiction starring Chejop, and… ugh. Okay, this one fails to hold my interest from the very first paragraph. “He often wondered if he hadn’t lost himself somewhere along the way.” The writing doesn’t get any better, either – the whole page is just Chejjy Kejjy contemplating to himself. That worked with the Physician, both because it stood out from the other pieces so far, and because it was a piece with some amusing humor, but here it falls completely flat. It's also a little too fond of the passive voice: “The east was where he let his eye rest.” Also, those freaking period-commas.

                                    (No mortals! Not even any mention of mortals doing things, I think.)

                                    Then we get to the infamously stolen art – which ain’t the authors’ fault, obviously, and kudos to them for taking steps to fix it.

                                    But, um. Entirely aside from that.

                                    … is Chejop Kejak secretly Rich Thomas? Like, compare this illustration with his Kickstarter picture.

                                    Two interesting notes here: first, a discussion on characters being habitually in debt, which is a nice touch, and second, a note that you don’t have to have Resources 3 just to start with a suit of armor. That’s an excellent fix; this was a really obnoxious restriction in past editions that typically lasted until about the end of the first fight.

                                    Detailed resource discussions in Exaled always make me a little bit uncomfortable – I feel like I’m playing the game wrong (or, well, in an unintended way), because I track “You have 1-5 dots” and then kind of otherwise ignore the system. When it starts talking about inflation rates and currency exchanges and debased silver… there’s just nothing in my games that plugs into that, and I don’t really know how to bridge the gap between Spice & Wolf-style “This coin is part tin!” and the order-of-magnitude pricing of a thing.

                                    Is this a picture of Swan, with the ghost lady? It has kind of his look – probably not, I guess, but it’s hard to be sure.

                                    We get a note that thaumaturgy can create warding talismans. That sounds useful, or at least mortal-level valuable, and I wish we’d seen more of it during the Thaumaturgy section (or am I just being forgetful?).

                                    Okay, weapons! The weapon system has been simplified drastically, which I very much approve of. Your weapon is either light, medium, or heavy; the distinction corresponds roughly to a 1:1 tradeoff between decreasing Accuracy and increasing damage. That seems not ideal, in this system; a weapon with +4 accuracy also gets +2 average successes, which translates to +2 damage even if we ignore that it’s more likely to hit in the first place. (If we don’t ignore that, its comparative damage does quite a bit better.) Heavy weapons also give a defense penalty, while medium ones give a defense bonus; I’m feeling pretty confident that you should never, ever take a heavy weapon, here.

                                    Some (very brief) math, here: say you have a base pool of 8 and a medium weapon, vs. an opponent with the same pool and a heavy weapon. You’re going to hit him (with 10 dice at Parry 3) something like 89% of the time, while he hits you (with 8 dice at Parry 5) something closer to 38% of the time. (These are very approximate numbers.) In his absolute best case, and ignoring the fact that you sometimes get extra successes, you’ll get soaked down to 1 die exactly, while he’ll be throwing 3 dice. That means you average ~.45 damage per hit, while he averages ~.57 damage - very very slightly better. If we shift that soak value by 1 in either direction, the medium weapon outperforms the heavy.

                                    My chosen pools are arbitrary here, rather than optimal – 8 felt like a good combatant without being purely optimized. Try your own comparative pools and see how the numbers stack up, but right now, heavy weapons are feeling like a trap. (And this completely ignores that hitting with a Withering attack always earns you 1i, which makes more frequent hits better, or that even a tap is enough to trigger most Charms that you’d stack onto a Withering attack, which does the same thing.)

                                    Okay, so if all weapons are in one of three categories, how do we distinguish them? Answer: weapons have tags, which mark special attack options (“I can knock you back a range band”) or persistent bonuses (“My minimum damage is one higher”). There’s no cap on the number of tags – this isn’t Wulin-style “normally 1, or 2 if they’re special” – so it feels like you mostly want as many as you can have. That’s an odd balance thing, if tags are meant to be a balance consideration at all.

                                    There follows a long list of tagged mundane weapons. None of the tags are defined until after the list, which is exactly opposite of how keywords have been handled everywhere else in the book. A number of the weapons have illustrations accompanying them, which are fine if not exceptional – no problem here; I don’t need a Picasso to show me what tiger claws look like. Whips have six tags, which starts to feel excessive. (Edit: so does the fighting chain; the hand crossbow has 7-8.) There are, alas, no bat’leth stats – er, I mean, there’s no entry for the spread-the-water knife.

                                    If tags are meant to be balance features, there are some odd inclusions here: the axe/hatchet is a strict superset of the chopping sword, and is also cheaper; the same is true of the fighting chain for the seven-section staff. The slashing sword and straight sword are apparently different weapons, but have exactly the same stats, as do the poleaxe and great axe.

                                    We reach the initial set of tags. Shield, interestingly, gives no persistent bonuses; you can use a shield to Rush and Full Defend at the same time, at a cost of it doing less damage, but once you’re actually swinging a weapon it’s no help. I’m not sure that’s a bad thing; shields were far too good in 2e, and it looks like actually being able to hit is pretty important here.

                                    Hm, two-handed weapons get you a +2 Clash bonus (as does dual-wielding identical stuff) – that’s not bad, but I’m not sure it makes up for not having a shield, not having a free hand, having a hard time in grapples, etc. We get a dual-wielding sidebar, which still doesn’t mention the off-hand penalty. The heck, game.

                                    Thrown weapons seem to get strictly better as weight goes up, in direct contrast to everything else. Eh? There are no heavy thrown weapons, which is also kind of odd.

                                    There’s a keyword indicating that some Thrown weapons can be used while mounted. Is, um, is there anything saying you normally can’t use Thrown weapons while mounted? (Edit: also applies to Archery, apparently.)

                                    Okay, so, throwing needles. First, strictly inferior to shurikens. Second, throwing needles are subtle – which means that they don’t actually inflict damage; they just inflict poison. So how does that work? Do you still roll Initiative, and the poison sticks if you get at least one success?

                                    Archery weapons have the same “increasing weight” problem as Thrown. On balance, it looks like Archery weapons are at least as accurate at all ranges except Close – where Thrown excels, and Archery stinks. Past Medium range, you definitely want an archery weapon, though.

                                    So I’m definitely not a gun guy, but why does the picture of the firewand include a lever-action, given that the weapon loads like a musket? Am I misunderstanding this? Like, literally all I know about lever-action weapons is that Arnold looks cool flipping one around in Terminator 2, but I’m still pretty sure they don’t fit here.

                                    Armor! Mundane armor gives no Hardness – huh, wasn’t expecting that. So mundane armor will not stop a Dragon-Blooded’s anima, then – this is good reason for no Solar to shirk artifacts, here.

                                    I like these armor illustrations quite a bit, actually. Simple, but they look cool.

                                    Artifacts next!

                                                    Okay, Artifact weapons. At a glance, you want an artifact weapon because it gives:
                                                    1. +1 Accuracy over its mundane equivalent.
                                                    2. +3 Damage over its mundane equivalent.
                                                    3. Better Defense, if it’s heavy.
                                                    4. Better minimum damage.
                                                    Those are compelling reasons, but heavy seems even more like a trap, here. Again, compare the Medium to the Heavy user: Medium now has (throwing 11 dice vs. Parry 4) something like an 81% chance of hitting, while Heavy has (throwing 9 dice vs. Parry 5) roughly a 47% chance of hitting. That’s similar to the numbers last time – but now, in Heavy’s ideal soak case, Medium throws 4 damage dice to Heavy’s six. In a case like that, Medium averages 1.62 damage successes per attack, while Heavy averages 1.41 per attack – advantage Medium, and it’s only going to get worse from there. (Note that, even going up against a higher Defense, the artifact version of a Medium does something like 3-4x more average damage than the mundane Medium.)

                                                    Plus all that was mentioned before; plus the Heavy is going to be two-handed. Don’t use Heavy weapons.

                                                    We do not get tags repeated for artifacts, which makes sense space-wise but makes me wish even more that all the tags were in one place.

                                                    Huh, attunement is almost instant now. That removes an obnoxious part of the “acquire an artifact” process.

                                                    The artifact pictures are not bad – a little World of Warcraft, maybe, but they look clearly magical-er than the Melee versions. I don’t quite catch the sense of oversized that I would want, though. No mention of hearthstone sockets?

                                                    The only artifact shield described here is… a wrackstaff? What? That feels kind of like an oversight. (Also, it seems like it makes the wrackstaff a better shield than an artifact shield would be, since it also has the Reach tag.)

                                                    Aw, no non-Slow flame weapons. Well, that’s what Righteous Devil is for – still, I miss that five-shot alchemical flame piece.

                                                    Huh – the artifact armor gets +2/+3/+4 damage over its mundane equivalent. That’s interesting. Heavy armor actually gives quite a lot of soak and Hardness – even at -2 Evasion, that’s pretty tempting.

                                                    Then we get to utility artifacts. Is there no just general gear list? I was expecting at least a table of prices for things. Several of these are familiar – and okay, good, we do get a mention of hearthstone bracers – but I like the traveler’s staff; not a showy tool, but free food and shelter isn’t an awful thing to have.

                                                    Illustration: Ken, having left Barbie, has slipped into a poorly fitting Ninja GI Joe outfit and gone out to pick a fight.

                                                    The Belt of Shadow Walking is really good: free dematerialization; free +3 non-Charm dice to Stealth. That’s more than a little nuts. … Wait, hang on, no; all bonus dice from artifacts count as Charm dice. So, er, my die cap is higher with a high-quality mundane lockpick than it is with the Bracers of Universal Crafting?

                                                    Hearthstones! Not Evocations? Well, whatever. Holden famously hates hearthstones; let’s see what they look like now. We already know there are only two levels of hearthstone. Hearthstone keywords come first, which is right and proper. Apparently only some stones actually depend on the manse staying healthy, which is a big change; others only grow in a wild demesne. Hm – that’s an odd distinction to require, and I’m curious of the reasons.

                                                    We get four or five stones from each element, as follows.

                                                    Air:
                                                    1. Generate cool, refreshing breezes.
                                                    2. Maintain perfect memory.
                                                    3. Know one additional Terrestrial spell.
                                                    4. Control the direction of the wind.
                                                    5. +1 Essence for calculations when supporting a Defining Intimacy.
                                                    Earth:
                                                    1. Minimize earthquakes and collapses in a city.
                                                    2. Protect all within 100 yards from the Wyld. The fae hate you.
                                                    3. Turn raw earth to stone or back again. Doesn’t work on buildings.
                                                    4. Disable an enemy’s weapon or armor by cramming the stone into an empty socket.
                                                    5. Use your weapon’s next Evocation.
                                                    Fire:
                                                    1. Minimize fire in the city.
                                                    2. Store 1 Willpower.
                                                    3. Know one additional fire-based Terrestrial spell.
                                                    4. Make it always summertime in a city.
                                                    5. Evocations: bonus damage on unarmed attacks; shoot fire out to short range.
                                                    Water:
                                                    1. Water in the city is always safe.
                                                    2. A spring appears for the next day.
                                                    3. The sea nearby is never stormy. (What happens if magic conflicts with this?)
                                                    4. Evocations: bonuses to escape your bonds; successfully get away from a grapple faster; use dodge to contest a grapple Control Roll.
                                                    Wood:
                                                    1. Food keeps longer in the city.
                                                    2. Negate armor Mobility penalty for jumping and climbing.
                                                    3. Fields nearby produce extra.
                                                    4. Commit an Intimacy to the stone; sense when someone else has the same Intimacy.
                                                    Solar:
                                                    1. No creature of darkness can enter a home containing a sleeping child.
                                                    2. An area out to two miles wide is filled with daylight.
                                                    3. +1 success on shape sorcery.
                                                    4. Commit 2m; gain one Evocation on an orichalcum weapon. (Is this just worse than Earth #5?)
                                                    5. Learn one Charm or Evocation requiring +1 Essence.
                                                    Abyssal:
                                                    1. Speak with the dead in the city.
                                                    2. Cause a corpse to rise as a zombie.
                                                    3. Anyone robbing from those in the city is afflicted by poltergeists. Those apparitions are not for hurting. They are for haunting, and distraction.
                                                    Lunar:

                                                    3. Animals nearby have exceptional offspring.

They don’t appeal, and even if you did have one, you’re mostly going to leave it somewhere to do its thing undisturbed. That’s not bad, but it’s a pretty big change from the focus of 2e stones – very few stones have any kind of combat potential. (If you’re playing Make the Ultimate City Guy, though, I suppose you want as many of these as you can grab – they’re definitely quality-of-life improvements.)

I’m not really sold on the examples, though. I mean, for 30 XP, I could buy Brawl to 5 and get a grappling Charm, y’know?

It’s not bad, and it doesn’t look overpowered, but it’s not really inspiring, either – there’s nothing here where I said, “YES, I want that for my next character.” Why take a hearthstone that grants a Charm, when I could just take another Charm? Why, at chargen, do I want to have a “city gains some benefit” power? These are by no means useless, and again, I’d want to cultivate them if I was city-building, but they seem more like flavor than really useful tools worth multiple merit dots.
