        Any other Quest-writers have quasi-writer's block happen, when you have a scene planned out in your head, yet can't get it down "on paper" satisfactorily?
        I've had that before. This is what I do:

        1. I get something down on paper. Anything. It may be crap, but I find it's easier to figure out what is wrong with the post when it is out of my head.
        2. I'll delay the post a bit. Most of the time, the post isn't coming together because I got other things on my mind, like work.
        3. I'll give up, post the damn thing, and move on. No post is perfect and frankly, a writer is their own worst critic. This is why I have a schedule. To tell me when it is time to stop messing around with a scene and move on to the next scene.

        I write things.

                    Downplay your relation to your Grandfather/ describe how Hill-Tribe society works

                    You blink, rather startled by the semi-positive reception such a revelation has brought about. Usually, when you bring this up, most people respond extremely negatively, usually with cries of “ savage heathen” and “ marauding bastard” being the “nicest”, leading you to outright refuse to acknowledge the issue at all. The flak you catch from your obvious ancestry is enough.

                    “Lord it…. the situation is far more complicated than what you make it to be, if I may say so without giving offense.”

                    Lord Isyen leans forward, cheek resting on her hand, “Is it now? It does not seem so. I may not be as well-versed in Hill-Tribe culture as you are, but I know enough to recognize that your grandfather has more warriors under his command than I do, and has influence spread across the entirety of the western Tribes. If he were to attack Xia outright, as opposed to the cattle-raids and border-skirmishes he limits himself to, there is not much I could do to hold him back, if I may be frank. And you are telling me that you are a direct relative of the man. What am I misunderstanding, exactly?”

                    Swallowing, you continue, “If I may address my relation to my grandfather, first?”

                    “Of course. I find the implications greatly interesting.”

                    “Firstly, the Tribes do not practice fealty and oaths of servitude like the Hundred Kingdoms do. That means that they do not have the same ideas of inheritance. Back when my grandfather was the Chief of the Nemasket, his daughter was, socially, “just” another tribeswoman, albeit the daughter of the chieftan. Myself, as his grandson, do not stand to receive any honor or glory from his position, the exact opposite as a matter of fact. If he were to die, or be removed from his post, I would not inherit the position of Warchief. The position is elected by the other Chiefs of the Confederation, and I would not be welcome to it, to put it lightly. “

                    Spreading your hands, “I mean, there is a reason I am a wandering sword, not dressed in furs and gold.”

                    “Secondly, I haven’t seen, much less talked with, my grandfather in years. I started Oathsworn training when I was nine years of age, and during that period I lived with my father on his estate. I am 21 now, so I haven’t been face-to-face with him for 12 or so years. The last time I heard from him was about 4 years ago, when he wrote me a letter.”

                    She sits up, raising an eyebrow, “Your grandfather speaks Riverspeak?”

                    Nodding, “Yes, amongst several other languages. He reads it as well.”

                    “Interesting. My sworn lords always portrayed him as an unlettered savage, to be honest. To learn otherwise makes me reconsider several preconceptions I had about him. Please continue.”

                    “Thirdly, my grandfather’s position is quite different from what it appears you view it as. While he is the Warchief of the confederation, his powers as Warchief are actually pretty limited.”

                    Now Lord Iysen looks confused. “What exactly do you mean?”

                    “As Warchief, his authority is only to lead the confederations warriors in battle, and to represent the confederation to outsiders. He doesn’t actually “make” any decisions, like matters of state or declarations of war. The decision-making power of the Tribes is instead invested in Tribal Elders, who make day-to-day decisions for the tribes and the confederation as a whole when they actually gather.”

                    You shrug, “To be honest, I have no idea where the yearly elder council meets, or what they actually “do”; “normal” tribespeople weren’t supposed to attend, and the whole thing is steeped in ritualism and mysticism. In order to do something like declare war or make peace, my grandfather would have to petition either the Nemasket elder council, for matters pertaining to my tribe, or the Confederation council, for the confederation as a whole. And, just because he is the Warchief doesn’t mean he always gets what he wants, either. I can remember plenty of times when he would come home upset with some decision the Elder council made.

                    “But the position must carry some degree of influence, correct”?

                    “Of course. He is the head Warchief of the Confederation, elected by the 11 other Chiefs of the tribes. By my understanding, his word carries great weight on the Council, just not enough to always get what he wants. He still has to wheedle, compromise and coerce the Elders into making favorable decisions.”

                    She laughs, “Politics, as always! It is a pleasant thought to think that all men are the same.” Sitting back, she picks up her coffee again. “So, what does this all have to do with the nigh-constant raids and skirmishes that my marcher-lords always complain about?”

                    Again, you shrug, “Pardon me, Lord, but I really do not know. When I left, the Dumoniapoccasset were not at war with Xia.”

                    “But these raids have been occurring since my father was Lord, how are we not at war?”

                    You think for a moment, “As far as my understanding goes, there is a difference between “war” and “raids” among the Hill-tribes. If you were at war …… trust me, you would know. There would be fewer cattle-raids and more burnt villages, if you understand the implication.”

                    “A common statement amongst the Tribes is “deeds, not blood”. Chieftans and elders are not appointed based on who they are descended from, but elected by their supporters. A warrior gains fame by engaging and overcoming enemies, and warleaders gain warriors by hosting feasts, giving gifts, and through success in battle, leading though charisma and reputation as opposed to loyalty.”

                    “With that in mind, many young men seek to gain fame and status through raiding on their own initiative, without the leadership and …. moderation of a warchief. They band together and attack others almost indiscriminately, other Tribes included. My grandfather spent more time warding off intertribal squabbles than he did fighting your lords.”

                    “To the chiefs, these bands are little more than excited boys, burning off energy in a way that prevents them from causing problems in their own villages. More to the point, because they are not under the command of a chieftain, their actions are not considered “war”, and effectively, the chieftains have very little, if any, control over their actions.”

                    “So ….. if I were to lead a retributive strike against these raiders, how would the tribes react?”

                    You cringe, “Almost certainly overwhelmingly negative. As far as they are concerned, such an attack would be entirely unwarranted, and the Tribes would band together to retaliate. Entire sections of your country would be laid to waste. I …. Cannot recommend such an action, it would be effective suicide.”

                    She nods, considering the implications. “Good thing that is what I have been telling my marcher-lords, for all the good it has done. Some of them are positively chomping at the bit to charge into the mountains.”

                    Lifting her head to look at you directly,” Finally, are you on good terms with your grandfather?”

                    You are taken aback, never truly having considered the issue before.

                    “I … I think so. I remember he was disappointed when I left to live with my father, but I never understood why. Thinking back, I get the feeling that he wanted me to stay with the Tribes and learn more about the other side of my family, but I had made up my mind at an early age that I wanted to be an Oathsworn, like my father.”

                    “If I remember correctly, I am the only current member of his family still alive. He had only one wife, and one daughter, and my mother left to live with my father at an early age, younger than I am now. He had grey hair when I was living with him, so he must be pretty old by now……”

                    The room is silent for a minute, as you realize the implications of that thought. You are startled from your introspection by Lord Isyen idly tapping her fingers on the table, herself deep in thought.

                    “Hm. If what you are telling me is true, then hypothetically, if I were to send you with a message, how would it..”

                    She gets interrupted by the wood-and-paper door suddenly sliding open, startling the diners seated around the able, who were apparently leant forward listening to your story. In strides a young man, about your age, dressed in fine silks in spite of the early-morning humidity, a fancy sword (seemingly too light in the blade, yet handle-heavy with ornamentation, a show-piece effectively) thrust through his sash.

                    He stops, offers a perfunctory bow, little more than a nod of the head, to the Lord seated at the head of the table.

                    “Please, pardon my tardiness. I had business to attend to early this morning, down at the tournament-grounds. It took longer than expected.” Realizing you are present, and looking down his nose at you, “Lord Iysen, who is this?”

                    Lord Isyen raises her hand to introduce you. “This is Kasai Harutane, an Oathsworn who has been assisting me with a very important task, of which I am incredibly grateful. Kasai, this is Haichi Matsu, the eldest son of the Lord Yue and betrothed to Mino Shiba.”

                    You rise to your feet, bowing at the waist. Even if you are lower in status, at least you were raised with manners.

                    Essentially ignoring your greeting, Matsu grabs a piece of bread, smearing it with jam and pours himself a cup of coffee. “If I may ask, Lord, what is he helping you with? If he is so proficient, perhaps he can help me as well.”

                    You shoot a quick glance to the Lord, who acknowledges it with an almost-imperceptible nod.

                    “I am assisting the Lord Xia with matters concerning security of the tournament, Master Matsu.”

                    Matsu stops, bread halfway to his mouth, “Really?” He turns to the Lord, “I thought Captain Ito and Captain Reeds were collaborating on that. Did they find anything that would affect the wedding?”

                    “Ito Aori is no longer in my employ, Haichi.”

                    He starts, surprised, “Why not?”

                    The Lords face hardens. “His performance was no longer satisfactory, that is all you need to know. Why is this so concerning? Both the tournament and the wedding will proceed as planned”.

                    He sits for a minute, then bows, embarrassed. “Forgive me. The man owes me money, and to suddenly learn that I will not get it back is …… disheartening. I forgot my place”.
                    The Lord forgives him with a wave of her hand, and her eldest daughter Mino leans up next to her betrothed.

                    “Was it the dog-fighting again, Haichi?”

                    He laughs, “No, the ponce wanted some coin to impress some tart, downby the market. I leant it to him, supporting his professions of love, which in hindsight was a bad idea. May the bastard catch some pox, that is retribution enough!”

                    The boy Aketchi giggles and Mino facepalms again, and Lord Shiba chides the man for his inappropriate language. After a moment, the awkwardness of the sudden interruption fades out, and Airi leans forward.

                    “Please, sir Harutane, if you would tell us some stories from your childhood, growing up as you did”.

                    At another nod from Lord Iysen, you begin to regale the group with tales of your childhood, some of your more memorable experiences, some of the legends and fables your grandfather used to tell you. Aketchi Shiba was most interested in the stories of hunting and weapons, while Mino and Airi are enthralled by the stories of harvest festivals, when entire tribes would come together and celebrate the ending of the year, encapsulated with the Feast of the Moon. Throughout the conversation, Haichi interjects often with various comments, usually disparaging, and you find yourself becoming more and more irritated, far more so than from the usual peasants superstitious banter. His jibes and snarking, while less outwardly offensive than most, cut more deeply from being aimed at your culture’s traditions and history as opposed to yourself.

                    “So, your people are heretics, worshipping the Anathema? Makes sense, considering their savagery”.

                    You bite your tongue, mindful of the possible repercussions of insulting such a social superior.

                    “No, not at all, Master Matsu. The Tribes do not worship the Anathema, instead they worship the Moon itself, in the aspects of the Hunter and the Mother. Hunting is an important food source to the Tribes, as there is little land suitable for agriculture in the mountains, and the Mother, as without new births, both man and animal would disappear. Without the guidance of the moon, the Tribes would not survive. “

                    “Of course, the Tribes also worship other spirits, animal-totems, the very plants they grow to harvest, etc. Most aspects of life are devotional in some metaphorical fashion, and many aspects of life can be expressed as a craft: marriage as basketry, war and struggle as smithing, and many Tribespeople sing songs of praise as they work, to thank the gods for giving them the opportunity to show their skills.”

                    You shrug, “Not that it matters in my case. I am Oathsworn, and stood my vigil overnight in an Immaculate shrine.”

                    With that, you sit back, perhaps a bit more forcefully than politeness allowed.. “My Lord, I thank you for the meal, you do me a great honor by inviting me to your table. I now ask your leave, I must go prepare myself and my equipment for the tournament.”

                    A quick burst of irritation crosses her face, but after a moment, Lord Iysen dismisses you with a nod.

                    As you walk back to your sleeping chamber, it suddenly occurs to you that you are actually pretty angry, angrier than you had ever gotten before with regards to your heritage. Perhaps it is because you have thought about your grandfather for the first time in many years, or maybe the sneering disdain of Haichi Matsu set off a nerve that the whispers of peasants didn’t. Or, more likely, you are just short-tempered from lack of sleep. Either way, you stride down the halls and stairs of Teriake Castle with purpose, and the few servants to cross your path back away from your scowl.

                    After a few minutes, and after getting turned around in the twisting internal passages of the keep (to your increasing annoyance), you make it back to the chamber where you spent the night. Upon opening the door, you find your previous clothing, neatly folded. Upon picking them up, you realize that they have been washed and ironed, tightly-creased and pleasant-smelling. At least one thing is going your way this morning.

                    Once changed, you pick up Xuansu by the stables, who has also been brushed and fed, and you are almost through the castle gate when you are stopped by a manservant, who apparently ran all the way down the 6 stories of the keep. He passes over a folded piece of paper, which when unraveled reveals a family crest and quickly-scrawled message in Riverspeak.

                    Kasai,
                    We have much to talk about still. If you will, return back here tonight, after 9-bells. Show my mark and give the password “northern pines” to the guards at the gate.
                    I

                    Crumpling the note, you jam it into your jacket and thank the man for bringing it to you so promptly.

                    Turning Xuansu down the cliffside path back to town, the potential ramifications of this entire mess bounce through your thoughts. On one hand, going straight to Lord Iysen with your findings went amazingly well, far better than you had hoped (and feared). On the other hand, it now looked like she, in turn, wanted to actually get you involved, something which you hadn’t really foreseen.

                    You exhale in a huff. Politics really aren’t your strong suit, all efforts of your father to the contrary. You really lack the ability to parse the double-speak and hidden meanings of social interactions, yet another reason why you almost preferred your time in solitude in the mountains.

                    Plus, politics is confusing, especially compared to combat, or upholding your Oaths. In a fight, you generally knew which side you were on, and understanding the messages of your Oaths was practically easy: Defend the weak, serve as an example, treat others with honesty and fairness, be mindful of honor and dignity, so on and so forth.

                    Not so with politics! In any situation, an ally could just as easily become an enemy, if certain seemingly-small criteria were not met. Constant “favors” being asked and given, to sway a decision, lies being told, emotions flaring, and all that just in managing a village and settling the petty squabbles of peasants! Now, you are almost certainly getting dragged into the affairs of kingdoms…

                    Morose and lost in your thoughts, you retrace your path back to the run-down doctors shack, where Saiko and Blue-Pines were waiting. When you bang on the door, it takes a few minutes for the woman to make her way over and answer. When she opens the door, it is readily apparent that she got about as much sleep as you did.

                    “Can I help you? You don’t look hurt… don’t tell me you’ve got the clap! It’s too early in the morning to deal with that”

                    “… What? No, listen, I was here last night, with a girl with a stab-wound and a woman. The woman had a broken nose and a face covered in bruises. I need to know where they went.”

                    “Oh, yeah, I remember. The woman said if you came back, you could find them at the hotel down by the docks, marked with the sign of a dog.”

                    With this, the doctor closes the door, leaving you standing on the curb. Various ne’er do wells are watching you with some degrees of interest, and you quickly beat feet on Xuansu. Getting mugged is the last thing you need to have happen to you.

                    It takes you about an hour to find this dog-marked hotel (stupid cross-streets), and you are not all that impressed by the exterior. The façade is crumbling at the corners, roof-tiles are cracked, and the place just exudes tackiness. You are confused, you left Blue-Pines with enough coin to rent a room at a better place than this!

                    Upon tying Xuansu to a handy pole and opening the door, you are greeted by the surly, suspicious glares of several daydrinkers, already deep into their cups at only midmorning. Ignoring their leers at your comparatively-fine clothing, you make your way over to the publican. He tells you Blue-Pines and Saiko rented a room early this morning, before the sun even came up, and gives you the room number.

                    The stairs creak as you climb them, and you half-heartedly hope they don’t collapse under you as you make your way up to the third floor, to the garret-room Blue-Pines rented. Once at the marked room, you knock on the door, only for Blue-Pines to crack open the door and peer out into the hallway. She seems (pleasantly) surprised to see you.

                    “Oh, Lord. You aren’t dead! Or in jail. I was kinda concerned.”

                    When you enter the room, you notice that Blue-Pines had her dagger drawn and ready. Slightly impressed (and amused), “What were you planning on doing with that, exactly?”.

                    “Hey, in my defense, you tromped up the stairs loud enough to make me think you were the Guard.”

                    “If I were the Guard, woman, I would kick the door in, not knock, but good instincts nonetheless. Anyway, how is Saiko doing, and what the hell are you doing in this shithole? I gave you more than enough coin to rent a room in a fancy hotel, much less one that doesn’t have lice in the sheets!”

                    She shrugs, “It’s kinda hard to rent a room in the middle of the night, when you look like two mugging victims. This was pretty much the best I could do on short notice. As for the girl, she is asleep; the doctor doped her up on opium before kicking us out.”

                    “Fair enough, but we can’t stay here. Saiko needs a decent place to recuperate, preferably not with beds reeking of piss. Come on, pack your gear, I’ll take Saiko”.

                    After a few moments, you descend the stairs with Saiko carried bridal-style(who is sleeping rather uncharacteristically quietly. Must be the drugs.), Blue-Pines following close on your heels. Once outside, you wake up Saiko, as much as you are able, and lift her up into the saddle, making sure she is seated securely, before leading both Xuansu and Blue-Pines down the street.

                    You make your way closer to the mainmarket, where you pay for much more adequate accommodations, where the rooms don’t reek of spilled booze and vomit, the hallways are swept, and, most importantly, the locks on the doors actually work. There, you leave Saiko in bed, with order for food to be brought up (and for her to be told where you went), along with the majority of your gear.

                    Blue-Pines chimes in with, “So, what is the plan, boss?”

                    “Well, first of all, I am going to get dressed in my full armor, as I have to present myself to the Master of Ceremonies for the tournament, and sign up for the events I want to participate in. After that, we are going to hang around the tournament-grounds for a while, to kill some time and to scope out the opposition.” you reply, grinning sheepishly at the last comment.

                    While Blue-Pines is helping you put on your armor, she reveals that she also purchased a quantity of medical supplies from the doctor: bandages, ointments, various medications in jars, etc. It was rather expensive, but considering how you had pretty much none of that before, you consider it money well-spent. After all, you would rather have it and not need it, than need it and not have it.

                    After locking Saiko into the room, you head out, with you cutting a fine figure mounted on Xuansu. The mid-afternoon crowds part around you, keeping a healthy distance from your warhorse, and you and Blue-Pines make good time, passing through the main gate in only a few minutes. Outside, a distance away from the city walls, sits the wooden structure of the tournament grounds, constructed only a scant few days ago by laborers contracted by Lord Iysen. However, in between you and the outbuildings is a veritable sea of tents, stalls and fenced-off paddocks.

                    The living quarters of the majority of not only the Oathsworn competitors in the tournament, but also their hangers-on, vendors, entertainers, sword-sharpeners, smiths, thieves, murderers, and plenty of prostitutes, this press of barely-ordered construction is filled with a press of humanity, all looking to make some coin, score some tail, and generally cause a ruckus. The Town Militia is hard-pressed to keep even a semblance of law-and-order, in spite of heavily-increased patrols, and in the few days since the tent-city sprang into existence, there has already been a rash of stabbings, fire outbreaks, and even a rumored outbreak of smallpox, despite no evidence being found. The gaily-colored pavilions of the richer Oathsworn warriors stands in stark contrast to the literal rivers of shit flowing between then, both horse and human, and the smells of roasting meat and sweets mingles horridly with the stench of rotting garbage and unwashed humanity. Worse still, the terrain prevented a breeze from clearing out the reek, and the miasma often condensed into a semi-permanent cloud over the “settlement”. The general consensus from the townsfolk concluded that the tournament was hopefully over as soon as possible.

                    On top of that, the completely-unorganized placement of the tents and horse-paddocks meant whatever travel took place was forced into a “main thoroughfare” of sorts, running between the gates of the town and the walls of the tournament arena. This “road”, little more than a wider path between the chaos, was choked with humanity and vendor stalls, pressed shoulder-to-shoulder.
                    Xuansu, testy at the best of times, is downright pissed from being jostled and rubbed against, and snaps out at someone that passes by too close, and Blue-Pines presses close to your saddle in order to avoid being separated by the crowd. She turns to you, gagging, and says,

                    “I always meant to ask you why you preferred spending money instead of just setting up a tent, but now I get why you wanted to stay in town.”

                    Keeping your mouth firmly closed, you nod your assent. If anything could give a glimpse of hell, this was it.

                    It takes almost an hour to press through the teeming mass of humanity, and you are almost late to the signing-in. Once there, the MC, harried to the point of frenzy, presses a pad of paper into your hands and hurries off. On the paper, there is a list of events you can participate in, with space for your name or mark next to each.

                    Keeping in mind your earlier plan, you jot down your name next to both spear duels, the mounted archery competition, the dismounted sword duel, and, almost on a whim, the mounted melee bout.

                    As Saiko so exuberantly said; the more competitions, the better the chance of winning. You don’t hold the same level of enthusiasm (after all, you, not her, could possibly lose some teeth), but she has a point regardless.

                    Just as you finish penning the final characters, a selection of horns sound, and the MC gestures wildly for you to follow him, out of the antechamber and up onto the arena floor. Leaving Blue-Pines behind to watch over Xuansu, you quickly fall in with the rest of the Oathsworn making their way through the staging-room doors, out onto the sands beneath the crowds.

                    The sudden noise is almost deafening. Asides from the horns, wildly blowing to announce the arrival of Lord Iysen and her entourage, the entire stadium is awash with screaming, shouting, and just general noise. Hawkers walk up and down the stands, selling bread, booze and other foods, audience-members bang and stomp on the stands in patterns, from excitement or to show support for a favored competitor, and tourney-staff gesture and shout in order to move you and your fellow Oathsworn into proper position, operating according to some unknown plan.

                    Once you are all in position, on a sudden signal the Lord Iysen raises her hands, asking for quiet, and the conversation and noise peters off. On another signal, you and your fellow Oathsworn raise your weapons in salute to the Lord, then drop to one knee in just about the only form of bow that a heavily-armored figure can adopt. After a few seconds of silence, the Lord speaks, thanking the community and the competitors for coming together to celebrate the wedding of her daughter, and she brings up an Immaculate Monk to recite a benediction for the competing warriors, to ask the Dragons to look on them with favor, and may the best combatant win, yadda yadda yadda. The noonday-sun is very hot, standing as you are out on the sand with no shade, and the sun beating down on you means your armor underpadding is soon soaked in sweat. You, to be honest, tone out the various speeches and benedictions, shifting from foot to foot irritated, only belatedly reacting to the various whispered orders from the tourney-staff.

                    It feels like forever, but you find yourself eventually being shepherded off the field, to the sound of a cheering crowd. Once back inside the cover of a building, you gratefully open up the buttons of your mail-hood and armored jacket, allowing heat to escape in a blessed rush. You make your way to Blue-Pines, and the two of you work your way out to a stairwell and up to the stands.
                    Initially, the press of the crowd prevents you from getting much of a view, but a few moments of elbowing and the sight of your sword and armor quickly clears a path for you to sit and watch the first few events. After buying some snacks from a passing vendor, you settle down to watch the common archery bout.

                    Taking the form of a simple elimination bracket, the contest was pretty straightforward: 10 arrows from a longbow, and the lowest scorer each round was eliminated. There were around 80 competitors at the start, so working down to a final champion took quite some time. The winner, a young peasant from the northern hills, is awarded with a fat purse of silver and a great double-convexed bow, made from ironwood from Halta.

                    In between the various bouts, while targets were moved and arrows were retrieved, performers where brought onto the stage; puppeteers, tumblers, fire-breathers (who interest you greatly. How do they avoid burning their throats?), sword-eaters and snake-charmers, all to entertain the crowd.

                    By comparison, the second “common” competition, the melee bout, is over in only a few minutes. About 60 challengers, armed with staves and clubs, rush each other en masse, some fighting as individuals while others band together to fight off other groups, only to turn on each other as the numbers of targets dwindle.

                    In seemingly no time at all, the bout is reduced to a single combatant, who raises his bloodstained club in victory and accepts his prize (another sum of silver and a quality backsword). All around the arena floor, there are great bloodstains, unconscious or dazed challengers, knocked-out teeth, and other assorted detritus from the scrum.

                    Leaning over to Blue-Pines, who watches the bloodshed with fascination, you quip, “Now you can tell why I was leery about competing in a melee!”

                    “Damn, you weren’t kidding when you said it was dangerous! I think one guy down there lost an eye!”

                    The rest of the “noble” competitions will start tomorrow, so you and Blue-Pines go to make your way back to Xuansu, stabled underneath the stands with the rest of the Oathsworn horses. Getting caught up with the press of humanity slowly making its way back towards the city gates, the two of you stick together as best you can. With all the people leaving the tournament grounds, the overcrowding is even worse than it was earlier, if that is possible, even if members of the city militia are present and directing traffic, and you can tell that Xuansu is starting to get stressed again.
                    In order to avoid any possible bites or kicks, you direct Xuansu off to the side of the road, in between two tents. Once you are back away from the path aways, you dismount Xuansu and try to calm him down some, loosening the straps of his saddle and bridle and stroking his mane. After a few minutes, the horse relaxes, and you are about to start tightening the straps up again and get underway when you hear a sound that sends a sudden chill down your spine.

                    The sound of a blade being removed from its sheath.

                    The whisper of the steel being pulled from the wooden scabbard is faint, barely noticeable over the murmur of the crowd only a few dozen feet away, but to your practiced ears it sounds as loud as a thunderclap. You whirl about, half-drawing your sword, to Blue-Pines confusion, to see at least 10 or so assailants coming your way, armed with various blades and clubs. None of them are wearing armor, but they all are wearing tied lengths of cloth wrapped about their faces, covering all but their eyes. Silently, they move to surround you and Blue-Pines, who by now has recognized the situation and has readied her spear.

                    In spite of their poor equipment, the combatants move as professionals, covering each other’s flanks (as much as ones flank could be exposed tight in between two tents), and advancing in unison. In but a few moments, they will be upon your group.

                    You:
                    1. Fight! You, and Blue-Pines, are wearing armor (you in mail, even!), and the tents arguably limit the directions you can get attacked from, meaning the effects of their numbers are limited.
                    2. Run! 10 assassins are 10 assassins, combat ability or not……
                    3. Call the Militia! If you can get some militiamen over here, the odds will be more in your favor.

                    OOC:
                    1. Tournament
                    I read a book a while back, about large gatherings of people in the European Middle Ages, specifically about how a French army was mustered, and how the protagonist had to camp literally 20 or so miles away from the town the muster was taking place in in order to avoid the stench of human/horse shit, from all the men and animals being gathered.

                    20. Goddamn. Miles. Imagine what it was like for the poor townsfolk?

                    2) Cleanliness

                    I have always imagined Creation as an intrinsically “post-apocalyptic” society, one that was previously broken by disease, and I almost always portray disease as an ever-present and almost-all-encapsulating fear of the surviving societies. Rumors of disease are taken very seriously in my games.

                    This is because, of course, disease was taken pretty seriously in real life. Contrary to popular opinion, the European “middle ages” were actually not nearly as dirty as people make it out to be. In many places, Roman bathing culture remained intact for several centuries, and the decline of bathing and public health wasn’t so much a decline in the desire for cleanliness, but for economic and “practicality” reasons.

                    In many cases, Roman public baths were heated with wood. After the (long and slow) collapse of Rome, urban populations migrated to the countryside in order to take up farming so they, well, didn’t starve. In order to grow enough food, they needed to fell forests, removing the supply of wood available for heating bathwater. Plus, pre-industrial agriculture is hard. At the end of a long-ass day of digging, plowing, raking and planting, are you really gonna want to haul around a couple heavy buckets of water, build a fire, wait for the water to heat up, ,and so on and so forth?
                    I don’t think so.

                    However, that doesn’t mean people didn’t bathe, or otherwise clean themselves. Anglo-Saxons, for example, tried to bathe at least once a week, and wash their clothing regularly (with soap, even!). The Norse did so even more frequently, having more access to ample wood supplies, as well as geothermally-heated water.

                    There was a documentary I watched a while back, that stated that soap was plentiful enough, and easy enough to make, that even peasants could wash their clothes pretty cheaply. I can’t find it, but I’ll look for it.

                    So, while I am basing the Hundred Kingdoms largely off feudal Japan, “most” people in “my” Creation are not going to be stumbling, dirt-covered ham-and-cheese-smelling morons, even “tribal peoples” away from centers of civilization. If not because they just like being clean, then because of a social terror of disease.

                    Now, keep in mind that my education is in Biology, not history, and I learned this largely through internet-research and personal interest. I can, as always, be wrong.
                    1. Japanese sword/weapon making
                    Can I just bring up the topic that, as someone largely immersed in Migration-Era weapons armor, and the means of production thereof, that Japanese weaponmaking is …. Well, kind of weird?

                    I mean …… using tanged weapons just seem like a Bronze-Age thing to me. Sockets, in my opinion, are much more “reliable” as you don’t have to compromise the integrity of the handle in order to fix the blade. With a tang, you have to drill out the center of the handle, then some holes for the pins. All of that weakens the structure of the handle and makes it more likely for it to break.

                    I mean, obviously it worked, but why did the Japanese go from using sockets (Hoko Yari) to using tangs (Su Yari), or didn’t use pommels on swords or knives? Most of their older weaponry was pretty much straight copies of the stuff from China, which used pommels and sockets. Was it in effort to use less metal?

                    His spear is a Su Yari
                    His bow is, uh, like a traditional Chinese composite bow, being equal in both limbs
                    Saiko’s glaive is a Naginata
