            Okay, lengthy break, get a move on.

            Ah, so the illustration was meant to depict a scene in this Paeon Prefecture. Hmm, it’s a shorter prefecture than has been usual for these full write-ups, but it strikes me as a bit deeper than the small sidebar entries. It’s not the first instance we’ve gotten of a more arcadian prefecture tucked into the seams of the vast Blessed Isle, but I think it’s clever to utilise its idealised qualities in the manner of an especially pleasant and easygoing retreat for members of the Scarlet Dynasty; in addition to being a general setting for some stories that are more low-key and less intense, used for a bit of socializing between the larger than life Exalted personalities, I’m seeing compelling plot hooks based around the possibility of elders dangling a bit of downtime there for player characters, or them being assigned to impress upon one that is being derelict in duties that they need to return. Come to think of it, it also looks attractive as a place to let players actually take a break that gets run in play following any particularly draining event in the game. I like how it’s home to a few tall tales around a historical Dynast, both as a point of characterisation for the prefecture and letting the Scarlet Dynasty occasionally be the focus of more lighthearted attention, and the idea of people taking small boats or swim to visit one another seems like a cute way to bring characters into scenes.

            Ah, I had not made the connection before between the name on the map and the reference in Arms of the Chosen to the Plains of Rusted Iron. Reading about it reminds me of when I first read about the Realm Defence Grid, one of the earliest things I took from the Exalted wiki back in the day; among the descriptions of its manifestations, the image of clouds of iron thorns falling upon the Fair Folk stood out the most to me. It was one of the first things that drew real intrigue from me for the setting, its approach to magic and its ideas for ancient superweapons, so I find it fitting to make that quality the basis of the weapon having scarred the landscape of the Blessed Isle. In its own right, it’s an idea that I think makes for a more vivid impression of the impact of using it than descriptions of earthquakes in the distant past, and I’m also liking how that serves as a basis for providing a spot in which some fae stragglers might persist in hiding at the heart of the Realm. Even if no actual raksha are to be found there, there’s a good foundation for this kind of Wild West narrative of the frontier town treating the mysterious stranger with fear and distrust when it’s crossed over with superstition and folklore of worrying that the person may be something actually otherworldly.

            All right, finally gotten to a prefecture on one of those western peninsulas. Falling Rain Prefecture is one that I’m curiously processing largely in terms of reference points from other media: the image of a vast wetlands that attracts a disproportionate amount of precipitation and electrical storms enclosed in highlands puts me in mind of Zora country in The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, while the descriptions of the inhabitants in their stilt villages, their cuisine and the manner in which they’re looked down upon by neighbours recalls the portrayal of Quadling Country in Wicked to me. Mind, I think wetlands on the whole have a distinct quality to be compelling; the distinct challenges they provide for people to build and plant and move, the abundance of resources they can still provide to those ready to harness them (and complementarily, the variety of interesting neighbouring fauna and flora it entails), a landscape that can undulate while still being very open, and the typical rustic colour scheme (even if this one suggests a nice bit of variety in the form of decorative lotus flowers). Although it might just be that they’re such a biome with which I’ll have more personal familiarity, given how much of Ireland consists of bog land, so they stand out to me when featured.

            But there’s more to it than the prosaic qualities, although the capacity to preserve sunken ruins is intertwined with that; part of their featuring in my background would be references to how valuable Ireland’s wetlands have been for archaeological purposes (so many cultural treasures have been drawn intact from out of the peat). The function of ancient engines for drainage purposes is straightforward enough (and the juxtaposition between mythic machinery and something as basic as water pipes has been used to good effect in the game before), and I’m interested in the premise that having interacted with such has given some of the locals strange mystical experiences (I’d propose that as a result of malfunctioning magic more than emerging from anything intentional). I like the notion that living in proximity to such things with such effects forms the basis for explanations for mental disorders. While the Cloud Dragon Oath, well, first off there might as well be a little call out to the Water Margin novel in the Realm somewhere. I like to imagine that they would be looked on as something more unnerving to the Dynasty; bandits are nothing exceptional, but a group of openly defiant Dragon Blooded on their own threshold is a challenge and a threat to the familiar order of things. Makes me wonder if the western prefectures might carry a bit of an “edge of the world” vibe in their minds. I don’t think there’s anything else to say on the Daoshin Peninsula.

            Wading Crane Rookery, hmm. It kind of looks like a territory that has little going for it by design, which does convey the image of that kind of minor coastal fishing territory, but doesn’t exactly leave a whole lot to talk about. Still, I like it being used as an opportunity to present people engaged in that practice of decorating their water buffalo beasts of burden with colourful ribbons, something I always liked as an example of people adapting to unaccommodating environments to spruce up their lives a bit. I also like the sense of transition in environments from the prior prefecture; that one had a combination of environment and potential support populace to accommodate something like a prominent outlaw band, where this one seems more focused on being so far in the Space Boonies that it functions as a more general refuge for outcasts and people of a more frontier sensibility. It is what I anticipated the western Blessed Isle being like, and I like the presentation of it becoming increasingly out of the way like this.

            Actually, I see my assessment of it having little going as not giving it enough credit, because it’s also deepening the picture of areas that preserve ancient mythical ruins. First off, the image of the water being just shallow enough for those ruins to be visible to the people going around in their little boats is striking, and I think would make a good backdrop or object of focus for stories set here; the idea of an ancient lighthouse having toppled into the water and still being visibly lit from beneath is especially compelling and ominous. And between the two prefectures I’m getting into this underlying narrative of these parts of the Blessed Isle, currently so impoverished and backwater, were once home to some aggressive powers of the First Age. Makes me think of the empire that the ancient Scandinavians made of the smaller, more northerly wind-blasted islands of this Celtic archipelago. It has me wondering, would the image of it all gradually be sinking away be indicative of a land that was once more stable, but is breaking down under the current conditions of the world (kind of a microcosm for the encroach of the Western Wyld), or did the people of that era build in defiance of the other kings saying it was daft to build on a swamp, and the removal of their maintenance routines ultimately proving the point?

            In any case, the combination of those elements do make this feel like an appropriate retreat for Berit.

            Let’s see, Myion Prefecture… oh that’s right, I keep forgetting that House Cathak is based down here. I suppose it makes sense that if there’s going to be any choice land on the peninsula, it’s going to be reserved for the home of a Great House. Indeed, What Fire Has Wrought already said that they live in an area of former Unconquered Sun worship repurposed for the Immaculate faith, so it seems to me that’s now being placed in the context of a background developed for other local prefectures; of a last surviving remnant of ancient construction being preserved through investment by local magnates to reap the value they can derive from it. One could even read an implication that part of why this prefecture is more arable is specifically because of the dedications to the Unconquered Sun. More overtly, I’m intrigued by the implications of the idea that when stray obelisks to that god are uncovered, the demolition from the Order is undertaken in a very respectful manner; it makes me think that nobody would have the nerve to overtly undermine his worship, but given the general beliefs of the Philosophy I could see it being rationalized in terms of the Sun being far too holy for mortals to dare worship, and it’s best to remove any temptations to do so. Hmm, that would be an interesting take on one of the things for them to decry the Realm Before over; the conceit and sacrilege of tempting the morals towards improper worship that profanes such a mighty god. Conversely, I’ve been interested in old writer statements about some of the personas that people worship the Sun under, so I like seeing something like a nod to that in the form of people syncretising some of his image and mythos into Immaculate orthodoxy to create this very unique brand of heresy. Particularly the way they’ve adopted a belief that Terrestrial Exaltation can be compelled as a reward for proper obeisance.
            What about the city itself… takes the status of being a former centre of Unconquered Sun worship with having that combination of military installations, religious buildings and sources for entertainment and athleticism, makes sense. I feel as though some Solar with good past life memories could be really insulting to Cathak’s piety by asserting that their maintaining the aesthetic continuity of the place means that they’re just following in the footsteps of Anathema. I’m motivated to properly picture this idea they’re putting forward that the local architectural styles have evolved over time, but manage to smoothly flow together. Although that gets overshadowed a bit by the gigantic immortal ash tree on one of the two major hills. I think that might be one of the most stark features that has been placed in the Realm, and it makes me want to extrapolate what the mythological significance the people might give to that particular tree and being shaded by its canopy (I also admire their restraint in not making it a cherry blossom ).

            I find the description of it as a major port to be fairly standard, but it does convey to me a sense of being one with a more eclectic range of destinations than most others; knowing that one of the Cathak satrapies is Fajad far to the north helps with that, although a major contributor is the emphasis placed on how their position provides a major focus for trafficking to other parts of the Realm. Mind, between the description of “stranger scents” mixed in with the spices, recollection of some of Fajad’s exports and what was said in the previous prefecture about how the ancient inhabitants would sail back with sorcerous treasures, I’m inferring that Myion might be one of the premier spots to import exotic prodigies. That certainly complements the image given of some of the local oddities; I’m very fond of the description of the Singing Tides Temple and how it uses fluted passages to generate musical tones from the rising and falling water, with the emphasis to how devotees of Daana’d meditate on the improvisational nature carrying a nice idea of some of the spiritual connotations of water. While the idea that it’s home to various strange phenomena is both a fun contrast with the overt piety of its rulers and a possible source of tension or focus for their investigations and opposition, in addition to the face value of it motivating cabals of occult investigators.

            I think this may be the most overt and detailed description given yet of the assets a place might rely on in the event of war. I’m thinking about the manner in which the city has been built to contain multiple separate forts, a premise that could make for stories of uniquely layered and grinding sieges. I find this being their home base to effectively build upon Cathak’s stated policy in the succession crisis, that they feel secure to sit back and play kingmaker in the knowledge that they can fall back upon a hardened defensive point from which they’ll also have a vantage point on the Isle’s sea lanes. I like the legion currently assigned to patrol having a name referencing the most iconic local feature, and the way it describes Cainan as being a leader that enemies fear bringing to the field when he’s otherwise taking a bit of a backseat. Ah, and I see that with Fifteen-League Redoubt they seem to be bringing attention to the fact that this territory has greater agricultural productivity than the surroundings as a result of geomancy. I think the description of the fort’s structure is detailed enough to give some ideas for challenges without going overboard, and I find the strategic policy of destroying the manse if necessary to scorch the earth for conquerors or besiegers is characteristic of Cainan’s underlying objectives against his overt charisma. I’m also amused by the idea that they need to give a public justification for fortifying the manse that is more palatable, although still completely plausible (I mean, for one thing, there are friggin’ Lunars out there who would definitely make a play for a manse that might sabotage a breadbasket if they thought it was vulnerable).

            Aaaand it’s straight from them into another Great House base with Peleps’ Voice-of-the-Tides prefecture. Two things right off the bat; one is that I like the name of this one for its implications of nautical music similar to the temple from a few paragraphs ago, and the other is that these Houses being practically neighbours compounds some of the tensions referenced in What Fire Has Wrought concerning the possibilities of alliance or rivalry with Cathak.

            Now, I’m all for the idea that this prefecture (and looking ahead a little bit, apparently the more northern peninsula as a whole) is home to a distinctive ethnic mix as a result of being a nexus for sailors and immigrants out of the otherwise more isolated West. And it does make sense that such a mix would make it another region that would foment some heterodoxy, but I must admit that yet another entry of such a prefecture being under the authority of one of the more stoic, conservative Houses is a tad repetitive. They’re not bad examples individually, but when it’s a well that it keeps going back to it does feel a tad on the nose. Even if I was to ascribe it a subtext of a deliberate policy of assigning such Houses to prefectures that they would be most invested in keeping in check (which could also function as a distraction for their considerable power), that still amounts to a bit of a Thermian argument. I don’t think it makes the subject matter bad, it’s just my honest reaction to the pattern. I’m also slightly feeling as though the descriptions of the multiculturalism making it a hotbed of conspiracy and heresy is a bit contradictory of the following paragraph saying that respect for House Peleps prevents rebellion, unless the idea is supposed to be that there’s a natural friction between the inhabitants and the Dynasts that might be expected to erupt into rebellion were it not for the fact that the character of the reigning Exalted is admirable even when disagreed with.

            I do like how the Rivanoa people provide the place with a bit of visual distinction that seems exotic even among the variety of the Blessed Isle’s inhabitants, and the way that their cultural imports serve as a means of updating the House’s relationship with the sailing goddess Rudhira. Particularly for reconciling such a thing with a House that is now rendered more concerned with Immaculate orthodoxy, even while maintaining the good will of maritime spirits would be a high priority for them. I will say that the irony of the House most concerned with standards of law enforcement being located in a prefecture that is seeing an increase in crime and banditry works much better, and more of their own thing, especially for how it is logically another function of their losing the revenue stream of the Merchant Fleet. Indeed, the idea that a lot of Peleps would be frustrated by the decline of their ancestral seat and the humiliation of needing to sometimes keep the villains at bay with payoffs seems like another grievance that many would want to make House V’neef pay for (with the added layer that the process of shifting resources west to make V’neef pay only renders Voice-of-the-Tides ever more vulnerable).

            Just a moment to acknowledge the sidebar on the Silk-and-Pearl Peninsula and how it stands in stark contrast with the prior one for more favourable climate leading to more general prosperity. Between its waters being home to rare treasures, the variety of people brought in by seeking refuge from the Contagion (and maybe any more recent disasters that might crop up in that Direction) and being a major importer of goods from the new trade routes, it strikes me as an area primed to allow a game to feature a good deal of that Western flavour and aesthetic without needing to commit a game to travelling off to such an out of the way section of the map.

            Okay, the Isle of Wrack, which I recall as the headquarters of the Admiralty. I think it’s introduced well with setting the scene of the craggy Daana’d’s Hand and a bit of the local history of the place having functioned as a naval fortress (albeit evidently one that has been repeatedly stormed). I think the Admiralty Board might be one of the more sinister authorities of the Realm, so I find it fitting that they possess such an imposing home base of towers dotted around a broken cliff face. I think such residences in fiction tend to imply isolation if not active rejection of interaction, so I think it’s interesting to make that the setting of so many visitors that would want the ear of the commanders of the Imperial Navy. That’s another nice example of how the reputation of the House is currently hobbling it, that showing off to and entertaining such guests was a major expense that they now need to carefully navigate being unable to manage while petitioners keep flowing in. I had not considered that angle on them being the basis for an international standard of the qualities of the Realm, but I find it fitting to the mutability of Water that they can do so by alternately sailing out to distant lands and pontificating for the Realm by example of their grand adventurers, and having lots of foreign officials drawn in by concerns of realpolitik who can then be bombarded with instances of Realm wealth, culture and entertainment provided as a result of their worldwide reach. I can imagine some prince of a satrapy or its neighbour who has to visit the Admiralty for practical reasons, gets feted with treats from across Creation, and comes away with a notion that the imperialism might be worth it for drawing together such far-flung valuables.
            Hmm, alternative burial practices were described in What Fire Has Wrought, but this being the first time any such has been noted suggests to me that Peleps is supposed to come across as a House in which it’s particularly popular to lay one’s bones to rest alongside the founder at the sea’s floor. It’s a bit morbid, but not unfitting to how the nature of the House might be informed by its bleak heartland. I also think the idea of needing to descend into the water to visit the founder’s tomb would make for an especially compelling experience of paying such respects (in play or flashback), in and out of character. As for the rest of the island, I think it does a good job of presenting the key functions of serving the Peleps residents and their guests and seeing to the upkeep of the naval ships and their crews are enough to create a thriving and varied economic base; it reminds me a lot of things I read in history classes about the rise of Belfast. The point about how there are a variety of establishments to cater to aristocratic visitors to Peleps stands out a lot, and makes me wonder if such occurrences are comparatively uncommon but lucrative enough to be relied upon for bringing yearly accounts into the black, or if it denotes that such visitors are frequent enough to be an eatery’s bread and butter, even if they still have servants and sailors to provide service to in between. The former possibility is making me picture an inn or teahouse that keeps the top-quality fare in small, heavily secured cabinets to be broken out on the occasion of some wealthy dignitary turning up; come to think of it, it feels like such a practice would be found across the Blessed Isle, keeping a choice reserve handy for the unlikely event that a Dynast stops off.

            And there’s a neat bit of payoff to the military chapter describing the manner in which legionary soldiers and naval sailors hold each other in contempt with the idea that now that Peleps finds itself with several legions, keeping them bivouacked at the heart of their naval operations is leading to a rise in tensions. It’s not described in What Fire Has Wrought, but I would find it to be a key aspect to assessing their military disposition; that their forces might not only be less overwhelming than they appear on paper because there’s the possibility of some key divides existing within them, but that the officers may be unaware of this fact and caught off guard should they try to wield their military in the field and find that discipline and command structure falls apart.
            Hmm, perhaps I jumped the gun a bit and Bittern is more of the analogue to Belfast in terms of actual ship manufacturing, with Wrack a bit more focused on secondary services. That’s giving a nice reference to somewhere being a major importer of wood owing to local depletion that I’ve been looking for, and it adds a bit more on to that idea that this can be a transplanted locale for Western features with that very detailed list of the trade goods that they’re bringing in. Huh, I don’t recall the connection between the Realm systemizing networks to that Direction and Peleps needing to make up for revenue lost from the Merchant Fleet being laid out like this before, and I like it adding a greater sense of interconnectedness to the Realm’s unfolding history as well as further detail for the House being shifted towards the West. It also gives the place a sense of ongoing economic transformation that I like to see (especially with the shift away from the idea that the Houses are systematically taking control of the peasantry); the matter of old houses and shops being demolished for processing and storage space for the new products kind of puts me in mind of an urban version of merchants and nobles transforming crop and common land into sheep pasture, a feature of Medieval English history that I’ve always found compelling as an example of predatory rich people. It’s certainly not being discrete with some of those points when it’s depicting the city as having a hill dedicated to the homes of the elite overlooking the common people who are getting less than they ought from the new revenues. This governor Peleps Arida also sounds like a real piece of work, and a bit of that old school Peleps nastiness helping to grease the wheels of their revived fortunes contrasts especially nicely against their usual reputation for being upstanding. I’d say one could make a good scene out of her being confronted or pushed out by fellows and having a nice rant about how they needed to bend the rules for the greater good of the House and nobody cared much when they were benefiting off of it (and maybe there would be more than a few who snickered at her impounding V’neef ships to ruin their cargos). Heh, perhaps it’s fortuitous that I was delayed reading this prefecture until now; the point about how the House is flying under the radar because the new wealth is being invested rather than spent on indulgences hits more after having recently read descriptions of the contrast between Spanish spending of American silver (that ultimately ruined them) and the actions of the Central Europeans that they bought things from. It also plays off of the references across the two books to how Peleps actually did previously spend extravagantly to show off; all of the details together are effectively characterizing it as a House that is undergoing a rather dramatic transformation, and that feels quite fitting to the one sanctioned Water House. Aaaaand their increased association with corruption and criminality drawing greater magistrate attention further ties into their conflict with V’neef by way of that House setting itself up as a refuge for the magistrates. Beautiful.

            What’s here at the end then… ah, a scary sewer city, that’s delightful. I’m fond of this opening line about treasures draining beneath the earth, and sinkholes cover a couple of bases; a continuation of that trend I observed earlier (wait, was that actually in this post? Jeez this has been delayed for ages) of Old Realm constructions being sucked down in this quadrant of the Isle, while being an instance of the consequences of lost magic and engineering that is both refreshingly prosaic and invokes one of the most terrifying and destructive phenomena that can afflict a city even up until this day (I’ve seen too many clickbait thumbnails at the edge of my screen!... It’s possible that my information is wildly inaccurate). In any case, I like the image it gives of the city having a literal seedy underbelly that reads to me as a kind of subterranean Venice, right down to being able to get a stake in the spice trade in addition to the more overt crimes like the blood sports and freaky magic. It’s a pity that the attentions of House Peleps have shifted out to sea, because the practice of lone warriors for justice descending into the depths for investigations and clearing out problem spots is pretty neat, although a relief period works as a set up for somebody who is compelled to head down and try to do so encountering gangsters who distantly remember that terror but have gotten pretty confident in the meantime. And damn if the Blue Chimney isn’t one of the most intriguing things out of barely getting a mention.

                    All right, I’m going to push through the last four prefectures plus change of this chapter in a single post, I don’t care how long it is.

                    Starting to venture back north now with Ayreon prefecture. It’s not the first one to be described as being home to dairies, but wondering about whether the Wan people are similar to various Asian groups in having lower lactose tolerance and recalling how Iron Siaka comes from somewhere around these parts got me thinking about a demographic history of the Isle in which a fair number of immigrants came from the northern and southern Threshold, and over time drifted west in pursuit of work as that quadrant became increasingly developed, but that’s all a bit tangential. Hmm, and perhaps not entirely fitting to this prefecture when, dairy land and mines aside, it’s supposed to be sparsely populated; offhand I can’t recall if there have been other ones that have been described as such, but I’m liking the way that this one comes across as not being strictly uninhabitable, just one where they might not have gotten around to clearing land yet, and apparently have reservations against doing so with lots of primeval surviving forest in which ominous supernatural oddities can be found or imagined. Since that has them sticking more to the coast, I like how we’re given a brief overview of the fishing practices employed by the people here, particularly when they’d contrast with a lot of the rest of the Isle in having lots of open ocean to venture into. It makes sense that this would result in people with a more superstitious bent, another little microcosm of Western distinctions, and while the Immaculates are described as keeping a careful eye out for local heresies the practice of blessing fishing fleets also seems like a little bit of heterodoxy (heteropraxy? Is that a word? It seems like it should be a word) necessary for putting the people’s minds at ease. I imagine that this can feel like a very isolated part of the world, with the sea stretching out on one side and old growth woods closing in on the other.

                    Eye of Creation, there’s a name that has stood out on maps of the Blessed Isle for ages. Hmm {checks back on write-ups from previous Editions}, interesting. Anyway, it’s a name that is finally given a bit more weight with arising from the placement of a Shogunate lighthouse, and I’m glad to see that standing as an example of a patrician family having made its name and its niche by rebuilding and seeing to the upkeep of something old and useful; it’s not a thing that I think should show up much, but it’s an idea for what the odd patrician family is dedicated to that I like (I thought it was a suitable narrative back when I thought more of the Realm having a bit more magitech, whole families invested with proprietary knowledge on warstrider maintenance and the like). I take it that the growth of the city from minor fishing boat refuge to full industrious municipality is supposed to have emerged directly from the restoration making the area a much more useful berthing spot, which is a nice thread of history. It’s funny, the prefecture being presented as so far out of the way would mean that it would still work with the First Edition idea of being a place that is being governed far outside of Realm norms with domination by local toughs and an official amassing a small private army; still, I have a preference for it housing a bit more local competition for people to play off of, since I’d still find that to be something that encourages more invested stories than just “deal with the Talon League and move on”.

                    Huh, it appears that I spoke a bit too soon back at Chanos prefecture when I felt it could be a setting for an Inns…mouth kind of community, these ones have actually pulled something strange and compelling out of the sea, at Calibration no less. Mind, I shouldn’t run too much with that first impression (and any visions it might conjure of Mother Kos from Bloodborne); for one thing there’s the fact that it being described as being sealed in a vat and hidden suggests to me something a good deal smaller than I first pictured, and there doesn’t actually seem to be anything sinister about the creature at all. On the contrary, it rather appears to be a victim, and while the whole Lovecraft impression made me first feel that was a bit of a subversion of expectations, I’m suddenly remembering coastal folklore in which the idea of pulling something innocent and beautiful out of the water and keeping it imprisoned (not necessarily out of malice) being fairly common; I see hints of the Irish selkie in it, for one thing. So I’ll say instead that I like having something that serves as both such a reference and a bit more of a benign encounter with the supernatural, with the neat edge of being another situation inadvertently brought about by propaganda about and legitimate reputation of the Lunar Exalted. And hey, the idea that children around the city are noticing its calls sets up a bit of a different style of story in the game.

                    Let’s see, core conceit of the town of Werck remains as it ever was, mining commune where the governor balances managing permits to ensure the veins aren’t tapped out and investing in growth of local agriculture. It gives a bit more for that conceit to do in the idea of tension arising from people concerned that the permit selection process is not handled honestly, but that’s a rather hard turn into strangeness by adding a colony of exiled Mountain Folk looking to team up with dissatisfied miners. That sounds like quite the wrench in the works, that a bunch of backwater miners might find themselves equipped with powerful occult weapons because of an unlikely alliance with some Jadeborn, the kind of thing that feels like it has a lot of potential to escalate far beyond what anybody involved would have expected or wanted; something like them using weapons that packed quite a bit more punch then they expected, the imperial authorities look to come down hard on such a gross violation, and suddenly the townspeople find themselves at the core of a rebellion for the sake of their very survival. I’m enthused by the idea that something so out of the way could so dramatically go off the rails.

                    And I was wondering if they were going to bring back the golden lion spirit in league with governor Tower Stone or just leave that as a called back to in the prefecture’s reputation for heresy; Feverview Valley seems to be indeed bringing it back, but with a twist. I’m gonna say that this is a change for the better, a necessary escalation; I found the idea as presented in other Editions interesting, but it didn’t quite have enough going for it. Being just a hiding god made it a bit unexceptional (a bit of illicit sacrifice was still probably something to be expected in numerous parts of the Realm), it had an eclectic mix of features that didn’t exactly give the strongest sense of identity compared to most depictions of gods in this setting, and when the sacrifices are of something so insignificant and intruders are turned away unharmed… who cares? Like, I can get in-character somebody around that area caring (which the First Edition write-up might have given more teeth by suggesting that the pious community that was placed nearby might catch wind of the practice and some conflict might arise, but that wasn’t how it was written), but I think it lacked a bit of punch for a reader when it was otherwise something being laid out specifically. Honestly, I always found the most interesting reference to that narrative to be in the Second Edition chapter comic where the lion calls Stone out to show him the magistrate that it took captive for having gotten too close and suggesting that she’d be a grand sacrifice, at least that had a bit of bite to it (even if the actual setting write-ups barely makes the situation a footnote). Yeah, I think these changes make the idea stand out a bit more. I wonder if the Lloroch should still be vaguely leonine…

                    Okay, last sidebar of places to come from. Off to a good start with Berm-and-Tiger Hollow; that’s some good use of House Nellens’ significantly reduced age to provide one of the original outcastes who experienced the dramatic uplifting in his autumn years. For one thing, it adds a nice extra dimension to the margins of an already invigorated House, that in the background of the one’s alternately scheming for survival and further social advancement are elders who already rose higher than they could have ever dreamed and are dealing with wherever it placed them. I wouldn’t quite call Nellens Rellusa somebody that has fallen prey to nouveau riche indulgences, since I would presume he’s had a couple of centuries of managing himself better, but I can imagine him being among the type perceived as being so in the House by its rivals, and contributing to their low stature; it would be one thing for Dynasts to have to share the table with those who started out as literal peasants, quite another for those homines novi being judged as lacking the proper dignity and restraint. That’s how I’m picturing a lot of more established and conservative Dynasts seeing his menagerie. The menagerie itself is very much an elaborate crossover of the greatest indulgences of the excessively wealthy (or those with decent credit and eager creditors) and some real lurid pulp fantasy extremes; I particularly like the idea that exotic slaves are taught to provide flowery tales of their homelands for visitors, and that the whole thing is not above embellishing to make it more compelling to jaded Dynast tastes (with a touch of dehumanising bodily violations). I also appreciate it not shying away from the point that a private zoo put together to entertain guests by a patron of struggling finances would place a lot priority on adequate animal care; it’s a very curious source one might call upon for some unusual assassination tool (or perhaps a general familiar). And if Dynast children being concerned for their inheritance is necessarily rare, I do like there being at least one example given of it and conflict arising from the feeling that it’s being spent away. Given his background, I wonder if it makes more sense for those children to be what few Exalted Rellens could bring out that could have survived the decades, or if they’re mostly elderly mortals who have begrudged their father’s Dragon-given longevity holding out on them for so long. Anyway, some very good stuff here.

                    The Sideshores, there’s another one that stands out a bit on the map even if it’s apparently not giving the full picture of how many landmasses it consists of. I like how the development of this one is less of an evolution and more like a series of ventures for an area that would seem to warrant a function until they hit upon one that worked. I’m wondering if it having a boreal environment means that its value as a summer resort is in being somewhere with scenic snow views or if the season… no, no I imagine that most of the ice will melt at that time and it has the attraction of picturesque hill and forest in addition to privacy and ideal boating conditions. It also sounds fun to give the Blessed Isle its own small zone for the equivalent of assignments to Antarctica, although the chance to work with sky-mantis towers and contribute to shielding the northern Isle from bad storms might come with a measure of prestige. And the prospect of it serving as a staging ground for naval attacks and being fiercely contested feels like it could have a strong juxtaposition with its more casual application, especially for a Dragon Blooded game that takes place before and after a civil war begins.

                    And Silent Thunder Prefecture finally gives an instance of an Iselsi territory that was put through the wringer in the course of tearing the House apart. That’s a particularly nasty sounding retaliation as well, taxing the locals in a manner that would drive them to push all the fields to their limit until overall productivity was sapped; it makes me wonder if the peasants are being targeted due to having provided soldiers for Iselsi, or if it’s merely an example of the working classes being part of the basis for aristocratic wealth means that this method of attacking their finances comes with a lot of collateral damage. Immaculate monks aiding rebellions is nothing new, and given the imperial policy towards Iselsi I’d wonder if this one was particularly coordinated to provide the pretence for ending the campaign, but I like the impression that this one was viewed as particularly righteous by some of the monks, and that leaving a secret legacy of martial arts traditions is especially neat, feels like the strongest bit of wuxia influence in the Realm yet. Heh, makes me think that it would be the perfect staging ground for a scenario akin to Kung Fu Hustle, somebody turning up expecting to push the impoverished residents around only to be surprised by a remarkable degree of combat skill.

                    Huh, just comparing maps across Editions and noticing for the first time that Third removes two sizable islands from off the Blessed Isle’s western shore. Ah well, I’m not sure much was ever done with them anyway.

                    So, Eagle’s Prefecture has surely changed quite a bit, not least for no longer being the seat of House Sesus (I had forgotten about that). It gets off to a good start with slightly more poetic language than usual in setting up the prefecture’s relationship with the sea and how this is currently being embodied by the juxtaposition between familiar common fishing vessels and grand new treasure ships, followed by a nice vignette of the general lay of the land. We’ve gotten prominent prefectures whose cultures were significantly older than the Great Houses that resided in them, so I think it’s only fitting to get the example of the youngest House coming into the inheritance of one such, and it rather typifies their place on the ladder for them to receive a land characterised not by a singular old and prestigious pedigree but one that carries the fingerprints of all the Houses that came before. I’m picturing a private scene between the Empress and Chejop where he gives her that certain look and she insists that she wasn’t trying to be cute about it, it just so happens that the strategic placement that has given it such a high turnover also make it ideally suited for V’neef now handling the Merchant Fleet. What Fire Has Wrought gave the House a keen sense of being under extreme threat, and I think that’s carried well in the image of this otherwise placid landscape being fortified and drilled to face some of the fiercest assaults.

                    Eagle’s Launch itself also begins with a significant departure, going from having been the traditional gateway to the West into a minor port town that traditionally only functioned as a stop along the way for coastal traffic until it became the base of V’neef’s control of the Merchant Fleet and the major collection point for goods from across the ocean. Ohh, that’s also giving it a surge of new immigrants, that would be a real shake up; apart from the reference to the typical local hostility and scapegoating of newcomers, I’m picturing something like distant cousins of the inhabitants of Silk-and-Pearl, or just general Western people coming in en masse for the first time in centuries (some overflow from Wu Jian seems like a strong candidate), and either option seems like a major basis for culture shock. I like how V’neef would have priorities that lead them to ensure none of the bad blood towards immigrants turns to violence, since apart from general peacekeeping to make a good impression with their early assignment, I imagine that many of them see potential in bringing in a new populace with primary loyalty to them (especially in a prefecture they don’t entirely trust).

                    So we’ve got another description of civic beatification projects as a reflection of new wealth, seemingly with a particularly high priority on being impressive to visitors; it will probably take a few years before they start decorating with monuments hauled back from the islands. I take it that the significant disparity in wealth along class lines owes something to it being a very new market with less processing that V’neef and their subordinates can keep a lock on, since it seems that other major trading cities manage to have peasants who can make their own fortunes crafting and trafficking and what not. I wonder if that level of disparity could represent a weakness in V’neef’s approach that her fellows could recognise and exploit; other Great Houses are probably no friends to the peasantry, but maybe one could run it as them knowing from experience that it doesn’t do to dip too deeply into all of the markets and leave nothing for the enterprising and favoured peasants to build with, lest you put too much burden on your scions and patricians and leave too much resentment in the populace at large.

                    I recently recovered my old book about thirteenth century Troyes, it’s description of how common guild members could rise in wealth and status over generations is at the forefront of my mind and it makes me read this section as an instance of V’neef’s inexperience. Although the other way to read it is that it actually does mean peasantry as a stand in for workers, and is referring to the fact that there’s a lot of wealth and a lot of labour brought in, but the two aren’t meeting because the elites are motivated to keep a majority for themselves and there are enough workers to keep wages low. Hmm, might kind of tie into my image of a lot of northern and southern folk having drifted west after rumours of work; they hear Eagle Prefecture has exploded with the new trade and tribute processing, V’neef makes arrangements for large numbers to be permitted to travel, and they all end up in Eagle’s Launch to find that way too many people had the same idea so they can be hired by the day, and none of them are given permission to leave.

                    Also, I feel that there should be some Dynast whose mind is just blown the first time they eat pineapple.

                    All the ports have been presented as tumultuous places and hotbeds of intrigue because… of course, that’s what a port city is in this kind of world, but I do think that paragraph effectively lays out how this one being the stage for competition over shares in the new frontier and that the fresh new House is playing a bit fast and loose with their fiscal responsibilities makes it especially vulnerable to a lot of opportunists, scoundrels and spies. For that latter one especially, it feels a bit different to have the city being scrutinised by ministries looking to audit the new revenue streams; all the Houses might be trying to cook their books, but V’neef would feel like the ones that the ministries are best equipped to contend against if they’ve got the least experience and a lot of the industry is still fresh and untested. It’s another avenue of attack on the vulnerable House I would not have considered, and has a certain irony to it considering points elsewhere suggesting that V’neef and the Thousand Scales could be very helpful to one another. It particularly strikes me as a ripe hook for conflict with the magistrates; V’neef offers them a refuge that they desperately need, but what might they do should a line be crossed in opposing attempts by the ministries to take the Treasury’s share?

                    Oh hey, the topic of the House established in a city older than it is turned on its head again; where before there were a few conservative Houses attached to slightly more liberal cities, we now have the point that a city previously so out of the way and insignificant fostered a rigid and traditional ruling class who experience friction with charming, adventurous, experimental V’neef and her court of intellectuals and foreigners. That would probably be difficult in any case, but when the House’s main rivals are Peleps of all people it isn’t hard to imagine that she needs to keep on top of more than a few patricians who find themselves thinking that they’d prefer a much more upright and respectable House to reign over the new gateway to the West. Mind, I’ve observed that Peleps is in a transitional phase, so one could portray that as a scenario that is unsatisfactory to the conservative faction as well (a point that some canny scion of V’neef might illustrate in order to curtail any simmering treachery).

                    And finally itself bringing up that point about how the place is a sanctuary for magistrates, who still bring some of their own problems with them. I like the idea of giving an example of one along with an impressive resume and personalised plot hook. Hehehehe, the idea of secret heirs of the Empress has never entirely sat right with me for a couple of reasons, but pointing out that the Empress could impregnate another woman (or at least bring forth a child shared with her) via sorcery, that brings it up a notch. One gets to preserve the narrative while maintaining the ambiguity of whether or not the Empress is the actual parent, by way of her own mystique and the uncanny nature of Creation, and even give prominence to her sexual orientation. If Cynis Curan is going to be the self-appointed protector of a supposed secret child of the Empress, I think this is the best way to do it.

                    Probably not many of these regional sidebars left now. Let’s see, the Fields of Rue: plains, hills and woodland, erratic weather that tends towards the wet… local version of the Celtic Isles, got it. I wonder if I should read any deeper environmental significance into having been the site of a lot of conflict or just take it as simply happening to occur here. In any case, nice to see the Blessed Isle also carrying a bit of scarring that has nothing to do with the Fair Folk, and I like the image of a lot of settlements growing around temples to keep a lid on the resting places of the dead; it’s a nice motif in its own right, and also carries a little something that an enterprising Abyssal might take advantage of.

                    Okay, almost four thousand words and about halfway, keep going…

                    Ventus Prefecture, which I gather is located smack dab in the middle of one of those mountain ranges that branches away from the Imperial Mountain and its attendants, apparently making it one of the wildest regions on the Isle. There was a picture… I’ll come back to it. The advantage of being so enclosed being that it offers an abundance of resources, so this part of the Directional microcosm affords the Blessed Isle its very own domestic untamed frontier territory, surrounded on all sides by more curated domains. It even provides somewhat more accessible frozen peaks to be a source of commercial ice, without regards for how many slaves are lost in each expedition (if you can think of a better way to get ice, I’d like to hear it). Hmm, it says that there are merchants who’ll follow ancient roads to get further in, but I’m not quite sure as to why. I do rather like that description of how the Realm harnesses the Skyhewn Mountains as a symbol of cruelty underling beauty and power and an ideology of so-called stark necessities to preserve secure lives; it’s kind of bullshit, but it’s clever bullshit that uses available resources well (and it does seem especially fitting to Sesus if they’re prominent here).

                    Now that’s an interesting complement to the image of harsh frontier life and industry, that there are clandestine official operations in the area that sometimes bring out inexplicable troop manoeuvres accompanying sensitive individuals, and I like how it uses that to sidle in a sparsely detailed plot hook about an escapee who drew a lot of pursuers slowed down by unexplained human and devil hostility. I like these kinds of mountain dwelling communities enough to have been looking for them in the edges of a few other prefectures, so I’m happy that there’s something so overt about it here; I’m not sure why, but I’m especially drawn to the idea that in addition to the typical furs and fleeces they forage for precious medicinal herbs in the sheltered forests. It’s also neat to give the Dynasts a particularly challenging hunting ground.

                    Ah, and of course people living the frontier life would bring a lot of value as skirmishers to the legions, but for the fact that the legions don’t offer many benefits. I would imagine that there’s the occasional oddball who could see past the disrespect, danger and lack of coin out of a desire to see the wider world, but I expect that most of the kinds of people who live in a place like this have no desire to be anywhere else… For that matter, if they’re anything like real life, I’d expect even the ones who do to find themselves sick and uncomfortable when in an environment drastically different than one they’re probably highly adapted to. Whiiiich ends up going anyway for any of the ones dragged along after being… something… that idiom for forcing somebody through a justice system way too quickly, I’m drawing a blank… regardless, I can imagine that many of them view the prospect as a bit of a death sentence one way or another (that opposition for the search party is looking a bit more explicable). There are a lot of ways that I think House Sesus is a bit too big for their britches, so I like the idea that they’re bringing more pain on themselves than they’ve bargained for in trying to appropriate (and presumably step up) that racket; going by the description of how the locals are ready to rebel in the name of the Empress, I take it that it was easier to tolerate such liberties even while resenting them when they thought it arose from somebody with the mystique of herself. And hey, there’s something rather gratifying in the idea that Sesus might find itself with an unexpected thorn in their side courtesy of people employing much of their own playbook against them.

                    Okay, last year we were promised a bit more context for Ice-Above-the-Water, and it certainly seems to be settled amidst sufficiently inhospitable landscape. What else have they got? The structure itself is sufficiently impressive (I particularly like the idea that it’s very heavily lit up to make night time approach or escape more of an ordeal), and it’s nice that the Silent Legion gets another thing to do. I must admit, the identity of the prisoners is a bit more mundane than I was expecting; a place to hold high ranking political prisoners that are sensitive enough to not merely execute makes enough sense (I wonder if their accommodations within are a bit more comfortable), with my particularly liking some of the potential and implications of holding foreign dignitaries hostage, and you might as well have a dedicated storehouse for any spirits that need to be packed away for a while. The idea of keeping the likes of rebels and heretics though… I can reconcile it with the Empress, but it strikes me as something motivated a bit more by cruelty than pragmatism, the idea that they need to be kept against martyrdom more of a rationalisation. After all, is there much difference between openly executing somebody, executing them in secret and tossing them in a hole in the woods, and claiming to everybody that they’ve been locked away in a secret prison in the inaccessible mountains where they’ll never be seen again? Seems to me like even if those left behind bought it the lost person would be as good as dead, and it’s still a bit of a hard sell. No, in a majority of cases my outlook would be that if the Empress wants to keep some mortal who defines her reign locked up forever, it’s a show of force and indulgence of ego a bit more than offsetting some greater drawback from killing them outright. Which I’m on board with. Still, at least there’s one more overt oddity in the form of that unaging serpentine prisoner; they’re using the term “snakewoman”, but I take it that this is supposed to be a general physical description of somebody of unknown provenance and greater power than an actual beastperson. Given that the prison was constructed around her cell, I wonder if the Empress interrogating her is supposed to include questions about how and why she wound up there, or if they at least know the monk jailer’s motives if not his means of confinement, and the queries go to other subjects. I find it in the grand tradition of the Houses’ placing short term benefits over long term needs in the idea that without oversight, Sesus are about ready to slay their charge and have done with it. The text references the legend of another of her kind roaming the mountains looking for a chance to help her escape, but my mind is going back to that creature they dredged up in Eye of Creation; I wonder if they aesthetic similarities there are purely coincidental, or if a deeper connection is intended to at least be an option for somebody to pick up if they’re interested.

                    All in all, the feeling I get from this range is that while it’s mountains may not be the tallest on the Isle, they’re liable to be the meanest (not least from more people having cause to try living here).

                    Bright Obelisk benefits from some hype earlier in the book around the subject of failing magic in the Realm, and I find a fine aesthetic in it for being built to see an exceptionally beautiful white-water mountain river. I see that this is also moved up from prior Editions, albeit given a lot more to recommend it to outsiders; I like giving an identity to another specialist secondary school away from the big four, and forestry and mountaineering do seem like they’d be endeavours with no small value in the Realm (the latter particularly since the Blessed Isle itself is pretty mountainous). I’m often trying to think of mystical public utilities, so I like getting the example of this pylon at the city’s centre, particularly how it plays off of some of the precisely engineered surrounding houses. The function of holding off bad vibes is interesting; I wonder if that’s meant to be something to help this city function more in these harsh and isolated conditions, or if it has a deeper purpose from an older era. This would be another instance in which I’m on board with being straight about what it is that’s caused the power to start going wrong in the interests of keeping a consistent presence for Lunar antagonism, and the actual nature of the curse is suitably ominous; people being haunted by shadows of their own negative impulses until they’re possessed by them and became a danger to themselves or others feels very reminiscent of A Wizard of Earthsea to me. I will say that I find the order of information to be a little bit clunky, but that’s not a huge problem. But I do like how one of the ultimate functions of this being a Sesus prefecture is that they’ve got a lot to hide from anybody who might come and look into the nature of the present affliction.


                    With that settled, I’ll spare a moment to say that the illustration of what I’m presuming is Bright Obelisk makes for a very attractive image of a coniferous forest amidst the mountains, and a particularly grand picture of the cascading river. If that’s the city, it certainly conveys the idea of being a shining jewel amidst one of the harshest living conditions to be found on the continent, visible from far around to draw people in. I also like that pair of hunters depicting a bit of the local ethnic variations.

                    All right, last and kind of least of the home lands of the Great Houses is Tepet and Lord’s Crossing Dominion. That Dominion has always struck me as having a good deal of potential, and used a fair amount of it in descriptions of its economic output, but a bit too focused on the aftermath of the destroyed legions. So I’ll be interested to see what is made of it here; expanding on a good foundation, or another major overhaul.

                    First off, I’m glad to see them laying out the significance of the Dominion’s central location as key to its economy; I’ve thought for a while that it would make a lot of sense as a crossroads through the extremely rugged centre to connect the quadrants of the Isle, and been surprised when other books didn’t capitalise on it. I think it works especially well to have this laid out in terms of specific locations that we’ve already had set up to us, coming together at the end; I particularly like the connection between Mnemon-Darjilis and Arjuf after descriptions of the latter’s employment for architects. I also like how it builds on the idea of being a home to a lot of Shogunate history as a source of local legends and pride and ties that into the old idea of being a prime vacation spot for the wealthy with them playing up that retro quality; the description of how local architects have become well practiced at hiding modern constructions behind older styles reminds me of when I visited Gent.

                    The first couple of paragraphs about the city itself address my concerns about it having lacked a certain underlying character; as another city that would be heavily enclosed, I can buy Lord’s Crossing as one that would more effectively preserve traditional Shogunate culture, while also being advantaged in a steady income with which to fund emphasizing and expanding upon that legacy. With the changed character of Tepet, this feels very much like a home where city and House are made for each other, being born together and effectively reinforcing one another. Still, for describing itself as being the picture of a bygone era, I could do with a bit more description of what the distinguishing features of Shogunate architecture are. I also like how it plays off of being the home of a very Mela-focused/Air Aspect military tradition, with the strong emphasis on art and intellectualism as the means of expressing and strengthening their martial world view. I also like how they keep the dog breeding as a particular local industry; I’m not sure why, but something about the Shogunate does feel very much like its ideology would be the kind of thing that would find a strong expression in the relationship between hound and master, so I feel very responsive to that being used as a basis for why this land in particular would engage in that.

                    All of that established, it’s reasonable to set aside a bit of room for an exploration of the city’s grief. I find the paragraph tastefully understated in how it doesn’t go into too much detail about the subject of their mourning, which creates an impression to me that the House and the city have yet to fully process their loss; something about that feels a bit stronger to me than the prior Editions focusing on the schemes to prevent the population from turning against the Dynasts and trying to get them back into the legions. That also goes for how the prefect and governor have been consolidated; I have a fondness for the descriptions of Sawyer Shadow and Carpenter Reason, but given the current emphasis on internal friction in the House it seems appropriate to drop them in favour of Tepet administrators who are struggling to get their family on board with the old concept of trying to reinvigorate the city with lavish festivities.

                    I find it fitting to give the city a sidebar to elaborate a bit more on its history when it’s being played up as so culturally distinct, and I like the emphasis on it having functioned in many respects as a little nucleus of discord in the heart of the Empress’ domain; it conveys not only how it manages to be so distinct, but also gives a bit of context for why the Empress might ultimately turn against House Tepet, characterizes her a bit with some insecurity concerning the legacy of the shoguns she purports to be a successor to, and hints at its potential as a fortified base in the future. That middle one in particular… Lookshy has always been defiant, but the matter of how the Empress might view and present her claims of succeeding the old order have never had much focus, so I like the idea of putting an old signifier of the previous regime standing off to the side where it can be just at the edge of her reach and playing on her attention. It certainly gives rise to a neat little ritual of continuously reinforcing her having subjugated that order; having somebody walk barefoot to present her with a stone pried from the defensive walls hits that nice spot between pulpy exaggeration and believable practice of monarch and empire. I also like how the issues with the city would include the potential for relative isolation in combination with being an alluring target might make it vulnerable to pillage in a peasant revolt, it suggests additional anxiety around the possibility that such a hardened point could be sufficient to hold out against the rule of herself or Exalted in general entirely, which could very well hurt the cohesion of the Realm as a whole; you could see how such a holding would call for a military House to keep it in check (in contrast with Cathak and Sesus being based at the coast from which they can more readily launch expeditions into the Threshold). And that’s a nice call back to the old descriptions of the memorials of Lord’s Crossing to have the people reassign their ritual into one of using the city wall as the structure to commemorate their lost family; in this context, the inclusion of Tepet names feels a bit closer and more intimate than a calculated move to maintain good public relations.

                    Another little sidebar; I was wondering if there would ever be a repeat of the point that a long history of cultivation and the appetites of the Dynasty has left the Isle largely devoid of unruly animals. Nothing much different here, just slightly more emphasis on how there are a few zones of wilderness for more dangerous ones to survive in and that the private hunting grounds of the Exalted pull in more dramatic beasts to occasionally wander out and harass the peasant folk until some Dynast puts them down. But that leads into the final entry for the Blessed Isle, and it looks to me like a bit of a doozy; a little zone of unbelievably ancient forest nestled among the mountains. I like the premise of this for the idea of having one central thing to emphasize that the Blessed Isle is far older and more historied than the reign of its current inhabitants, and that for all the shifting eras of Exalted rule there could be something that is isolated and resilient enough to have preserved that legacy. The Imperial Mountain is all well and good, but it is by definition lofty and inaccessible, and I’m liking this as something that would ostensibly be more inviting (allowing the potential for people to have engaged with and be altered by it) and clearly something that even the Exalted should be wary of tangling with. It’s clearly a place that people have sometimes entered, and then ended up coming away with more questions than answers, and especially with a mystical forest I find that image more compelling than something that is completely terra nullius. It recalls a lot of the use of such a thing in fiction, the ones at the forefront of my mind right now being the Forest of Rot from Nausicaa and the Foggy Swamp from Avatar (especially in the quality of providing visions across time). Sure, such a place could be found in spots of the East, but I see value not just in the premise of it being nestled at the heart of the whole world but in the very idea of such a forest having clearly defined boundaries and manageable external dimensions, compared with the endless sprawl that can come from the Wood Direction. The list of wonders that adventurers might go in search of is compelling, and I like how it runs the gamut from the relatively prosaic of pulling out travellers that have become embedded too deep within all the way up to finding a mythical structure that is older than the very world.

                    And that’s finally the end of the extremely dense Blessed Isle chapter. There’s been a lot of grand stuff here, a lot of things to bring the lands of the Realm to life, loads of plot hooks, effective context for the character and backstories of several Great Houses and dotted with a lot of strategic avenues and resources that would be relevant to games concerned with war coming to the land from within or without.

I’m pretty sure that setting info in a smaller number of broader packages will be easier to keep a consistent pace with than this mammoth has been.

It’s a bit of a mixed bag to me. Some of the prose is just a tad too flowery for me to read naturally, and I’m not quite sure to make of a lot of the character dialogue; I do find that representing speech for characters can sometimes tend towards modern colloquialism and that it’s good to veer away from that, and considering general characteristics of the South I could find some of their aphorisms or turns of phrase to come across as being particular to the culture, but it’s a little bit hard for me to read it as a natural way for people to speak rather than… well, I won’t be mean, just say that it reminds me of some fiction that tries to hard. However, I do quite like things such as the way that the characters have differing opinions on the Realm and exactly what bears the blame for their current state, and that it doesn’t exactly frame the one that is more pro-Realm as being totally in the wrong. I also think it’s got a good idea for depicting the tension that people would feel around what is known or speculated about the capabilities of their overlords, and I enjoy little tidbits such as the specifics of how people might be taxed to provide the tribute. The idea of a magistrate sneaking in to take part is interesting, although I’m at a bit of a loss as to where he's going with the story of a hungry fox. The image in the art of the two people discussing the matter looking into the distance is straightforward enough, but I like the framing created by the buildings at the edges, the warm colouring of the sunset, and the designs of their clothes (particularly how the scribe who talked about having some respect in the offices of the satrap has some nice gold jewellery to look a bit more ostentatious).

                        Now, chapter six, The World We Rule (nice little callback to one of the more memorable headers for the Aspect Books). The opening paragraph is the standard abstract of the subject, but it includes a few points that stand out to me; the description of satrapies as components of the empire’s war machine (carrying implications of a greater focus on the Realm perceiving a need to defend itself or proactively assault enemies of itself or the world), and the description of satrapies carrying the influence of the Realm, which gives me an image of being able to use the association to throw weight against their own neighbours (although not forgetting that they’re also fed upon by it).

                        Huh, off the top of my head, I’m not quite sure if there was something in books from previous Editions that specifically laid out what the satrapy system was, although it doesn’t matter much as comparison anyway, since this book has already established that some of the premises would be different. The start of this section has some very vivid ways of creating a tone for the nature of satrapies; not just in descriptions of how Dynasts quickly get used to regional nobles falling into bows before them and calling it a vast hunting ground, there’s also the specific phrasing of it being the world made by the Empress and her children. Something about that specific wording gives me a distinct sense of history and legacy in the empire, that plays off nicely with how this Edition has gone for a more varied and detailed timeline and especially with having given more identity to the House founders and other leading figures in the Realm’s beginning. When it’s conveyed like that, it makes it feel a lot like modern Dynasts have the ghost of their forebears’ personalities and achievements hanging over them, leaving both a responsibility that they’re invested in and standard to live up to and exceed, and I think that gives a deeper sense of stakes to the Scarlet Dynasty while not exactly softening them.

                        So, the basic definition of satrapies is states sworn in fealty personally to the Empress, sometimes proactively when they see benefits to the Realm’s protection, but often through force. The military chapter had a lot of exploration of the intricacies of how those ventures are used to extend the Realm’s holdings, so I like how a point placed before that is the use of financial pressure to make independence untenable, I’d like to hear more about that. The use of assassins and internal insurrection to replace rulers with more agreeable ones is kind of an extension of the military applications, but one that implies the existence of players or factions who would more enthusiastically serve the Realm, providing stories around such extension with a few more options and some incentive to grapple with and manipulate complex societies. It also emphasizes how it’s not always quite typical annexation (even if these methods haven’t quite been unknown to historical imperialism). When it comes to the idea of a satrapy being brought into existence by the Realm wholesale, I had expected that there would be cases of large kingdoms broken into more manageable chunks or small polities consolidated into a single authority, but colonisation is a new one. I hope that also gets a bit more description later on, because I wonder how that works with the typical layout of the Realm and its people. The functions of being a satrapy appear to mostly be the same as they ever were: accept an Exalted overseer and military garrison, let the Immaculate Order in, support the Wyld Hunt and pay the tribute. A few of these have some minor differences, as I don’t think the Immaculate Philosophy was quite required to become the established faith before and there’s the note on how the tribute in currency accepts forms other than jade (already referred to as providing a reserve to do trade abroad); I also like how the tribute is also described not just as a means of enriching the Realm and Dynasty, but a tool by which the Empress could exert power over the Threshold (particularly confirming my sense that some satrapies are intended to become more militant in their region to acquire the means to pay their dues). There’s a reference to how satrapies need to abide by Imperial law, but I’m not sure if that’s just a general sentiment of not violating their subject status or if it outright means that Realm laws are intended to take precedence over local ones.

                        On the face of it, the function of the Great Houses in satrapies is also retained from prior Editions, with a few minor alterations; leases referred to as purchased rather than bid over, and the troops to acquire tribute and function as garrison being the minor House soldiers rather than proper legions. The main difference is in the background of satraps, but I’ll wait to see if this part has anything more on the subject before I elaborate on my own thoughts. Other than that, it continues a line from the military chapter and establishes a general tone for the satrapies with the point of how Houses were expected to keep their satrapies in line and functional without imperial aid (although I presume this refers mostly to getting the legions, and doesn’t extend to how they were supposed to coordinate with the Foreign Office, although even that merely advised rather than provided staff), and how the dangers posed to Houses that don’t has winnowed out a lot of sentiment in their management style. I think that emphasis makes the point of how serious an escalation Anathema threats are stand out more, and I like it being described as something that supersedes the usual political rivalries or the need for a satrapy to make money; it gives this image that the activity of a Lunar is enough to put a whole nation on a war footing that shifts a lot of how it relates to the Realm.

                        Heh, for a moment I was thinking that a header such as “the benefits of empire” was referring to how it benefitted the subjects, but no. Now, I more clearly remember ways in which the prior Editions approached the originating motives of the Realm, which tended to be a bit discrete about it when they weren’t just taking the existence of an empire as a given. Under other circumstances I might go with that, but when the Realm’s foreign policy is such a big deal to the setting and the reasoning of many of its characters, I think a clear elaboration on the how and why is called for. So first is placing it in the context of the Empress herself; a perceived need to build a new order for the world in the wake of the disasters brought by the Contagion, and the limitations underlying why it’s not a new Shogunate. I like how the latter is described not just as a case of not even having the resources that would be necessary to do so, but the Shogunate system itself being recognised as non-functional and a liability to the personal security of the Empress and general well-being of the world. I find a bit of the phrasing about making a strong central power from which to point Dragon Blooded at the Threshold reminiscent of expressions I’ve made in the past. But it also doesn’t quite phrase things in terms to make the Empress out as altruistically motivated; there’s an ambiguity here in which one can find characterisation that prioritises giving herself dominion over all.

                        Now we get the expansion on the described function as staging grounds, in which the Realm is outright called an engine of war. We’re given a picture of the Realm seeing the need to maintain world-spanning defences against any possible invasion from without, as well as a perception of the neighbouring Threshold requiring a means of easy military deployment to ensure that order is kept. Satrapies also seem to be part of a self-perpetuating loop in which they provide an easy means for further conquest and expansion over new territories that are themselves staging grounds for conquest and expansion. Given the circumstances, it’s not a completely irrational world view, and not just from the active threats provided by Lunars and memory of the Fair Folk invasion; I find that the expanded map benefits attributing these motives to the Realm, since it provides space in which strange and powerful nations can grow beyond their reach and feed apprehensions about being attacked. The Realm is not participating in a world of competing empires, but it’s not hard to see them imagining the possibility of such and feeling driven to delay the possibility by constructing ever-growing buffer zones. It feels like a rendition of the Realm that is true to the original depiction and intent, but is updated a bit to reflect some contemporary realities that have become much more apparent and familiar since the first publication, in a way that makes them both more understandable and in many ways more disturbing. On top of this, the satrapy system is also described as providing more robust security against the Anathema by the combination of creating a wide zone in which the Wyld Hunt can move freely, a large intelligence network with which to track the appearance and movement of Anathema, and a support structure that can swing overwhelming force or close a net around those enemies. This one does actually feel like a bit of a reverse face on the original structure of the empire being described as a means of creating a lot of space in which Solar players are free to move, but I see a few conceits that have changed for that (particularly a world that is no longer small enough to be written as though virtually all of it was under Realm control), and it still comes across as a system of competition rather than being inescapable.

                        Still, lest it come across as purely… well, not objectionably reasonable, but something that they could purely rationalise to themselves, there’s still the strong emphasis on satrapies as sources of profit. There’s an interesting modification in exactly how this works; it would appear that, strictly speaking, the tribute is owed by the satrapy to the Great House that has been given charge of them, with the portion that goes to the Treasury being their continuous payment on their leases. I like this phrasing for four reasons: it places a stronger emphasis on the Great Houses that have authority over the satrapy, making for a stronger relationship between the local people and rulers and the Dragon Blooded that they’ll have the most direct contact with; describing it in terms of the Houses owing hefty payments to the government for the privilege reinforces their sense of subordination to the Throne, as well as their stake in the functioning of the imperial government; it feels like a more sophisticated and legalistic system of financing that is in keeping with the usual descriptions of the Realm’s system of bureaucracy while maintaining the broad image and terminology of a more ancient world system of extracting tribute; and it implies circumstances in which a House needed to go out of pocket to maintain control of a satrapy, which I think could make for the occasional interesting old resentment (either towards the memory of the Empress, the underperforming satrapy, or some external saboteur who meant that a few dozen scions needed to pawn their ivory backscratchers to ensure they made their payments). It’s a bit more of a fraught system, but the writing is now being very candid with the fact that a major thing the Dynasts get out of it is not merely the wealth, but direct access to valuable and exotic foreign commodities so that they can live in splendour, and the reminder that Immaculate tenets hold that it is righteous to do so as reward for the protection and guidance the Exalted provide for all humanity (giving the force of scriptural authority, even obligation, to whenever a House needs to ensure that a satrapy provides its dues). So we’re not only getting the very direct statement on the empire’s function in aristocratic greed, but the manner in which they rationalize it as not being greed.

                        It’s an order of priorities that I personally find affecting because of how long I thought of and discussed the imperialism of the Realm primarily in terms of being a means of funding the Scarlet Dynasty. It complements the shift in my perception brought on by the more elaborate picture of their motives and world views provided in What Fire Has Wrought. I also find that placing the highest emphasis on the satrapies being a system of war cements the picture from the military chapter of a more dynamic and aggressive Realm, as well as plausibility for circumstances in which the empire is operating on a deficit, which can both increase the range of options for writing satrapies and might be satisfying for people that would find it implausible to portray the Realm as consistently and universally profitable. Most importantly, I think it presents a strong ultimate point about why the Realm is unsustainable in the long run, an impulse that players and readers can draw on to offset any sense of “things can be let be” for a Realm that felt as though it had been more static.

                        So, if we ever got the outlook of the Realm on its holdings before, I think we got a lot less on the experience of it from the other side. Particularly the matter of how it originates, since I think that on the whole the previous Editions regarded the majority of satrapies as having been secured centuries before the playable timeline so it wouldn’t be a pressing memory for most. In any case, I’m very interested to be given the playbook for the expansionism: how first of all the Realm typically feels confident enough to not need to bother to speak softly, and that in cases where brandishing the big stick does not suffice to get compliance with demands for fealty then actually swinging it against them will almost always work out. The taking of feudal and other political hostages as a means of securing the good behaviour of an enemy kingdom or family is a matter of historical diplomacy that I’ve found among the most interesting and unfamiliar to the modern age, so I’m happy about it finding its way into the policies of the Great Houses (in particular for the range of characters it can introduce to their tumultuous social scene, whether they’re foreign dignitaries who are relatively recent arrivals and still figuring out their new situation are venerable old folks who have lived their entire lives as “guests” of Dynasts who still have a good many years on them). The line about how a new satrapy can be depleted enough to be unsuited to providing tribute has unsettling implications all on its own, but while the point about how the House in control can redress this creates ideas for a lot of interesting and engaging work they can engage in during the early years of making a new holding profitable, they also speak to one of the more sinister qualities of the Realm’s vast reach. I’ve read before about periods of war and invasion before the modern era that resulted in large areas of land being rendered barren, and they gave an impression of armies that were ultimately spoiling their own victories, and maybe there have been cases where it encouraged a measure of restraint. But the Realm has extended itself so far that it needs to worry a lot less about how far it goes in a given conquest; if they depopulate vast stretches in one part of the world, there are surplus populations in several others that they have ready access to which can be assigned to make up the difference. Creation’s unique geography might give them an advantage in this shared by almost no other empire; most satrapies are ultimately not too far from the sea, and the Inland Sea especially is among the easiest to transport large numbers quickly over long distances. After that, I don’t have much to say about the point on Houses differing on how they’ll secure large general populations (partially because I’ll have to think of distinct methods for Houses that haven’t been attributed any yet) except to think about how Tepet’s practice of drawing on them for janissaries as hostages gives them a curious background of having created a lot of loyalty out of people whom they took with the intention of killing if their homes caused trouble. That and maybe there was the odd case in which new janissaries were employed in a military action that was a horrible disaster, which their homeland interpreted as malicious and rose up specifically as a result of it; seems like the kind of thing some clever Lunars could have planned for. The point is, you might be playing with fire if your system for taking hostages for the good behaviour of others revolves around involving them in life-risking actions.

                        Okay, that’s all a good solid idea of what they do with the subjects giving them trouble, what about the ones who cooperate more? The reasons of ones seeking protection against their own troubles, simply having rulers who lack the will to resist and even states whose existence is owed directly to the Realm are typical enough, although that last one… when it’s phrased as clients founded with Realm funding, it makes me think that at least one avenue for the aforementioned colonisation is when the Realm acts as a partner facilitating somebody else’s attempt to establish a holding over (nominally) unclaimed land. Maybe even cases where migrants from further out from the centre transition far enough inland to encounter Realm emissaries who welcome them with open arms, direct them towards good territory to settle, provide them with some starting tools, seeds and experts for the establishment of towns and farmland and even initially phrase the satrapy status in terms of natural repayment of debts rather than submission (this all being distinct from the idea of forcing nomads to settle in one place; there have historically been many instances in which large numbers of people moved with the intention of finding somewhere new to stay put in). Still, the example I find most interesting is of the states that should be secure and far enough from the Realm to not be immediately at risk who voluntarily engage in the relationship because they think it will benefit them to establish ties for trade purposes or get ahead of a conquest in future while they can negotiate from a position of strength. It’s good that there are large parts of the world beyond the easy reach of the Realm, but I also think there’s a lot of potential for being able to put the odd satrapy as far afield as one wishes, where they’re even distinguished by being a lone island of imperial and Immaculate influence in a region where it’s otherwise unknown. Especially if the standard is that the Empress offers generous starting terms to invest in eventually being able to improve her position when something goes wrong for them, since that gives a bit of a mission for any scions of the House in charge or others who sought favour from the throne to pursue while hanging around there.

                        The next part talks about “other satrapies” seeing the presence of the Realm as a necessary evil, but I’m seeing in this more of a general summary of what constitutes a benefit of being a satrapy whether it was accepted or not. I like the phrasing of how many depend on house garrisons because I can read it in two ways; one is the more straightforward of the Realm being a necessary evil because they can’t get by without the garrison, but the other is that the very act of establishing the garrison creates the dependency, because if the Great House is going to station troops anyway the locals might see less of a point in maintaining whatever practices or expenses keep their own armies up (which will very quickly become “necessary evil because they can’t get by without the garrison”; hmm, now I’m imagining a satrapy in which some of the people are focused on their proud military tradition that has been defunct for centuries, which serves as a framework in their underground resistance activities that needs to contend with their own people who argue that trying to drive out the Realm is foolish when imperial troops protect their borders). Satrapies can benefit from trade relations with one another, but when it says that they get favourable status from the Blessed Isle which functions as Creation’s breadbasket, well, that would seem to me to finally put the kibosh on descriptions of the Lap being valued as a source of surplus crops. That’s fair enough, it was always hard to square with consistent descriptions of the Isle’s agricultural abundance. And it creates an interesting inversion between the Realm and prominent historical empires such as Rome or Britain, where rather than the purpose of empire being able to acquire greater sources of staple food abroad, their own surpluses become part of the mechanism of empire by which they acquire the concessions of military avenues, proselytizing and specialized luxuries for their wealthy. Mind, if the Isle produces an abundance of food, I imagine that means more than a few satrapies find their good land converted more and more into providing cash crops for consumption in different parts of the empire. Still, it’s an interesting idea, an power that might feed people into submission. And sure it’s not hard to imagine a lot of places being enthusiastic about Immaculate expertise in maintaining class lines and keeping overbearing spirits out of the way. There’s enough to make it apparent that there’s a pretty big “if” for the satrapy that needs to buy all of this by means of ensuring that it can keep order and make payments, but it’s a plausible picture for what people might get out of the arrangement.

                        When describing the factors going into how the general population of a satrapy views the Realm (which comes with a simple to understand list of the key influences, prominent among them being which House actually gets the satrapy), I notice that more reasons are given for hostility than seeing them as providing safety. The description of a distinct attitude towards the Realm’s Exalted that is more uniformly compliant and respectful brings a couple of considerations. On the one hand, it would reinforce that the apparatus of the Realm is far larger than the Dragon Blooded even while they dominate its structure; that a lot of people have a lot of places to touch off the Realm that have nothing to do with the Dynasts, that a lot of subjects of their foreign lands could never meet one, meaning that it’s easy to have a bad regard to the Realm that does not cooperate whenever possible while maintaining the policy of “if a Dragon Blood shows up, be polite”. On the other hand, it creates an incentive for agents of the Houses to show up often and act widely and visibly, to make it hard for anybody to display open resistance to the Realm that does not entail an immediate punishment from a difficult to resist overseer. I imagine that it’s another thing that varies by House and region, that there are some Houses just to busy to keep such a visible presence, and some satrapies that just aren’t seen to be worth the effort (or the most attractive for any available Dynasts to show up in, although it does give a very low effort excuse for why Dynasts might show up just about anywhere, and can be a low investment thing for otherwise unoccupied Dynasts to do with themselves while also generating a bit of good will in the family). I appreciate how the basis for the consensus of showing respect is not being in awe of the Terrestrials but just having an expectation that they’ll respond with extreme violence to any slight on their honour, and I like how the Dragon Blooded side of it is playing the image up to cultivate a desired reputation (even if I readily imagine that many who aren’t nearly as touchy as they present themselves are still a good deal more touchy than regular people).

                        And finally there’s the subject of how an effective satrapy is a prosperous satrapy, since a big part of the point is paying tribute. This is very distinctly not phrased in terms of the Realm investing in allowing for a satrapy’s economy to improve; the Houses put pressure on the government and people to acquire the necessary wealth and materials, and it becomes their problem to figure out how to comply. Hence a tendency for satrapies to become raiders on their surroundings, taking advantage of the garrisons provided by their overlords to keep victims from following them home. I’d say this might be the most damning indictment of the Realm’s imperialism, that they preference a system in which their own violence begets violence in their new subjects. The basic principle is not a new idea to me, since I’ve thought for ages that references to extracting tribute in slaves mean that they greatly encourage satrapies to either engage in the trade, take neighbours for it, or convert members of their own populace, although this goes a few steps beyond that. The most extreme implication has to be in the idea that the Empress was happy to let satrapies rob from one another as long as they all paid their tributes, and that even if such violence rose to the point of war the Realm would simply select one side to support and unleash legions on the other as though it was their fault. Sure the Great Houses have an incentive to keep rivalries between their tributaries from drawing that kind of attention, but if it doesn’t completely undermine the idea that being a satrapy entails greater security, it at least shows practical cracks in the premise, that their ruler ultimately values their safety less than what they send back to the centre. And even in cases where the satrapies get along, they’re still encouraged to be centres of violence and exploitation of neighbours. It would be another thing that makes the history of the Realm more outrageous, a more immediate concern for people in its sphere.

                        Oh yeah, it had been so long that I forgot the practice of concluding descriptions of the status quo with breakdowns of how it’s breaking down and what might be done with it in play. First off there’s a clarification for what it might mean that satrapies are subject to Imperial law, which doesn’t really amount to any changes from the Empress to local laws that are unrelated to matters of keeping their economy useful and defining obligations to the Realm (which can probably still sometimes go deeper than one might expect). On the other hand, the latitude suggested for the Great Houses suggests that there are cases in which they’re very interested in making large scale changes to suit their own needs or ideals, so long as they don’t undermine the function as a satrapy (and all probably technically mediated through a satrap who encourages and pressures officials). It also seems as though, while the Empress wouldn’t have a direct policy for any given satrapy’s management, she reserved judgment for any given House making mistakes with correction via controlling the lease prices. Satrapies are otherwise described as a key element in preserving and revoking the status of a House, both in terms of her own will to revoke leases as a tool for hobbling a House and their own needs to carefully manage holdings to preserve their power base. This makes the satrapies a central element in how the disappearance of the Empress is unbalancing the Realm, as Great Houses feel little restraint with sucking their holdings dry and dealing with protest or defiance with severe violence. I actually think I need to look back over the book and see how much it uses the familiar lines of “the Empress being gone is bad because the government doesn’t know how to function without her”, as I’m wondering if this side is being made the more drastic consequence of her absence. Even if it isn’t, it does still look like a further layer of separation between the Great Houses and the ministries.

                        But it’s also focused on as another area in which the lack of restraint is having the Scarlet Dynasty go all in with exploitation that will hurt the Realm in the long run. Really, it’s going even further than any of the prior practices, since it’s something based on withdrawing forces from their longstanding territories abroad, shrinking their own borders. It’s not exactly expounded upon here, but the book has already brought up that the policies of recalling legions and paramilitary forces is driven by the desire to have them close at hand for when fighting starts, as Houses are supposed to be starting to find inevitable. Yet, the way it’s phrased here also cultivates an impression to me that it goes a bit deeper than even that; almost as if this is a period in which the expenses and inconveniences of handling satrapies is something that many Dynasts want to be done with, and they’re ready to just suck as much as they can get out of them before leaving the scraps to fight amongst themselves or deal with whatever powers beyond their borders move in to fill the vacuum. I do feel as though part of this is meant to come off as Dragon Blooded who are too used to their supremacy not really appreciating the intricacies of Realm foreign policy and thinking they can get by in their familiar position without it. I’d also see at least a few high ranking figures who are better informed, and might be acting along an assumption that resolving the succession crisis has to take the highest priority, after which it won’t be too hard to simply return and conquer the satrapies all over again. Come to think of it, if their subservience was expressed as a fealty to the Scarlet Empress, it would make sense that there are some in the Dynasty who would see the position of the satrapies as uncertain until such time as there is a new autocrat to be the focus of that submission and re-issue leases for it.

                        In any case, it’s written to be the setup for a big world in which figures like Solars, Lunars and the dead have vast opportunities to stake claims that would have been more challenging before (while Sidereals are in the unenviable position of needing to try and keep it together). In that sense, I suppose it does ultimately preserve the original intended purpose of the satrapy system, just with a higher sense that the loose form of control so enabling players to move as they please is a more recent affair and function of how the empire has withdrawn a lot of its stakes in outlying territories. I like how this part uses a more mythic and ambiguous tone to describe the appearance of the Solars in this situation, both for building up a slight sense of ominousness to them and leaving an implication that there’s a connection between that return and what the Realm has started doing. Although, Lunars are actually given the first word on the matter, which feels appropriate to their more long-standing grudge and struggle. Between this and the previous descriptions of what the satrapies did for the Wyld Hunt, I do think it successfully conveys an idea that Lunars fighting against the Realm for the last few centuries has been hard, that Solars returning as its power ebbs are getting it relatively easy, and that there is just as much chance of Lunars getting the chance at achievements they likes of which they haven’t had through their history.

                        Right, so this portion of the chapter before it even gets to the write-ups for specific satrapies is much longer than I was expecting. I suppose it’s only fitting, since this is the empire’s raison d’etre and it requires a fair amount of detail. It does mean that I’m going to put a break here, in what I think it approximately the middle of this introduction.

                        I have approximate knowledge of many things.
