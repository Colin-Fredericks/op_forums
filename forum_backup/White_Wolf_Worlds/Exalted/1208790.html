      As long as it flows with the story... then you can tell them anything. It also helps if you just, as ST, seem confident in your presention. You are, after all, telling a story.

      Example:
      I mix in a little swahili and other exotic verbage when describing southern locations and whatnot.

      So when I give my players what at first looks like a fetch-quest to get some royal prince hiding in a village, to return him to the king's royal war camp for his special forces it might go like

      "The king's vizier informs you [the circle]: You are to retrieve Prince Usiku from the village from Maleli - do so quietly! He has been living there in secret - he thinks he's an orphan and doesn't know of his birthright, but our enemies will no doubt also be looking for him. He needs to be bought to the ikanda to the west of the city so he can learn and live agoge until he is of age to join the court. Now go"

      Yes I took "ikanda" and "agoge" from Civ 6 - it's a great game to steal cool terms from.

      point is - that the above, delivered with certainty and confidence will very much so make any player think "Hell yes, this is what we're doing!" - never mind that I just dropped hints of how the local king seems to be hiding his children among the civil populace to keep them safe in their youth, or hinted of the king's war camp or the warrior culture he fosters among his soldiers.

      It's the closest you can really do to show, not tell - when you're playing a P&P RPG.

      It's the exact opposite of an exposition dump - it's a quest description with lore sprinkled through it.



      When you fuck up on that you get into trouble - either because it might strain your player's suspension of disbelief, or because a massive and boring info-dump might easily be forgotten.


      As for wildest fact introduced? Probably some of the fluff I threw at a former group of players when they arrived in Yu-Shan for the first time - they were awestruck.

      AS for most trouble from a declared fact? I told a player that yes, conservation of momentum exists in the game unless I say otherwise, combined with me having previously given a rough weight estimate of a yeddim. Said player then calculated the force of impact that a steel-armored Yeddim going full solar flying yeddim rocket. This was then swiftly used to absolutely DESTROY two flying DBs in dragon armor. Sure, it also pulped the yeddim, but those can be replaced. Engineering students make for "fun" players.

      and then my players had two suits of dragon armor. Oh boy.

                                                  My players have introduced several things, mostly small factoids that gave them temporary advantages. Nothing too game-breaking.

                                                  For example, the group was being pursued by political opponents out for blood. One character used their knowledge of local geography to bring up a possible hillock they could fight from the top of, in order to gain terrain advantages.

                                                  After the fight, one of their companions was direly wounded, and they had to bring them back to town before a winter storm set in. They ended up not making it, and the geographically-knowledgeable character introduced a hot spring where they could take shelter.

                                                  So on and so forth.
