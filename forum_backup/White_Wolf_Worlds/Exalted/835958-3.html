    I just ran a fight with two good battle groups and 2 high-stat DBs, one of whom was using Single Point, against essentially the Eclipse and the Changing Moon, and a large but rubbish battle group (the other PCs were 5 range bands away). The enemies also rolled really well. One battle group got 14 successes to hit, with +19 damage, and Ringing Anvil Onslaught (so they got to make a second attack), in the first turn, on both PCs and the battle group.

    During the whole fight, the Changing Moon took 2 bashing. And healed one of those. The Eclipse took no damage. Right at the end, when they turned up, the Zenith and Twilight took 1 lethal each.

    Challenging 300xp Celestials without throwing 2nd circles at them or other Celestials (which they have not much reason to be facing) is really, really hard.

    I was pleased however, that the fight only took 2 hours. Which considering that it was the most complex fight I've run in 3rd (and probably more complex than a lot of 2nd ed fights I ran), I think was good. Fights seem to take less time in 3rd ed. They're still long, but not so much. It's probably the lack of flurries.
    Took me like 2 hours to prepare though, checking all the stats and charms.
    Hopefully I can re-use some of the stuff though.

        I felt I should update this with the results of my most recent game.

        So we did a big Caste swap this last week and now the player most adept at playing the game to win is playing the Dawn and he built a defend other melee tank beast. I always get great RP from this player, but he dominates game systems and is about twice as hard to challenge as anyone else at the table. This became very evident during their one combat encounter involving a Size 2 Might 1 battlegroup of Great Cats, four Great Cat single combatants and an E5 war god I spliced together: anyone in range of the Dawn's War Lion Stance took no damage that combat, and everyone outside of that died or was grappled into submission during turn one.

        Observations:
        1) Rolling multiple ambushes for multiple actors is more challenging, but probably not worth the effort. I was concerned that if I rolled one Stealth check for all the enemies that someone in the party would overcome the results and the Great Cats would lose their biggest combat advantage, so I had each actor roll Stealth or Awareness and compared the results to determine who could ambush who. This worked great in a challenge sense, since the battlegroup got an obscene result on their Ambush strategy roll and ambushed everyone in the party, but then there was all this nonsense about how Great Cat A could take an ambush round if it attacked player D but not C or B, and it was just a mess. Probably wasted a full half hour just on that surprise round.

        2) Defend other should always apply to one ward against one attack, not to several wards against one area attack. I allowed this because the imagery was cool and kicked myself in the butt the rest of the fight. If I could do this over again I'd allow my Dawn to defend other as many people as he wanted using War Lion Stance but only if he activated it for every character he defended, not every attack. If I had done this I would have mote-tapped him in two or three turns.

        3) Powers and abilities that allow you to follow a withering attack with a decisive attack in the same turn, especially with extra decisive damage dice adders, are very effective. The Great Cat that killed a party member (I allowed them to get a revival by the medic afterwards) used Leaping Pounce and Mauling Bite to slay the second most combat capable character in round one, without the benefits of Stealth. It wasn't even an exceptionally great damage roll.

        4) Keep track of how many friendlies you have on the player's side. We have five actual players but two animal familiars, one human and one terrestrial retainer and one bound demon in the fray as well and honestly I don't think the power variance is that steep in this new system. If you have five players and five lesser minions, then really that's ten characters you gotta prepare for in combat.

        Today's helpful hint is: If you have a scene where all the party should be challenged by something that only one of them specializes in, write one challenge that will keep the novices entertained and a harder, separate challenge for your specialist to sink their teeth into. Otherwise your novices will feel outclassed or your specialist will feel like they wasted their charm investment.

        Examples might include writing a super strong "boss" for your Dawn to fight while your party keeps the minions off him, letting the party do research legwork while your Twilight puts the final pieces of a mystery together, sending the party out to deliver bribes while your Eclipse continues treating with a regional power, setting the party with a series of fantastical feats while the Zenith exalts their accomplishments to the town, or have everyone else cause a distraction while your Night sneaks into a fortified position. These are also great ways to justify the Caste-related Solar XP for everyone at the table.

                            Originally posted by kindledRadial

                            Today's helpful hint is: If you have a scene where all the party should be challenged by something that only one of them specializes in, write one challenge that will keep the novices entertained and a harder, separate challenge for your specialist to sink their teeth into. Otherwise your novices will feel outclassed or your specialist will feel like they wasted their charm investment.
                            This is a very important thing in Exalted fights, where, unlike DnD, some people are just literally much better at combat. A boss and his lieutenants, or a boss and a battle group. When one of my players had a Dawn with DV 13, and the rest were at most decent at combat, I realised this was the way to go.

                            I have also found that the number of minions the PCs bring can be overwhelming. Both to fight, and to run. I mean, the player can run them themselves to some extent, but it's still a thing.
                            In the last fight I ran, there were only 2 PCs in the combat, but one of them brought a dragon, a Jokun and a Vaktri with them.
                            And his battle group of Greenmaws would have joined them were it not for the fact he'd left them watching the rear in case the Wyld Hunt ambushed them.
                            There was also a ghost that the party's necromancer had sent to join the combat too.

                            I actually once got all the players to each play one of the sorcerer's bound spirits, but they weren't at all familiar with their charms, so it didn't work, and they weren't interested in it really.


                            I will say that 3rd ed is better about this than 2nd ed though.
                            Firstly, when a sorcerer has 6 blood apes, that's one battle group, for whom you can roll one attack against most or all of the enemies, rather than rolling 18 attacks.
                            Secondly, spirits don't have a ton of charms that no-one's really familiar with which take ages to find in the back of the book. They're much easier to run for both players and ST.
                            Third, it normally takes your action to make your mount attack, or use one of the attack-enhancing survival charms for your familiar, or to make a roll to enhance your battle groups attacks.
                            Fourth, demons aren't making a ton of attacks with Principle of Motion.

                                            Just had a thought vis-a-vis rolling ambushes: if you want your ambush strategies to be more effective, try house ruling that each character can only defend themselves with a successful application of Awareness. Technically, there is no rule stating whether or not a character can reflexively warn their party about an ambush to negate its effects. Though that's generally how I have run games in the past, perhaps simply shouting "DANGER!" isn't truly enough to negate the benefits of a well-timed and well-placed surprise attack.

                                            Some possible house rules:
                                            1. Run on "easy mode," where a single player can reflexively warn their party about imminent danger if they pass their Awareness check.
                                            2. Passing an Awareness check reduces an Ambush to a Surprise Attack (-2 def instead of no defense and a free attack round) for everyone present.
                                            3. Passing an Awareness check gives you enough time to warn one character about an enemy's location in detail, and/or declare Defend Other which negates the effects of ambush for that player.
                                            4. Allow a successful Awareness check to negate or reduce the effects of an Ambush, but only with a stunt. Perhaps for a Minor stunt (+2 dice) you reduce the Ambush to a Surprise Attack, but for a Major stunt (+2 dice, +1 success and +1 willpower) you negate the effects altogether. Examples of the latter might include singing a song in a foreign language only the party understands that informs them discreetly of the ambush without letting your attackers know they've been made.

                                            Obviously not house rules for every table, but I for one have not ambushed a party of PCs in any system since my high school days. Someone ALWAYS brings a Notice/Awareness build to the table.

                                                    So just an update of sorts: I recently discovered that my party's Dawn had "hacked the system" a bit, taking physical stats as his tertiary stat block and dumping all his bonus points there at character gen to start with most or all of those stats at five (because it's cheaper to raise your tertiary stat block). Now, in a normal game this has the drawback of leaving no bonus points for your much-needed favored and caste abilities, but since we started as mortals who have no favored skills and pay two BP for all their ability dots, it was pretty much the one true build.

                                                    I wouldn't say this approach is "wrong" or even "broken," but it's definitely a thing to be aware of moving forward.
