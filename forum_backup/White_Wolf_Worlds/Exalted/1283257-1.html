        Killing SWLiHN would not destroy hierarchy, no. However, the Yozis are very much bound by their nature, to a far greater degree than those who came after them.

        That is, I highly doubt that SWLiHN could do something truly anti-authoritarian.

        In terms of what happens if a god (or other celestial being that has control over a portfolio) dies, I imagine that it would explain how things change over time. To use a real-life example, the very nature of war as an element of state influence changed after WWI. (It was changing slowly over time, but WWI definitely nailed that coffin.) Before WWI, the idea of going to war to gain a positive (and generally limited) goal was widely accepted. After it, only 'evil' states (such as were present in the Axis powers of WWII) held this view, with all others restricted themselves to the idea of war as a defensive tool only... (at least on paper... don't want to get into proxy wars)

        Anyway, one way to explain this shift in a world with a literal Celestial bureaucracy is that the old god of war retired/died/whatever, and a new god of war, with a different view on war, ascended. (Another is that the god who held the portfolio of war's view shifted, but that is less... dramatic.) This can also explain how to change Yozis over time, if the Soul Menagerie concept of them still holds in 3E.

                            Originally posted by Elfive View Post
                            Actually I think in 3E one of the defining qualities of the Incarnae is supposed to be that they break this rule. The Unconquered Sun really is the sun, and if he died it may well go out.
                            The Incarnae are slightly more connected to the things they manage than most other things in the setting, but even then, it's meant to be a "specific" sort of connection arising from their circumstances rather than a transcendent platonic form sort of thing. That is to say, at least in 2e, it was implied that the sun would be affected (though it wouldn't snuff out) if the UCS died, but that's because the sun itself is a physical thing directly tied to him and not because the UCS is some sole platonic embodiment of the concept of "sun" (or "perfection" or anything else.)

                            Similarly, if you killed the Maidens, Fate itself wouldn't immediately grind to a halt or anything like that - there was a time when they were manually running the Loom, but that's long gone. But the Sidereals would have to probably do even more work to compensate for some things that the Maidens were accomplishing by their simple presence or occasional attention, and might have to seek out substitutes in some form in order to keep Fate running smoothly in the long term.

                            The Primordials once probably had similar connections to various parts of Creation, but even before the war those were being offloaded to the Incarnae (that was the point of the Incarnae), and those that were left were largely severed long ago, with their roles either replaced or whatever damage their loss entailed already being fully-felt. There are likely a few exceptions - killing SWLIHN shouldn't be completely without consequence. Viewing Creation as a program again, I wouldn't be surprised if there's still a few object references to her still tucked away in hidden corners of the setting where the Exalted failed to root them out (although she herself probably ripped most of those out in the Three Spheres Cataclysm.) But it wouldn't cause hierarchy itself to collapse or anything of that nature - more like occasional weirdness for a while similar to fate errors, or the odd thing sealed deep in Creation getting released, or plot-hooks like that.

                                            This.

                                            Deliberately Deleting Good Mechanics

                                            One of the things I admire about Exalted are the mechanics - being able to articulate previously nebulous ideas (from other RPGs) into firmer rules (especially social/mental/virtues). Clear mechanics eliminate doubt and ensure folks feel they actually earned their success (by luck or skill) and that the ST isn't just going easy/hard on them arbitrarily.

                                            The first way I could imagine "things falling apart" is when "hard-coded" rules now become vague guidelines - let's go backwards!

                                            Cecelyne's charms often hard-code social hierarchies into charms via UMI - but imagine forever-deleting those charms and being a god or Exalted scholar trying to remember them?
                                            • Social charms suddenly can hit anyone - low-Essence spirits can hit high-Essence spirits just as effectively.
                                            • Hierarchy charms suddenly fail altogether - the comparison between relative Essence is no longer sustained and thus charms predicated on that come up with a #null when trying to get an integer for calculation.
                                            Or kill Malfeas - and the closest thing you get to his charms is self-shaping (Lunar style) or earth-aspected emulations.

                                            Or kill Luna - and the closest thing you get to shapeshifting is D&D Druids (abandoning all the awesome heartsblood stuff and warform and the like). No more Gifts, no more Warform. The remnants of her champions being Beastmen. Or if you like Werewolf crossover, breed them with Totemic gods (so God-Blooded Beastmen tribes).

                                            Every time you kill off some Primordial-tier creature, mechanics crumble....so in-character, intellectually it seems nothing changed (concepts haven't been deleted) but it feels like things aren't working like they once did (because results are no longer reliable or the same).

                                            Tangent: History Falls Apart

                                            This makes me think of how much of mythology, folk legend and even paintings - where people of the period think their references are universal - and then they fall apart centuries later.

                                            Example: Atlas & the Sky
                                            • Modern depictions often erroneously depict Atlas holding up the globe...
                                            • BUT he's supposed to hold up a celestial sphere (Uranus).
                                            • Modern minds would object "but it's impossible to hold up the sky/space!"
                                            • BUT many classical cultures thought the sky was solid.
                                            • Modern minds would object "if the sky was solid, it'd fall and crush us! or it'd block the sun!"
                                            • BUT there were even some Middle-Eastern cultures who believe the sky was a dome of metal (and the stars were gods living on it, looking down at us); or look at Taoism associating metal with air.
                                            • Modern minds would object "but metal belongs with earth & minerals - not air!"
                                            • BUT people observed that when metal gets cold, moisture condenses on it - ergo "water comes from metal". And metal reflects light. So the sky must be a giant metal dome - when the sun comes out (like a torch), the dome lights up - and when it gets too cold, you get rain. Science!
                                            You get a flying Saint like Saint Joseph of Cupertino? Historians misremember it as "madness from ergot-infected grain" or "an extremely agile and gymnastic idiot-savant".

                                            Three Spheres Cataclysm

                                            From all of this....the TSC as a cascade failure when the Exalted slew the Neverborn and/or crippled the Yozis.

                                            They blame it on SWLHN and Hierarchy - but it's not her fault that the Exalted destroyed the foundations and then freaked out when the house crumbles in.

                                            It's like blaming Malfeas & Cecelyne for the corruption of the Celestial Bureaucracy - their charms could quickly enforce order - it's not their fault they're imprisoned. (But of course, no one would lodge this complaint, since admitting Heaven is corrupt would be politically incorrect).

                                                Again, editions matter in this. 2e took very hard to the "Rules are physics" thing, but 1e didn't and 3e explicitly rejects this. Mostly since while "rules as physics' Is a good experiment, it's not actually (I think) a good way to make a broadly usable game setting. It becomes self-serving solipsistic writing rather htan useful for like, playing hte game.

                                                My own view is kind of hinted at above. If you kill She Who Lives in Her Name, you don't suddenly change Hierarchy. You just canned its greatest advocate. wastevens I think had the best take on it. Hierarchy isn't like, a real thing. At least, it's not something that you can go out and say "That's Hierarchy" in the world like you do an object. At least, anymore in Creation than you can in our universe. It's a relationship, that has some criteria and values to it. The being who probably first emphasized it, recognized it and wanted Creation to engender it, and so on. But it's not like, a thing floating somewhere in a box that didn't exist before SHLiHN and will not work anymore if she's not. It's just that it wasn't able to be expressed until she was around (maybe) and she happens to like it when it emerged in her contributions of Creation.

                                                A world where there is all these programing analogs that could be analyzed is interesting as a thought experiment, but it ultimately is getting kind of in weeds that don't matter for a lot of games. There's a lot of ways to describe the ways the Incarna are related to their purviews, without having them be some special case where aspects of Creation immediatley break or stop making sense for humans from Earth if they were no longer there, I feel.

                                                        I think the second post gets at an important point. (Which, in retrospect, I should have emphasized in my post a bit more, but I just sort of took it for granted that people would see it.) We're talking Watsonian reasons, but we need to step back and look at the Doylist ones for a moment.

                                                        Having the entire universe collapse when SWLIHN dies (or drastically alter itself to the extent of an entire defining aspect of reality snapping out of existence) isn't very fun. It might sound fun to some people, but in practice it isn't playable in most games, which means that if someone says "killing SWLIHN destroys hierarchy" what they're really saying is "you can't actually allow your players to kill SWLIHN ever." I suspect there's some people who find that statement cool, but that leads into the second reason why killing her can't be that seismic of an event.

                                                        Because there's also story / thematic constraints to consider. In Exalted, as a story or a setting? SWLIHN is not that important. She's a side-character. Yes, she's a really big powerful side-character, but the game is not about her, and while you could make her the focus (in the same way you could make any character the focus), she's not the focus by default. Making her death seismic on a level that drastically alters the setting is bad because it sends the message that SWLIHN is more important than she is; and, by extension, it diminishes the weight and value of the rest of the setting. It means that all the kings and kingdoms of Creation don't matter as much, because if someone could just go beat SWLIHN to death the rug would be yanked out from under all of them. That's not a message the game should send.

                                                        That said. The whole point of the Yozis is to be ridiculously, stupidly big and scary so Infernals and Sorcerers can look cool by stealing their stuff and so, sometimes, in certain kinds of games, you can punch their faces in anyway. And in service of that goal, it's fair to say that punching SWLIHN's face in should do something. There's room to have her death cause Creation-wide ripples without having it just yank the rug out from under everyone as if she's the only thing that mattered.

                                                        So what should those ripples be? Some of this ought to vary based on the nature of your game and the expectations of your players, but I would argue that:

                                                        1. You want to avoid the idea of broad, open-ended changes to reality, because that breaks "the game is not about SWLiHN" constraint. Maybe for specific games that would be fine, but if I was writing a general rule... no. Nothing about Creation being irrevocably lessened or the true majesty of what was lost being impossible to measure or anything like that. Maybe that was fine in the backstory, but this has to exist in a world where the core focus is on game-of-thrones, queens-and-castles play. SWLiHN's death might cause disasters, but it should be stuff that can be addressed on that level of play, not cosmic transcendent metaphysical disasters that render the knights-and-castles meaningless.

                                                        2. You want it to be stuff that leads to interesting gameplay going forwards - in other words, story hooks. Look at the stuff I suggested. "The Maidens died, so now the Sidereals have to find someone or something to replace the role they played for the Loom" is a story hook. "SWLiHN died, so now the seals on the ancient Behemoth of Anarchy that she sealed in the time before time have begun to weaken and crack" is a story hook. "The UCS died, so you must go on a quest to recover the First Flame so that it can be used to rekindle the dying sun" is a story hook.

                                                        "SWLiHN died, so now everything is worse forever in some vague undefinable way" or "Malfeas died, so now we're going to arbitrarily change the rules of the setting" isn't a story hook. It doesn't improve anything. It's just wanking an NPC. In some situations, yeah, maybe your players want it to really drive home what a big deal it is that they killed SWLiHN, but it certainly wouldn't be my default assumption.

                                                        (I think it is absolutely worthwhile to come up with technobabble / magical-metaphysics reasons to justify the stuff I mentioned as a good outcome - that was a lot of what my post was going for. If a game is at the point where your players are actually punching SWLiHN in the face, I think they'd probably want to hear some sort of meaningful-sounding statement about how grand and significant their actions were. I just assumed that people would realize that in actually all of that is just written in reverse in order to reach a predetermined conclusion, which is determined by what makes a good game.)
