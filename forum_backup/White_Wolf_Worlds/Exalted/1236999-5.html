        Originally posted by Epimetheus View Post
        I hate having to say this before topics like this, but yeah, I am gay and I still don't agree. I thought the old system made sense. Hardship happens and nobles had to suffer a lot for there status. To wave it away, kind of just disrupts the setting and makes characters like Les Kadaal look pointless. You could still pretty much do whatever you want as a dragonblooded but you still had to marry out obligation. Most of the Dragon Blooded marriages are emphasized as being loveless, so I don't see why this exception exists without any stigma.
        I'll say the same thing I did about Arianna encountering constant misogyny and the problems involved in deciding what to do about it. People want several things from a game in this regard:

        1. Some players just want to just be themselves in the game, without having the game insisting that you must make a big deal of it or demanding that it reflect all the ugliness or limitations of real life. That's obviously a legitimate thing for them to want - sometimes you just want to have a big fancy gay marriage and not have the game tell you you can't or that it would be a big controversial deal.

        2. On the other hand, given that Exalted is also a power-fantasy, some people want that ugliness to be there so they can punch it in the face. Having the corrupt dynast, wounded in the floor, howling "Defeated? By a mere slip of a girl? IMPOSSIBLE!" is part of the fun of playing a super-powered hidden-tiger-style martial artist. Some people like telling stories of forbidden love or using their power to defy and even change the rules of an unjust society. That's a valid reason to want to have eg. societies that don't allow gay marriage, or places where homophobia or misogyny are common (so the players can kick its teeth in.) Sometimes you want to have a big fancy gay marriage and have it be a big controversial deal. Sometimes you want to tell the story of Exalted Brokeback Mountain or something, or about people navigating the unjust restrictions of their society as best they can. It is notable that, for instance, the TV version of Game of Thrones dramatically increased the homophobia of the setting and its main religion in order to allow for big passionate confrontations and dramatic injustice; certainly the writers knew the viewers' sympathies would be with Loras Tyrell - that was the point.

        3. On the other other hand, I'm skeptical of situations where the game goes for the generic assumption of this conservative-Victorian ethos (which isn't necessarily as historically accurate as people assume it to be.) While I could see the value of having these misogyny cultures for Arianna to (proverbially) kick in the teeth, Arianna's write-up bothered me because it sort of seemed to be written from an assumption of "oh, this is a historical-inspired fantasy setting, so of course people will hate women because that's what things were like in this cultural vision of the Dung Ages that all fantasy has to replicate." Just doing it for that reason seems like a failure of our imagination - Exalted is meant to seem like a strange and new and exciting world, and challenging those assumptions can be part of it (although it's most interesting to me when it draws on real-world cultures and traditions that aren't used enough in fantasy.) This was also something that bothered me about the Game of Thrones plotline I mentioned above - yes, I get what it was doing, but it was also grounded in a lazy assumption of "this is just what the Dung Ages were like."

        4. I somewhat agree about the nobility aspect and especially about Ledaal Kes, which was a very good write-up on what it was like to be gay in the 1e Realm and the complexities of his situation. The emphasis on doing your duty as a dynast even if you don't like it fits the setting. And if I was just stepping back and talking anthropologically or politically, I'm somewhat less sympathetic to dynasts who end up forced into loveless marriages given that this is part of an elaborate network of power-relationships that they use to relentlessly oppress the rest of the setting. Ledaal Kes (unlike many other people in the setting) absolutely has the option to run off with a guy he loves and marry them somewhere if he's willing to give up the power and prestige that come with a noble position. His decision to stick around in a loveless marriage with a woman he'll never be attracted to due to their incompatible orientations is entirely his choice and I don't feel any sympathy for him over it given the larger context of his position (nor, I think, is his write-up intended to make us feel any, nor would he expect any.) This is something that frequently bothers me about arranged-marriages-among-powerful-nobility stories in general. But, and I think this is what SLS is getting at...

        5. The game also has to consider the actual play experience that people have when they pick up the DB book. 1e Ledaal Kes makes an excellent story and if I were writing a Dragon-Blooded novel I would go for that. But not everyone who makes a Dragon-Blooded character is interested in that sort of long-view "anthropological or political" assessment. Sometimes you want to get into character as them and feel what they feel and experience cool stuff (like big elaborate weddings) in an aristocratic context. Focusing on big elaborate weddings or other aristocratic trappings is sort of the 'dumb' way of playing Dragon-Blooded, but the game has to support that basic sort of play as the foundation for other stuff - a book that is all cerebral meditations on aristocratic duty and society wouldn't be very approachable or fun for most players. And in that respect it makes sense to me that the game would include a note saying that gay marriages are OK, especially in its big main central aristocratic setting; if you really want to tell stories like the ones outlined above or like the one 1e Ledaal Kes went with, Creation is huge and it's pretty easy to wedge things like that in somewhere.
