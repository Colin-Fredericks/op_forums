    An Idea for the Metaphysics of Language

    04-01-2026, 06:15 PM
    I've always thought the way language works in Exalted is a little weird and limiting. Well, a lot of people have that thought about the mechanics implementation of them, but while implementing my idea would involve fiddling with some of those mechanics, I'm more interested right now in language's place in the setting.

    Lacking the equivalent of a Tower of Babel narrative, it's strange to me how all the myriad spirits of the setting speak Old Realm and it kind of aggregates all the functions of a sacred and academic language to itself (up to stuff like all sorcerers having to speak it in prior editions, though I don't recall that being mandated in 3e), yet hardly any humans speak anything close to it and cultures keep inventing different priestly languages, and the gods I guess are picking up human languages to bridge the gap despite otherwise being presented as pretty averse to the amount of interaction and effort that that would require. Not that I want to get too far into 2e-style "giving everything a perfectly logical explanation," but 3e does focus a lot on the development of cultures and their relationships with the supernatural, and it seems like from both an ancient/mythological "why were people made like this" perspective and an evolving "how did people end up where they are" perspective, the current presentation makes it feel incongruous that people should speak all these different languages when the Original Language is right there and it's what everything you and your ancestors have ever worshiped would have the easiest time talking to you in.

    (Saying all that out loud, I vaguely recall that 2e might've had language fragmentation as a consequence of some She Who Lives In Her Name information warfare attack during the Primordial War, so I guess we do have a Babel narrative kicking around somewhere, but that's too sci-fi for my tastes.)

    My idea is basically that Old Realm doesn't function at all similarly to an ordinary language, with a set vocabulary and grammar, and that it's closer to a process or practice of emanating meaning from your spirit, in a way shaped and filtered by the nature of both the speaker and the receiving listener/environment. I wouldn't necessarily attach a huge amount of mechanical weight to this, but I imagine it working in a manner reminiscent to Truenaming from D&D or Dragontongue from Elder Scrolls, where the very act of speaking it tends to be an imposition of your nature and power on the world around you, so it's less useful as a general-purpose lingua franca and instead contributes to the sort of booming, semi-cataclysmic presence of divine forces in full bloom - demons of Kimbery speaking in acid tones that scald the air, the volcano god's wrathful condemnations making the land shake before he's actually willed an eruption into being, things of that nature.

    Producing Old Realm as such wouldn't be something most people could even learn, being a miraculous art for sorcerers and the Chosen - instead, the interpretation of the Old Realm spoken by various ancient spirits would probably form the root of language as used by mortals, and the ongoing evolution of languages would be shaped by both all the normal forces that change language in real life, as well as ongoing interactions with the supernatural.

    For most everyday languages, interactions between mortals would dominate the shaping of the language, but you'd also have space for various types of sacred languages based on leaning into certain supernatural influences, like using a particular dead language for priestly use because it's more pleasing and comprehensible to a certain entity or pantheon involved in its formation, or for scholarly and miracle-working purposes because it better captures and communicates some facet of divine truth and power.

    For an example ported from prior editions, I'm thinking of the High Holy Languages of the Dragon Kings, the priestly languages of the Unconquered Sun as produced by each of the four Dragon King breeds, which could be efficacious in prayers to him or when worked into crystal and vegetative artifice, particularly that devoted to him. Again, not going so far as to strictly define any advantages in hard mechanical terms, but the sort of sympathetic affinities that might make a spirit like you more, or color the powers of an artifact you produce.

    On the flip side, most day-to-day mortal languages, while comprehensible, would likely feel simultaneously simplistic, vacuous and difficult to parse for many spirits, lending an added structural component to the distant, erratic, and miscommunication-laden relationship between mortals and the supernatural that underpins much of the setting.

    I'm running out of structured thoughts... Oh, hey, if you're a huge anime fan, you could pull a little harder on the "speaking words of miraculous power" thread and use it as an excuse for calling your attack names. :P

    Anyway, does this sound like it has potential as a positive setting change to you guys? Are there negative ripple effects from this that I'm not considering, or other positive additions/consequences that I should think about roping in? Let me know any thoughts y'all have!

    (I sense the impending doom of Isator Levi punching me in the throat for taking something working perfectly well as a strictly cultural phenomenon and trying to pry into supernatural underpinnings for it... Edit: this is a joke btw I am actually excited for Isator's thoughts if they have any)

      I'm not sure how much of a positive it would be, and it feels like there's two major assumptions that're being treated as facts of the setting:

      That Old Realm is the "original language" of Creation, and it is so because all the gods and spirits speak it.

      You also only need a Tower of Babel type of etiology if you presume all of a group started as a small homogeneous social unit that would have spoken the same language. A bunch of disparate people that already have their own languages don't tend to simply forgo their cultural communication even if there's a lingua franca around to have at least some skilled speakers in to handle interactions with others.

      Since Creation was born from the Primordials, who don't cooperate well and have vastly different desires from each other, there's no particular reason to assume they created a universal spiritual language for Creation; and that at best Old Realm is already a diminished form of them speaking in a fashion lesser entities can comprehend. Before the gods, Exalted, and their allies started to work to overthrow the Primordials... who would expect the Primordials various creations and servants to actually naturally speak the same language? The rebellion itself is the first event in the setting that really necessitates a common tongue. And if anything Old Realm originating as a way for the rebellion to communicate works both as something the Primordials wouldn't really comprehend at the time or bother unraveling, while also explaining its somewhat odd nature as being easily learned by the Primordial's creations but the full understanding of it somewhat lost on those without access to Essence. Normal humans might be able to broadly understand the language but can miss the nuance of something created for spiritual entities to conspire together. The Exalted serving as a bridge to the construction of High Realm as a more human natural tongue works from there.

      Another consideration on why so many priestly languages exist in Creation is simply a matter of creating worship monopolies. If you're a terrestrial gods and you want to make sure your cult only worships you... turning a local language/dialect into the priestly tongue of the cult means they don't know Old Realm and no other being can just waltz in and supplant you with raw power. The residents of Yu-Shan might not have to worry about any of that because they deal with more wide-spread phenomenon to need to protect their worship in such a direct way, but the terrestrial spirits most certainly have to figure out ways to maximize exclusivity from their adherents.

      In general, to my estimations anyway, Creation's languages are already less diverse than they really should be, and the influence of Essence users can explain that. An extra special magical language isn't really necessary to add to the verisimilitude of the setting, or do I feel it would really add to any themes I can think of that Old Realm doesn't already do as it is.

          So I actually kind of share this on both routes. I think the game on one level often forgets that the things you buy with dots of the Language Merit are not languages, they're entire-ass language families. This was inherited from 1e Trinity Continuum, where the same system made it so you needed one dot to speak say, all Romance languages or something for simplicity's sake. Writers often forget this, and it result sin as you note, a kind of narrowing of total languages out there.

          For my own table canon, I do side with you on the whole idea that Old Realm is a special language that has inherent supernatural qualities, and being able to speak or understand it is actually a knack in itself. The analogy I use is Star Wars: Folks seem to just understand droid beeps and wookies. Us, the viewer, don't have half the clue how. And I see the same language used for field spirits, ifirits, jeweled demon wasps, fairies, and rando behemoths, I kind of assume that something like that's going on. If you aren't "in", a wookie and a droid is just SFX.

          It's actually something I've sat and written a whole-ass section in my house rules. This is it on its own, since it be relevant for ideas. But for me, there's no even Babyl thing. Every single human language spoken today is pretty much that, human. Even ones "distilled" from Old Realm dialects eventually turn into human-derived stuff that doesn't necessarily grant access to Old Realm. High Realm even claiming to be "closer" to Old Realm is just the Realm saying that to impose language on folks imperially and all that.

              I'm curious how other speculative fiction RPGs handle the subject of multiple languages. In any event, the treatment in Exalted is certainly an artistic licence for the sake of ease of use thing.

              It's not really to my taste personally, but I wouldn't really object to Old Realm as a more metaphysical kind of language just because that's a concept with a lot of artistic pedigree. I don't think the setting as written strictly plays into it, but nor does it actively contradict it.

              In the setting as written, I think an origin for the language could be as simple as being the common speech of the souls of the Primordials (who, Third and Second Circles together, would still have numbered a community that might only be around twenty thousand), and then taught to the gods as they were created. I could imagine terms in which other languages still develop, but Old Realm maintains connotations of being sacred in its own right and at least presumed to be better suited structurally to communicate certain topics to remain in common at least for the gods. Something similar to the history of Sanskrit.

              I'm inclined to see the topic in more grounded terms, because I think languages as a whole have a lot of remarkable connotations that can be taken for granted.

                  Even as the one who posted the thought, I go back and forth about which part of the use of language is really doing the trick for me.

                  I find something undeniably evocative and compelling in the idea of accessing sacredness through learning and utilizing older languages, on both the relatively cosmic scale of using the god-tongue for sorcery, and the more day-to-day scale of a divine monument having more power for being inscribed in an old priestly language. So I like to look at the setting in a way that provides for that.

                  But even that doesn't have to come from there being something baked-in that makes one language mechanistically different from the other. A more people-oriented way to look at things that meets the same outcome is to look at that sense of sacredness as something imparted by human effort - that what gives that extra weight to those languages isn't something intrinsic to them so much as the personal and cultural devotion demonstrated by learning/maintaining a whole separate language for sacred usage.

                  Though another less-easily-sidestepped part of what roping off Old Realm as not-really-a-language-at-all does for me is cleaning up the relationship between mortals and spirits, because there's kind of a presentation throughout the text that most spirits view themselves as superior to mortals and wouldn't bother themselves with a bunch of extra effort to engage with humans on their terms, but also it's usually taken as a given that a spirit who meets a mortal (whether in person or in their dreams or whatever) can probably communicate, so either spirits are studious polylinguists or their communication probably shouldn't be on the same terms as mortal languages.

                      Originally posted by MJ6373 View Post
                      either spirits are studious polylinguists
                      This is one that I think can at least be put into perspective by considering that modern language homogeny is historically atypical. Many if not most historical societies make immersion in multiple languages more of a social constant, and by virtue of familiarity and necessity becoming some form of multilingual becomes natural. It might be mediated by a lingua franca, a creole, or a pidgin, but varied people intermixed with each other will develop a basis to communicate, barring something like a dedicated ideological basis to resist learning other languages and marginalize people speaking particular ones.

                      The thing I always find to be the object example of that is European contact with the Americas. They want from a space of having had no pre-existing cultural contact to indigenous people that could act as effective interpreters between natives and Spanish speakers in less than a decade.

                      Historically, a person stood out as a studious polylinguist if they spoke something like more than five languages.

                      Gods might not necessarily be assumed to be physiologically built for understanding language the way that humans are, but I think the comparison can be made close enough to facilitate plausibility in the idea of many of them understanding human languages that they don't regularly speak themselves. Yu-Shan is isolated sure, but gods can have the advantage of existing for hundreds of years and hearing prayers.
