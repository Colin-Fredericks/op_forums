    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey View Post
    I'm not quite sure there's enough meaningful difference between building a harbor full of shipyards and constructing the Great Wall. I mean, there is clearly a huge difference, but I think that it fits with the way the resources system is designed, where each dot represents more of a leap than the last.
    While I realize that the system is basically exponential, I still worry that once the PCs get to the point of ruling a significant chunk of territory in their own right, it will be a matter of "all the projects are now NA level", and that could get both boring, and irritating to the ST if there aren't useful guidelines for different levels of projects at this scale.

    What I'd suggest is something along these lines. Have Scope NA, written up as

    "Scope N/A: Truly massive projects, beyond the scope of even the wealthiest of individuals to finance. At this level, such projects require mobilizing the resources of entire regions of Creation, which means the initiator either has to convince the rulers of such a region to back them, or be the ruler themselves. A project undertaken solely by a region of a size that matches the requirement will usually involve a notable fraction of the populace making sacrifices, whether in the form of labor, wealth, or other resources. As such, complications at this scope frequently involve unwillingness on the part of the populace to support the project. Since these sorts of projects can often serve as the centerpiece of entire chronicles, the normal guidelines for complications don't apply - the Storyteller can and should continue to add complications to projects of NA scope for as long as that would be narratively satisfying. The following levels of Scope NA are available:

    Scope NA (City): Such projects need the resources of a community of significant size (usually 10,000 or more people). They can include such endeavors as creating a massive shipyard capable of mass-producing warships on a production line, damming a major river to form a huge reservoir, or opening and thoroughly exploiting a resource such as a forest or a complex of mines that has not previously been utilized. Projects at this scale can take years or decades to complete.

    Scope NA (Provincial): At this level, a project needs the resources of a large region, containing multiple cities, to have a hope of completion (a single huge metropolis, on the scale of Nexus or the Imperial City, might suffice). They typically take multiple decades to finish. Examples include building a truly monumental tomb for a pharaoh, constructing an entirely new city, or forging a trading empire that links multiple rich areas of Creation together in a network that enriches all participants (though particularly the originator).

    Scope NA (National): A national-scope project will demand the resources of an entire country. Such massive projects include fortifications that protect entire regions, monumental architecture that will go down in history as a wonder of Creation, or the herculean task of setting up a functioning bureaucracy for a new nation, starting completely from scratch. Projects of this scope require multiple decades or centuries to finish.

    Scope NA (Imperial): Projects of imperial scope require the resources of multiple nations, all yoked together and pulling as one. In the Age of Sorrows, only the Realm is capable of projects of this scope, and even they almost never initiate them. Examples of imperial projects include converting a formerly-wilderness region into a fully functional nation-sized area, building the absolutely grandest architecture possible, or creating a system of economic exploitation that draws on the resources of many peripheral nations to enrich the imperial center, without absolutely devastating the periphery in short order. These projects require multiple centuries.

    Scope NA (Universal): At this level, a project demands the resources of entire plane of existence. Only all of Creation working together, the united Nations of Autochthonia, or the entire Underworld could accomplish something of this level. Such projects require many centuries or millennia to finish. Examples include setting up defenses that protect a significant chunk of the entire dimension, or radically reorganizing the economy of the whole world such that totally different groups now benefit. No polity in Creation in the Age of Sorrows is capable of projects of this scope, and even in the Age of Glory when the Exalted Host was more unified, they were extremely rare.


    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey
    Maybe some example complications? Or what about complications that have a value, and the combined value of complications shouldn't exceed the scope of the project? I'm trying to think of a way to do complications that just delay work without making it too intricate, because if there's one thing Exalted doesn't need it's another really finicky system. It might be enough to add in to complications that some merely cause long delays?
    Example complications would be nice, for sure. I do agree that there should be some guidelines for limiting complications, though I'm not sure they need to be quite so codified as giving them a value and totaling that up. I think I'd prefer to say that a few complications of a given level just combine into one more significant one, and then say the ST should introduce only a certain number of complications per project. Here's a suggestion for complication levels:

    Minor Complications: Complications of this level aren't worth bothering with - they reduce a project's efficiency and make it a bit more annoying to finish, but don't really threaten to prevent its ultimate completion. Examples include the sort of petty corruption and mistakes that dog practically every endeavor. A good roll when putting together a project or the use of charms like Speed the Wheels can be stunted as virtually eliminating complications of this level.

    Significant Complications: These sorts of hindrances don't necessarily prevent a project from finishing, but they certainly make it cost more or take much longer if they aren't addressed. Significant complications can include a major supply of material needed for the project being cut off, heavy embezzlement or other corruption, or active hindrance by other, less-powerful, groups that don't want the project to go forward. A project experiencing a significant complication will usually take time to complete appropriate to the next level of scope. A ship-construction project, for example, when the complication of the best source of timber for its hull being cut off appears, could take most of a year to complete instead of only the few months it would normally require.

    Critical Complications: At this level, complications not only delay projects, they threaten to prevent them ever being completed. Corruption that drains most of the capital invested, destruction or other forms of denial of basic infrastructure, or the opposition of figures with the authority to prevent the project, are all forms that complications at this level might take. Until a critical complication is resolved, a project cannot be completed, though progress can sometimes continue depending on the exact nature of both project and complication, so that completion is relatively swift once the complication is removed.

    Overwhelming Complications: An overwhelming complication is one so great that it prevents both completion and any effective progress. Examples of such complications include disasters that destroy all significant infrastructure and material for the project, corruption that removes, not only the wealth invested in the project, but also the overall resources of the individual or polity that finances it, or the opposition of political figures with the power of life or death, usually to both the project itself and the entity supporting it. Overwhelming complications must always be dealt with before any progress can be made, and if this is not done swiftly, they often effectively become impossible to overcome, meaning the same project can't be attempted in the same region at all.

    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey
    Oh right, I actually also DID think of this but forgot to put it in. Committing Resources equal to one dot higher than the project would normally require treats it as one Scope lower than it is, although that's the limit. It doesn't really matter how much money you throw at a problem you simply aren't building a blue water sailing ship in a single night, that's the domain of sorcery or maybe highly specialized charms.
    I definitely agree that there's a limit to how much resources you can throw at a project practically. Based on my suggestions above, I'd recommend allowing someone to spend up to 2 Resources more than required (or, if you're powerful enough, requisitioning the resources of a higher-level region), and letting the project organizer choose to reduce the Scope by 1, leaving a dot as a buffer against significant complications (each dot spent this way means ignoring the delaying effects of one significant complication), or, if they spend two full dots of Resources, effectively immunizing the project against a reasonably wide category of critical complication (so, for instance, the organizer could declare that they're spending the extra resources on making sure they have lots of redundant infrastructure, thus ensuring that destroying that doesn't count as a critical complication).


    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey
    It would have to be at something of an appropriate level though, having one dot of followers (Carpenters) doesn't drop building a nation sized palisade wall down from N/A to 5.
    No, that's definitely true. I'd say offhand that Followers count as Resources equal to their dot level if they've been defined as having appropriate skills (100 carpenters can probably build a ship), but as two dots lower (but 2 or 3-dot Followers always count as at least 1 dot) for projects that they aren't appropriate for. Multiple sets of Followers don't count as more Resource dots, though - you can't stack 5 3-dot Followers merits to get the equivalent of Resources 15. You can, at best, get a 1-dot boost if you commit 10 sets of Followers of the same dot level.

    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey
    Although I have to say it's making me wonder how those carpenters are feeding themselves.
    Personally, I feel that Followers and similar merits (Command, Retainer, etc.) should come with the assumption that you have sufficient income or other resources to feed and pay them, at least in normal circumstances. You could be a merchant prince with several sets of Followers and a Command, for instance, but no Resources - the assumption would just be that pretty much all your income from your merchant activities was going to support your entourage, and didn't leave you any disposable income left over. Note that is only for "normal use", whatever that means for the people in question. If you're going to be having your hundred Followers shipped across the Dreaming Sea to help on a project somewhere far from their home, you'd best have the Resources to pay for their travel expenses, or at least the spare treasure.

                    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey
                    Okay I think I’m understanding a bit better. You’re saying you might want complications that don’t necessarily require action to keep the project going, so that maybe if two crop up at once one can be ignored?
                    Yeah, and to provide more scope for interesting complications that actually stick around and continue to cause problems.

                    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey
                    I mostly ask because, knowing PCs, if there’s a problem with their work they’re going to fix it. Actual humans are often lazy, but PCs tend to be rabid about going on adventures to fix problems.
                    Sure, that's my experience as well. But that's actually why I think not all complications should totally halt the project. If a complication stops a project the PCs want to do, they'll probably drop other stuff and go fix it, because the tasks they choose themselves are always going to weigh more heavily on them than stuff that's come up incidentally. But if a complication only delays a project, I think it's much more likely that there'll be an actual dilemma. If the PCs' choices are "go and deal with the Guild member who's raising prices for timber and delaying our navy construction" or "go fight the invading army that's coming over the northern border right now", I think there's a much better chance that they'll choose the latter, whereas if the choice is between "deal with the Guild Factor who's totally blocking our shipbuilding project from proceeding at all" vs. stopping the invasion, they're more likely to go for the former.

                    Also, by having lower-level complications available that delay projects without absolutely stopping them, I think the ST can throw more of them at the players without inspiring as much resentment. If the ST says "the following complications have arisen, stopping all three projects. Which one will you deal with, leaving the other two stalled", that's more likely to make people mad than if the option is "which problem will you deal with first, while the other projects happen more slowly?"

That way, the ST could still throw complications into the mix, but the PCs would get some ability to narrow down the possibilities of which ones arose, customizing them to their skillsets.

                        Say I want to build a dam. There's not much more I can do than to plan the project. And I might plan it really well. I roll 100 successes.

                        But that doesn't tell me what I don't know.

                        I don't know if the people I'm relying on for wood are going to be attacked by the undead. I don't know that the land I plan on flooding is protected by powerful gods. I don't know that there's a First Age city under the river.

                        With 100 successes, I could plan for all these contingencies... if I want to pay for all these contingencies. I'm cognisant enough of the risks to know they exist, in the abstract, and I know what steps I should take to mitigate them. But there's a thousand thousand things which could go wrong but almost certainly won't. Planning for them all will slow the project to a snails pace and massively inflate the cost.

                        So sure, I know building the dam will flood upriver. But there's a nice basin, and I've chose to build the dam there (with Intelligence, not Charisma). I didn't know that a rare animal the ecosystem depends on lives in that basin, or that the basin has high levels of lye which will be washed away, or that zombies would eat my lumberjacks. I could have found these things out, but there's a reason I want that dam and I don't fancy spending the next forty years investigating every single thing that could possibly go wrong and planning contingencies.

                        100 successes doesn't mean nothing surprises you. It means when zombies eat your lumberjacks, you've got enough money in the slush-fund to hire ghostly loggers. You still need to treat with the undead, which is why it's a complication -- these people just gave their lives for you, now you're ruining their deaths, they want some serious workman's comp.

                        Further, I've come up with some pretty darn flawless plans in my life. Every single one of them was fouled up by someone not doing as they told. If you plan a perfect dam but your brother ignores the plan, embezzles all the money, makes the dam hollow and frames you for murder then that's a pretty serious complication.

                        If I understand you correctly, your concern is the player thinking that a really good planning roll means nothing can go wrong because they're so smart. If your players consider themselves smart IRL, ask if nothing has ever gone wrong in their life. If they think people not following their plans or something unexpected happening makes them less smart. If your players don't consider themselves smart, and think their life would be perfect if they were more intelligent, then take a moment to validate them as a person and reassure them that even smart people aren't omniscient.

                        The Exalted are not omniscient.

                                            Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey View Post

                                            Ah, bingo, I think I found the cause of our misunderstanding. I was thinking that, for the leadership system to be meaningful, the leader would have to be watching over it pretty much as a full time job, with days off, maybe a vacation here and there, but otherwise pretty much they're managing it.
                                            Yeah, I wouldn't use the system if that was the assumption, to be honest. While I have no objection to the idea of the Heroic Project Manager being a viable character concept in Exalted, actually playing out that time, or even just having the other PCs put their activities on hold and go for an extended downtime sequence while the Bureaucracy character handles her projects, doesn't strike me as fun at all. Note how Exalted 3e has handled other extended projects, like artifact crafting or sorcerous workings: they're all explicitly described as stuff you can make progress off-screen on, as long as you've got access to the materials/location. Bureaucracy projects should be the same way, I strongly feel. In fact, the typical project is probably easier to narratively justify that sort of hands-off approach than most projects, since, in all but the smallest projects, the leader isn't the one doing the actual work, they've just provided direction. An upper manager who has to constantly tweak and micro-manage a task is a sign of a poor manager, not a supernally skilled one. The truly great planner can create a scheme that anticipates most problems before they happen, and give her underlings instructions that are easily followed and account for any minor issues that occur. To continue a project's progress while the leader is away, I'd only require the leader to be in somewhat regular communication with the people carrying it out, along the lines of "progress reports delivered via mail once a season". More immediate communication would only be required in the aftermath of a complication arising and being resolved, at which point some new instructions to get back on track would be useful.

                                                    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey View Post
                                                    How do you square that with projects that take place at a different timescale to other character’s plans though? If you’re building the Great Wall of Daraheim and it’s projected to take 5 years even with your greatest Solar efforts there will always be the need to insert some downtime otherwise you’d never finish.
                                                    Well, a couple of things - first, charms like Speed the Wheels exist, and can often greatly shorten the required time for a project.

                                                    I don't think they can necessarily completely shorten all the time required for a not-purely-bureaucratic project, like building a wall, but they can still shorten it a lot by making sure all the logistics perfectly line up. If the Great Wall of Daraheim would be a Scope N/A (National) level project that would take a century to complete, an Essence 5 Solar with Speed the Wheels could shorten all the required time to arrange supplies and workers, ready on the site to begin building, down to mere minutes. Of course, placing brick on brick takes longer, but probably still not that long, especially if the project leader also arranged for things like shifts working all day and night.

                                                    The second thing is that yes, there's definitely going to be times when it makes sense for there to be a downtime montage - the PC Circle doesn't go on adventures for a year, the Solar bureaucrat works on a variety of project to improve their nation, the Solar general boosts their armies' training, the Solar sorcerer completes a bunch of workings to buff their region, and so forth. In fact, I'd call opportunities like that pretty essential for the pacing of the game. Without them, the PCs just hit Essence 5 way too fast, there's no sense of the grand scope of Creation, and it's very hard to justify seriously threatening responses from other nations or other actors. What I don't like is the idea that any time a bureaucrat starts a project, such downtime is automatically required, or the project leader has to stop working on their projects in order to join their Circle for adventures.
