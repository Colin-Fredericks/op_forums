    Originally posted by Sakii View Post
    It doesnt really matter because i alredy got my answer. Terrestrial ambition 2 or 3.
    My response was dependent on the fact that you'd need to have the armor to be tattooed on hand, e.g. a moonsilver chain shirt to be rendered down and "painted" on someone's skin, and that it'd still have attunement costs as per other artifact armor, just like artifact tattoos from 2e. It's fine to take that light artifact armor you found in your tomb and have it converted into a tattoo, but if you're looking to completely bypass the crafting process and create the equivalent of light artifact armor from whole cloth (so to speak), that's definitely more than a Terrestrial working; Celestial 2 includes the ability to bestow supernatural powers, for instance, but even then I'd be very reluctant to pass over the artifact crafting process.

    Consider that, while Wood Dragon's Claws provides light artifact weapon traits until dismissed, that comes at the nontrivial cost of being incapable of fine manipulation, and that whole thing about having giant wooden arms that are impossible to conceal. The listed spells that grant soak do so for one day (Invulnerable Skin of Bronze) or (Essence) hours (Incomparable Body Arsenal), which are Terrestrial and Celestial, respectively, and have their own nuances - having bronze skin or basically turning into a metal golem for the duration of the spell.

    If you wanted all-day soak without an artifact, I'd recommend thinking outside the box and looking to history and legend for inspiration. For instance, consider Gawain of Arthur's Round Table, whose power waxes with the rising of the sun. Just like with the restrictions on sorcerous immortality, I think something flavorful like "gains soak and hardness equivalent to light armor during the five hours the sun is at its peak" would do nicely as a Terrestrial Ambition 2 working, or Celestial 2 if you want it from sunrise to sunset. In that case, I wouldn't call for attunement and would be fine skipping out on the artifact crafting process.

    As a parting note, here I'm talking about soak and hardness only; I'm divorcing it from artifact crafting with the understanding that the sorcerer (or her beneficiary) doesn't have any other benefits - she wouldn't be able to buy Evocations, for instance, since she's only benefiting from a working and not wearing actual armor. If she undertook a working to bond existing artifact armor to her skin, on the other hand, then she'd have to deal with the usual pros and cons of wearing artifact armor, e.g. access to Evocations, attunement costs, Charm restrictions, etc.

                    I can't answer the first with any confidence because I don't have MotSE, and a proper answer to the second probably hinges on having the Charm text handy as well. I will say that if the Charm allows your familiar to head a battle group, your familiar should be the one to issue orders, and would do so in the usual way i.e. rolling ([Charisma, Appearance, or Intelligence] + War). At my table, I'd allow you to roll for the order action yourself and have the familiar simply act as a relay, but that would consume an action on both characters' turns. Otherwise, the familiar can issue orders on its own without interfering with your own actions. Again, these answers are uncorroborated by the Charm text, based only on intuition and my piecemeal understanding of the Charm from hearsay.

                    Spirit-Tied Pet's effect text references a familiar, and the descriptive text mentions the Solar reaching out to her "companion animal". That indicates to me that the Charm is meant to be used on familiars, and doing so strengthens the bond in the listed manner. Keep in mind that as familiars are attained via Story Merits, and so don't necessarily cost xp after chargen; they're instead "paid for" with roleplaying and maybe a handful of dice rolls.

                                A question about poison. A poison's penalty applies to "all actions the character takes while poisoned". I take it this doesn't apply to static values like Defense or Guile because employing these does not strictly count as an action in game terms. For the former, "It’s safe to assume a character in a fight is trying to stay alive as best he can. As such, self-protection doesn’t require any action taken—it’s always happening!", and an analogous clause can be inferred to apply for Guile.

                                This seems a teensy bit odd to me because it closes off the possibility of, say, drugging the master swordsman in order to make him vulnerable to assassination, or getting the satrap drunk (assuming alcohol is modeled as a non-damaging poison) to weaken his stoic facade. From a natural language standpoint, it takes some effort to dodge or parry an attack, or to keep up a poker face. Would it make poisons too strong if their penalties applied to Defense, Guile, and other unrolled dice pools? Or would effects like these be better modeled as something other than poison?

                                                Yeah, I brought this up once before, and people were just like "wow, I never noticed that before."

                                                The fact that the protagonist is a man who suddenly gains incredible magic power that's cursed to make him go insane, and is hunted down by a religious order who think he's inevitably going to become a demonic monster, but is actually just the reincarnation of an ancient leader in a pre-apocalypse age of peace and magitech, and must now fight the 13 ancient wizards from that time who work for a force of pure evil and hate... is actually complete coincidence.
                                                Or rather, a lot of these things are standard tropes in fantasy, so come up again and again. The lost golden age, for example, and reincarnated heroes.

                                                And of course, you also notice the similarities more than the differences. I mean, the religious stuff is a lot more Judeo-Christian than Exalted, despite reincarnation, and the strange northern wastes are much more like Warhammer's Chaos Wastes than they are like Exalted's bordermarches.
                                                And there are nature-loving pacifist ogres.
                                                Wheel of Time does try a bit to break out of the Eurofantasy, and is more successful than some, but it's mostly fairly standard stuff.
