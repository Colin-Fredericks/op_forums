        So, I shall start the section describing life on the Blessed Isle with the sidebar that goes into life in the not-Blessed Isle. It’s a good thing to have, seeing how perspectives on and relations with the satrapies has often seemed like a neglected subject, and contributed to the sense that the Realm’s actual power abroad was limited.

        Hmm, it appears that Dynastic takes on foreign ideas are somewhere between haute couture and appropriation, adopted in exaggerated form and quickly forgotten while the lower levels of the upper crust go to significant expense to emulate them. For the time being, I’m going to assume that a stasis among the peasantry is something that owes a lot to actual policies and sumptuary laws that curtail social mobility.

        The satrapy side being less influenced on the whole by cultural projection from the Realm is a bit less than what I expected, but makes sense with how there’s a level of separation. I feel that it also speaks a bit to how the Blessed Isle doesn’t exactly cultivate a national culture that could be projected in the first place, seeing how the elites very deliberately hold themselves apart. Thinking about the description of the Immaculate Order’s presence, I do think there’s something attractive in picturing the Blessed Isle as relatively diverse culturally, but locked into a form of uniformity by centrally controlled administration and established religious organisation. Hence, the Order’s missionaries probably becomes the main way in which the Realm projects soft power, and some form of bringing them in line culturally.

        Okay, going on to the Blessed Isle proper. Speaking of diversity, I think this introduction does a good job of setting up the point that it’s quite large, so in addition to being able to hold a massive number of cities and enormous agrarian countryside in which to generate and transmit wealth, it’s still capable of holding a lot of nooks and crannies that go unobserved by the state and in which marginal people can take refuge. I’ve sometimes wondered about how the distribution of the Dragon Blooded can function on the Isle, so if it’s going for an idea that they’re primarily urban then I’m all for it (I imagine that many still maintain lavish country holiday homes, and that a rather sizable number of these contribute to the mentioned ruins when abandoned by the death or disinterest of their owners).

        Before continuing with the text, my eye is caught by the large splash art piece. I like the shading and colouring on this one; I find that it has something of a comic book style, but with a slight nod for the photorealism that has been popular with a few pieces in this Edition. I like how expressive and colourfully dressed and diverse in appearance the peasants are, and the inclusion of a few different character models for participating women (with the particular focus on one who looks like she was a normal farm worker prior to attempting to smash in the heads of soldiers with a mallet). The city makes for a nice backdrop with its towers; having recently read over the Making Of art book and its description of Realm architecture, I wonder if there will be an emphasis on how the overcrowded cities tend to build upwards.

        Speaking of building upwards, starting with the lowliest class in the Realm. I don’t think there’s very much new to the dispossessed (besides being called that rather than disenfranchised… which I’d say makes sense, as there wasn’t exactly a franchise to remove from them), just providing the circumstances in which they attain that status and the basically non-existent rights, although it does give a bit more weight to the cultural background of the practice (such as the strength of Dynast testimony). The detail on how dispossession is not a hereditary status stands out to me, as does the idea that in a lot of cases that can end up being a distinction without a difference; I can imagine a lot of interesting backstories or dramatic scenarios revolving around subjects such as having gotten adopted into a peasant family or needing to grow up with the limits imposed by your parents stigma. I want to say that this is a new detail, but can’t quite find a direct First Edition description of the disenfranchised; Exalted: the Dragon Blooded merely refers to them as a matter of course, the core book gives a picture that is very different to what I’m familiar with, and the idea of it as a punishment for failing to meet tax obligations seems to be introduced in Manacle & Coin (which in context now looks like a recent practice). It would appear that the version I’m most familiar with, resembling this one, originated in the Second Edition core’s (excellent) description of the Realm. Anyway, I also find it interesting to have a sidebar referring to the matter of enslaving them, how there are propositions to make doing so legal, and opposition from the morally and commercially inclined alike.

        On that note, it goes into the matter of actual slaves, being viewed as actually a rank above. I get the impression that slavery is somewhat harsher in some respects now, with the idea that they have no rights whatsoever (I take it that the point about owning property refers to a thing in practice, rather than something that they’re entitled to). I like the way that a sense of history and financial savvy is given to House Cynis (being now firmly established as less than three hundred years old) in the idea that they made even slavery non-hereditary to ensure that their peers would always need to purchase a steady stream of replacements; I suppose that such children are typically destined for Immaculate orphanages (between this and the dispossessed, the book does seem to have a bit of a priority of creating a significant number of parentless children). There’s a compelling contrast between the point that there can be Dynasts who view slaves as friends and confidantes, at the same time as there is liable to be abuse as a matter of course, and the state itself is inherently traumatic. It kind of puts me in mind of stories in which a point-of-view character becomes trapped in the orbit of some charismatic but extremely dangerous and volatile figure, like The Last King of Scotland. The sidebar on abolitionism is a curiosity for having such an out-of-character reference, but makes its point on how the sentiment exists enough (not entirely for moral reasons) that there are legal limitations imposed.

        Now, the peasantry, a particular subject of interest to me, and right off the bat it does indeed clarify how it includes everybody between the labourer and the wealthy merchant. Honestly, I question this shift in terminology, as it strikes me as being ripe for confusion and rather out of sorts with the colloquial and historical use of the term “peasant”. In discussion, it also seems to be something that is going to need to endlessly require qualifiers on whether or not one is speaking of the expected agrarian working class or people of significant means. I’ve been suspicious of this since the Third Edition core, and now that it’s laid down unambiguously I just do not understand it.

        That aside, it’s an interesting bit of nastiness that Dynasts are empowered to some degree to inflict summary executions, and I do like it making the point that the richest merchants can overshadow some patricians, and thus be afforded a form of mobility.

        Now, the patricians. I’ve often thought that the patricians occupied a bit of an awkward spot in the game, lying at an intersection of ideas and presumptions that never quite communicated with one another, ending with them being left in a spot envisioned as generic wealthy aristocrats (despite the idea that land ownership is all deferred to the Empress), and otherwise kind of ignored. So it’s good here to clearly and unambiguously lay out precisely who they are in the very first line: the administrative class of the Realm. In an empire where rent-seeking is curtailed and aristocrats need to work for a living, your lower aristocracy becomes strictly defined as “the people who are drawn upon for all of the middle management and general bureaucratic processing work”. It’s clear, direct, and effectively plays off of both the established structure of the Realm as a whole and gives context to their relationships with the Great Houses.

        What’s especially interesting is the idea that this leaves them in a position where Dynasts actually compete to secure their favour. I suppose that the luminaries of the Houses are practical enough to recognise that they need staff to manage their holdings and agendas, and that it costs less to make appeals to them than it would be to hold oneself loftily and find all of the good patricians scooped up by rivals; seeing as Dynasts need to work for a living, they don’t quite have the luxury of making patricians crawl over one another in seeking their patronage. And it gives another concreate thing for Dynasts to actually intrigue over, as well as people to fill out their social scene beyond their own somewhat limited numbers. It’s a good adjustment that provides a means for patricians to be a more engaging part of the Realm. The only thing I think it really sacrifices is my notion of Dynasts competing over control of wealthy heirs and heiresses, and really, who else cares about that?

        …Or it could just be transferred to aristocracies of the satrapies.

        And in the interests of making the Blessed Isle more engaging across all levels, there’s the point about how patricians end up in a closer proximity to the peasantry. For the sake of my sanity, I’m going to combine that reference to patrician landowners with a few other details placed around the book and read it as an intended shorthand for “patricians assigned to administer peasant-worked estates on behalf of the Empress or Dynasts” rather than literally being in possession of land from which they can live off the rents. Oy.

        And a little reference to the value of begetting Dragon Blooded from the perspective of patricians. I’m picturing an intriguing chain of interests and power plays, in which you have Dynasts plying patricians with vast wealth, who then employ that to attract marriage from outcastes, and work that towards a situation in which one of their own Exalts and they get to have higher negotiations with the Dynasts.

        What Fire Has Wrought of course does the bulk of the work describing the Dragon Blooded in the Realm, but a summary is not amiss, and I think it’s a prime opportunity to clarify the exact place of the unExalted Dynast; namely, still ranked far above the patricians and objects of significant wealth and privilege, at the same time as being on the lowest rung of their family ladders and knowing it. Outcastes have some of the same benefits due to Dynasts, but lack the proper status unless they can actually join a Great House, and otherwise end up being the top dogs in the lower patrician class. Cadet house members are technically Dynasts but are viewed as lacking the social graces, and are simply less wealthy, aaaand the true members of the Scarlet Dynasty are placed at the top of the pyramid, this being where it reintroduces the point that at any given time, about half are employed and the rest are engaged in grandiose personal pursuits.

        The description on Dynast naming conventions was something I quite appreciated in What Fire Has Wrought, since I think it’s helpful to get a feel for the community, actually guide the creation of characters, and offsets a bit of prior books putting out names without much apparent consistency. Hence, I’m glad that the same standard is being applied across the whole Realm society. Let’s see… most peasants having no family names and tend to go for short names referencing nature, makes sense; I’ll be interested to see if any of those modified immigrant cultures show up. I like the idea that patricians can often end up with family names that, in using natural imagery, speak to peasant origins; I’m imagining some cutting Dynast wordplay that draws attention to that in a manner intended to be derisive. I take it that their given names are similarly made up and euphonic. Dynast conventions are already known, and I like how outcastes include a point of people who’ll often change their name to reflect their newly achieved status; their relationship to family names is also quite distinct. And I’m amused by the idea that the Realm might occasionally press down on satrapies to remove the names of noteworthy rebels from circulation.

        Right, getting into the gritty details of daily life now. Interesting choice to begin with marriage and family; not much to say on that beyond liking how the matriarchal values are promulgated through patricians and wealthier peasants emulating the matchmaking of the Dynasty. It makes sense that the Realm is not actually sexually liberated (amounting to liaisons being kept discrete), and having recently started reading an autobiography from a Chinese writer, I’m intrigued by the notion that women of the Blessed Isle might engage in a system of concubinage similar to Chinese men. You know, thinking about that might put the matter of wealthy merchants being classed as peasants into perspective to me… setting that aside, it strikes me as another thing that might reinforce matriarchal qualities, as well as perhaps a tendency towards smaller families; in a system where a man can have multiple women bearing legal children, the size of his family reflects his status, but with a woman with multiple men I suppose it becomes a system where the identity of the father is given less significance and the family values become more concentrated. Perhaps that would lend itself to why the lower classes end up with closer relationships to their children than Dynasts have.

        And then you have slaves and the dispossessed engaging in the age-old practices of conducting marriage among themselves regardless of legal sanction, as an assertion of their basic humanity.

        I’ve always had a fondness for the description of contemporary trends in Dragon Blooded armour, so I’m glad that there’s a sidebar (again effectively abridged) here referring to the prevailing styles, while also making the point of how they fit into Immaculate aniconism; I’d also say it’s good to rename the somewhat generic Elemental style to Dragonscale. Festivals being such a significant aspect of pre-modern life makes them something I think the game ought to have given more attention to, so I’m glad that there’s a sidebar addressing the subject and laying down some standards, particularly for it going beyond the Immaculate calendar and into various local practices and state proscriptions. The idea of the Realm having a feast of fools during Calibration, that even Dynasts engage in, is especially intriguing.

        I’m finding that the approach to peasant clothing (even within the bounds of the definition being broadened) to be a bit more sophisticated; the working class being not presumed to wear ragged material speaks a bit better for them, as well as the notion that they might have some different wear for retiring. I like how clothing styles in general are given quite a bit more elaboration and variation, since I do think the First Edition rendering of “everybody in white body wraps” was a little bit… uninspired. Also the way that they’re attributed jewellery, which even if not made of precious material probably has a fine artistry.

        I find something amusing in the idea that patrician fashions include togas, as it gives me the impression of trying very hard to look ostentatious; similar goes for how they employ especially large, eye-grabbing hats. I like how there’s an idea that male fashions are designed to be slinky, for the sake of being attractive to Dynasts.

        After all of that, it’s fun how the Dynasts themselves revert to structurally simpler and more practical forms of clothing, while also being lavishly cut and coloured to ensure that they stand out. It gives me this impression that the status of Dynasts is intended to be denoted with fine materials and expert tailoring, but also a capacity to rapidly engage in action as needed; you wouldn’t want to go around in clothing that you could end up being entangled in, or that heavy action would lead to being torn up. It makes them stand out a bit amidst historical aristocracy, where the intention was to specifically flaunt one not needing to engage in physical strains with voluminous clothing and delicate features; I see some of that in the idea that their unExalted members like sturdy boots and gloves.

        Now, let’s see about this art and architecture. My memory of the Dragon Blooded relationship to aniconism was shaky, but I’m glad to see that they’re afforded an exception for the sake of their mansions and estates being decorated with portrait and sculpture, even if the general attitude has given the Realm a sophisticated tradition of calligraphy, illumination and filigree that I’d imagine even the less pious Dynasts would want to get in on. It’s also good that there’s a reminder that when sculptures come up, they’re liable to be painted. The description of architectural styles and priorities is less elaborate than I would have looked, but I’m happy it conveys a clear aesthetic, and it’s nice to have a detailed standard for the format of Dynast houses considering they’re likely to be a central feature of many games. It also speaks to their distinct luxury and wealth that they don’t merely go in for singular, if enormous buildings; that also lends itself to my presumption that their homes are frequently abandoned and left to ruin, owed to things like the probable expense of maintaining them and the likelihood that they’re so tailored to the needs and preferences of the individual that it’s often hard to find somebody else that would want them.

        Furniture and house decorations would be a major element of conspicuous consumption in such an environment, so good to focus a bit on the trends there. I’d say that it strikes a good balance of folding in the supernatural qualities of the setting by referencing the use of geomancy in construction of actual manses, while also grounding it in the idea that such patterns are valued even in buildings that wouldn’t overtly harness Essence.

        I’m going to take a break there, as I’ve got some thing to do. As I was hoping, thus far the cultural sections are more down-to-earth and humanising, while still playing service to the Realm’s particular stratification and varying oppression throughout.

        I have approximate knowledge of many things.
        Write up as I play Xenoblade Chronicles.
