            Yes, but the position of cashier is widespread. As is slave tutors for dynasts, that's standard until they hit primary school. Slave nannys are standard too.

            Widespread educated slavery on the isle is an important point here, mostly because it's likely to be at the forefront of abolitionist thinking in context. To repurpose your metaphor - you are unlikely to have much interaction with a stocker, but the fact that the store has a cashier is someone you talk to, someone you might strike up a friendly conversation with. Ditto educated household slaves - it's a lot easier to see them as people when they're not busy labouring in the sun all day.

            That doesn't make it 'good slavery', it just makes it more likely to lead to abolitionism. And it's worth noting that slavery in the Realm is generally kinda a weird mishmash historically of various traditions.

                    Originally posted by Lanic
                    Now, if we go to a society where slavery is an omnipresent institution, the idea of abolitionism may become virtually unimaginable.

                    In classic Rome, a free man never works for another free man. When Caesar Augustus was establishing something like state's office, it was obvious that those high-rank officials were chosen from among his slaves - they were working for him after all (later, equites started to take over these positions - too much money and power).

                    As far as I know, in the whole vast ethical literature of antiquity there is no single person to question the institution of slavery. This includes philosophers who themselves were former slaves. It just didn't appear to them that society can be organised that way, the same way it doesn't appear to most of us that we could intervene into ecosystem out of care for animals. Of course, if you were a decent person, you probably treated your slaves well, you agreed them to marry their chosen spouse, maybe you even liberated the most trusted of them - thus you were not even dehumanising these people. If you were a philopher, you might encourage others to treat their slaves well and argue that this is the right thing to do. You just never thought that there is something wrong with the institution as a whole.
                    So, this is a good point. But tbh, in the Realm slavery isn't an omnipresent institution (whereas serfdom is). Percentage-wise, slavery is quite uncommon (whereas in the Roman Empire, I think it was about a third of the population). Only Dragonblood are allowed to rent slaves, only the Government is allowed to own them, and only one family in the entire continent (a big family, but still) is allowed to import them.
                    If you live in Pangu, it may seem... well, not omnipresent, but common. Everywhere else, it's not.

                                                            Looking into some of the history of abolitionism, I'm discovering that a particular element that gave it momentum in Europe was actual slaves proactively taking advantage of legalisms by which they could argue that their own slavery was not enforceable. The jurisdictions of such cases might have been limited, but they'd serve as a basis for wide movements.

                                                            Obviously the Realm provides no such recourse, and very much encodes the function of slavery in its law. I'm not quite sure about the facts of Roman emancipation, but that would seem to have a thing they have in common; it seems that in Roman jurisprudence, they essentially codified a thing that was not only evident beyond memory in their own society, but in virtually all societies they were in proximity to.

                                                            It seems as though abolition in the Realm would depend very much on the rise of a new power that wants to specifically break from the old one ideologically, ala Revolutionary France.
