        Part of the problem is that tabletop games in the past have sometimes had mechanical failure states where the game becomes pretty unfun for someone at the table. Playing eg. 3e D&D as a fighter in a game where a wizard goes all out is often not very fun (because encounters will come down to "what spell does the wizard use to resolve this" -- all the important tactical options end up being made by the casters, leaving non-casters feeling like they're not really playing.) Ex2 had a lot of similar-ish failure states it could fall into -- though to be fair, even at its worst Exalted has never, in my experience, been as bad as D&D has been at its worst in terms of the "why does your character even bother to show up for fights?" problem.

        3e D&D also has a unique problem where most of your choices are irrevocable and where it's entirely possible for a bad decision to leave your character basically mechanically non-functional. (eg. trying to build a fighter-mage by taking equal numbers of levels of fighter and mage levels, which looks like a logical option at first glance, will lead to a character that is barely able to contribute mechanically at all.) Past experiences like these leave a lot of tabletop gamers paranoid that the game's mechanics are going to fall apart on them if they make the wrong choices.

        (I think growing up playing a certain kind of videogame leads to this, too, because unlike a tabletop game a badly-designed videogame doesn't have any way of recognizing that you've built a low-power character, and will proceed to crush you mercilessly.)

        But Ex3, at least so far, seems a lot more robust. The basic nature of the combat system means that having an additional combatant on your side with any sort of meaningful combat stats at all is always valuable and always puts more pressure on the enemy, while ensuring that nobody can just be casually taken out of a fight.
