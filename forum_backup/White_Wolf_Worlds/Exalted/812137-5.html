                                Hmm. So, I apologize in advance for the tangent, but...this is always something that's made me raise an eyebrow about Exalted.                                 So I have to ask: in what way are player characters intended to address massive, systemic injustices? Injustices such as, say, entire geographical regions needing to keep a thaumaturge (or kidnap one, if there aren't any born to the native population) more or less prisoner to prevent the world from falling apart (I mean that one's straight out of Dark Souls with the Fire-Keepers, and DS is one of the most pessimistic settings that isn't Drakengard)? Or, for an example that's been around a lot longer, the Wavecrest Archipelago depending on industrial-scale human sacrifice to keep the volcano gods appeased?
                                Okay, to answer those specific scenarios, then:

                                The dissolving edge of the world is often described in terms of there just not being enough people out there to reinforce reality sufficiently. Presumably those lands adjacent to the bordermarches could be made less twisted and less subject to the hungry Wyld if they were more fruitful and safe, so they could support larger populations. The short-term solution is inobvious because the edge of reality isn't going to stop crumbling until you get past a certain tipping point, but the long-term solution is to create a society with health, wealth, and happiness for all, at which point that fortune will serve as a bulwark against encroaching unreality. In the meantime, uh... you may need to keep the thaumaturgy going. Find volunteers? There have to be some people who have the gift of thaumaturgy for whom "Learn mystic secrets and serve as the barrier between Is and Is Not" is something like a calling.

                                (Of course, that just means the lands next door are unstable, and somebody's gonna move in there eventually. Maybe break out the reality engines or, I dunno, bend the world into an eternal sphere so it has no edge. That last one seems pretty popular in actual play! This is a sort of long-term project.)

                                Addressing the problem of the volcano gods usually just amounts to punching volcano gods and, again, raising up society to a sufficient level of health, wealth, and happiness that nobody is building their settlements within eruption range of volcanos. Modern Hawaii seems to do okay -- it's volcanically active and they just, uh, don't make anyone live where the lava will be. Again, this is an infrastructure problem -- if your society is structured such that you need rich volcanic soil to feed people, and your transportation is sufficiently bad the only way to reliably have access to it is to live close enough to the volcano that you'll die if it erupts, then you're in a bind, but it's not insoluble.

                                    Ah, good.

                                    To elaborate on where I'm coming from, it always seemed to me, ESPECIALLY in 2E, that virtually every problem in Creation had some magic-flavored but ultimately-technocratic solution...that RAN ON HUMAN BABIES. A particular, recurring case was "Horrible supernatural tyrant who makes hard choices that other people won't, and deposing them will only lead to chaos". I think that the reason it was hard to call out the Perfect of Paragon as notably awful was that "random mortal chances upon awesome supernatural power, founds Legalist dystopia with self as immortal God-Emperor for the greater good" could easily describe any given Solar (seriously, I've seen SO MANY players decide that the only problem with the Realm was that they weren't in charge).

                                    The weird thing was that the writing kept asking "But is violence really the answer, hmmm?" in cases where yes, violence was very definitely the answer. Not to seem like I'm picking on you, personally, but I'm thinking of a hypothetical you raised in a thread about the role of priests in Creation. It involved a river god who kept demanding sex slaves in exchange for not flooding the farming villages along the river's banks, and how decent mortal priests would be able to bargain him down, while a Zenith would just threaten to murder him unless he fell in line, followed by an footnote that you really shouldn't do this because then there wouldn't be a god around to keep the river from flooding. This struck me as odd, mostly because of the constant debates over the material importance of gods. If the god cannot be slain without harming the river itself, then he enjoys an irreducible advantage in negotiations, and any deals you make (without an Eclipse bearing witness) will last exactly until you're out of earshot. If he can, then he's merely a rent-seeking parasite, and killing or sparing him - or any god - is purely a matter of political expedience. Unless you can actually bring him around to being less of a horrible person, to deal fairly with the villagers instead of regarding them as cattle, which is an option I hope will get more shrift over baby-based fuel economies.

                                            Originally posted by Xanandithras View Post
                                            The river and the river's god are two separate things. It's quite possible to kill the god without causing the river to flood.
                                            Correct. But on the other hand, the god does have a valuable job; it's not just a parasite with no function other than to fuck with mortals. Its job is to observe when things happen to the river outside of the river's current destiny—which is rare, and can be the work of supernatural forces, unpredictable mortals, or just random chance—and correct the problem if possible, or call for more puissant divine aid for problems beyond its capabilities to handle. (Though the latter is often impossible due to the decay of the Terrestrial spirit courts.) If the river floods when it isn't supposed to, or it's contaminated such that everyone in the village will catch cholera, it's the god's responsibility to report the matter up the chain, and to intervene if it's capable.

                                            On the other hand, if Heaven has decided that half the village will die of cholera, it's also the god's job to make sure that no one cleans up that pesky contamination in the water. Suddenly, the desirability of a corrupt, apathetic, incompetent, or weak god—as opposed to one dedicated to its intended purpose—changes considerably.

                                                    Presumably, Ozashun isn't something you can just pray to or otherwise gain its attention without the ritual. Alternatively, the ritual guarantees you success in ways that prayer doesn't - you keep doing the same thing and getting the same result. Something which may or may not be possible with other supernatural beings, but sure is handy when available, especially for mortals who don't get much of an edge when it comes to bargaining. I mean, it's not like whatever you do is guaranteed to gain Alveua's attention unless you have Demon of the Second Circle on hand.

                                                    On a more general note, it's an example of a ritual that's tied to a specific place and a magical issue. It shows what weird shit can happen in Creation, and how thaumaturgy factors into it.

                                                        Originally posted by Stephen Lea Sheppard View Post
                                                        I would basically shy away from using actual numbers because I'm terrible at them and any time I nail a population number down someone else who's better at them than me seizes on it and goes "Aha, if that's true, then THIS IMPLICATION OF THAT must also be true" and then goes on to treat that (unintended) implication as gospel, but here:

                                                        Assume in sparsely-populated "tribal" areas where people tend to gather in groups of, oh, 150, there's like one thaumaturge every ten villages or something, with a new one usually coming along in time for the previous one to take as an apprentice. That is, any given population probably has access to one, but they're sparse enough that using that access may be a pain (unless you happen to be like the one village right next to the witch's hut). This probably means two thaumaturgist every 1,500 people in sparsely populated areas. As population density increases, the natural incidence of thaumaturgists decreases, purely because it's dumb for cities to have as many as that figure suggests. This is also probably inobvious or works on inobvious principles such that it is not possible for e.g. the Realm or Lookshy to deliberately set up a low-population area to "farm" for thaumaturgists to induct into some larger program like sohei-exorcists.

                                                        This is going to hold up about as well to rigorous analysis as vampire population figures in Vampire: The Masquerade or Vampire: The Requiem, i.e. hardly at all, and I don't care. If anyone wants to take a shot at a better set of numbers that achieve the same effect, go wild.
                                                        That is a FAR more common spread of them than I got from reading the entry in the Ex3 core book, but does make the idea that thaumaturgy is worth its space in the book more palatable. Though it still kind-of feels like it's back to being "folk magic" rather than "incredibly rare miracles," I think that's where it SHOULD be.
