            Originally posted by Ghosthead View Post
            Personally, I will always dig my heels in to speak for it being a great concept to have the Great Curse as "Great heroes undone by a moment of extreme pride or rigidity (of moral principle, of belief, of behaviour) that they cannot undo and which has terrible consequences" and for this to affect PCs right from the point of starting play (as opposed to *just* creeping moral decline at some point in the future).
            I've been an Exalted fan since you could pre-order the limited edition 1e corebook, which I did. I've seen a lot of people argue against the Great Curse in that time, and not once have I heard a compelling argument for how you generate epic tragedy without some kind of external mechanic like the Curse. Granted, I think the mechanics haven't been great - the Curse has always been more stick than carrot - but I that's a failure of implementation, not a failure of imagination, and the whole point of iterative design in successive editions is to improve on implementation. A proper Curse influences both player and Storyteller. It's been my experience that players will dig in their heels to keep anything bad from happening to their character and will only rarely sulk like Achilles in his tent, much less drag Hector's body around the walls - and it's even more rare that the Storyteller will have those decisions come back to haunt them.

            Neall Raemonn Price

                That's interesting because I noticed the exact opposite in my games : The mechanics of The Great Curse really acted as a kind of psychological release there the players could go all in, and usually they had a very strong relation to it following the Limit Break where I didn't hesitate to use it as a narrative tools to make them go forward. I must say that after each Great Curse I ask my player to change most of their intimacies, and make sure that they have at least a new Major intimacy that integrates somehow what happened during The Great Curse (and obviously I don't accept something floppy like "I must keep control next time").

                But yes it's true that players will still be the most reluctant to lose things for their character, that's why I think as a ST you have to present The Great Curse as a tool to make the character progress in some way and clearly establish it with the player. That's why in another thread I explained that I'm for a "Let's discuss a specific dramatic situation we want to happen" rather than "You now have X emotion deal with it and let's see the consequences later".

                For example, a Limit Break of one player was that we discussed how she could assassinate her superior because she considered she didn't take the good decisions. Of course if you present it like this, it's not engaging and the rather pacific character would have a strange evolution. So we discussed the deeper reasons she would do this : With her leader dead, she could take power and take the hard decisions that must be done, so it's also a consensus with the other players to let her take the power in some way.

                    Originally posted by Neall View Post
                    the Curse has always been more stick than carrot - but I that's a failure of implementation, not a failure of imagination, and the whole point of iterative design in successive editions is to improve on implementation.
                    If Limit imposed penalties on characters, that would match the idea that Limit represents a supernatural form of stress, and gaining Limit would represent becoming more distressed, and Limit Break would feel more like the euphoric moment in which your character returns to full power and in which the player has no choice (or the perfect excuse) to take that power and run wild.


                    Alternatively, a designer could avoid penalties and simply design Celestial Exalted so that their default power level is represented by a full Limit gauge, and that the euphoric freedom from distress is exceptional enough to be represented with bonuses.

                    These bonuses would gradually decrease as your character gains more Limit, which thus powerfully incentivizes players/characters either to construct scenarios in which they never gain limit (the super-empathetic Hierophant and his metaphorical ivory tower in the First Age), or else to seek out ways to actually GAIN MORE Limit as quickly as possible in order to achieve the rush of Limit Break. (And possibly even both in sequence; tea-totalling until you fall off the wagon, then going on a raging bender.)


                    Under either model, characters might have the ability to Limit Break at ANY TIME, by default.

                    So if you ever gain one point of Limit, and psychologically you just cannot stand suffering any penalty or losing the power of your full bonus, at ANY TIME you could have your character go mad in order to return to full power.

                    An evocative balancing act of selfless suffering versus selfish indulgence. Is your own freedom and power worth more to you than the people you would hurt?

                                We must have been hanging around with different circles. Granted, I have a relatively small group of regular friends that I have games with (and I recognize that I'm blessed in that regard) but we've had a great deal of Drama, often at the PC's own hands, without a Limit Meter showing up. Granted we're kind of atypical-

                                Also, Achilles sulking in his tent may not be the BEST example of things to try and emulate, because it's easy to think of that as "There's a WICKED AWESOME BATTLE and I'm forced to sit here and MISS IT because my Limit Meter went ding" which... I don't think that's a good way to use Limit Break. It should make things more interesting and fun, which yes, can involve disrupting plans and moments of self-sabotage...

                                But in my experience, if someone stats up a Fighter Exalt, they're not gonna want to spend a scene NOT fighting unless they're doing something equally engaging. And "Sitting in this tent while the fight goes on" isn't exactly fun to play through for most people.

                                I'll also say that I actually like the Limit Breaks of Ex3 (even if I don't think the Limit METER is a good way to do it, and instead would give the PCs chances to enter Limit Break and disrupt a scene for Bonus XP) but they never really FELT like they matched what the Great Curse said it was. A slow and steady slide from hero to villainy that can sometimes take decades or centuries to manifest may not be something that can be easily represented in most tabletop games. Let alone "Occasionally you just flip out, but it usually goes away" as a manifestation thereof. It just didn't ever quite mix for me.

                                Thus my thought of "Limit Break feels more natural to me when explained not as a result of a Curse, but as a sense of Sometimes Exalts have too many Feelings"

                                Edit: Also, as for "Most players dig in their heels to keep anything bad from happening-" This is why I liked the DB version of Limit so much, because heck yeah, give players some bonus XP (with the rider that it has to make the game more fun or interesting) and I'm sure Limit Break would go over so much smoother. The base Limit Meter rules even say "Limit Break doesn't have to occur exactly when the meter fills, but can instead just happen later at a dramatic point" seems only a quarter-step away from just going "Limit Break when the GM offers to gain Splat XP for the session"
