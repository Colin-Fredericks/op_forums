    Originally posted by Ferryman View Post
    I don't think anyone doubts that a Solar, given the chance and the right skills, can chew through a thousand-man army. But he can only do that if the army cheerfully engages him on those terms. If his opponent instead splits his army into five 200-man raiding parties and starts burning down villages, though, it doesn't really matter that the Solar could kill all one thousand of them given the chance; the Solar can't protect his kingdom, and his people will lose faith in him because of that.

    That's the sort of thing that organizations and bureaucracies and communications and supply lines and, yes, having an army of your own are for. The writers have made it clear that they don't think Solars should be able to cheat those things away trivially, and should instead engage meaningfully with them. And that's great! So...can we have some material to help us do that?
    Sure those things are very useful indeed in 3e, which is a marked improvement. Point is; Solars are not so restricted by scale as the OP is suggesting. Okay yeah technically your Solar Circle Sorcery can't just create random shit out of no-where, kind of, but you definitely can, by yourself, solve national scale problems through the application of spells, and you can do it at lower levels of sorcery through less potent spells and workings too. A Dawn can totally be the solution to "there's an army attacking and we have a shitty little city guard" problem; he can kill that army, and the threat of that means that you can engage in diplomacy with the line "well, if you do try and pressure your claim against this tributary, I am sure that Gorebastard the Destroyer will take personal offence to that" and it is a legitimate threat. One can imagine the rival prince looking at the gatehouse of his city, and the road to his citadel, and the ranks of his men, and thinking "...yet all of that will not stop this one warrior if he decides to come for my head..." and give up the claim. That's a thing! A cool thing! It's cool to be able to engage with stuff like that.

    Again (and I don't want to focus on one example) you can arbitrarily make it more difficult for one person to do these things in most examples, just like you can arbitrarily make it more difficult for a Solar to beat an adversary in an individual competition of excellence, and it's not a sure thing in either case in 3e, but the practical outcome of the rules in the book by players on a campaign setting include the overturning of scale problems, within reason, through individual capability.

    Which is cool. I like that in Exalted, you can do that stuff if you want to. 3e very much allows for it, too. Especially all the Sorcery!

                            Definitely. I for one am quite happy with the way 3rd ed does this level of things.

                            Honestly, I'm a little confused by many of the assumptions on this thread; I find geopolitics both reasonably easy and quite fun to run, without needing much of a system for the macro-level (I never used any of the Mandate of Creation stuff).
                            The real issue is time; I'm impatient for the players' actions to have effects on a regional scale, when of course, should take months to filter through.
                            The main trick is not overwhelming the players with problems on a regional scale, let the information come reasonably slowly, and give them plenty of downtime.


                            I should also say that, due to the themes and nature of the the game, monarchy-style rulership is the most likely way for PCs to interact with geopolitics. And monarchy is an extremely personal-scale way of doing politics: wars are fought because the princess married this guy and not that guy; when there's a famine or plague the King goes to beg the gods to save the country, and if he doesn't beg well enough or the gods are feeling dickish, he'll likely be deposed; control is maintained by the King literally partying his way across the country, personally meeting and greeting different nobles and charming (or threatening) them.

                            So, running the country is a story, interacting with other countries is a story. And when the PCs come up with a plan for economic rejuvanation, we roleplay through it, (with a few Bureaucracy rolls of course; Int+Bureacracy to notice problems they haven't thought about so they don't do a Mao, Charisma+Bureaucracy to order around bureaucrats, etc). And that's fine.

                            The thing about a bureaucracy or ruling a country system, is that it gives mechanical hooks for bureaucracy charms to hang off. But I think the 3rd ed Bureaucracy charms are pretty good (the 2nd ed ones were not so good), so that's fine too.

                                Interesting post Root.

                                Re: fighting a plague: you will find that a Solar with Medicine alone will have a problem, but one with Investigation, Lore, Survival, and Craft can do it.

                                Investigation and Lore helps the Solar figure out the source of the plague. Survival can help them confirm it in the case of getting close enough to carrier animals. She could be led to discover the source lies in bush meat trade and even isolate the monkeys causing it. In some cases, bad water supply is to blame, and Craft can help her engineer solutions. Medicine helps her figure out what kind of solutions are plausible. Lore can also help if historical records reveal any sort of effective response used in the past.

                                Bureaucracy can also help in creating market models that espouse safe practices, while Lore can be used to educate people on things like hygiene and how to avoid spreading disease when hygiene won't cut it.

                                There isn't a Charm to just end a massive plague outbreak, but with some elbow grease and time the Lawgiver can fight a fire that it takes a modern national relief effort to quell. All of these things require time, and an emphasis on roleplaying and storytelling.

                                We decided to try to avoid ever writing Charms that resolve conflicts, because those conflicts are where the game lives. It is impossible to feel like you are playing a Solar if you are never challenged to use your Abilities.

                                    Mantra of the Solar Hospitaler:

                                    If you want to know the illness, which medicine to use, and the dose: Medicine
                                    If you want to know which plant or animal parts are used to make the medicine and where to find them: Lore
                                    If you want to avoid dying in the jungle or touching dangerous plants while harvesting medicinal herbs: Survival
                                    If you want to synthesize medicines from harvested herbs: Craft
                                    If you want to figure out how people are getting infected: Investigation
                                    If you suspect a plague spirit might be behind it: Occult
                                    If you think market practices are to blame: Bureaucracy

                                                Originally posted by Solar View Post

                                                Weep for the un-included middle, comrades! I'm kidding, but I still think this is a simplification. A good bureaucracy system means that whole bit about implementing agricultural advances across the villages that the Twilight is doing is enhanced and made more enjoyable. A bad system does what you are suggesting, sure, but I don't think the system needs to be bad. It can be pretty good! The Sorcerous Workings system is a good example of a well written sub-system that enhances, rather than degrades, the games theme and the enjoyment of the players.
                                                Sorcerous Workings need a subsystem because they have no real life equivalent to base results on - IRL, the most you can do with being a sorcerer is scam people for money, or maybe get laid. We need rules to establish that Workings are a thing at all, the scope of what they can achieve, how hard it is, and what happens when the sorcerer fails. What makes them fun is that they exist at all, that the player of a sorcerer gets a license to do things beyond the scope of casting fireballs and summoning demonic slaves and such. The rules attached to it are a necessity, they don't enhance the game just by virtue of being a couple rules requiring a couple rolls.

                                                This middle ground I've excluded... it exists, theoretically, but I've yet to see a single bureaucracy/kingdom ruling subsystem that meaningfully taps into it, so forgive me for not paying it much heed. Such systems abstract and simplify things to a large degree by their very nature, and the end result is that you start thinking of things in terms of stats and modifiers rather than the actual problem at hand and how your character can contribute his singular skills to the task.
