                                        Originally posted by kindledRadial View Post

                                        The game could definitely use more elder Exalted QCs with heft, but on the whole, I'm a little skeptical that the answer is to buff the antagonists. A lot of the disparity seems to be the result of how obscenely strong a devoted combat-supernal character can get, but a mid-range combatant with 4-6 dice in their combat pools and two combat charms is supposed to be fun to play in a fight, so if you make it so the former type of character is routinely given a tough time, the latter is going to get slaughtered without a constant tanking escort, and then the game veers toward everyone taking extremely combat-focused builds because they "have" to, which annoys the people who didn't want to build that way, and annoys the actual combat monkeys because they're too similar to everybody else.

                                        I think this is a more structural flaw with the way the game is than perhaps people are giving it credit for. A Solar already starts out as among the best of the best in the thing they're good at, which has the perverse effect of reducing its ability to be an engaging challenge for them. The problem you are running into isn't actually any worse than what happens if you try to run a mystery for a heavily-invested Investigation Supernal character, or naval obstacles at a heavily-invested Sail Supernal character, or what have you.

                                        If you think about it from a setting angle, doesn't it seem like a Melee Supernal Dawn with max or near-max combat stats and a heavy investment in Melee Charms should be able to dispatch most other powerful supernatural warriors easily? Is that not the whole idea of the Dawn Caste, and the destabilizing threat of Solar excellence? If you uplift more than a handful of the greatest adversaries to the point where they're seriously threatening rather than simply having some shot at winning or surviving, doesn't that seem like it's short-selling the whole idea of the legend of war returned from death who shakes the world to its foundations, which is implicitly what someone wants to play when they design a character like that? Moreover, shouldn't a Solar swashbuckler with a much lesser combat investment still be able to put paid to serious opponents like the action hero they're trying to be? It's easy to make it feel like an MMORPG, where all the canned dialogue talks about what a great hero you are but you're still just fighting level-adjusted palette swaps of wolves the whole way through.

                                        You're absolutely right that the outcome is kind of unsatisfactory. However, I'm not sure how to untie that knot without just giving up and running a Dragon-Blooded game. If you look at the stories Dawns like that are based on, they're stories about characters who often seem to be in danger, dancing on the knife's edge between life and death, but always win anyway. You can't do that in a game -- if the mechanics say "by the way, this character always wins" then that's right there and it's nearly impossible to ignore it and pretend they're in trouble, but if the mechanics actually put the character in serious peril all the time, they're going to lose a lot, which feels wrong. The closest you can get to reproducing that narrative structure with actual game mechanics is something like FATE's lose-to-win mechanic, which doesn't capture the feel of it at all and makes the apparent tension of conflict seem like a sort of highly ritualized symbolic theatre performance that I think would not satisfy people who come to the table to play Kenshin.

                                                            What I have ruled in my game, which all my players loved and which definitely helps with this problem, is that anyone can negate an ambush (which negates your Defense and gives the attackers a free combat round) by succeeding at an Awareness check, and if they use a free action to warn the party the party reduces that ambush to a surprise attack (-2 Defense and no free combat round), but only by beating the Awareness check themselves can they completely protect themselves from the deleterious effects of a good ambush. This has gone over quite well, and I would highly recommend adopting it as a house rule to Storytellers.

                                                            Originally posted by Lanaya View Post

                                                            There are degrees of rarity. "Rare" in the sense of "once every month of concerted adventuring which we represent in a single session" is what I want, what I would consider reasonable from a pacing perspective. Rare in the sense of "a battle only seen once an age, only witnessed by a few and recorded in the tomes of legend" is what we have and that is frankly horrible pacing for an episodic weekly campaign.

                                                            Any battle that involves a second circle demon in Creation, or a celestial censor, or an ancient priest of Oblivion, should have massive narrative impact. It's the sort of thing you should be throwing in at the end of a chapter or even chronicle, but what are your players supposed to do in the mean time? It's boring. It makes the villains seem like jokes, even if the rest of the world treats them with fear and awe.

                                                            As a Storyteller I try to make every session fun and challenging. There are no sessions where nothing happens, there are no sessions where the players are completely safe. And I try to build tension over a chronicle, with smaller fights leading to bigger fights and that rising tension eventually becomes a boss battle with something like a directional god of war. But I can't do both of those things if the war god is the only toy in my toy box that actually will challenge the party.

                                                            Originally posted by Gayo View Post
                                                            The game could definitely use more elder Exalted QCs with heft, but on the whole, I'm a little skeptical that the answer is to buff the antagonists. A lot of the disparity seems to be the result of how obscenely strong a devoted combat-supernal character can get, but a mid-range combatant with 4-6 dice in their combat pools and two combat charms is supposed to be fun to play in a fight, so if you make it so the former type of character is routinely given a tough time, the latter is going to get slaughtered without a constant tanking escort, and then the game veers toward everyone taking extremely combat-focused builds because they "have" to, which annoys the people who didn't want to build that way, and annoys the actual combat monkeys because they're too similar to everybody else.

                                                            I think this is a more structural flaw with the way the game is than perhaps people are giving it credit for. A Solar already starts out as among the best of the best in the thing they're good at, which has the perverse effect of reducing its ability to be an engaging challenge for them. The problem you are running into isn't actually any worse than what happens if you try to run a mystery for a heavily-invested Investigation Supernal character, or naval obstacles at a heavily-invested Sail Supernal character, or what have you.
                                                            As I said earlier on, you really have to tailor each challenge to each player in Exalted. The same combat can't be both hard and satisfying for both the Dawn and the Eclipse. It's just not possible in a system that so heavily favors specialization and focus. One of the first games we did at Solar tier was telling on this point: a large battlegroup of Great Cats attacked the party, and within one round every character who wasn't within the Dawn's defend other range was defeated, and everyone inside that range took no damage over four rounds while he steadily decimated their entire army.

                                                            When I plan games now I try to include at least one story-worthy element for each character to show off in. For example, our current combat involves a war band of forest spirits attacking a Dynast town and includes a little something for everyone: for the Dawn I wrote up the above Lunar, the Twilight Crafter is busy combat-repairing the town's broken wall, the Zenith war leader is keeping the Treant army at bay, the Night is engaged in an Enemy at the Gates sniper fight with the trickster Lunar that started this whole mess, and the Eclipse will most likely storm the Dynast garrison and compel them to stop cowering like frightened children and actually do some of that stewarding they always brag about.

                                                            It is much easier to make five separate challenges that please each character than one challenge that pleases them all.

                                                            Originally posted by Gayo View Post
                                                            You're absolutely right that the outcome is kind of unsatisfactory. However, I'm not sure how to untie that knot without just giving up and running a Dragon-Blooded game. If you look at the stories Dawns like that are based on, they're stories about characters who often seem to be in danger, dancing on the knife's edge between life and death, but always win anyway. You can't do that in a game -- if the mechanics say "by the way, this character always wins" then that's right there and it's nearly impossible to ignore it and pretend they're in trouble, but if the mechanics actually put the character in serious peril all the time, they're going to lose a lot, which feels wrong. The closest you can get to reproducing that narrative structure with actual game mechanics is something like FATE's lose-to-win mechanic, which doesn't capture the feel of it at all and makes the apparent tension of conflict seem like a sort of highly ritualized symbolic theatre performance that I think would not satisfy people who come to the table to play Kenshin.
                                                            I think the best solution to what you are describing is to have there be a varied tier of powers in the setting, most of which the heroes can bulldoze right over and many of which they cannot. In the Ramayana for example, there is this segment where the "villain" Ravana goes on a massive power trip and duels all the great powers of the world one after another, starting with the gods in heaven, working his way down to the demons of Hell and hitting most everything in between. He trounces all those guys with style, but at one point Death itself threatens to kick his ass and is only stopped by the lord of the Underworld, who doesn't want Ravana to be his problem. Ravana also has a long-running feud with the manifestation of Time, who openly mocks his attempts at relevancy. So even in this upper-limit power tier that Ravana occupies he still has things that can humble him and that is important because it makes him seem relatable and human.

                                                            Ex2 struck something like a balance there. Yeah, your starting circle had perfects and that meant that about 80% of the setting had no chance against them in a straight fight, but there was still more than enough out there to justify a seriously difficult fight during every week's session. The problem I'm running into in Ex3 is that with only the antagonists in the backer PDF the upper limit on power is the only bracket that does this, and no one in this upper echelon has the time or wherewithal to mess with the starting Solars once a week.
