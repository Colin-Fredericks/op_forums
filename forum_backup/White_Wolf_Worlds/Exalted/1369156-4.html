    You set yourself on a much more complicated corner with your "purely cultural reasons" affirmation than you think.
    For starters, "culture" is often an ill defined, fuzzy term, especially over a casual conversation like this one. It's very hard to make predictive, reproducible, falseable arguments over that.
    That affirmation also demands proof in the form of linking every behavioral phenomenon you can see to it. It's such a demanding statement that it can break with any single study that spots behavioral differences tied to non-cultural elements.
    Symmetrically, someone affirming everything is purely instinct is also going nowhere, as they have to prove that two people with the same background can't come with different solutions given the same circumstances (a bit like the deterministic universe question). It also breaks the moment two similar people take visibly different courses of action.




    There are much, much simpler and useful affirmations that you can make: Humans are animals. Social animals, which is a whole category of animals based on how they behave and various cognitive traits that help. Humans develop highly complex societal behaviors with trends that we often label "culture".

    Both exist, and both can play into each other. These things that break the two arguments at the start? They Prove this one.


    A very basic example of "not purely cultural trait" is one of the more common forms of mimicry. When everyone is behaving in a certain way almost every newcomer also adopts that behavior, without knowing why it happens, even if it's nonsensical.
    This necessity to blend in propagates through generations and is visible in every single culture (it's also seen on other social animals, humans do not monopolize that). If it was "purely instinct" different cultures would have negligible effects on this behavior. If it was "Purely cultural" it'd not happen in societies that disavow herding like that.
    Instead what you see is that it happens "more often" or "less often" according to how demanding of conformity those societies are, and is more or less sensible to rebel individuals.


    Yet another is sexual selection. You can easily affirm people "have standards", with some people being very "high demand" and some "dejected". You will spot that behavior in every society. In every animal. But you can also easily show that those standards are tied to cultural perceptions. One does not exist without the other.


    Hells, even something like "empathy" includes traits that go away when the person is taking painkillers. Brain chemistry is not cultural. But how much you are expected to exercise empathy is very much cultural, and produces tangible results in these measurements.

                            I've got a few ideas of the nuances of the nature of the goddesses and their relationships with one another and their worshippers, but for the sake of simplicity I'll just present the subject in terms that take them being characterized as maternal deities at face value.

                            So we've got a society that does seem to have some values for gender roles related to maternity, albeit with a few details mixed in that might make them unfamiliar to us. For instance I think the Flower Band by way of its connection to Chuwe'Inan has ideas that associate femininity with beauty, but in the context of an environment in which beauty is also recognised as a flamboyant declaration of impending danger.

                            I think it does leave an open question of whether or not these are cultural values developed from an example set by proximity to those goddesses, if not actively promoted by them, or things that they already adhered to which is the basis for them adopting these particular goddesses over any others.

                            And that itself seems to play into how they've formed a relationship with Ma-Ha-Suchi. The role of doula might not necessarily be traditionally feminine, but in an instance like this I don't think it's a stretch to assume it is, so I would think that them perceiving the Lunar's magic transforming people into beastfolk in that role both takes the form of adopting that practice into a particular kind of privileged traditional gender role, while also being an instance of a society loosening or subverting their own tradition by describing Ma-Ha-Suchi as having that role.

                            I'd view it as such a subversion because their perception of Ma-Ha-Suchi seems to otherwise be focused on categorizing him as a man by way of him being the "Undying Uncle". I think that itself connotes the society's standards for gender roles, because he seems to hold status almost equal to a Mother but is not given that title, but nor is he given its direct male counterpart, but a more tangential one. One might suggest that Ma-Ha-Suchi presenting as male actually forms a check on the status he can hold among the Chante-Sa, although at the same time his being a Lunar (and having their associated complex relationship with gender) is a basis by which they can provide for him inhabiting an otherwise feminine role.

                            I don't really have a wider point here so much as a bunch of observations of a particular example that I think are interesting.

                                So, as discusses above by other posters, if increased resources are required to "make the most of male potential", control over resources is a logical cultural step required to meet that physiological requirement. It's established among chimpanzees, one of humanity's closest evolutionary cousins, that male chimps are pretty aggressive in leading hunts on monkeys to acquire meat, and also tend to be notably larger and stronger than female chimps, by a margin a lot greater than human sex differences. They also tend to carefully control who gets meat when they end up killing a monkey; this is often done to cement alliances with other males, and thereby gain leadership positions or concessions from a leader.
                                You can see a fair bit of humanity in that, being built in such a similar manner. One could also see how resource monopolization might end up setting a long-lasting bias up on the basis of male vs. female strength due to male humans taking control over kills and distribution of food to exacerbate what would otherwise be a much more modest difference - not only do the males get more of the high protein source, the females get less of it, and if cultural standards shift away from catching smaller animals in nets and snares for both (as was the case for most of human existence), that would reasonably result in a substantial disruption to readability of biology due to culture. If the groups in charge then also restrict access to exercise, that's another hit (that can come from pressure on both sides; kyriarchy and all). Eventually, it honestly seems like men are naturally stronger, even though these are due overwhelmingly to culturally-wrangled advantages. The biological basis then becomes very difficult to measure genuinely. That's where we are. That's our problem.

                                Edit Addendum: Control over calorie distribution without being physically powerful already can be accomplished through improved stone points, walking after prey until they becoming exhausted, or as a variation, walking after prey until it falls off of a cliff. Also, fat content is going to be as important as protein, since a skeletal muscle hypertrophy requires a great deal of energy for maintenance. Bones and meat also contain micronutrients to this effect; calcium is necessary to get muscle to move and build up stronger bones, of course, but improving zinc content in diet, such as from red meat or lentils, can ease healing, support eyesight, smell, and taste, and improve fertility, male fertility in particular.

                                            Re, upthread idea, in terms of protein, I would guess that, specifically, differential access probably didn't matter *too* much. My reasoning there is that the limit of biological protein you might need, may not be that high to develop strength, and it may be that most societies get there in terms of pure protein amount for most members (or if they're not they're generally not for both men and women).

                                            Sure, yes, we can talk about apes that fairly rarely eat animal food sources and use them in kind of a "luxury goods"-alliance "show-off" kind of way, but it's worth bringing us back to measured human HG societies who seem to get about at least 45% of calories from hunted and fished sources, varying up to 94%, mostly varying by latitude (https://academic.oup.com/ajcn/article/71/3/682/4729121). (It may have been more for Upper Paleolithic hunters who concentrated on large prey, before humans kind of exceeded our hunting capacity and those habitats where large prey were more concentrated and an open landscape meant they were easier to catch, and then had to go for smaller prey, more plants, etc - the "Broad Spectrum Revolution").

                                            That's probably because it's a pretty efficient way to get calories (with a lot of that actually being animal fat), and the actual requirement of protein probably far lower (and if you eat too much protein as a share of subsistence you may get ill, as per above, so hunters have to preferentially target fat-bearing animals to some degree).

                                            Where you have a lot of malnutrition through human history and pre-history and failing to thrive, that's possibly more because people just aren't getting enough calories period, rather than a lack specifically of protein content.

                                            So I don't know. In terms of allocation of protein, I think probably not. May work better as a theory that men tend to get more calories because they do a higher proportion of the calorie gathering work in societies, where women seem to tend to have more of their time used for childcare, in-camp work (tailoring clothing, pottery, etc), caring for elderly and injured group members etc (stuff that's skilled and vital for the group but not really calorie gathering).

                                            But then you'd actually have to test how food seemed to be allocated among groups and the likely effect size of that. Like, do you actually have a increased sexual inequality of malnutrition signatures in places where male share of subsistence tends to be somewhat larger (usually North Eurasian / North American very cold places where hunting and fishing is almost the only thing you can do for subsistence)?

                                            (One of the ways this can also be checked among ancient populations is by looking at the nitrogen isotope ratios is bone, and from that you can determine if there was a dietary difference between males and females in animal, fished, plant foods, and also they can look at indicators of nutritional stress like cribia orbitale, enamel hypoplasia and the like and see if there are any systematic differences between male and female samples.)

                                            Another last thing to add, getting back to the alliance thing, I think for the most part, the ethnographic stuff (from what I've heard secondhand) tends to support that hunting activity is mostly about provisioning for family (and for men then, actually making sure that wives and kids survive) and not really so much about distributing through groups for generalized high status (that helps men in war alliances, to rise in social status, to marry earlier, etc), and even when distributing through groups, not vastly preferentially to males so strongly.

                                            Although of course you can say then, still, perhaps they give more food to sons than daughters, so there's still that.

                                            I've probably posted too much on this by this point! This is all a really interesting topic, but is getting away from the purpose of the thread (which is about gender role behaviour in Exalted, which is a good and useful topic in its own right and probably not worth crowding out).
