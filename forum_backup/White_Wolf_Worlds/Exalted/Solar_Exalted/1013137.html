    How do I challenge solars

    12-06-2016, 10:12 AM
    Having run a few weeks of the game, and slowly escalated opponents for the PCs, I think I have hit a conundrum of how I can actually challenge these characters.

    I have three PCs

    A night caste specialising in stealth and an expert in the Single Point Shining into the Void martial arts technique.

    An eclipse caste who specialises in social expertise, but is similarly a master of Black Claw style martial arts.

    And a Zenith caste who specialises in Resistance Charms and grappling.



    My issue is that with a combination of charms, excellencies and artifacts the Solars are thus-far untouchable, but more worryingly, are so competent in their areas that they will never fail, and this can remove drama.



    Now, I understand that the Solars are Heroes (capital H), and doing exceptional things is pretty much their bread and butter, but I'm having trouble figuring out how to construct an game around this.

    If I introduce a threatening villain, to foreshadow future conflict, there is nothing (very bad luck notwithstanding) stopping the night caste from sneaking into their home and killing them in their sleep. In our last session, he was sometimes getting 15 or 16 successes on a stealth check, and only incredible back luck got him down to 8 successes. There is pretty much nothing in the book with a senses that is likely to get 8+, let alone 15+ successes to spot him. And even when battle is joined, the combo of blinding battle feint, stalking wolf attitude, a couple Single Point Shining into the Void charms, and 10+ successes a round means in round two of the battle he's cutting down enemies that cannot see him with 30 decisive damage dice.

    I'm not so concerned about the general battlefield prowess, but more his ability to get to and eliminate any threat he is aware of before it knows he is coming.



    I'm similarly concerned about the eclipse caste in social conflict.



    I have set up a situation where the PCs are travelling with a large number of mortals, and therefore I can threaten their friends, but it feels like every attack will need to be an ambush since if I warn the PCs that "There is a dangerous warband up ahead, led by a powerful lunar despot" then that despot is dead in his bed before the squishy mortals get within two days of the dangerous area.



    What have people found is a successful way to make Exalted challenging (and fun) while allowing the PCs the chance to use their cool abilities and synergies but still provides a challenge? (other than mote tracking).

      So, this is a problem that has to be dealt with on a per-competency basis, and sometimes the solution is just "you can't, and you have to accept that and try to create drama in other ways if you can."

      For the night caste assassin in particular I've had success making the villain really hard to find. He ALWAYS uses cats-paws, never ever reveals his true identity, and in fact your players never meet him until the end of his arc because if they did he'd be dead, they always end up getting to places just a bit too late. Without somebody really investigation focused this can work. That or give him a ton of powerful bodyguards and make up a total bullshit artifact or something that lets him pierce almost any stealth.

          Single Point style was a mistake.

          In all seriousness:

          The Influence Merit. Seriously.

          If people start learning that everyone who opposes the circle ends up dead in their bed as soon as the circle figures it out, a couple of things are going to happen:
          Their enemies will start using catspaws, they'll attack from multiple locations with multiple different groups (the circle can't be everywhere at once), and they're going to try to be stealthier in general, with a more obscure leadership style. Your night caste won't be killing anyone if they don't know who to kill.

          The villains will start infiltrating the group of mortals and learning about the circle's abilities and intimacies. They'll learn what pisses them off and what's important to them, and start playing that against them or against each other (*cough* limit break* *cough*). They'll do their best to separate them emotionally and geographically and strike them at once. They'll engage them from afar using bureaucracy to make things hard for them. If they have an organization, they'll attack its assets when they're not there. They'll hamstring them and make them waste time on pointless shit. And they'll useful idiots and each other: when you're faced with solars, the enemy of your enemy is your friend. And there will almost certainly be normal, decent folk for who the solars' plans would not make their lives better off, or might make their lives significantly worse. You don't feel like a big guy when you murder someone who just wants the best for her family.

          In terms of combat, battle groups pose a big risk to your whole scene. Your grappler isn't able to grapple the battle group, your shining point specialist can't gain initiative off them, and Black Claw is very good if someone wants to fight you. But if the battle group just ignores them...black claw can't do much. It's entirely about using social influence in responding to being attacked. If they're not attacked, they can't use their nifty tricks.

          And in spite of all of that, your solars will still likely prevail. But now that they're being harried at all levels, they'll have to make choices about what's important and what's not. Who to protect and who to let slide. What sacrifices are worth making and what isn't. Whether or not their friendship can prevail to the end. That's where the drama will come from.

          Also, if you want to be an ass, Abyssals.

              I've had similar problems, my PCs being 2 Dawns, a Zenith who mastered Crane style, a Twilight Righteous Devil stylist, a Stealth supernal Night Caste and a bureaucrat (seriously, supernal Bureaucracy).

              In combat, we have battle groups. Use them. A lot. And always pit your group against large numbers of adversaries (I found that twice their numbers, plus two or three battlegroups works best). They might still mow through a few encounters, but the death toll will quickly start to rise and gain them a reputation.

              If the Stealth guy isn't also highly invested in Awareness, use traps. A weary general stes up a few things before going to bed, like caltrops, or bells. Anything to wake up a camp if the Night caste isn't very careful.

                          Remember, that in Exalted the main drama driving the story does not lie in whether the players will be able to overcome the challenges. Nor does it even lie in how hard it will be for them. The real story lies in the consequences of their choices.
                          Yes, killing the opponent, if you can pull it off (and Solars generally can) is often a simplest and easiest solution. Only rarely however it is the best one.

                          So, if your players are soo eager to just kill of their opponents, sooner or later they will end up with some really negative reputation. They will also start running into problems that just cannot be solved that way(*). Their allies will start to distance from them, the neutral sides will get suspicious, and potential enemies will get more serious (and creative) in their approaches.

                          And, sooner or later, someone will get a hold of some other exalts, and prepare an ambush (since your players are so predictable in their approach).



                          (*)oh, who am i kidding, everyone knows that every problem can be solved by a right dose of carpet bombing...

                          The difference between fiction and reality? Fiction has to make sense.

                              Since it's not been said yet, depending on the needs of your circle and confidence in your abilities, look into more supernatrual threats. If none of the ones in the book have charms that can come close to the Solars', add a few. You shouldn't be afraid to use a little homebrew to even the odds.

                              Beyond that, try to throw wrinkles into those old tactics. I'm pretty sure you can get those stealth dice waaay down with situational penalties: bright light, multiple guards, knowing to expect the Exalt. Isn't there an automatic penalty for even trying to Stealth in battle? (and remember, it's only a full Ambush the first time he does it.) In social combat, I'd praise Guile: depending on the situation, not knowing where a character stands can be more important than straight persuasiveness.

                                      You could also always just present antagonists that aren't morally adverse to the play-group. I mean, the Night caste may well be willing to assassinate a tin-pot dictator, but are they willing to kill people who are genuinely just trying to govern their countries well; are not awful people?

                                      Or what about some child-king who got appointed entirely because of the Night caste assassinating their parents?

                                      If there's absolutely no one the Night caste won't assassinate for power, then you can go onto introducing consequences. If you legitimize the use of assassination as a political tool, then you destroy a lot of the political stability and legitimacy of the region. You won't be able to go five months without a king being assassinated and a new one being put in his place.

                                          As Zale said. Assassination is the sort of thing that doesn't get used frequently in the real world for two main reasons. First, it's hard (but obviously not for Solars). More importantly, it is profoundly destabilizing to the region, and only becomes moreso if it occurs with frequency.

                                          This might not bother the players if their opponents are always clearly Bad People Who Do Bad Things, though. In these situations they can easily justify their actions (i.e. "So, killing the horrible Lunar despot destabilized the organizational integrity of his cannibal murder-cult? Yeah I'm okay with that.") so you need to present them with slightly more nuance if you want them to consider exercising restraint. Opponents that are Bad People Who Do Bad Things But Also Hold Off Worse People Who Do Worse Things can be more of a conundrum. If my immediate opponent is someone I really don't like and who opposes my agenda, but who also manages to maintain order and safety for a lot of people who are not Clearly Bad, then I need to think more carefully about whether just killing him in his sleep is really a good answer. Especially if doing so makes other influential people nervous, or could threaten to erode the fabric of a social order that I actually value.

                                          And that last point is really the big one: you need to find out what the characters place value on and make sure that they are aware of how their actions will affect those things. People are very good at disregarding the importance of causes, places, and especially people that they are unfamiliar with. They'll even go out of their way sometimes to avoid learning about things outside their immediate spheres of concern, because it's easier to act with a clear conscience when you don't know the people that will be affected by those actions. But if you make sure that your PCs are aware of how different people and places in the world are interrelated, it becomes more interesting to watch them struggle with what the right course of action really is. Do I murder this tin-pot dictator even though I know that the grain shipments which allow his people to eat are dependent on him remaining in power? What if I know some of those people?

                                          Related to that point (and hopefully not wandering too off-topic) I'm fond of humanizing the enemies of my PCs, often by introducing them in the game as people first and only later revealing how their agendas clash with the PCs. That's why the friendliest person at an archaeological expedition they arrived at turned out to be the slave-driver, and why it was more meaningful when my Lunar player later decided to murder him in cold blood because he has a Defining Intimacy of hatred toward slavers. It's also why that same PC's most friendly and down-to-earth interaction at a fancy dinner party later on turned out to be with a young Terrestrial Dynast who is disgusted with her relatives, is a functional alcoholic, and who helpfully pointed out that the late arrivals to the event were there to recruit her uncle into their Wyld Hunt. She was totally ignorant of the fact that that Hunt will in time be coming for the handsome gentleman she was sharing a glass of wine with, though it was not lost on him.

                                          The PCs cleared out of that party shortly after the conversation. They claimed it was because they were afraid of discovery, but in truth I think they were just afraid that they might actually like the people trying to kill them. And that would make things much less simple.

                                                  Well, yes. Sometimes the PCs really should get to win. No caveats or complications, just be Big Damn Heroes. And that is more satisfying when doing so isn't trivial.

                                                  But the problem with topics like this isn't that the advice is bad. It's that you can't read any one solution or approach as the only one you'll ever need. Variety is necessary to a good story. I spend a lot of time making the scenarios I present my players with nuanced, but I probably spend a similar amount of time trying to present them with interesting mechanical challenges as well. Both are important parts of a satisfying game.
