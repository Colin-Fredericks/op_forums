                                Originally posted by MrStabs View Post
                                As of 2.5ed Exalted, not having someone with Medicine charms around means that they heal slower and lean heavier on defensive charms. Maybe they have to dip into resistance a bit. If somebody loses a limb, the Circle can probably go to another God or Exalt to fix the problem. A healer does not impact a Circles plans like someone who can summon demons or sneak does. Sure, they get to shine whenever a plague or poison shows up, but the ST has to go out of their way to make that happen. I would like a Medicine specialist to have as much impact on how a circle deals with their problems as the Awareness specialist, or the Melee specialist, or the Presence specialist. They should feel it when the healer is there and feel the sting when she is gone. They should branch out from just fixing injuries and curing diseases whenever it is plot appropriate and have the possibility to contribute meaningfully (but not overwhelmingly) to more situations.
                                The problem with "focus on how much the circle needs you" is that then you end up with the D&D cleric problem, where you have the group designing their characters and someone says "all right, one of us has to bite the bullet and take Medicine" and everyone groans and he's like "yes, but you all know how much the game sucks without it" and finally someone says "FINE, I'll do it but you owe me."

                                The problem isn't that Medicine isn't necessary; the problem is that it isn't fun -- it usually gives you fewer options to contribute compared to other powers, and when you do contribute, it often feels like a mindless healbot thing where you repair problems outside of combat when it would be obvious for you to repair them, with little room for creative use of your abilities. Making Medicine more essential without making it more fun (and giving it more applicability) would make things worse, because now you'd be forcing people who aren't even interested in it in the first place to take it just to make the game fun for everyone else. Or, to put it in simpler terms, you want to avoid deliberately punishing the rest of the circle because their Twilight didn't take medicine, because that sets up a very unfun dynamic for everyone involved.

                                In other words: The game needs to be entirely playable with nobody in the circle who has Medicine charms. Making Medicine essential isn't really solving the issue -- you don't want to force people to use Medicine, you want them to enjoy it. And in general, I think what people enjoy about their powers is when they're able to use them in interesting and creative ways, not when they do the mandatory healbot thing every time the Dawn gets a crippling effect from a nasty fight.

For my table at least, the rewards of these actions may be primarily emotional or story-based, but they are still 'carrots' that are sought eagerly by my players.

                                    Medicine allows you to make the harsh reality of the world slightly less stinging, which depending on the plot you put forward, may end up being the most important power in the world to your players.

                                    D&D doesn't emphasise these social issues as much, so of course the ability to heal is therefore significantly devalued. Viewed through the lens of combat utility, possibly the most miraculous and awesome supernatural power people could GET is actually kinda boring!

                                        Originally posted by Kukla
                                        I'm reading a book titled The Spirit Catches You And You Fall Down, which is about the doctors in the california town of Merced trying to provide medical care to a Hmong family with a daughter prone to grand mal seizures. The hmong family is illiterate and does not believe in western medicine and in fact intermittently suspects that the fevers and illnesses their daughter suffers are the fault of the medications she has been prescribed - that the doctors are intentionally making her ill. It is a very large cultural and language barrier that prevents proper care from being administered.

                                        A Solar doctor should be able to punch through that sort of confusion like a bullet.
                                        IIRC, the really awful thing is that while the parents were wrong about the doctors intentionally making her ill, they're weren't neccessarily wrong about the medicine making things worse. It's just that it was impossible for them to convey this to the doctors in a way that the doctors wouldn't dismiss in a well-meaning fashion.


                                        If you want more detail on cross-cultural stuff, I found Arthur Kleinman's Rethinking Psychiatry, which looks at cross cultural issues in mental health, to be very interesting. (Kleinman is at one point consulted by the author of The Spirit Catches You in order to find out what one should do when dealing with complicated cross cultural medical issues.)
