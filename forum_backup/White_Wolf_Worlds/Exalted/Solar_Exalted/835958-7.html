        Originally posted by kindledRadial View Post
        What I have ruled in my game, which all my players loved and which definitely helps with this problem, is that anyone can negate an ambush (which negates your Defense and gives the attackers a free combat round) by succeeding at an Awareness check, and if they use a free action to warn the party the party reduces that ambush to a surprise attack (-2 Defense and no free combat round), but only by beating the Awareness check themselves can they completely protect themselves from the deleterious effects of a good ambush. This has gone over quite well, and I would highly recommend adopting it as a house rule to Storytellers.
        I don't think that is even a houserule.
        1. Attacker hides.
        2. Party approaches, they get to roll Awareness to spot the attacker.
        3. If any of them is successful, they can cry out and start JB.
        4. The attacker gets to ambush only those characters that failed to spot him and failed the JB roll (are after him in the initiative ladder).
        5. Characters that failed the spot check, but placed before the attacker initiative are treating it as surprise attack.

                        Originally posted by kindledRadial View Post
                        There are degrees of rarity. "Rare" in the sense of "once every month of concerted adventuring which we represent in a single session" is what I want, what I would consider reasonable from a pacing perspective.
                        So...wait, you want to have an opponent show up once per session (meaning approximately once per month of in-game time) that is capable of challenging a pair of Dawn Caste murder-machines in a stand up fight? I can see why you are having issues. This isn't a problem with the system, it's a problem with expectations. Both yours, and those of your players.

                        Let's recalibrate for a moment. Set combat aside for now. Let's say instead of combat, I have a player whose character strives to be the fastest man in Creation. He loves footraces. Loves them. He lives to test himself against the fastest runners across the world. So of course, he goes heavy into Athletics, right? That makes sense! Supernal Athletics and all the relevant Charms. Then this exchange happens:

                        ST: You reach a new town. It's bigger than the last few, so there are several thousand people here. That makes it the biggest town for days in any direction.
                        Player: Sweet! I challenge the local youths to a footrace. Surely there is someone here worthy of competition!
                        ST: ...sure. Okay, they're game. You guys run the race the day after next. Uh...do you want to roll it out?
                        Player: Of course!
                        ST: Okay, go for it. Extended Athletics roll, difficulty, ah...2 for the uneven ground, terminus five. First one to 20 successes wins.
                        Player: This is my jam, baby!
                        *dice rolls*
                        Player: Aww yeah, 23 successes!
                        ST: The fastest youth rolled three. You win. Everyone is awed. Uh, what is everyone else up to in the meantime?
                        Player: Wait! Er...anybody want a rematch?
                        ST: They do not.
                        Player: Oh. Um...okay.
                        ST: Are you pouting?
                        Player: ...no.

                        So what's wrong with this scenario? I'm sure you can guess. The player obviously likes the idea of his character being defined by "the race" but made the mistake of making himself so good that he has no competition (or at least very, very little - the kind that won't come up every session, or even every story). He's like the dog who chases the car and doesn't have any idea what to do with it once he catches it, except that he's a Solar and he can come out of character creation having already caught the car.

                        This is actually a problem that has been investigated in dramatic media before - the warrior so skilled that he wanders the world searching for someone who can challenge him, but who is perpetually disappointed. Oh, the drama! Oh, the angst! It's a tempting idea to base a character around, except that it overlooks one crucial thing: these characters are goddamned miserable because the lack of challenge has sapped them of any ability to enjoy the thing they are best at, and to which they have devoted their lives. They are bored. So guess what happens to a player who makes a character like this?

                        Exalted lets players make Solars, and then it sets the ceiling for what they can accomplish very, very high. Players are often conditioned to reach as high as they can by other games, because not doing so results in you being unhappy when you struggle to have an impact on the game. Exalted introduces the potential for the corresponding, opposite problem: you can start so powerful in a given area of competence that you can only be challenged by a very select few, who will come along very rarely. Before you reach so high, it behooves you to ask: do I want this? If the answer is no - if it's not the trophy but the race that you find interesting - then maybe you shouldn't. At least, not right away.

                        Food for thought.

                                            I don't know if it matters, but I do think that the heights of what is available to a PC from character creation can present some serious challenges that are not easily resolved. One of my players in a game I will be STing has made a Twilight with Supernal Investigation and taken Charms up to Irresistible Questioning Technique. He also has a mentor/adversary in the form of an imprisoned second circle demon, from whom he wrested the secrets of Sorcery. The demon in question is a scheming, homicidal genius who clearly needs to be the Hannibal to my player's Clarice Starling. It's a great story hook! Buuut...

                                            I have lost far less sleep concerned with whether he can challenge the party in combat than I have wrestling with how the hell to let him actually be that scheming nemesis, when my player can just mark off some motes and Willpower and force my villain to spill the beans whenever he wants to. I have a few ideas. But it's hard! The wiggle room allowed by IQT is razor-thin, and I can't just make him immune to it without devaluing the investment my player made. I could just throw my hands up and say, "Welp, you win! That Charm is bonkers and I got nothing. Here's his devious plan." But that undermines the whole point - the player gave me this villain because he wants to have his Moriarty! How to both let the PC be the brilliant detective he is while still giving him a nemesis he can't unravel with trivial ease? This is the kind of problem that is easy to miss as a player but drives an ST nuts. Beyond that, it threatens to make a major part of the character moot in the future, too - what's the point of playing a brilliant detective when you can just make anybody confess right away? The Maltese Falcon would be a much shorter novel if Sam Spade knew Investigation Charms.

                                            So a warning to players! If you like robbing houses, a skeleton key is a great investment. But if you like picking locks, it's a terrible one. In that case, make a character who has a reason to need lockpicks instead.
