                            Originally posted by Allan53 View Post
                            So I was considering how Solar Exaltations work in 2E, and I've never been entirely comfortable with the lack of choice in it. Yes, I know, the person has to choose to stand up to whatever they're facing or whatever, but other Solaroid Exaltations require you to choose to become an Abyssal or Infernal or whatever, and there's so much story potential in someone refusing a Solar Exaltation that it seems a bit of a waste not to have it. Plus, I don't really like the idea of a person becoming an Eclipse because they get zapped with an Eclipse Exaltation (again, yes, I know they get that Exaltation because it suits them, but more variability is always a good thing). So, I was considering a slightly different mechanic of Solar Exaltation.

                            So, you've got Awesome Bob. He's pretty awesome. He is facing some terrible difficulty, and he makes the choice to stand up to it, rather than run away. ZAP, the Exaltation enters him and begins the soul-bonding-thing.

                            Bob then finds himself facing some kind of test. The one that occurs to me is a courtyard that he must cross, which he somehow intuitively knows will grant him the power to overcome his trials. Behind him is darkness, at the other end is a doorway filled with golden light, and in the middle is a group of soldiers in a ready stance. How Bob proceeds determines what kind of Solar Exalt he becomes. If he raises his weapon and attacks, he becomes a Dawn Caste. If he makes an impassioned speech and persuades the soldiers to part - or join his cause - he becomes an Eclipse. If he avoids the soldiers, or causes them to fight each other by slipping in between them, then he becomes a Night Caste, and so forth. And if he goes back into the darkness, he is returned to his initial situation, not a Solar (which he may well not want to be, if say he's of the Immaculate Faith), but no worse off either, other than maybe a mite confused, and the caste-neutral Exaltation seeks another host.

                            Obviously the test can and will vary somewhat, but what do people think of the basic idea? From what little I've picked up of how Exaltations will work in 3E, might gel well with that as well.

                                    Originally posted by TheCountAlucard View Post
                                    Your choice of words has left me lukewarm to it.

                                    You don't choose to be blessed with the illimitable power of the Unconquered Sun; the King of Heaven makes that choice. You also don't choose how the Highest of Holies blesses you; this, too, is on the four shoulders of Ignis Divine. He's never going to say, "I want to Exalt this warrior, but all I have is a Night Exaltation." If he reaches from heaven to touch your soul and spark that divine fire, it will be something compatible with you… even if you don't initially feel it, even if you don't want it.

                                    After all, if denying Exaltation was a thing, would the Immaculate Philosophy not have a chapter in their scripture about "Just Say No to Conky?"

                                    Furthermore, Abyssals and Green Sun Princes are different. In some ways, fundamentally so. This is one of the reasons the devs aren't throwing around the word "Solaroid."

                                    That, and it sounds rather dumb.
                                    Well, leaving aside the fact that the way you're describing it makes it far less interesting, which is subjective anyway, what if we characterise it differently: it represents the Exaltation getting to know your soul, which up to now it has no effective way of knowing. We already know that Exaltations need to be 'cleaned' periodically, in order to prevent tendencies and memories being carried over from incarnation to incarnation. What if we characterise this 'cleaning' as a kind of 'resetting' process (it's not like it's ever really gone into in any depth anyway). So, the Exaltation is zapping around, and finds a suitable candidate. It then begins the soul-bonding process, but each soul is a little different, and as such the bonding has to be a little different, and the fusion will be slightly different. The 'test' is how the Exaltation finds the best way of enhancing your existing awesomeness. Exaltations are already modelled as being kind-of-not-quite-sentient, which implies they have some form of "knowledge", which again is incidentally supported by Lytek's cleaning: why would you need to remove something that isn't there?

                                    So, Bob is facing his moment of glory, the US or the Exaltation or whatever approves, and the process begins. But what if we say that Exaltations aren't pre-set to a particular caste, but the castes rather represent broad catagories of results of the bonding, or ways of being awesome, or whatever? As people have said, just because someone's a particular caste by no means determines their methodologies, there's a lot of variability. And there's certainly no reason a Dawn Caste can't be just as skilled an orator or whatever as an Eclipse, apart from a fairly minor experience difference (which can be removed entirely by selection of Favoured abilities). But if they're just more prone to facing down situations through face-punching or crafting or sneaking or whatever, then it is more likely that they're going to approach situations in that way, and as such will fall within broad catagories of Solar, aka castes. Not because that's the Exaltation they were given, but because it's who they are. It doesn't matter that it wouldn't happen any other way, but if you got the biggest, burliest, least subtle face-puncher and the US gave him a Night Caste Exaltation, then that's what he would be, regardless of his nature. So, while it's an academic point, the Exaltation does determine your Caste in the current setting. Which is silly, in my opinion. Also, this way, or at least a variant thereof, makes Exaltation a more personal process, rather than some big golden guy in the sky saying "Yep, he's good enough. Send along Exaltation #23." He'd still be involved, but in a more symbolic, 'just has to be around for the process to work' way, rather than being actively involved, which he isn't really because he's a bit distracted by the Games of Divinity.

                                    Plus, adding choice to a change never takes anything from a story, and always adds. It might add problems, or not add much, but never is anything lost. Unless there's a strong thematic reason why there's no choice involved, but that's not present here.

                                                        OUR SOULS THROUGH OUR EYES
                                                        RAJMAEL
                                                        I was raised in a library in the city of Arjuf. The library was my father; its keeper was my mother. They had snatched me from the river of ignorance, a young boy, wandering the streets of some city I was never told the name of, orphaned and alone.

                                                        Life among those timeworn volumes was my world, and everything that I knew. I would play under my Mother’s desk, and she would smile would tell me of far off times and long ago lands where golden monarchs reigned and where the limitations of man were mere suggestions. Every time I would listen, captivated and engrossed in these tales, though always she would warn me not to pursue my curiosity too far.

                                                        I was not lacking for playmates; the small children of Dynasts and Patricians who had come, seeking some esoteric secret, would join me, and they would take momentary respite from their viciously proper upbringings, where we would imagine ourselves to be these great and glorious heroes from a bygone time. When I was caught at such play however, mother scolded me harshly, and forbade me such games again; it was a long time before I could understand why.

                                                        In the fullness of years, I lost my mother; and I wept bitterly; the inevitable march of mortality and wasting malady is hard to grasp for a young man of sixteen. The library was not the same after her passing; new librarians came, Immaculate brothers and Lectors, and many of the scholars there asked that I take her place, but to me and my mind, mother would only be the one to occupy it, and the library was no longer my home.

                                                        I left, taking but a few scrolls with me. I knew that my mother had given me a great gift in the form of my educated and inquisitive mind; and it was one I was eager to share. I decided I would wander from place to place, offering what I knew for my rice and tea. Common wisdom said the wilds, filled with strange beasts and Lunar Anathema would consume me, but my trust in the wisdom I had accrued and the providence of the Immaculate Dragons assured me.

                                                        My meanderings brought me to many places far beyond Arjuf. I saw the glories of the Imperial City, wandered the foothills of the great Mountain. I was the servant of Dragon-bloods and Patricians, a private tutor for their young children, whom I saw deprived of childhoods in the name of politics and the ever sought canniness of the paranoid.

                                                        I came to realize, that my mind had been but one gift that academic isolation had given me. I saw the world’s excesses in clearer relief in my first roving year than accounts in scrolls and wax tablets could ever have provided. I learned in greater detail of death, and hatred and fear, of avarice and ignorance, cupidity and dominance. I saw the world anew, and travel provided a more thorough and crueler teacher than could be found in writing. And yet always there was a drive; if this was to be my world, I needed to understand it.

                                                        The Order promised easy answers and I entertained the notion of becoming a lore keeper for the Immaculates, that I might plumb the depths of the universe for the secrets of Enlightenment, always there was another drive; a shrill tongue calling me back to the earth, to hoary ruins and moldering texts. Perhaps Enlightenment would satisfy, but, since I had first heard tales of the past at my Mother’s knee, my passion was the enigma of antiquity. This then was my resolution; my heart would not rest until the secrets of yore laid themselves bare to me; and I knew I would not be free to seek what I wished to know on the shores of the Isle.

                                                        Hesitantly, I left my homeland behind; which I will freely admit, was one of the hardest things I had ever done; homesickness aside, everything that I had been told said the Threshold would chew and spit whole a man like myself. And I left anyway. The Realm’s satrapies were the first of my many steps on a long journey to nowhere in particular. I had many patrons during my travels; nobility, kings and powerful merchants who desired that I render them and their children erudite, scholars who sought young men with sharp minds and clever hands to draw forth ancient treasures and tease secrets from the morass of history.

                                                        Malfeasance and rapacity abounded wherever I walked. The petty aristocracy of the Scavenger Lands were ill better than their Realm counterparts; I grew weary of watching the politics and manipulations of powerful and learned men. Better I thought that I had never left my cloistered existence, better that my belief in the goodness of my fellow man had never been challenged. The drive no longer mattered.

                                                        So I found a place to call home. A small village, free of a liege or king, had a space for a teacher; though I say that as if there was a great deal of competition for the position. In truth, I made my own space. The village council accepted me, finding the idea of an education for their children novel.

                                                        So, I founded my school, slid into a quiet life once more, and was content.

                                                        Then, the bandits came. Ruled with an iron fist by a bloodthirsty thug named Gao, they became the bane of our otherwise peaceful existence. Arriving daily, they would bully the town into feeding and paying them, naming it “payment” for their “protection”.

                                                        The town quickly tired of Gao’s “mercy”; but there was little that could be done; none among us were warriors or trained soldiers, and the braver or more foolhardy of the young men among us had already tried to oppose them, and failed.

                                                        Noon would come, and each day we would leave an offering of food and silver to fill their collection plate, hoping to drive them off with our acquiescence, though we knew this could not last. One day would come when there would be no silver nor valuables to be found among the town, or even enough food to feed ourselves when Ascending Air came.

                                                        During each of their “friendly visits” I would send my students to hide in the hills surrounding the village, along with the rest of the village children; better they not be found, we thought, and used by Gao to further tighten the leash about our necks; or worse, that they become little more than chattel that the brigands would take and enrich themselves further on our despair.

                                                        And that is where things went even more wrong. We had taught the children to return when they heard the call of a silver whistle the headman kept. One day, they did not return. In no time flat, we had convened a town council; several men volunteered to go find them; myself among them. I filled up a pack, and set out into the plains to go find the lost children

                                                        The grasslands around a place as small as the nameless town where I resided were deceptive; deep canyons and hills fond of gullies and easily broken “caps” of hardened earth, baked dry by the Sun over tunnels leading to the Dragons only know where, had multiplied our worries, and the difficulties of finding our quarry. My first few days of searching availed me little, and I
                                                        despaired of ever seeing my charges or their bright little minds ever again.

                                                        All the more worrying you might say it to be then, when, skirting the edge of a deep canyon that ran for I knew not how many miles, I tumbled down one of these sinkholes, tearing straight through into a chamber far below, accompanied by the distinctive crack of my leg breaking, leaving me battered and barely conscious at the bottom.

                                                        EXALTATION
                                                        I had no idea of how long I laid there, infection working its way into the cuts and injuries I had sustained in my fall, my leg slowly worsening as my fever began to rise higher and higher, the beating, merciless Sun shining down upon my broken form through my makeshift skylight as I wandered in and out of consciousness. In that time, my burning mind conjured voices from the aether.

                                                        I dreamed, and saw many things; myself as the librarian my mother had raised me to be, clean and well dressed, living a secluded and cloistered life. There I was as the quiet-voiced schoolteacher of an insignificant hamlet, humility and brown robes weighting down my shoulders; I saw the monk of the Immaculate Order that the brother-scholars at the library had urged me to become, my head shorn and my sandaled feet buried in the sand of a rock garden that I worked with a rake; a proper follower of the Mouth of Pasiap.

                                                        Leaning against a rock, I saw a man who was like myself, and yet, not like me at all. His brow glowed with golden fire, and he wore robes of red silk and spun sunset. “That’s not truly who you want to be, is it, Dimasa?” he asked me, his arms crossed before him. “To be a cloistered bookkeeper, the lives of others beyond his walls meaningless to him as anything but numbers and accounts? Do you truly wish only for the small victory of teaching one peasant child when the world itself could be your classroom? Are you truly satisfied with mere mediocrity when secrets so much greater than you know await an astute hand to awaken them?” I stared at the man in awe.

                                                        “Look out upon the world, scholar.” The man said to me, the windows of the library, the shutters of my school, the doors of the garden flew open, three sets of eyes that were mine and not all at once peered through; Creation was laid out before me, shadows and darkness spread over it like oil poured upon a diorama. “Do you see the darkness? The ignorance and the fear?” he asked, plucking fire and light from his brow, long russet hair snaking in every direction as he fixed me with a stern gaze.

                                                        “Yes.” I answered. “But I don’t understand; who are you? Why are you so interested in me? What does this have to do with… with anything?” He smiled once, white teeth showing through a short beard. “I am you. Or rather, I am what you were and might be again.” I stared at him, my mind boggling. “You have the power to undo this Dimasa.” He said, reaching down and laying the fire in the Scavenger Lands, where it floated above them, pushing back the oily shadows. I watched enraptured as he spoke again. “The world is not as it is meant to be.” The man said, his body dissolving into a golden radiance that, where I anywhere else, I was sure would have blinded me.

                                                        “I have chosen you, Dimasa; and have made you my own. I Exalt you above mortal men, schoolteacher.” He spoke as motes of light began to pour off him into me, causing me to gasp in shock. “B-but I don’t understand! I implored as the light intensified around myself.

                                                        “It is precisely because you do not understand Dimasa, that I have chosen you.” The voice of the man rumbled, growing fainter as he faded from my vision, the light consuming everything. “To admit one’s ignorance is the first step on the road to understanding.” He said as my, now no longer mortal, eyes snapped open.

                                                        I lay on a cold stone floor, scoured clean of dust and growth by some kind of soot-less flame. A distant voice echoed in my hearing. “I Exalt you; go, and raise the world from ignorance as best you know how.”

                                                        I pulled myself into a sitting position and examined my surroundings. My body no longer heated itself with fever, and my searing injuries had seemingly vanished; the only sensation that remained was my burning brow. The walls all around me were filled with complex designs and intricate scripture, seemingly traced in gold by an invisible hand. Before me sat a massive round door, covered in similar intricate designs, dominated by a mandala depicting the sun at twilight.

                                                        As my hand met the cold stone, it yielded easily to my touch. I have no words for what I found beyond, but beyond the door was an achievement in architecture; a place I now know to be named the Library-Tomb of Auriaga.

                                                        More of the beautifully inscribed writing spread off in every direction, spiderwebbing across stone in sufficiency I was certain to contain thousands of books in its tumescence; I could even see shelves surely filled with crumbling scrolls and texts of peculiar interest. If even a tenth of them were whole and readable it would be a stunning discovery. Beyond that I could see colonnaded arcades and delicate balconies stretching into the mouth of a vast shadowed canyon, a tributary of the one I had fallen avoiding above, stretching down for a great distance, filled with vegetation and flowing water.

                                                        Dominating the center of this entry chamber however was a great sarcophagus of stone and baroque design. I know not what instinct drove me toward it, but my fingers itched to open it. I felt no fear. I had risked my life once that night already seeking my students and my curiosity demanded satisfaction; the drive was there again. Was it perhaps a mighty king who laid here? A great hero of days gone by, or perhaps a powerful sorcerer with the skill to manipulate reality in ways I could scarce dream of.

                                                        The lid crashed to the ground, surprisingly whole and undamaged by its ordeal. As I peered inside, the glint of gold looked back. Shimmering there, clutched by the skeletal hands of a young man perhaps my own age, lay a magnificent sword of steel and bright gold; a metal I now know to be called Orichalcum, the blade had been turned all the colors of sunset by its tempering, blues and purples and oranges intermixed with glittering steel seemed to twinkle with its own source of light before my eyes. Upon the shoulders of the corpse lay a magnificent arming-jacket, formed of tooled red leather, velvet and Orichalc.

                                                        My fingers brushed the metal, and I collapsed screaming. For an instant I had been two men. I was myself, and also someone else, the father of the corpse before me. Momentarily overwhelmed with grief for a boy I had never known, I stood and dried my eyes. The ancient bones had crumbled beneath my touch, leaving the sword and jacket lying in the casket’s bottom. I grabbed them, heaving them over my shoulder, something urging me not to leave them as I stole away from the magnificent charnel-house I had found.

                                                        OBLIGATIONS OF THE CASTE
                                                        RAJMAEL

                                                        It took me less time to return home, my head hung in shame at my failure to find my charges, than it had to go in search of them. I arrived at noon, the hour of Gao’s collection when I saw them filing into the town square; a brass whistle in Gao’s hand, and a broad grin working its way across his piggish face as his men began to herd the children together with their weapons, shackles being pulled from their belts as the village folk looked on in despair, mothers wailing, tearing at their clothing and fathers staring death at Gao and his men.

                                                        I later learned that the children had wandered into a gully not too far from town and been stuck there when the path they had used had crumbled behind them; they had been found long before I had even taken my fall into the Library-Tomb. How Gao had figured out the system we had put in place for their protection, I will never know, but at the time I was unconcerned with anything else but the boiling rage in my heart as he grabbed the wrist of a young girl, screaming for his food and money.

                                                        I’m not entirely sure what came over me. I stepped from behind the one of the outbuildings; sword clutched in one hand, the buff-jacket upon my shoulders, its feathered epaulets spreading like the wings of a predatory bird. Almost instantly Gao recovered his composure, and began to laugh.

                                                        Gao announced to the village that, for my defiance, my head, as well as the “gaudy costume” I was wearing would be satisfaction enough for him today; he would even leave them their “brats” if they brought me to him. No one moved. Two of his men stepped forward to grab me. I have never been trained with a sword, but I was no slouch, work in my own garden as well as my daily regimen of katas and sutras had kept my muscles strong and my wits sharp. The sword; my sword snapped forward, slashing each of their arms and legs, dropping them to the ground, living, but bleeding. I could hear the metal screaming for blood in my mind, but I pushed it out, refusing to become the same kind of butcher as the men like Gao who had driven me here.

                                                        Gao snapped for his archers to fill me with arrows. By the time those arrows arrived, I was standing next to their leader. Gao barely had time to register my presence before he lost his hand. The girl he clutched ran for the safety of her mother’s arms as the bandit screamed, his men backing off in fear; light in the colors of the sunset, deep reds and purples splashed with gold and orange shimmered off me, a half-full ring gleaming upon my forehead, I looked a figure of terror.

                                                        Whimpering and sobbing, he stared up at me with an equal mixture of fear and hate.

                                                        Faking a steely resolve that I’m reasonably certain I didn’t have, I flicked my sword and told him to take his men and never return, galvanized by the day’s events, I saw several of the men of the village advance threateningly, carrying pitchforks and farming implements. Clutching at his stump, he ran. His men followed.

                                                        My fellows looked on me with an expression of fear upon every face; they knew what I was; Anathema, the Unclean, a wicked sorcerer who could steal the souls of mortal men. I turned to flee as applause broke through the crowd, leaving me stunned as they buried me under a press of celebrating bodies.

                                                        RAJMAEL ON CURIOSITY

                                                        An inquisitive mind is the birth of wisdom. When I took my Second Breath, the Sun spoke to me and explained that to admit one’s lack of understanding is the first step to achieving it. If that’s true, then the force that moves one along that path, the drive, is curiosity.

                                                        Nothing is more important to wisdom than a curious mind. Knowledge is nothing more than a mastery of facts; gleaned by a desire to know, to understand. Eagerness will drive one onward even without a course to go by; an interested scholar will find his answers, a seeking monk will discover his Enlightenment, a concerned student will eventually find his master.

                                                        My thirst for knowledge then is what awoke me to the wellspring begging to be tapped that was the Library-Tomb. It had been days since Gao had been forced out, and the timbre of my relations with my neighbors had shifted dramatically. I awoke each morning to people at my door, asking for my blessing, as though I were some protective deity; my students were more interested in hearing again of how I bested Gao, an event they bore witness to themselves, than hearing my lessons. And always the strange jacket and sword sat on a rack by my bedroom door as if to consciously remind me of the reality of what was happening.

                                                        I knew I needed to go back; I didn’t even know what my questions were, but I knew the Library-Tomb would have the answers.

                                                        I made my arrangements; I prepared my students and left one of my older pupils as my replacement, I packed up everything I felt I would need, spoke to my neighbors and my friends, and boarded up my home. I didn’t expect to be back for some time, if at all. Better that they be inured from what I was if the Realm’s reach decided it wished to grasp this piece of nowhere in its quest for me.

                                                        My feet carried me swiftly to my destination; finding it again was far simpler this time. Immediately I set to work; the texts, tomes and written inscriptions were vast and varied enough that I was sure it would take me a dozen lifetimes to read them all... though something in my heart told me I had that kind of time now.

                                                        Ever onward my wandering path led, and I learned much of who and what I now was. The Library-Tomb of Auriaga had been built in a time long vanished; designed as the final resting place of a great sorcerer-king named Rajmael the Thrice-Blessed; his face was one now intimately familiar to me; that of the bearded man clad in silk robes I had seen in my waking-dream. That face that was both his and mine.

                                                        Where the contents of the Library-Tomb ended and my own memory began, I could not say; it seemed that for every line I read about this mysterious sorcerer, and the time he resided in, recognition bubbled forth to my mind’s forefront. Dazzling my vision with images and apparitions; fleeting spectacles of the man I had once been.

                                                        I even saw my own death. An incandescent conflagration pouring forth from my fingertips and wrists; I knew I needed to avenge myself upon them; the children of the Dragon, pouring like a frothing wave from every direction. There was no time. I had grabbed my Phaeton, my bright, precious son and ran. He wanted to fight; ever a hero my brave boy but there would be no victory here.

                                                        Pain and steel! Phaeton’s lifeblood pouring upon floors of glass and chalcedony! Rage! A charred skeleton would greet this Dragon-Blood’s fellows! I had but one place, a secret and hidden one; there I could make Phaeton whole; I had intended it for my death, and now it would serve to give Phaeton new life, we could hide there from the greatest of seekers and the most meticulous of scrutinization.

                                                        I had though it impossible they could know; I had shaped my tomb personally, secretly, from the Dragon-Lines in this remote and foreign place, the canyon wall hiding it beneath overhang and riotous flora, but somehow they came not long after I arrived.
                                                        My beloved child… his breath left him as I looked on, all of my great powers and sorcerous might insufficient to save him from the vicious wound given to him. I laid him there in the arming jacket that I had worn in long ages of war, ever his favorite toy. My sword, clutched to his breast; I would need it no longer. I had given my dear Phaeton everything, and now I gave him my tomb.

                                                        Bleeding tears of fire I went out to greet them, my son’s murderers; I would char the flesh from the bones of my own hands and die a thousand deaths if it meant punishing them from taking him from me. Their bodies would be my pyre!

                                                        Drowning in memory and silent tears I had lain in the place where another man who was also myself had lain his son to rest.

                                                        I had found my answers; the Realm would find me, and I would die again under a crush of Dynastic bodies as their Wyld Hunt ran down its prey. I knew what I needed to do. I needed to win the sorcerous might of my forebear.

                                                        I needed a teacher.
