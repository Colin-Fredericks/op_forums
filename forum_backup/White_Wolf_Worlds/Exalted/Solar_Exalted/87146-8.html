                                I think the real problem with defining Solars as good in the context of Exalted is, it takes some of the punch off the idea that consequences are important. In my experience, people tend to go easy on those who make mistakes or take crazy risks when the intent behind their action is judged as good, downplaying the possible or actual negative consequences of failure, because it doesn't sound like a good idea to dissuade people from attempting to do good, even if the effort is less than perfect.

                                Exalted, it seems, likes to take a more cynical view of that - no, it doesn't matter how noble your intents were. The road to hell is paved with good intentions; what really matters are results. If you screwed up, it's all on you, and nobody will ask why you tried in the first place. If you succeed, nobody will argue morals with a winner. I think this outlook is much more appropriate for the genres Exalted is supposed to emulate - many myths and pulp stories tell of the exploits of heroes of relatively low morals, and morality plays are way more interesting when it's not predetermined who holds the moral high ground in the particular conflict.

                                Note that it doesn't preclude the idea of particular Solars, or any other heroes of Creation, trying their best to be good people - it just makes it more challenging and complicated for them, but when have challenges and complications ever stopped a Big Damn Hero?

                                        You're posing a problem to which the solution is "don't run a game full of black and white morality". Yes, you can depict non-Solars as broadly evil or degenerate, but there's no reason you have to, or even that you should. The situation is analogous to the Virtue system at character creation. Every Exalted character has at least one Virtue of at least 3. That statement is entirely focused on the Exalted, and it doesn't mean that normal mortals seldom have high Virtues. It doesn't mean the setting is designed so that the Storyteller should play all non-Exalted NPCs as having Virtues of 1 or 2 across the board.

                                        It's not useful to say that a setting proposition chokes off story options. Literally everything about a setting constitutes a constraint to story options. Creation is flat? You can't do a story about being the first person to circumnavigate it. Whitewall is landlocked? You can't do a story about a naval blockade there. Solars are always great people and have some minimum of 5 Willpower? You can't do a story about a self-effacing genius with imposter syndrome.

                                        Sometimes you have to dispense with parts of the setting to make the story work, and that's okay. We shouldn't be shy of defining a setting in the first place just because it constrains our possibilities.
