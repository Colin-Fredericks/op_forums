                        To be fair, when Exalted 1st edition was released it was till being pushed as the pre-history to the World of Darkness. So things went wrong, everyone failed, the Wheel of Ages turned slowly, Creation decayed and became more boring, and we ended up with the world of the World of Darkness.

                        Exalted also drew heavily from pulp fiction, much of which took place during ancient eras when men were men, somehow bigger and grander than they are today. -

                        "Long ago, in the ancient times before now..."

                        "A long, long time ago in a galaxy far, far away..."

                        etc. That was the tone that the writers were going for; that Exalted took place in an era of splendor unimaginable in the "current" era in which we live.

                        That's why the first paragraph of Chapter 1 of the core book sets the tone for how the Exalted failed and Creation was ultimately bent and broken.

                        I don't think 3rd edition is going for that.

                            My two Obols:

                            ALL of the Exalted are Heroes.

                            They are all Chosen to fight for something, and there's a community that lauds them for it. Even the "villain" splats! When Abyssals walk down the streets of stygia, dead mothers hold up their dead babies for the Deathknights to kiss. A Green Sun Prince can't walk down Malfeas' streets without demons crowding them for autographs and offering to buy them drinks that would kill a mortal, not because they're Cool, but because they are the key to getting them the hell out o hell, and retaking their rightful homeland.

                            The problem with mandating that Solars, or anyone else, must be a "good" person, as I see it, is that you then must define what "good person" MEANS. And that kind of nailing down closes off way too many story/character possibilities. Better to keep it that they are Chosen for their willingness to use their power to change the world. You can Exalt among the underclasses of Grayfalls, and play out a Heroic story of raising up the people to retake their land and lives from the Realm occupiers...but to the average people among the dynastic families living there, you're a Villainous monster out to destroy their way of life!

                            As for why the First Age started out a genuine Utopia, I suspect that a lot of that had to do with the fact that the early Solars were still taking moral instruction directly from TUS and his entourage, esp the Golden Lord and Nysela, and only later stopped consulting them and doing whatever they wanted. Some text on the Heirophant also indicates that he really did receive answers to his prayers to the Most High, before he insulted him by taking his name in vain...

                                                    I think it might be worthwhile to talk about what "Hero" means in a classical context. In Ancient Greece, "heros" originally referred to warriors and protectors, then later came to refer to demi-gods; In Latin, it referred to demi-gods and men of renown. In Plato's Republic, the Guardians (though closer to the DBs, as written) are a class of individuals who forsake any hope of an ordinary life or any claims to worldly property in order to protect and govern a society so that it flourishes. This touches on what I think is an important point for Solars: As a baseline, Solars are chosen from people willing to live outside of established patterns for the sake of something greater. So, I think any Solar content to live an unexceptional life is unlikely.

                                                    More important than etymology are probably examples: Hercules was not particularly interested in making the world a better place. He was interested in atoning for his personal sins due to his tragic flaw (Wrath) and spent twelve years in service of a mortal king, refusing payment for all of his tasks. In doing these tasks, he sometimes saves countless lives, but was sometimes simply asked to steal an artifact for a King who wasn't particularly deserving. He was a hero because he was amazingly good at what he did and he held fast to his ideals, even if we or Aristotle might accuse him of a lack of critical thinking. Most Greek heroes follow the same template: Odysseus, Achilles, Theseus, Perseus, Jason: They often do individual things that might not fit with our 21st-century morality, but they are amazing at what they do and, while they may not have the same morality we do, they exhibit moral courage: being willing to risk their lives for what they believe is important. That may be family, the kingship, defeat of an ancient enemy, sworn brotherhood.

                                                    In another part of the classical world, thousands and thousands and thousands of people are killed because Liu Bei refuses to give up his quest for the throne. He is a great ruler once he acquires the throne, but he reigns for only two years before being killed, and the Shu kingdom afterwards only lasts a few decades. One could easily argue it wasn't even worth it. That said, Liu Bei would still be a hero in the classical sense because he was extremely able and totally committed to his dream of ruling with the help of his sworn brothers.

                                                    Gaius Julius Caesar might even be the best example to illustrate the contrast. He was a consummate imperialist who spent most of his career crushing foreign powers into dust, looting the remains, and taking whoever was still alive as slaves for Rome. He becomes unbelievably powerful through doing this and uses his loyal soldiers to end the rule of the aristocratic families forever, concentrating all power in his own person, the eternal dictator. Yet, he brought great prosperity to the common people of his society, made extremely useful reforms, and prevented anyone from disrupting the stability of this new Roman society. Even though he was murdered by a conspiracy of detractors, those defending his legacy proved far more powerful than those who opposed it, and the name Caesar of Rome was so influential that it continued to be adopted by rulers all over the world: The Sultan of Rum, Tsar, Kaiser.

                                                    tl;dr: Gaius Julius Caesar, one of the most iconic historical examples of Solars, was a gaping fascist asshole by 21st century standards, but he was a hero revered above virtually all others by the standards of his ancient culture. He exemplified the Roman ideals of personal competence and service to the state, with the ideal of the best possible state winning out over the will of the actual rulers he was supposed to answer to.

                                                    Ultimately, I think Solars have amazing potential for good, but I think they are destined to be great rather than good or evil.
