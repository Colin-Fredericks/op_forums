        Originally posted by Morangias View Post

        Why not? It would work, maybe not as effectively as some other options, but it'd work and potentially those options are not available. Armies are not cheap. Manpower isn't unlimited. And sending out your own 1000 troops to fight and die when you don't need them and can defeat the enemy army by yourself could even be something that a virtuous warrior sees as immoral (none of my people need die while I protect them!)

        Let's say you're the ruler of a half dozen villages around a small town that a thousand men could bring to heel. Now let's say that a Solar has shacked up in the town, for whatever reason, and that Solar is a White Reaper style martial artist, or has a bunch of Melee charms, or is a sorcerer etc etc etc. Now those thousand troops are in trouble, never mind the locals, that Solar is a threat to them by themselves! Suddenly this minor little province is a much trickier prospect. That's what I mean by saying that Solars can overcome elements of scale; simply by the nature of how individually powerful they are, they overcome certain elements of scale.

            I don't think there's any disputing that Solars can do a lot. What doesn't make much sense though is for them to not be human, that is, to rule over a society or run an organization for which they have no need. The Solar could fight his kingdom's enemies alone if needed, but why do it? How does he even know where his enemies are, how strong they are, or what they've planned? Even if he destroys armies, what does he do against small, numerous, more mobile groups of raiders and saboteurs? There's a reason superheroes in the comics don't dissolve the city's police force and just use their superior capabilities to tackle all crime alone. Sure, Batman can solve any given crime better than the police, but it's absurd if he solves all of them alone. It's impossible to be everywhere and know everything, and wanting to do so is pretty bizarre (not in the "I wish I could do more," sense, but in the, "I actually want to be an entire government/army/industry," one), so why not let other people handle what they can?

            Just as we don't have charms for the Solar to know where every criminal or bandit or spy in her empire is, so we also don't have - and shouldn't have - charms to let the Solar replace all the craftsmen, ministers, farmers, and other workers and experts she ought to rely on at times. The Solar rules her lands, but she isn't the only one with an important job or meaningful agency.

                Originally posted by John Mørke View Post



                One of the big things we want to do with supplemental setting material is to make the material seem alive and current. The mood should be the farthest thing from reading like a scroll you dusted off, a concrete history that is dead the moment you lay hands on it.

                One of our ideas for handling that is to link setting material with dice rolls. For example, "This is the Lore difficulty to introduce a fact about [First Age secret you just read] and this is the Lore background you need to even recognize this material in play" and "If you roll [this many] Lore successes and have the appropriate background, you can introduce one of the following facts: [list of details about a lost culture being discussed]."

                IE, we have recognized a link between higher levels of interactivity with the setting material and the perception that the setting feels alive, and thus setting books are necessary.


                Sorry to reply to this a page later... but that's an excellent idea and you should definitely do that, because people constantly wonder exactly what their characters know and don't know about the setting. Which historical, political, and magical details are common knowledge and which are not? So this will tell them.

                    Even apart from the fact that there's a scope at which the resolution of something like battle group mechanics becoming fuzzy or just plain not returning coherent results, for me it's mostly a matter of priorities. I want to frame the issues of large scale warfare in terms other than "you fight a few dozen regular guys with some +5s to their attack stats with your gaming group sized Exalted attack force" so that things like the Realm having armies makes sense. And I want the Realm having armies to make sense, because stories of the scope and organization of war are very interesting to me.

                    Plus, there's a point at which one person or five people fighting tens of thousands just looks silly rather than impressive to me; the bad fight choreography of "the ninjas surround the hero and then politely go in one at a time and roll out of the way after a single punch" taking to a ludicrous scale, a scale where you've got some of those ninjas miles away from the individual they're standing in line to be punched down by*.

                    Hence, when the story has to go off into territory that the system has not mapped, and I have to describe what is found there, I will describe something that fulfills those priorities.

                    * That image of them waiting in a line that stretches over miles makes me think of another historical point, referring back to that point of why the army is not moving.

                    Because of course, historically most armies have not stayed around to actually be slaughtered once it's apparent that they have lost. You have an army of 30,000, you don't need to lose much more than 2,000 before seeing that it's time to pack it in, preferably in a direction away from that line consisting of thousands of spears and horses.

                    It's a matter of picturing basic geometry to see how that army that is not going to stay past the first few thousand casualties can go in a greater range of directions, because no matter how dangerous he is, one person is not quite the obstacle to a group spread over several miles that an army is.

                                    A Solar fighting an army can at least be made to make sense and require some thought. Maybe she positions herself at a bridge or ford or narrow pass. She uses her abilities and charms to hold her position and prevent enemy troops from passing her without being scattered or killed. She's risking her well-being and diverting her effort from the real tasks of leading.

                                    What makes almost no sense to me, if we're interested in the challenges of leadership, is having the Solar roll Bureaucracy to pull spare peasants out of the woodwork, War to make them into perfect soldiers, and then Craft to kit them out in the finest arms and armor. And then she repeats this trick in the next place the next day or week, as long as she feels like it.

                                    That's why I like that finding people and getting them to do what you want has more to do with story considerations and common sense than dice rolls. Tiger Warriors are a thing, but the Solar can't afford to keep more than a unit or two of such elite warriors around. And of course the scope of Craft is such that the Solar is much better off letting other people make things than trying to CNNT her way to 1,000 shields, spears, and breastplates. Even sorcery can't just shrug off all these limitations on one person's effort and grasp.
