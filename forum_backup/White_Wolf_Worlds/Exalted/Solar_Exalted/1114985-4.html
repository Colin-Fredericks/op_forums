                                On a tangent, I came across this description of the influence of the gods in the Iliad:

                                Originally posted by Julian Jaynes, The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind*, Chapter 3
                                When Agamemnon, king of men, robs Achilles of his mistress, it is a god that grasps Achilles by his yellow hair and warns him not to strike Agamemnon (I :197ff.). It is a god who then rises out of the gray sea and consoles him in his tears of wrath on the beach by his black ships, a god who whispers low to Helen to sweep her heart with homesick longing, a god who hides Paris in a mist in front of the attacking Menelaus, a god who tells Glaucus to take bronze for gold (6:234ff.), a god who leads the armies into battle, who speaks to each soldier at the turning points, who debates and teaches Hector what he must do, who urges the soldiers on or defeats them by casting them in spells or drawing mists over their visual fields. It is the gods who start quarrels among men (4:437ff.) that really cause the war (3:164ff.), and then plan its strategy (2:56ff.). It is one god who makes Achilles promise not to go into battle, another who urges him to go, and another who then clothes him in a golden fire reaching up to heaven and screams through his throat across the bloodied trench at the Trojans, rousing in them ungovernable panic. In fact, the gods take the place of consciousness.

                                The beginnings of action are not in conscious plans, reasons, and motives; they are in the actions and speeches of gods. To another, a man seems to be the cause of his own behavior. But not to the man himself. When, toward the end of the war, Achilles reminds Agamemnon of how he robbed him of his mistress, the king of men declares, "Not I was the cause of this act, but Zeus, and my portion, and the Erinyes who walk in darkness: they it was in the assembly put wild ate upon me on that day when I arbitrarily took Achilles' prize from him, so what could I do? Gods always have their way." (19:86-90).
                                I would guess that in the world of Creation, omnipresent religious experience are not part of the world in this way. The gods (including the Incarna!) don't speak directly into the minds of humanity, don't have an omnipresent influence. This doesn't seem to be part of the fiction, and various factors in the setting militate strongly against it, not least how social influence and integrity is construed, and the idea that the Mandate of Heaven is given to the Exalted.

                                Instead human in Creation mostly influence each other, or act according to their own psychology. They are those kind of heroes.

                                So curious what happens in people's games, with the Solars and other Exalted. Do your games emulate at all relationships like the above between human and god or is it much more hands off? Would it be fun to do Exalted, or a mythic fantasy roleplaying game, the way that the above excerpts work and with the gods having that kind of influence and presence?

                                (Truncated from a longer reply. This thread kind of inspires a few of thoughts in me about the tension between polytheism and monotheism, between moral religion and amoral religion, how necessary divinity as the creator is to religion. But I couldn't go anywhere with them!).

                                *You can ignore the text it's from, as the argument that makes, about the degree to which this literally represents a description of the ancient thought process, is not really so relevant to what I'm talking about.

                                                    Originally posted by Jen View Post

                                                    Religion, belief and worship in Exalted as presented in the book felt kinda weird to me, so if you guys don't mind me asking some question.......

                                                    1/ If a Solar is the roaming Creation freely type, then most of them will become less religious as they travel, aren't they ? Since meeting other spirits will makes them realize that their God is just merely a Terrestrial sky god and not some almighty creator who birth the world in seven day (this'll happen even early for Zenith).

                                                    2/ Is "Cultural Christian" a thing if you are a non-religious Solar, as in you celebrate a festival not because you believe in its backstory about a guy coming back from the dead, but more because it's your city's traditions/cultural identity and you aren't gonna stop eating those delicious cake just because the stories aren't real. Kinda like an atheist Jew who still attend Jewish holidays.

                                                    3/ Just in my house alone are the altar for my deceased grandparent, near it is the altar for the Buddhism pantheon, scattering around the house are altars to god of kitchen, the ground, luck, wealth, longevity...etc. In the street are temples and shrines to river gods, wandering ghosts, deified people or even animal like tiger.

                                                    Is this suppose to be what most the religion landscape in Creation looks like i.e People follow a mixture of different beliefs and deities from various religions ? Since I heard that Creation is suppose to be animism and Shintoism or something, does that means place like Haborhead and Whitewall are oddballs since they only worship one supreme deity ?
                                                    This is my understanding:

                                                    1) Creation has no concept of an all-powerful sky god who birthed the world in seven days to lose faith in in the first place. Why would their beliefs change (in this manner, anyway)?

                                                    2) Keep in mind that gods are established facts in Creation as obvious and immutable as gravity, and that (iirc) you don't need to actually believe/trust in the god you're praying to in order for your prayer/devotion to give them power. Most of the gods require a particular ritual be performed in their honor, rather than that those who pray to them have actual faith.

                                                    3) It's more accurate to compare the gods to a local government (see the term Heavenly Bureaucracy). They're certainly powerful, but there are other gods elsewhere who manage similar tasks, and gods within their own area that outrank them. The god of This River in the South isn't also the god of That River in the North East, even if functionally speaking they're similar. There's little reason to pray to gods on the other side of the world when one nearby can do the same thing for you for the same effort (the obvious exception being extremely pious priests and the like who the gods will empower no matter where they are)
