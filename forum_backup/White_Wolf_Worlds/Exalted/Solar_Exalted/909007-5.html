        Originally posted by Caffeine Delusions View Post
        Not saying that this needs to apply to Exalted, but... The classical concept of the hero is 'paragon of his society's virtues'. That can make a lot of classical heroes seem ... pretty not-moral. Achilles was a killer and a rapist, with a major case of the rages, but he's considered a hero by the ancient greeks because he was an awesome fighter who swayed the course of a ten year war, and when motivated by the loss of someone he loved, tore down an army. Odysseus was a philanderer with a cruel streak, but his cunning won a war, and his journey home threw him into conflict with sorcery, monsters, and gods which he prevailed against through an application of that same intellect. A lot of the things we see as 'evil' in these heroes are simply not important to the greek culture because their values are different from ours. They didn't consider women to be... well, people. They saw martial skill and loyalty as important, intellect doubly so. The other things didn't factor so much for them as a simple matter of values dissonance.

        When it comes to Solars, I'd say in some sense they should reflect that. Creation is a big place, there's room for a broad range of cultures, each of whom have their own values, their own perspectives, and their own dissonance. Some Immaculate-raised son of a Great House is going to have vastly different opinions about what 'good' or 'virtue' means, as compared to the various threshold societies.

        Again, not universally applicable here, but worth considering.

        I wanted to refer to this one specifically, because the last game I ran was Fate, set in the mythical mediterranean. They were all flawed heroes, as the greek heroes are, but they were all unequivocally -heroes-. The game ended a bit bleakly, because they had been unknowingly unsealing the binds that kept Thanatos (represented here as an angry child throwing all his toys out of the pram) and one of the heroes sided with him for the promise of a better world once the Olympian gods had been purged. The unbinding was completed by one member of the party at the same time that the gods were alerted to the whole scheme, Thanatos killed everyone except the loyal PC, and then the PC got a suitably eternal punishment for trying to destroy the world.

        I would class every single member of that party as a hero, for all their faults. Not necessarily by our standards, but by the definition of "would someone have written their tales on papyrus?"

                        Originally posted by Rowanas View Post
                        Top up question - I'm personally unhappy with the idea of using the powers of a solar to instill intimacies in other PCs, but is that a legitimate thing to do in most people's opinions? I think it probably depends entirely on the group, and I need to ask mine before I go all Fulminating Word on them.
                        It's a legitimate use of powers, and here's why. The social influence system models all human interaction. If you and your fellow gaming nerds are trying to decide to order pizza or chinese for dinner, and you say "I had pizza last night. Can we skip it this week? We can do pizza next week." then you just engaged in social influence. You are bargaining with them, or instilling an Intimacy of sympathy like "I don't like eating pizza two nights in a row, either." There's nothing malicious or coercive in it.

                        However, players often perceive ANY actions upon their character sheet as coercive, and particularly inexperienced roleplayers react defensively and negatively to such things. That situation is, again, something that should be addressed at an OC level with the group at large. I think many groups will knee jerk their way to "no social influence on fellow PCs," and that might work for them. But, they have just removed the only guide the system has for resolving disputes between PCs, which means OC social dynamics are more likely to influence the game.

                        At my table, I took pains to explain how the social influence system worked, and what it modeled. My players are mostly new, but they are mature people, and they get along. They have done a fantastic job playing their characters well the few times they used social influence on each other. It helps that I made them all build up Intimacies of trust and loyalty to each other as a requisite for their concepts. I can then say "Dexanus Ilessa is telling you to put the sword away and trust her. She has successfully convinced you that she knows what's going on. What do you do?"

                        So, if your group agrees to interact with the system for what it is, rather than as a vector of attack, and your ST actively narrates the outcomes of the rolls such that they prompt the players to contextualize the rolls, it can work rather well.

                            I think that for the vast majority of players, if you end up in a situation where they're saying "I want my character to do X" and another player across the table from them say "no, I'm gonna roll dice and make you do Y instead", they're going to see that as an attack. The terminology isn't important and isn't going to change how players feel about it.

                            Players are at the table because they want to play a specific character, who acts in a specific way. To a certain extent, saying "no, it's not magical compulsion, it's what your character actually wants" makes it even worse, because you're changing their basic character concept by doing it (rather than just making one attack they can later recover from -- even if they later lose the intimacy or stop doing what you said, they can't change the fact that you defined their character as someone who would sometimes think or act that way.)

                            Sometimes you might be able to sell them on a change that makes the character more interesting; but sometimes, changing their character's intimacies will result in something they have no interesting playing, and that is absolutely something everyone at the table has to respect. If I was an ST and a player insisted on trying to instill an intimacy in another PC that that other player clearly wasn't comfortable with, I'd probably boot them, because you just do not do that. The game requires that everybody be on the same page, which means that (unless you've agreed in advance that this will be a PVP-ish game), if you're making a social attack on another PC that their player doesn't want, you are at least very close to the point where the entire game collapses under mutually incompatible expectations.

                            And because of that, I am extremely leery of arguments that go along the lines of "you just have to accept the social system as it is and not get all uptight about it", because the underlying subtext is "if you're not a wuss, you'll play out this scene even though you obviously really don't want to", and that is grade-A nuclear-class bullshit.

                            Now, 3e's social system tries to reduce this a bit by giving players a way to map out their character and making it really really hard to make a character do something that wasn't at least notionally on their initial map of what the character is like. But ultimately, everyone at the table should be trying to ensure that everyone else has fun; if it's clear that what you're doing isn't fun for the other players, and you're still pushing it, you're ruining the game for every else and don't belong there. And this means, to me, that most of the time the social system is not going to be used between PCs unless it's to do trivial things or things both players are obviously happy with.

                            (Keeping in mind that another thing the 3e social system does is provide places where you can use social "support" roles on other players to eg. play a therapist who removes unwanted intimacies or adds ones they want; it's not like forcing people to do things is the only use for it.)

                                (And, to be fair, the man was presumably motivated by a desire to see the British Imperial Presence in Burma burn, i.e. he just wanted them to fail and die)
                                That's... not fair at all.

                                "He wanted to hurt the British Imperial Presence in Burma specifically" (because they're doing awful shit in general / because he wants to free his nation / because he hates them for killing his spouse or child or parent or dog, etc) is very very different from "he wanted to make everyone in the entire world suffer for lolz because he was irrational and evil." And more importantly, by automatically assuming that it's the latter, Alfred is implicitly whitewashing both the government of Burma and his own involvement with it.

                                He can say "well, that man was just twisted and sick and evil and needed to be put down" without looking at the situation in more depth, which is convenient for him because if he did look at it in any more depth, it's very likely that the guy actually had some sort of motivation or goal which might have made Alfred question what the hell he was doing working for the Burmese government.

                                (Of course, this is an underlying theme when it comes to Batman and vigilante comics in general. Some versions, like TAS, managed to avoid this trap, but when Batman is written badly, it doesn't let you look at the underlying causes of crime; it can't even let you suspect that the villains might have a reason to do what they do. For Batman's shtick of dealing with crime by running around punching people in the face to make sense as the best way a fucking billionare could deal with crime, you have to assume that most criminals are soulless monsters who just want to hurt people for the sake of it and will only stop when terrified of someone bigger and darker and more bat-like than themselves.)
