    Why Solars Can't Change the Setting

    01-08-2016, 05:37 PM
    Full disclosure: the title is clickbait. Solars can indeed change the setting, but not by means of Setting-Changing Prana. I've seen a few posts and threads over the last few months asking where the charms are to do big stuff. What does a Solar need to do to cure a lot of sick people, build a lot of stuff, or manage an organization or state? What do we roll, and how can we enhance the roll?

    I went back to the "What we know" wiki and found some stuff John said on the matter:
    Macro magic doesn't make sense. The Solars will roll up the setting in a rug and carry it off within a year and nobody will be able to stop them, most especially other Exalts. The CRM is about macro magic, about ignoring personal, individual conflicts and abstracting those things into a dice roll. The biggest obstacle to a Solar's ascent is that he is one supernal mind/spirit among millions of mortals with various flaws, blindspots, failings, weaknesses, etc., and he has to work with and through these people to get things done. You can't make running a nation a matter of rolling his dice and using his Charms, because:

    1) The Solar disappears completely on the macro scale.
    2) The Solar's stats and Charms appended onto an organization makes that organization a Solar, and unstoppable except by another Solar or Exalt with comparable powers.
    3) #2 entails erasing the obstacles and the roleplaying involved in this process. It also doesn't make sense. Solar Charms enhance their own actions. It smashes the setting into flinders when their Charms also enhance the actions of thousands of other people. While Exalted is an extremely high powered game, it's not okay to publish systems that make the entire setting implausible.

    The net result is that you stop playing Exalted and start playing a purely mechanical system that projects advancement that is wildly, hysterically disproportionate to how fast an Exalt-managed organization actually advances. I will never put a dot on your character sheet that says "and when you buy this one, you have successfully resurrected the First Age. Hooray for you."
    Think about it: Exalted is about playing an Exalt. If you don't want to actually roll out what your subordinates are doing because it would detract away from playing your Exalt, I'm not going to make a system to abstract doing exactly that. No abstraction would make sense or have any bearing on your character.

    Your character can influence, inspire, and unite people. He can educate them, arm them, and train them for battle. We have written rules to reflect these things in the corebook. But there isn't actually a roll to decide whether they succeed or fail because no such roll would make sense and no such system would be calibrated to the actual rate of progress an Exalt should be able to push.

    There are very real reasons wars are lost, projects fail or succeed, etc., and these instances can be recreated in Exalted and exploited by your Charms, and that is what I am out to reveal in EX3.
    Some people have pointed out that we could use more information about the kinds of obstacles there are to achieving big goals, and I think that makes some sense. While there are resources outside Exalted for learning more, it would be nice for future books to help us figure out what characters in Creation might have to overcome.

    What we won't see, and what is far from an accidental omission, is a way to make Creation really nice to live in really fast. When large scale change is a matter of rolling dice, the Solar will succeed unless something bigger stands in her way. With that in mind, we may as well skip the bit about the dice.

    So what's bigger than a Solar in this context? Time, finite resources, and other people, for starters. The Solar doctor can drag even the frailest patient back from the brink of death, but she may not be able to save a village from the plague even if she pushes herself to the limits. A Solar craftsman can quickly produce arms and armor for his circle, but not for a legion of peasant conscripts. A Solar cannot run a state without regard for the physical or social makeup of the place or the people; she cannot force others to succeed or create prosperity out of nothing with no means other than her essence and her will (OK, WST maybe breaks some rules there - no wonder it's such a big deal).

    Whatever was achieved in the First Age certainly depended on the Solars and their magic, but it was not an inevitable result of their existence. And however the Solars shape the current Age, they will still have to try, and they may err in more than their aims and they may fail for more reasons than not enough dice or not the right charms.

                                                      I don't think anyone doubts that a Solar, given the chance and the right skills, can chew through a thousand-man army. But he can only do that if the army cheerfully engages him on those terms. If his opponent instead splits his army into five 200-man raiding parties and starts burning down villages, though, it doesn't really matter that the Solar could kill all one thousand of them given the chance; the Solar can't protect his kingdom, and his people will lose faith in him because of that.

                                                      That's the sort of thing that organizations and bureaucracies and communications and supply lines and, yes, having an army of your own are for. The writers have made it clear that they don't think Solars should be able to cheat those things away trivially, and should instead engage meaningfully with them. And that's great! So...can we have some material to help us do that?

                                                      EDIT: Maybe I can expound on what I want a little via an extended example:

                                                      Say I have a player facing the above situation. He has risen through his might and prowess and is hailed as a hero in a certain region, and they now look to him for the just rule and protection that he has promised to give them because...well, that's what he did! He kicks the old ruler out and installs himself as the benevolent warlord of this province. But the (not yet dead) king-in-exile has removed himself to sanctuary in a neighboring province and sworn to reclaim his territory. Story basics: handled.

                                                      Now, the king-in-exile is a canny sort and knows that the Solar can hack through any number of soldiers sent against him personally (not least because the Solar has boasted of this publicly, and done so on several occasions), so he doesn't lay siege to his old haunt where the Solar now hangs his hat. He starts sending out smaller but still dangerous raiding forces to burn crops and torch villages and in general plunder his own former subjects to undermine the Solar's rule. It works, because the Solar can't be everywhere at once. So the Solar wises up and decides he wants to raise forces of his own to patrol his lands and defend against these attacks and prove that he really can protect his people. So how does he do this?

                                                      Well, first he has to recruit soldiers. No problem, lots of starry-eyed young farmers eager to be heroes, but...he needs a lot of them, and he can't do all of this personally. He needs to send out recruiters. Okay, a dozen reasonably convincing people can manage this. Also send scribes, because you should know who it is you are signing up.

                                                      Okay, now you need to train them. That also means feeding and housing and outfitting them, which means supplies. You could make them, or you could buy them from the local Guild factor. That means setting up standing supply contracts. You'll need to pay for all this too. Also, probably you should start paying your employees, since you're starting to have a lot of them, too. And the soldiers, they're going to need to be paid (you don't want to end up like Caesar, after all). You could make your own equipment, but then you'll need raw materials and infrastructure to do that - did you already have all that? Where is the money for all this coming from? Shit, you're going to need to start collecting taxes and tariffs, aren't you? And that means you'll need accountants to keep track of your expenses, so that you know where the money is going.

                                                      Training. Probably going to need some instructors to keep the recruits in line and drilling when the Solar isn't doing it himself, but let's assume this is something he really can manage. League of Iron and Tiger Warrior Training are pretty sweet, after all! Awesome, we got some badasses up in here. Who's going to lead them? The Solar can't be everywhere, after all - that's why he's doing all of this. Gonna need to recruit some trusted lieutenants.

                                                      Huh, all this military buildup has made one of his neighbors nervous. They've sent an ambassador to establish regular diplomatic relations with the Solar's government (because he has a government now!). They'll need accommodations. Also, diplomats are basically spies, so he'll need to set some people to keep an eye on this fellow and make sure he doesn't start...doing things he shouldn't. Probably time for a spymaster too, unless the Solar wants to spend all of his time managing intelligence reports. And military reports. And the budget. And tax receipts.

                                                      We're going to need some ministers, aren't we?

                                                      Now, the above is just spitballing, and it's not that complicated for someone with some foundational knowledge of history, political science, and government. But here's the thing: how much of the above is the average Exalted player (or ST) expected to bring to the table with them? Because so far the game itself doesn't offer even the barest framework for players to start with. I haven't even gotten into things that are setting-specific to Creation, like how the above might be affected by the existence of spirits or magic. But even without those complications, one has to ask: if it is the position of the writers that 1) the players of Solars should be able to tell stories of conquest and empire-building, and 2) that they don't get to cheat their way past the necessary frameworks that make such things possible, then 3) is it not reasonable to expect some advice on how to go about doing this?

But what will they use this for? This is the nature of macro vs micro in the terms of OP/the developers. It's not that scale is impossible, it's that... Well... Lets back up just a bit.

                                                          In second edition, Solars had powers that could simply end a story before it was told. It wasn't just raw power, but the guidance of the sun's prowess was such that there was such a thing as perfect government running. And if your goal in storytelling was to gloss over the making of a perfected world, and instead just do the cool fight scenes? Well, sure. That's fine! But by making that the default, it meant that anyone who wanted more nuance would struggle uphill. It's much easier to decide you don't want to include a thing than it is to say you want to make something wholesale. So in third edition, the game switched stances and no longer allows solars to have charms that grant perfect judgement or story-cutting charms.

                                                          But when you say things like.. a solar can outfit a leigon of peasants. A solar can cure a plague. They don't have charms that just DO that... they have charms that let them do an action supernally. You can cure this person, guaranteed, in 1 hour. How are you gonna get past that limitation? Triage, some people die, but you save the most possible? Or do you go, while you can, to learn a spell or charm that will let you do it faster? Do you make a bargain with a local god? Do you beat up the abysal who released the plague so that you can more effectively make a counteragent? Etc etc. This is micro level storytelling. This is focusing on the minutiae. We make the charms at this level because we assume that if the stories aren't interesting outside of micro level, we can abstract it to go more quickly, it's easier to cut details than add them.

                                                          Now... in macro level setting resolution we say that this the minutiae of this action aren't important. roll for how many patients you save, use charms to say how many more you save. What matters is purely in the numbers. Don't have the charm that increases scale? Well, you weren't good enough this time. It certainly does a good job of illustrating that the character was amazing. But... we lost out on the individual stories. Which isn't to say that macro storytelling doesn't have it's place, just that it isn't the good default place. To say that Solars can overcome all limitations is great, but it's also great to take a moment to figure out HOW. Are there consequences? Because that might be interesting!

                                                          It's not that solars can't interact with the setting at large, it's that skipping straight to that cuts out all the really interesting stories, and we wanna be able to tell them!
