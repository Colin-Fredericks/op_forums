                                        Originally posted by MissMaddy View Post
                                        I find the best way to deal with this is to step out of the D&D paradigm and give people something to DO - most D&D games don't focus on curing somebody of cancer and then observing the joy you have brought them, or repairing a community ravaged by leprosy. For my table at least, the rewards of these actions may be primarily emotional or story-based, but they are still 'carrots' that are sought eagerly by my players.

                                        Medicine allows you to make the harsh reality of the world slightly less stinging, which depending on the plot you put forward, may end up being the most important power in the world to your players.

                                        D&D doesn't emphasise these social issues as much, so of course the ability to heal is therefore significantly devalued. Viewed through the lens of combat utility, possibly the most miraculous and awesome supernatural power people could GET is actually kinda boring!
                                        Realistically, previous editions of medicine (again, barring a few admittedly-oddball DotFA charms we've been specifically told are not coming back) have not given you any tools that would help you approach a large community ravaged by leprosy. I don't mean that it's weak, I mean that you can functionally do nothing beyond maybe cure a handful of people a day as an inspiration -- which is cool, but will have no practical effect whatsoever on the larger problem.

                                        A typical Zenith or Eclipse caste, with no Medicine whatsoever (or maybe just a few dots to give them a general idea of what usually works), is going to be vastly better at handling a major disease outbreak than a dedicated corebook Solar doctor -- even if they have no idea what the appropriate medical approach is, their charms will help them ensure that the sick are quarantined and cared for, and allow them to encourage and implement systems that can provide better nutrition and sanitation. No corebook Medicine charm will help in that situation at all.

                                        Yes, you can throw the player a bone by giving them a small number of important sick people to heal, or just let them use their power like a drop in the bucket and gloss over the fact that they're accomplishing almost nothing (beyond maybe a small morale boost, which the Zenith would be vastly better at). And some players are probably perfectly willing to suspend their disbelief, activate their medicine charms on a handful of people, and go home feeling like they made a difference. But when it comes to confronting the problems most circles naturally tend to focus on -- even including medical problems -- you would be much better served with charms from other trees. And -- in my experience -- most players are happier when they have powerful, general-purpose tools that let them decide overarching goals and work towards attaining them. Social charms do that. Combat charms do that. Sorcery does that. Crafting and infrastructural charms do that. Medicine doesn't. They can solve that one specific sort of problem if you, as the ST, hand it to them; but they tend to not have much applicability for anything else.

                                        (This leads to the situation where Presence, Performance, Bureaucracy, and Socialize charms -- backed up with maybe a few dots of medicine to tell you how to use them, at best -- are vastly better for dealing with large-scale disease outbreaks than Medicine charms, because social charms are flexible and versatile. I can come up with a complicated, clever plan and use social charms to implement it; with Medicine, I'm generally limited to one tightly-boxed effect which does one specific thing, and my charms provide no real assistance when I want to do anything beyond that.)

                                        Keep in mind I'm not talking about huge sweeping things like 2e Taboo-Inflicting Diatribe here. Even at very basic things like "save a small town from the plague", Medicine is ineffectual compared to things like Presence, Performance, Socialize -- honestly, nearly any non-combat ability beyond Ride or Sail (which have similar issues) would be better.

                                                Originally posted by Elemental View Post
                                                Sure. But the difference is, the effect of tactical planning is to make the actual combat more effective, and the social rolls make the investigation more effective. They don't (and shouldn't) edge Melee or Craft out of their supposed specialties, which seems to be the problem with Medicine being overshadowed.

                                                It keeps coming back to this--if someone goes with "legendary healer" as their concept, and wants to cure a city of a plague, they shouldn't be told "Well, you should have bought social / crafting skills if you want to do anything for more than a few dozen people."
                                                I think you missed my point. For example, the character with "legendary warrior" as a concept still is told "well you should've gotten War if you want to fight more than a few dozen people at a go." The "legendary investigator" is told "well you need social Abilities if you want to carry on more than a few investigations at a time. The "legendary crafter" is told "well you need Bureaucracy if you want to do anything more than hand-made crafts and not take forever."

                                                By the same token, as the warrior or crafter are not edged out of their supposed specialties by having other Abilities that extend the scope of their powers, nor should the medic be overshadowed by requiring other Abilities to extend the scope of their powers.

                                                Game design can really go either way: you can fill an Ability with Charms that cover pretty much everything someone of that archetype would want to do, or limit it so such characters would have to dip into other Ability trees. Some Abilities (the ones that are significantly limited in some way, like Sail) could have more of the former while other Abilites (that are more ubiquitous) could be more the latter. It's not a bad thing to encourage cross-Ability synergies, though.

                                                        Originally posted by Reflexive Actions
                                                        Some actions are reflexive, meaning they do not require any deliberate attention from the character to perform, nor do they cause or suffer from multiple-action penalties. If a character approaches an ambush, his player could certainly make regular (Perception + Awareness) rolls if the character was feeling paranoid. However, even if the character is not actively looking out for an ambush, he might notice the glint of moonlight on a crouched assassin’s knife with just enough time to shout a warning and respond. The latter sort of check would be reflexive. Resisting poison and disease also count as reflexive actions (as do any action where the character has an innate defense that is rolled, rather than modifying the difficulty of a roll as most defenses do). Of particular note, the activation of a reflexive type Charm is always a reflexive action.
                                                        Stopping Bleeding is reflexive, it doesn't require any deliberate attention from the character to perform.

                                                        Originally posted by Bleeding:
                                                        Characters suffering from lethal or aggravated damage continue to deteriorate as a result of hemorrhaging until they stanch their wounds. Exalted and beings of similar resilience may draw their wounds closed with a successful reflexive (Stamina + Resistance) roll (difficulty 2) on the part of their players, and they may attempt this feat in combat on any tick in which they may act. Outside of combat, characters can try this every five seconds. Furthermore, bleeding stops as soon as anyone with Medicine 1+ takes an action to treat the wounds
                                                        Mortals are not so lucky as the Chosen. Stanching a wound requires a successful (Wits + Medicine) roll at a difficulty equal to the number of lethal or aggravated health levels associated with that injury. This is a standard miscellaneous action in combat. For instance, a character who was stabbed twice in the belly for two and then three level health levels would need to succeed at two separate stanching attempts, one at difficulty 2 and one at 3, respectively. Stanching requires bandaging materials, but these materials can be improvised under duress from any torn strip of cloth. Any strenuous activity, including combat and anything requiring Athletics rolls, will reopen a mortal’s stanched wounds, but the Exalted and other magical beings with an innate lethal soak are immune to the dangers of reopened wounds. As long as a character suffers from bleeding wounds, she suffers one level of unsoakable lethal damage every increment of (Stamina) minutes. Mortals who do not receive prompt medical attention can and will bleed to death.
                                                        You're an exalt. You have Stamina 2 and Resistance 0. You will bleed every 120 ticks (2 minutes). You can reflexively roll 2 dice (Stamina 2 and Resistance 0) with a difficulty of 2 to stop this. The chance of failing 23 times is .72^23 = 0.0523175524% about 1 time in 1,911.
