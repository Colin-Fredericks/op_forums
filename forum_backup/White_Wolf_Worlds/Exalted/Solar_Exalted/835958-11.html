                tl;dr: give your big bads a martial art style and a couple of charms meant to compliment its strengths. Soak SERIOUSLY prolongs a fight and increases the viability of social influence in combat. It is really good. Battle groups are also great, and sprinkling the opposition with lieutenants giving Command bonuses gives only mildly fighty characters some high priority targets to focus on.

                Hey Northgame people, don't read this. Spoilers! Sort of!

                I had great fun playing an obnoxious (and damaged) elder Changing Moon against my Circle of Essence 1 Solars in a scene that was half combat and half social influence. She's playing the role of a secondary but potent adversary right now. They caught her in the act of sabotaging their followers and vessel, but she managed to temporarily incapacitate a few mortal lieutenants before they rolled Join Battle. That is surprisingly important, because War God Descendent is a hell of a trick, and you do not want even mediocre battle groups rolling attacks against you with even mediocre leadership backing them up.

                They played 'nice' for three rounds before talking her down, and she completely depleted the Dawn's essence pool and a great deal of their willpower with the following tricks:

                Full Mastery of Dreaming Pearl Courtesan Style.
                Bitter Medicine Bait: When someone spends a willpower to ignore her Inspire attempt, she may spend 4 motes and 1 willpower to immediately Instill a Minor Tie of Guilt towards her, without needing to roll. This effect applies to everyone affected by the original Inspire attempt who chooses to resist. Willpower paid to ignore this automatic Instill effect is awarded to the Lunar. 1/scene per target.
                Hide Like Tusks: +8 soak until her next turn for 4m1w. 1/scene, reset when she resists a withering attack of at least 15 raw damage without suffering initiative loss.
                Hidden Mirror Smile: When her Solar mate (the Dawn) rolls in opposition to her for any effect, she may spend a mote to roll all of her mate's 1s as dice on her own behalf. This effect may not be used if her mate says her name while executing the action, and every time this charm is successfully used, the Solar mate is entitled to a Wits+Integrity check (difficulty 5) to remember this name, even if she's never heard it before. If the party ever figures out the trick to this, I expect "TETSUUUOOO!" style shouts with every stunt.

                High combat pools, influence pools, and guile, with matching Lunar Excellencies. No sorcery, lore, medicine, or other support tricks.
                With her high Essence Lunar hax, Fragrant Petal Fascination Kata, Seven Storms Escape Prana, and Demure Carp Feint, she was exceedingly difficult to pin down, much less kill, even for a very combat-heavy Circle. They ended up convincing her to leave, but she gave the Zenith a 10 HL kick to the face with Pillow Book on her way out. (It's cool; he's supernal resistance, and it's his own fault for Fluttering Cry of Warning! every damn attack.)

                In reserve, she also had:
                Predator Grace Method
                Deadly Beastman Transformation
                Full Mastery of White Reaper Style
                Moon Healing Escalation: When assuming Deadly Beastman Transformation, she recovers half of her spent motes and half of her wounded health levels.

                Playing rough was not necessary. IIRC, she used Revolving Crescent Defense once.

                I don't know whether Lunars can actually attain use of the Mastery keyword for martial arts styles, but DPC and WRS seemed like good approximations for Changing Moon and Full Moon style ass-kicking. After reading the charms and figuring out how the fighting style worked for DPC, I asked myself what sort of native charms would make it absolutely infuriating to play against (along the lines of Solar Socialize and Black Claw Style's synergy), and invented some probably-too-cheap-to-be-balanced supplements for it. It was an entertaining encounter, and the willpower-stealing drama bomb that was Bitter Medicine Bait was most satisfying.

                ---

                Previous opponents that got a satisfactory stunt out of every player include literally a hundred first circle demons split into three battle groups with competent leadership, under the overall command of a Terrestrial Ebon Shadow Stylist. Against four Solars and two Battle Groups aboard the Supernal Sailor's personal vessel, this was a hell of a fight. Naval Maneuvering and an Ambush reversal by the Dawn turned it into a surprisingly quick encounter, but enough of the Circle's followers were killed in the melee to make for some interesting character moments afterwards. The Zenith got his caste Solar XP by starting a tradition of raiding the captain's personal store of liquor to honor the dead.

                ---

                Since the Circle is relatively combat heavy (and to establish the depth of their supernatural opposition--House Ledaal wants their Twilight dead, and the Lunar wants to bleed the Dawn slowly for her role in her own previous incarnation's demise) the first four sessions have featured some big fights. I'm looking forward to pivoting into a more political environment for the next few sessions.

                    This may be too little, too late sort of advice, and may have already been covered, but I didn't see it in my somewhat hasty read-through of the thread. Apologies if I missed it.

                    In my experience, the best way to re-introduce challenge to a party that is mighty in combat is not to scale up the combat, but to change the stakes. Don't make it about life-or-death for the party. Make it about the goals the party is pursuing, and the goals the opponents are pursuing. The combat-focused Dawn will probably slaughter whatever he puts his daiklaive to, to whatever degree of slaughter he chooses. Let him. Challenge him not with something that can "take it" or that can "dish it out" to him, but with the need to prioritize what he kills, or to be cautious with his kill-choices. With the time taken to beat something providing crucial delays before he can get to the victory condition for the party, or prevent the enemy from achieving theirs.

                    As an example that doesn't QUITE fit here, but hopefully illustrates the notion, consider a classic: the party is serving as escorts for a trade caravan, and it comes under assault by bandits. The bandits' goal is not murder (though they're not above it); it's getting as much loot as they can and getting away with it. The party's goal is not mere survival, nor the slaughter of the bandits (though the latter would achieve their goal); it's preventing damage and theft of life and property.

                    The challenge is not even binary, here; each time the bandits damage something or kill an NPC, it's a mark against the party. Each thing the bandits get to run away with is a mark in their favor. Driving the bandits off before they do any permanent damage is total victory as much as slaughtering the bandits outright before they can do any permanent damage. Having the bandits come in, kill a few NPCs, and ride off with 10-30% of the goods is a pretty hefty loss, even if the PCs never take more than withering damage.

                            I'm glad it's helpful to some. I will say that I actually think this kind of encounter design leads to better games, overall, for many of the reasons you noted, kindledRadial. I think too often GMs get into a habit of viewing combat as a "kill or be killed" thing, "to the death," and that leads to a trap wherein one might accidentally start equating "having a fight to the death" with "a challenge." Really, that isn't the challenge, even if it is the source of difficulty and risk. The challenge should always be something with greater stakes to the player than merely "will my character survive?"

                            I know, life-or-death is one of the greatest true risks out there, but because this is a game, we often trivialize it. It also means, with stakes at the "do or die" level, that the PCs must always win. If they don't, they die, and that means that the game is over, if only for that character. You can have victory become a more meaningfully uncertain quality of a conflict if there is something at stake that, even if lost, does not stop the PCs from being able to continue participating in the game's ongoing events.

                            So honestly, while I voice this advice for GMs whose parties are hard to challenge in combat (often due to one combat monster overshadowing the other PCs, making it either "challenge him" or "kill everybody else"), it's advice I think is good in general.

                            If you really need the "fight is challenging just because the stats are that grueling" thing...save it for when you've got a chance to do one-on-ones or other things that will allow you to separate the PCs into groups that fight at more even levels, and pit them against challenges for them. It will likely be rare, so you can do it this way without always splitting up the party every time conflict breaks out.

                                            I would hesitate to make withering damage have "real" consequences, in and of itself. It's meant to be the back-and-forth of attacks that don't "do" anything, but make whoever just hit harder come off as more "in control" of the fight. It's a very cinematic thing. Swiping something really should be a Gambit (which deals with decisive attacks), and IS a good way to have non-lethal decisive attack actions.

                                            The advice about non-kill objectives is more meta than the mechanics, honestly. It's about using the mechanics as tools rather than about adapting them to represent something new. Which...probably isn't clear, but what I'm trying to get at is that you don't need to refluff withering attacks for this. Use the movement rules, the choice of targets of attacks, and the chance to take non-attack actions. The bandit raiders move away from the heroes and towards the "stuff," and spend actions taking stuff and fleeing rather than trying to fight...if the PCs let them (or, rather, fail to stop them). The assassins choose to target the squishy mortal targets, and are trying to avoid the tanky dangerous Exalted bodyguards. That kind of thing.

                                                Well I think both interpretations are valid, depending on how much narrative weight you give the loot in this scene. If the heroes are very much dedicated to defending it as their main objective, then I think tying it to their initiative (perhaps with each one guarding a different wagon) makes sense and is fun. If it's a secondary objective such that they "lose" whether they are crashed or the loot is swiped, maybe roll the loot itself into combat with its own depletable initiative pool. Then allow players to transfer initiative into it as a misc action, for a fun tug of war mechanic.

                                                Anyway, setting the vagaries of this particular example aside, my point is that withering damage can model many many things whereas decisive damage always models physical damage. You can also do these secondary objectives as gambits (effectively trading initiative from combat for a non-combat advantage) but that means you still need to be fighting the very tough PC exalts in order to gain the points to out fox them, which as I have said is a very very tall order for anything currently statted in this system. If you want those secondary objectives to be more challenging than standard combat, then I recommend using a system that allows enemies to succeed without getting into opposed rolls with the Solar exalts.

                                                    One thing you can do to challenge powerful PCs without trying to beat them down with ever-larger hammers: force them to make choices about where they are. When the enemies queue up in a conga-line to get their asses beat it's easy to feel like the players are invincible. And you are right that issuing ST-fiat without a PC having the opportunity to contest a loss is likely to lead to resentment. But if you instead present the players with the sadistic choice of "Your enemies are taking the following actions in multiple locations; where do you want to stop them?" they can't really complain about the ST not fighting fair. Instead of the challenge being "How strong is your sword arm?" it becomes "How many fires can you put out at once?"

                                                    This is the kind of evolution that a Solars game ought to naturally take. When the stakes are small and local, it's easy for the PCs to overwhelm any opposition through personal force of arms. But as they become invested in larger concerns, the scope ought to grow as well. The simple fact that the PCs cannot be everywhere at once is something to consider. If they can win any single battle, great - but what about when there are three battles to fight at the same time? Now the problem is not whether they win, but where.
