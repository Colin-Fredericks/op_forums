        Abyssals draw obvious inspiration from vampires, from their gothic imagery that's at odds with the swords-and-sandals feel of the rest of the setting, to their state of walking the boundary between life and death, to the fact that they can (or at least could) drink blood to replenish their strength.

        None of this is predicated on their link with Solars, though, as Solars have nothing in common with vampires. I'd argue their vampiric themes are the only thing that clearly distinguishes them from Solars in editions past.

        That, and I'll take death over Oblivion as their thematic basis any day. Death is the intrinsic part of the human experience, which both grounds the Abyssals and lets them draw from the wealth of inspirations, stories and folklore across all times, places and cultures. Oblivion, on the other hand, is a very particular, narrow and artificial view of death that may have worked for Wraith (which I've never played, so I don't know), but doesn't really work with the cosmology and themes of Exalted.

                                                        Originally posted by Morangias View Post
                                                        Abyssals' connection to Solars was always indispensable to my understanding of them. It gives them a place in the setting's overarching narrative that no generic chosen of death could fill - the Lawgivers of old, come back from the dead to take revenge on the world that betrayed and murdered them. Abyssals as something that just crawled out of the Mouth of the Void would never have this much gravitas.
                                                        Well okay then. Give the people what they want, I'm apparently more of a hedge case than I thought.

                                                        Originally posted by Morangias
                                                        Abyssals draw obvious inspiration from vampires, from their gothic imagery that's at odds with the swords-and-sandals feel of the rest of the setting, to their state of walking the boundary between life and death, to the fact that they can (or at least could) drink blood to replenish their strength. None of this is predicated on their link with Solars, though, as Solars have nothing in common with vampires. I'd argue their vampiric themes are the only thing that clearly distinguishes them from Solars in editions past.
                                                        If I were to boil the themes of this down, I'd say the core difference here is adding a theme of consumption to a superman archetype. In many stories, especially the pulp fiction that heavily influences Exalted, Vampires are supermen equivalent to any other big bad in the setting, with the only drawback being that they have to kill or at least harm the living to survive. If Solars are pulp hero supermen archetypes, then would you take a deal to get those same powers knowing that you'd have to keep killing people to maintain them? What do you care about enough to make that worth it?

                                                        It's a great foil and theme for Abyssals to have and maintain, but it does come with some challenges. For one thing, the player can just as easily choose to play a normal superman without this serious handicap. Why would you choose to play the inferior product, many would ask (I am infamous for loading up on flaws and disadvantages, so this is not a question that troubles me). For another, the core theme of the Solar charmset is "excellence" so taking that power also means copy-pasting a lot of those charms over in a frankly quite boring way.

                                                        Originally posted by Morangias
                                                        That, and I'll take death over Oblivion as their thematic basis any day. Death is the intrinsic part of the human experience, which both grounds the Abyssals and lets them draw from the wealth of inspirations, stories and folklore across all times, places and cultures. Oblivion, on the other hand, is a very particular, narrow and artificial view of death that may have worked for Wraith (which I've never played, so I don't know), but doesn't really work with the cosmology and themes of Exalted.
                                                        Death and the Underworld are pretty indispensable to Deathknights, but Oblivion is also important. As has been pointed out, reincarnation via Lethe can be interpreted several ways depending on philosophy. The degree to which you will see Lethe and Oblivion as indistinguishable comes down to whether or not you think a person is their memories. If Lethe canonically becomes the complete destruction of ego and perspective, then Oblivion becomes unnecessary. But what is important is that there be some canonical fashion in which things in Exalted cease to be and never come back from, to maintain the possibility of true loss. Because loss is the cornerstone of tragedy, and Abyssals are tragic antiheroes. Not "tragic" in the sense of "cannot accomplish anything and ruins everything they touch" as they were in Ex2, but tragic in the sense that they know they can't save everyone, and that what's been lost can't always be returned.

                                                        Personally, I think a division of death and Oblivion proper is good for the setting. You have "death is a doorway" in Lethe, where the memories of this life are erased but there is something essentially you left over that gets another lease on life, and then there is "true death" which is the objectively terrifying thing where everything you ever were ceases to be. Oblivion works great as a backdrop to a tragic hero of death story, but not to the cheeseball heroic antics of the Solar exalted. Being able to pick and choose your themes is essential to a setting this big.

                                                        Put another way, in some stories you want the little boy to stay dead, but in others you want them to make a star wars force ghost cameo where he smiles before disappearing into a beam of light once the bad guy is beaten up.

                                                            Certainly, if you assume that a soul cleansed of it's memories by Lethe is effectively a blank slate, interchangeable with any other soul, it doesn't look significantly different from oblivion. And given that the society we live in places such a high value on intellectuality and reasoned, logical thought, it's an easy asssumption to fall into.

                                                            I think if you want to avoid that, it's worth not just looking at real world beliefs about reincarnation and past lives, but also looking at them as a story element: why we find the idea fascinating, and continue to tell stories about it. And from the point of view of our stories, it's very clear that events in one life are expected to have some effect on the events of subsequent lives, or else we wouldn't bother making the connection and saying that the one person is a reincarnation of the other.

                                                            In many stories, this connection takes the form of karmic law, where say, a king who waged needless war which brought suffering to his people might be in his next life a peasant in a war-torn country, so that the experiences balance out. This kind of system of cosmic justice really doesn't fit the tone of Exalted, but it this scenario it's pretty clear that the peasant doesn't remember being a king, and probably doesn't have the same personality the king did, his soul still retains information about his choices and experiences in his previous lifetimes.

                                                            Lethe in Exalted is inspired by the river of the same name in Greek philosophy, and it exists to explain why, if people reincarnate over and over again, don't they remembember anything about their past lives? But we don't always remember events of our current lives, yet our present knowledge of pshychology suggest that those events still can have a great deal of impact on how we think and act, especially formative experiences we have as children that are often no longer conciously available to us. Part of how Tibetan monks find the reincarnation of the Dalai Lama is by making very young children select objects from among a group and one who chooses the objects that belonged to previous Dalai Lamas is considered a possible candidate. The children aren't expected to remember anything about their previous lives, but it is still assumed that they would be drawn to the familiar objects rather than the unfamiliar ones.

                                                            We also have stories about people who do remember past lives at least partially, and also people who believe that using specific practices or techniques they can gain access to information about their own or other's past lives. Previous editions of Exalted have included a past life merit, which allows Celestial Exalts to remember events from their previous from their previous incarnations, but is also allowed to Dragonblooded and mortals at low ratings to represent retaining memories of their Hun soul, so perhaps Lethe isn't always perfect either.

                                                            And while little has been written about exactly how the process of reincarnation works in Exalted (probably because this is one of those things where giving STs leeway to do what is right for their games is important) it is clear that Gods can interfere with the process, such as Taru-Han with her collection of souls, and Ahlat, who can make tassels for his cloak from the souls of brave warriors. I don't think it's ever indicated whether politcally powerful gods could somehow influence which soul gets put into which infant, but there are certainly stories one could tell using that idea.

                                                            TL,DR: If you don't want reincarnation to seem pointless, don't assume that it removes all meaningfully distinguishing traits of souls.
