            The problem with Oblivion is that it tries to reshift the focus of the entire game. It sends the message that the battles over the Realm and the like don't really matter compared to this inevitable world-destroying force at the root of the world that must be confronted. Making the Deathlords and Abyssals into its enemies rather than its soldiers helps them a bit, but doesn't address the underlying problem.

            I expect that 3e will remove Oblivion (at least as it was before) entirely. The problem with it wasn't just that it made Abyssals too dark and evil; the problem was that it shifted their focus away from the mortal affairs of Creation and tried to pull everything else along with it.

            Originally posted by Murcushio View Post
            I'm not trying to be a jerk here, but it sounds like your friends didn't actually love Abyssals. They loved the idea of Abyssals they had in their heads that was utterly unsupported by the actual Abyssals the setting gave you.

            There's nothing wrong with that per se, but... why bash your head against that wall?
            Well, if we zoom out and look at the bigger picture... the problem is that most people didn't love Abyssals. It had few fans. And the fans it did have often caused the problem I mentioned above with Oblivion. Abyssals actually had all the problems 2e Infernals did regarding disrupting the tone and focus of the game.

            I've mentioned this with regards to Infernals, but it affected Abyssals, too... I think that if you look at, say, your typical Final Fantasy game, there's a moment when the game is done introducing you to the world and says "all right, now you're going to fight a superdemon." And from then on the story is all about killing a monster clown or an evil tree or a Sephiroth or whatever, and the other stuff stops mattering so much. And I think that because that kind of storytelling has become so widespread in videogames, people expect it out of Exalted. But I also think it's really really bad for a tabletop game, since it means you're discarding most of what matters to the main game.

            So this whole "Oblivion as the cosmic enemy of the entire universe" or "The Ebon Dragon as Big Bad of the entire setting" is a bad thing, not just because it takes away from the rest of the game, but because it's so much the default in many fantasy stories nowadays that players (and, yes, writers) will gravitate to it if you're not careful. And when you start having it seeping into canon, it's also a bit like... that problem you run into where you make an NPC more interesting than the PCs, sort of? You're taking one specific story and letting it take over the setting, which makes the whole setting more one-dimensional and harder to use to tell anything else.

            One of the biggest complaints people had about Abyssals was that they were really only good for telling two stories. (And I'd take that a step further -- they were really only good for telling one story, you just got to choose which side you were on in it.) I think it's reasonable for players to look in askance at that.

                            There are some themes that are appropriate to Abyssal stories that arent appropriate to any other splat. This is also true for Lunars, Sidereals, Dragon Bloods and Infernals. That is thematic distinction at work, and it's a good thing! It means those splats feel unique, and it means you can approach the setting from angles you could not before. And I think part and parcel of the Abyssal experience is death and loss. Not pointless futility necessarily, which was what we were forced to swallow in 2e, but a profound sense that life is finite, that the here and now matters, and that you can lose everything if you make the wrong choices. Another theme of great importance is that life is consumption. When one man lives another dies for him, we all live on borrowed time and we take big loans out on each others' credit.

                            For the Abyssals and their stories to feel unique and to offer this narrative (which is the main reason I find them interesting) I bellieve that Oblivion is a necessary setting element. Because without Oblivion, nothing in Exalted actually ends. It just gets recycled, or repurposed. And that narrative works great for Lunars and Solars, but personally I prefer a story where death really matters and overshadows the players' actions. When I want to tell a story with those themes I write an Abyssal story, because that splat gives me those options.

                            That said, Oblivion might be changing in 3e for the better. There was no mention of the Maw in the backer pdf, and it seems that the labrynth may be getting a metaphysical rewrite as well. Oblivion might not be seen by anyone, but the Deathlords know its there, or fear it enough to take these sorts of precautions. Maybe it's only accessible by the labrynth, and the mad ghosts that return upon seeing it give conflicting reports on its size and threat. The Abyssals would by no means be the first to fight this unknown force, but they are the greatest champions on the field at present.

When a person dies, their soul may linger for a while, even a long while, but will pretty much inevitably pass on barring becoming a nephwrack. Once you go through Lethe, that person-who-was is irrevocably gone. They no longer exist. The soul is fundamentally destroyed for all purposes that really matter, like identity and experience.                                     Death is an ending even without a big malicious hole at the bottom dating to prehistory that is "real death" versus some notion that Lethe, aka the in-setting permanent cessation of a life-experience, is somehow "fake death," or even that people who die and linger as ghosts aren't lessened irrevocably in the process. Death matters for all Exalted. When they kill someone, they can't just take it back, ever. An Exalt that dies loses their Exalted status and is in no way guaranteed to carry on - the vast majority don't. Not near everyone lingers as a ghost, and it's not an experience anyone should really rationally desire - wanting to do so should be inherently irrational and tied in some way to the fear of non-existence that, as far as we know IRL, is death, and what in-setting would be Lethe, not that many people in-setting really know the details of how that works either. Death is still scary when you can linger on in the Underworld - you're you in some ways but narrower in others, and I think every ghost knows that they won't persist forever, and then what?

                                                            Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                                                            I see good prospects in the corebook that oblivion will now be what happens to people who get lost in the rivers, wells and encircling sea of the Underworld.
                                                            Yeah. Oblivion as "sometimes people don't reincarnate" is fine.

                                                            Oblivion as an embodied cosmological enemy of the entire universe that will inevitably consume all things, though, was never really a good fit for Exalted. It's hard to tell a story about human-scale heroes when your enemy is the cosmological concept of Entropy across the entire universe. The inevitability of death is better used as an abstract than as an evil, sentient force.

                                                            I think that that's part of what I hated about it, too. Like -- it can be cool to look at Lookshy and philosophize about the inevitability of its fall, or to look at ruins and consider how everything eventually comes to ruin, etc. But when you embody entropy as this sentient evil force, you can't do that anymore, because now the cycle of empires falling into ruin is all the handiwork of a single cosmic enemy whose ultimate victory is assured. It actually makes death itself less interesting by making it axiomatically impossible for anyone to ever come to terms with it in a narratively interesting fashion.

                                                            Like, in a setting with Oblivion, the end of the Epic of Gilgamesh becomes basically "and then Gilgamesh came to terms with his death, and somewhere deep in the underworld, Oblivion cackled evilly at another inevitable victory, having crushed Gilgamesh's heroic spirit at last by getting him to realize that Oblivion would always triumph." That's not what that ending is supposed to mean! And it's a problem that hits Abyssals the hardest, because they're the ones who are supposed to be thinking the most about death, and making Oblivion this sinister semi-sentient cosmic force stripped them of the ability to do that in a classically-heroic manner.
