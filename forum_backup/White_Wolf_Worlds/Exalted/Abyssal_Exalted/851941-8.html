                    Oooooooooblivion! Let's walk through just what kind of antagonist it really is.

                    Is it a personal antagonist like Sylar, who can shoot your dog and kidnap your wife and have a gritty yet tragic past that the writer seems to think excuses anything? Fuck no, it's a black hole, it doesn't care about you or anything you do.

                    Okay! Is it a horror antagonist like the Great Old Ones or the Reapers (before Harbinger became an xbox live kid), where the point is you are so insignificant in the face of it that it doesn't even notice you or identify you as any less than an ant and you just sort of have to deal with it? Well yes, except that you aren't insignificant by virtue of being one of the Exalted and Exalted doesn't do horror, it's sort of antiethical to the whole thesis. I think there's even a writer quote lying around somewhere to the effect of, "hey writer, how inevitable is Oblivion, really?" "it's not. At all. Nephwracks are fuckin' bonkers."

                    Well okay then! How about, is it an elemental force of nature that you must struggle against or survive against or mitigate, like outrunning global warming in the Day After Tomorrow? Nooot really. It drives ghosts crazy sometimes, but other than that your chances of getting fucked over by it are basically nil. Try not to jump into the giant fucking black hole in the middle of the Labyrinth, a place you really shouldn't be going anyway.

                    Okay! Is it an elemental antagonist like Lavos, who you have to fight and overcome and who drives the plot by the reactions of other people to it? That sounds the most accurate, except you can't actually 'fight' Oooooblivion, or really even affect it because again, it's a black hole. Also, while the plot of Chrono Trigger is kicked off when Lavos destroys the world, Oblivion hasn't done anything yet, it's just sort of abstractly scary at some point in the distant future or when the deathknights fulfill a grand murder-plan that probably won't even work.

                    The fundamental problem with Oblivion is that it manages to peg an entire section of playable characters as generally awful without some serious rewriting (because what kind of dumbass would tirelessly work to bring about an omnicide that includes him?) while at the same time it's neither fish, nor fowl, nor foal, nor the heat death of the universe, winding up as just about the most ineffectual faceless elemental villain that's ever been. It's just sort of... there. Vampire Nic Cage is scarier than Oblivion.

                    And, I mean, jegus, if you for some reason needed omnicide as an endgame, at least let the stupid bastards figure out a way to make a new world in their own image. You'd have lots of takers for ultramurder if that was the carrot! If you had a horse with a broken leg and it was the only horse left in your corner of the world you bet your fucking ass you'd try to save it, fragile or no.

                    If you need to keep the mass murder, let the Abyssals be the "good guys" in their own heads that way. They're going to make a paradise or at least something better, just as soon as they murder everyone. It'd still be wrong, but it would at least be the right sort of wrong. (It'd be a cliche, yes, but are you going to tell me "villain who kills everyone and wants to end it all" or "villain who seemed like a villain but was really keeping back a greater evil" is sparkling fresh?)

                                        I think one of the advantages of Exalted is that although it doesn't handwave sociopolitics, it's also set at a point in technology and population where dealing with nations isn't an unwieldy mess. If you've got a problem with a neighboring country, it's entirely possible that what you need to do is set out and have a talk with its king, or some of his companions. Outside of the Realm you can generally get enough individuals and goals to fill a cast list without feeling bare-bones. Also why Suikoden has the advantage of being about wars in a time of kings, and the plot would tend to involve some sort of personal betrayal regarding said war.

                                        Edit: The animism helps a lot too! To end a drought you might have to perform a convoluted series of favor for an important god, but there's someone you can talk to about the drought. Don't underestimate that.

                                        (This is also why media that delves into political intrigue loves medieval or renaissance eras - the politics are personal enough to be relatable. Also why House of Cards enormously misrepresents the American political process - because watching Kevin Spacey sit in a cubicle phoning people for donations for six hours, or spend 75% of his time at fund-raising events, or sit on a completely meaningless series of committee hearings for a week because one of his allies wanted to make a point... none of that would be compelling.)

                                        The Final Fantasy adage of "every problem in the world can be solved by finding the right long-haired prettyboy and beating him up" is... well it's not a great technique but it's an effective one for making things memorable at least, to concentrate your antagonism into something you can grasp and beat the crap out of. FFXIII attempted a lot of what you're describing (but, you know, with Square-Enix at the helm) and... what happened in that game again?

                                                These are all loyalist character concepts I would have loved to play in Ex2 but couldn't because of the Neverborn. None of these ideas rack up the kind of world-destablizing body counts that the dead titans are interested in. If I had played one of these characters, then eventually my Deathlord patron would have told me to stop playing around and go start a plague or war somewhere, because no one is interested in half a dozen deaths when the end game is world-destruction. And it's impossible for me to empathize with a character who wants that sort of pointlessly wanton destruction, so all these characters end up boiling down to a Redemption arch, which I've played a billion times because that's really all I can do.

                                                Point is, more character options are usually a good thing. None of my examples are faultless hero archetypes, at all, but at least by transitioning from an objective of slowing Oblivion instead of hastening it, they become somewhat sympathetic.
