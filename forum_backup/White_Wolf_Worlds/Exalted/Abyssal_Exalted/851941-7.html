            I feel that in real life, when people invoke the "hard man making the hard decisions" argument, it's often people using it to defend indefensible things they wanted to do anyway. I compared Kejak to Muammar Gaddafi above; I wasn't entirely joking. Gaddafi is what I picture most "hard men making hard decisions" as being when you cut through their self-justifications. I am extremely skeptical about anyone who claims that they needed to seize power for the greater good; that is to say, absent evidence to the contrary, I would usually automatically assume that anyone who claims that is actually seizing power because they like to have power. And I'm particularly bothered by the default defense the Sidereals books seem to employ for this ("are you kidding? Ruling the world is hard and painful and brings them no pleasure, but they do it for the greater good"), because, again, that's exactly the line real-world people like Gaddafi trot out. I'm sure Sidereals have a lot of work to do, but they're doing it from gold-lined palaces in literal Heaven while they decide the fate of the entire world with their decisions. If they think they have things rough, hey, they could swap places with the Lunars any time they want! Nothing stops a Sidereal from walking out the door, giving up their temporal power and prestige and so on.

            Some of what they do is literally necessary, but a lot of it, like everything to do with guiding and supporting the Realm, say, is not. Except without the DB / Sidereal hegemony the world would be in chaos and anarchy and unlead and etc etc etc because of course having the firm hand of the Exalted guiding the world is necessary... ugh. Some of the common viewpoints on Exalted have issues when you look at them too closely, and not just regarding the Sidereals.

            Now, I'm only half-serious. Sidereals are playable and the Bronze Faction is meant to be at least somewhat sympathetic, so having Kejak be, canonically, Literally Gaddafi is not good for the game. But "they wanted to seize power for the greater good" and "they wanted to seize power because they wanted power" are not incompatible. In fact, the second follows inevitably from the first. I would imagine that long before the Usurpation, there was a faction of Sidereals that felt that they could use their understanding of Fate to guide the world more directly to better ends if they were the only ones in charge... and I would assume that this faction served as the core of what would eventually become the Bronze Faction.

            (If you assume there was a faction of Sidereals that had "things would be better if we, who understand Fate the best, were the ones who were ultimately in charge" even before the Great Prophecy, and that this formed the core of the Bronze Faction, this also explains why the Sidereals have failed to make any sort of peace with the Lunars over the past 1500 years. I would assume that not all Sidereals, even in the Bronze Faction, are part of this Sidereal Supremacy faction, but the history of Creation makes a lot more sense if you posit that it exists. This doesn't mean that they don't want peace, but if you assume that there's this hard core of the Bronze Faction whose ideology states that Sidereals should be in charge and that Creation should be run according to their understanding of fate, then they are always going to be structurally incapable of making any sort of lasting peace with other Celestial Exalted.)

            It's not so much how individual Solars behave so much as setting creep about what it means to be a Solar, the Unconquered Sun’s semi-regular characterisation as a paranoid dictator and by extension a sense that history is just going to repeat itself.

            I can see how someone working from 2e especially can enjoy their Solars, their friends Solars but remain sympathetic to the argument that the Solars as a whole represent a danger to Creation.
            Oh, Solars definitely pose a danger to Creation. I'm just bothered by the basic shape of the story. I don't think there's any way to avoid the problem or to fix it; you can't present Kejak Chejop as Literally Gaddafi. If you poke at the heroic narrative too closely from a more cynical modern viewpoint, it all falls apart. That's obviously not limited to Sidereals.

            But... I'm still bothered by how the Bronze Faction is portrayed and by the fact that accepting the story requires, on some level, that we accept their statement that they were at least motivated by the greater good. This is necessary for Exalted to tell the kind of story that it wants to tell, but it also has some nasty implications if you look at it too closely, because if we were discussing anything in the real world remotely analogous to that story, I would be extremely skeptical, not just about whether they made the right decision but about whether their motivations were really what they say they were.

                        Bullshit. Ugly, ugly bullshit.
                        I haven't read this particular fic, but I think the point would be that the benefits of the atrocity are debatable, not absolute. Moral choices are only interesting, from a writing perspective, if you can conceivably imagine a situation where both options are the right one. It sounds like this story could have done a better job playing devil's advocate, but the idea that Applejack is a willing participant at least adds one element that might make readers stop and consider the situation before decrying the Alicorns and their pony-sacrifice.

                        I was actually just talking about another story, Dragon Age Inquisition, that does a pretty good job posing these moral quandaries:

                        Iron Bull's Decision: Iron Bull is a loyal follower of the Qun, a religious/social order that has dictated his every major life decision since birth. Operating as one of their spies, he begins leading a small mercenary band called "the Chargers" which the player gets to meet and develop attachments towards. Eventually though the Qun come calling. They need Iron Bull's Chargers to serve as a distraction for one of their warships, which has over one hundred soldiers on board. You the player then have to decide whether to (A) save these six mercenaries that you know and love, or (B) save one hundred faceless soldiers that might potentially help you save the world.


                        What is most interesting about this example is that most people I talk to think that option "A" is the "right" choice, and would have very nasty things to say about those who chose option "B." But a person motivated by altruism might reasonably pick either choice, and we wouldn't conclusively be able to say they were right or wrong in doing so. That to me is the benchmark for a well-written moral quandary, a benchmark Ex2 Abyssals fail miserably because at no point do any of the books even attempt to make a compelling case that Oblivion is preferable to life.

                        The Usurpation fairs slightly better in that regard: at least a few of the Sidereals might reasonably be assumed to be operating on altruism and a sincere belief that the Solars were nutso-crazy-banana-town (because many were), though with what we know out of character about their Great Curse and the functional limits of Fate it seems very unlikely that this was true for all of them. And ultimately, it is very hard to empathize with the victor of a bloody military coup, no matter what their intentions were.

                        But good intentions that don't produce the desired result are a cornerstone of Exalted storytelling. All I'm hoping to inject into the Abyssal splat here is the same moral latitude the other Exalted have to be good or evil depending on player choice, instead of being pigeon-holed into the bad guy role because their exaltations are inescapably tied to a mission statement no good or sane person would support. You are of course well within your rights to say that no goal is worth torturing and killing millions of people over, but I am within my rights also to say that "one is less than many, and many is less than all," which I think is pretty solid ground to stand on when writing gothic horror anti-heroes.

                        EDIT: Another great story where the bad guys are pretty damn bad but not entirely irredeemable: Cabin in the Woods. In fact, that entire movie could serve as source material for a loyalist Abyssal game in my proposed setting.

                                    It occurs to me that I've not even shared some of the other reasons a Deathknight would be this kind of a loyalist, besides the "ends justify the means" argument. Here are a few:

                                    "The Underworld is our home, and Creation stands against us." Many Abyssals had close ties to the Underworld before their exaltation, and care deeply for the people there. Creation might have been your home at one point, but now its people are alien and hostile to you. The ancestor cults that keep your loved ones alive are persecuted by the living, who would like nothing better than for you and your ilk to disappear forever. If a few or many of these enemies have to die painful deaths so that your friends and family can enjoy their peaceful lives for many, many years more, then so be it.

                                    "We can have nice things." Everything in the Underworld is the phantom image of something in Creation that got there by dying. Even the landscapes are only memories made real by the ghosts who come here. So if you want something, anything really, and it doesn't already exist in the Underworld, then that is categorically going to require somebody to die. Sure, you could all "get by" without killing Bob the farmer, but how happy is everyone going to be when his apple orchards start filling the local markets with fresh produce? The living kill people for stuff all the time, but when we kill someone we make sure everyone benefits for centuries.

                                    "                                    They'll be happier as ghosts." Not existing is a terrible choice. But between rotting, painful flesh sacks and untiring, un-aging ectoplasm, many would choose the latter. Being a ghost is not without downsides, of course, but you'll never have to worry about a whole host of things you did in life, your friends and family are always nearby, you'll finally have time to catch up on your reading, and if your Abyssal patron is kind you can expect to be well taken care of after only a brief period of intense discomfort. It's more of an early retirement plan, really.

                                    "Some people deserve it." No one ever said you had to kill indiscriminately to meet those ghost quotas. There are plenty of objectively awful, evil people alive in Creation right now and you can easily keep Oblivion at bay without ever shedding innocent blood. Whether you have a personal vendetta against rapist, sadists, child abusers, career criminals or politcal tyrants that mortal laws can't touch, your Second Breath has given you all the power you need to bring these scumbags to justice and protect innocent un-lives doing it. And once they're in ghost-prison you can lock them up and throw away the key for eternity, because undead justice never takes a day off.

                                    "It's always a party in Halloween Town." Making a ghost requires a special mix of negative emotions, strong sentiments and unfinished business that is impossible to mass-produce. Every ghost is made-to-order and you're an expert when it comes to brewing that miserable ectoplasmic cocktail, but you haven't killed a single person since your Second Breath and you don't plan to. You're too busy arranging midnight hauntings, terrible nightmares and tailored psychological traumas to get your own hands dirty, and the dangers of the Second Age pretty much guarantee that the last step in ghost production always gets done with or without your help. Not all your clients appreciate the work you put in, but there's nothing half as satisfying as a blood-curdling scream that you made yourself!

                                    "It won't always be like this." The Deathlords know that the current system is cruel, and they wish it didn't have to be. Oblivion is by its very nature hazardous to study, and is barely understood even by its most devoted experts. You are continuously researching better, less destructive weapons in the fight against Oblivion, and one day soon your efforts will bear fruit and the Underworld and Creation will be able to live in harmony as they always should. But no one is going to live to see that better tomorrow unless you knuckle down and bring us a bag of dead kittens, so get to work, Deathknight.

                                    ...to name a few.

                                            To be honest, if the Usurpation "hard choice" really felt like a big issue, I would be more tempted to make Sidereals more like Hari Seldon and the Foundation that was part of their inspiration.
                                            Seldon's psychohistory allowed him to predict the fall of the Galactic Empire, but he wasn't making any decision other than to set up his Foundations.

                                            The Fall of the First Age could go the same way - the Sidereals did not cause the Usurpation, but foresaw it, and planned for it, out of both altruism and more self interested motives, as an opportunity to gain the reins of history, and chose not to intervene to spread awareness, viewing the Solar regime as ultimately unworthy of that chance, an act for which the Lunars still blame them.

                                            Parenthetically, you really need to avoid the Sidereals and Dragonblooded becoming just part of a Solars' "revenge movie" plot, where it's simply the case that "We were robbed of our rightful place, by the usurpers who had no justification than greed and we are back to claim it". And then from there just playing out like any tired revenge flick like The Revenant, anything like that. My view of the core Solar story is that, for most games, it's meant to be about it slowly dawning on the reborn Solars, as they find their way in the world that, "Shit. We really screwed up last time. How are we not going to make the same mistakes again? Is this our fate? Can we really be trusted with any power? Should any man be? Do I really have the ultimate right to make these decisions, that I feel I must make?". A story with strong elements of the humbling and the self doubting, trying to wield power justly while doubting to a degree whether you should wield it at all, not a total story of revenge and self belief. (Although of course there are many, many Solars who don't quite fit that template.). So you can't take away responsibility for the Usurpation and you can't make it a wholly unjust wrong against them, or you step all over that.

                                                            I totally did think that, no joking. I mean, I didn't think I'd control it or make those choices, but I thought the plot would be primarily about fighting Shinra. I went into the game without being spoiled on it at all, and my reaction for most of the second half of the first disk was "when is this sideplot about chasing around a black-cloaked man going to end so we can get back to fighting Shinra?"

                                                            I have no idea why people think Sephiroth is an interesting character. Hell, I have no idea why people think he is a character, he has like three lines. Does he even say anything in the last part of the game when you fight him? I don't think he does. I'm not sure he's still even sentient at that point. Honestly I'm not sure he was sentient at any point after being killed and falling into the lifestream, he was just sort of an... echo-thing? It wasn't very clear. My reading is that he didn't actually manage to resurrect completely, it's just that some sort of echos of him possessed people. But it felt like an entirely valid interpretation that at no point during actual gameplay was there an actual, awake, thinking Sephiroth; he died years ago and every time you saw him, it was just mindless echos of him that occasionally took over people who shared his genes or whatever the hell it was. As I recall, even after he theoretically reassembles himself, he doesn't have a single line in the "real world" as opposed to in people's heads.

                                                            I could be forgetting something? But I'm pretty sure he never does or says anything that would imply that he's sentient in the modern day, or in any way cognizant of what's happening outside of a few reflexive instincts to attack certain people and grab / use certain things. Like, seriously, Exdeath was a less wooden character, and he was literally a tree.

                                                            But more generally, I felt that Midgar was the only remotely interesting or fleshed-out location in the game. None of the scenes set outside of Midgar were remotely as effective to me as the ones inside it because of that; the rest of the world was pretty much just generic Final Fantasy places.
                                                            I think the reason the game isn't about that is it's too real. Basically, you're playing a hyper-violent version of Occupy Wall Street+Greenpeace. You're fighting corporate greed crushing the majority of people, sometimes literally, for its own good. They own the police. They own any politicians. And... a lot of people want to, at least once in a while, play games to get away from the awfulness of real life and relax in a story about playing people who CAN win. The heroes can't beat Shinra. Everything they're doing? Never really scratched the company. Sephiroth? He's a more possible enemy to defeat compared to the multi-headed monster that is a corporation grinding up the people to feed its gluttonous hunger for more money and power.

                                                            I'm all about fighting those structures. But I'd rather focus those efforts in the real world, and save my game time for something more relaxing.

                                                            tldr; You're playing a progressive Social Justice Warrior at the start of Final Fantasy VII. You're not when you're fighting Sephiroth. Or not as much.
