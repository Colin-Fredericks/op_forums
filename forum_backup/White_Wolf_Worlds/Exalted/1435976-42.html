        I have no doubt they'd get something out of it. How much is hard to say, this stuff isn't very detailed. I suspect there's a big difference between "enough for them to get something out of it" and "enough for them to particularly notice your prayer/sacrifice among the others they receive."

        In 1e/2e, prayers that weren't directed to a specific god (e.g. prayers to "Heaven" or "the gods" generally) became a lesser substance called "quintessence" (as opposed to "ambrosia" from prayers to specific gods); I don't recall if the books were clear about prayers to a specific group of gods. In 3e, there's just one Heavenly currency called "ambrosia" but it comes in different grades (and unlike previous editions, Exalted can't learn to make stuff from it, which strikes me as very odd).

        The 3e Sidereals book says that the lowest grade of ambrosia is "blended from various sources;" I'm not sure if that means "composed of lots of weak prayers from many different people" or what (if so, that suggests higher grades come from smaller numbers of "stronger" prayers, but I'm not confident in any of this).

        It would make sense to me if more specific prayers were better than more general prayers (i.e. the fewer gods you're praying to, the better), but any prayer is surely better than no prayer, in the same way that one eighth-dinar is better than no silver at all (but won't, by itself, shift your Resources rating in game terms).

        Of course, if you're Exalted and you blast a 10-success prayer roll to a pantheon of half a dozen deities, they might genuinely all take note of it.

            Originally posted by MJ6373 View Post
            If, for example, you devoted a large sacrifice to a whole pantheon, or worshiped a divinity through an intercessionary, would the benefits of prayer be split among the targets, or would you have to engage in separate prayer activities directed at each individual object of worship for them to notice/get anything out of it?
            This is one of those things where I think the premise of the Cult Merit needs to be seen through the lens of how such religious activities tend to work. Historically, polytheistic societies do not have rituals that are directed at the pantheon as a whole. That tends to run counter to the point; different deities have different areas of responsibility (and different things that are sacred), so what you're asking for intercession on or giving thanks for are necessarily different and separate. What would you pray to the pantheon as a whole for?

            That said, there's also the point that the system doesn't engage with the level of granularity down to individual prayers. Your cult rating is based on your status derived from the scope of consistent worship, so there isn't really such a thing as the benefit of any given singular prayer. That one group of outcastes in What Fire Has Wrought introduces the notion that certain exceptional prayers are uniquely gratifying to gods, but not in a way with mechanical resolution, and not with any particular contribution to their Merit benefit. It's a luxury that they can pay for.

            As far as making gameplay out of it, I'm thinking that such a ritual would be largely incidental (even if the culture does have some festival or ceremony that makes a thing out of honouring the gods as a collective), and if it becomes the primary mode by which the entire society worships any god, well... I think that would be cause for dispute even without mechanical implications (although I'd say the mechanical implication is that nobody is being worshipped specifically enough for that to count towards their Cult at all).

                                            Games of Divinity established the basic terms of "gods are not supposed to make themselves generally manifest in the lives of mortals", and no books have really overturned that.

                                            At most, Third Edition's description of the activities of Yu-Shan has given gods a bit more latitude to do something other than document the workings of the mortal world... within the necessities of keeping destiny on track. A god may intervene to help set something in motion or offset interference by happenstance or contrary wills.

                                            Divine intervention as a direct response to solicitation through prayer? Quid pro quo? Extortion? Flagrantly illegal. Gods are not supposed to be making their activities conditional, or show favouritism based on who has the commitment to make the grander rituals or material means to make more satisfying sacrifices. In the proper conduct of the Celestial Order, the work of the gods is not based on their personal proclivities or how well they're being compensated, but the harmonious unfolding of a divinely ordained future. Worship would always be a thing, but existing in a space between "the rulers of Heaven and Earth arrange between themselves for proper compensation" and "people will tend to be inclined to gratitude for a job well done".

                                            In the Age of Sorrows, this is widely disregarded. The planning of destiny is mired in the personal agendas of Celestial gods. Terrestrial gods are constantly interceding in the lives of mortals, whether to order things to their own tastes (dogs of the unbroken earth are not meant to be violently resisting the extension of habitation), or exert their power in response to solicitation, or just straight up threaten mortals into worship.

                                            These things are emblematic of how it's a fallen age in which the greatest authorities are hopelessly corrupt and the result of that is the majority being open to exploitation in the absence of anybody to keep them in check. That human lives can be subject to the whims of gods is one of the key injustices that the return of the Solar Exalted is meant to redress.

                                            That doesn't mean that it's without nuances, especially in Third Edition. Gods might be self-interested but not necessarily monsters, and the centuries in which the proper order of things has not been enforced allows for a lot of relationships to have formed that are functional or mutually beneficial, never mind when the practices of dealing with gods have become deeply ingrained as part of a society's culture.

                                            That may be cause to change the law, but it doesn't mean the status quo isn't a violation of the law.

                                                    I'm not positing the gods' corrupt behavior as "normal" in a sense of righteousness or moral acceptability, but normal in the sense that abusive power structures are normal. Exalted has changed a lot across editions, and while I don't dispute your accuracy as far as 1st edition goes (I never played, so you're certainly the expert in the room), I do dispute the general methodology of assuming that things from previous editions are still in play unless something goes out of its way to explicitly contradict or overturn it. 2nd edition's more starry-eyed depictions of the First Age did flirt with the idea that there was a perfect Everyone Will Be Happy If They Know Their Place order to the cosmos, but even then the harmony displayed by the Terrestrial gods was generally framed as being due to the Solars' active work coordinating them, and beyond that I don't think 2nd or 3rd editions have really leaned into the idea that there's any more intrinsic justice in Heaven's laws than any other legal system.

                                                    This is part of the theme of the cyclical nature of power. The Divine Revolution had just cause; the gods were slaves yearning for autonomy, and humanity were small fish in a big terrifying ocean who wanted to be able to flourish. Yet they are no more immune to the corruptions of power than their predecessors. Worlds, peoples, and great magics are sacrificed to get the old boss out; in with the new, who's about the same.

                                                    The trade of perpetual thankless labor for the luxury, leisure, autonomy and worship enjoyed by the Primordials is what the gods were in the war for. The Creation-Ruling Mandate is a nice cherry atop that, a statement of The Rewards of Victory Are Justice and a way to avoid a second war between the Exalted and Heaven by giving each side half the pie, but I don't recall 2nd or 3rd editions portraying it as any more concerned with the welfare of mortals than was necessary to keep peace with the Exalted, nor that the operation of Terrestrial gods in Creation was especially more circumscribed than that of the Exalted in Heaven.

                                                    I'll return to the point that Whitewall and Great Forks are labeled as really specifically and exceptionally in violation of Heavenly law and that there are consequences each are trying to avoid on account of that, in a way you don't see with, like, Hamoji.
