    After a long pause I continue with my analysis of this book.

    The second story continues to deepen the corruption of Realm using the bandits of the first story. Those who should eliminate corruption are now persecuted and killed by the criminals who have much more support among the higher echelons.
    The part of the magistrates I liked a lot, it is brief but it transmits all the necessary information. I like how each magistrate is different and they operate in a non-rigid and structured way.
    The section of the ministries was great. I knew that Realm's politics were deadly, but I did not remember that the rise in the bureaucracy was based on murder as much as for passing exams. It is a pity that I do not detail more in what the exams consist of, but between the name they receive and the bureaucratic examinations of imperial China I think it is not difficult to be inspired. The description of the different ministries in one place is always useful, as well as the reminder of how the Empress secured the overlapping of ministries and how she created or divided ministries as needed. The bureaucracy is an aspect often overlooked but I think the book does a good job of making it interesting and useful for gaming groups. Some ministries already knew them but others are new, all are very interesting, like the sidebars, especially that of sorcerers.
    The situation of the All-Seeing Eye is transmitted quite well and reading the section I came up with some ideas of adventure. Very interesting.

    The deliberative is as I had always imagined full of betrayals and stabbings in the back. Condemned to become literal as the political situation deteriorates. The Regent has not changed much between editions, although at least now he recognizes what situation he is in and everybody hates him, but at the moment it is too useful for him to be killed.
    Finally a good explanation of how the economy is affected by the disappearance of the Empress. Giving a foothold to the Guild in the Realm is one of the biggest mistakes the dynasties have committed so far, but that will only show with time.
    The story of the next chapter is still a consequence of the first, in what I suppose will be the theme and shows once again the internal decomposition of Realm. This time with such audacious bandits who attack the legionaries and how nepotism negatively affects the legions. I like continuity and I'm curious to see how this story ends. I must say that the mini stories in rpg books I have never liked, but using them to tell a unified story has always seemed the best possible use that can be given to these stories.
    The chapter is very interesting, not only speaks of the imperial legions, also speaks of the military forces of the Great Houses, gives more details of the current state of the Tepet House (2 Solars dead, which intuited the story of Arada in the Dragon -Blooded book), and the paramilitary forces of the Kingdom and the garrisons of the Satrapas. It could be said that both sides suffered irreplaceable losses. What is important is that it was shown that Realm can be defeated in an open and clear conflict. At least in its current state, which gives encouragement to each ambitious warlord who reaches the news and things will only get more in their favor as the Realm continues to deteriorate internally.
    Like everything else in Realm, it has gotten worse without the Empress.
    The detailed treatment of the legions in more detail than ever is very interesting. A typical legion has many more Dragon-Blooded than expected, had always thought more in numbers between 12 and 20. Even if the vast majority are Essence 2, 40 dragon-blooded are extraordinarily dangerous by themselves, much more with support of the legion itself. Suddenly a Legion of Realm is much more threatening than I had ever thought.
    The martial orders are new and are a good addition. The Vermillon legion is now the best legion. The legion of silence has always pleased me and two of its merits seem perfectly applicable to PC who wish to have them.
    The Navy and the legions despise each other very much, like everyone in theRealm thanks to the Empress. There is not much detail about the fleet, the truth is that I would have liked more detail, especially types of boats used.
    Of course the whole defensive system of Realm is collapsing and most ignore the emerging anathemas, many dynasts are going to have a sudden awakening.

        After a few days of highly invested obligations, I think I'm ready to get back on this horse.

        Now, for the fiction opening the Immaculate Order’s chapter. I find it gets off to a good start with the introduction of the varied peasant origins: you get a number of names to get a sense of how the Blessed Isle might designate locations, some agency in the common purpose of far-flung people in seeking ecclesiastic aid, and the interesting practice of such petitioners not only having to provide service in kind, but due so with grace and humility as designated by the Perfected Hierarchy. Indeed, for all of the snippets setting up the relationship between the Order and the peasantry, beginning on this foot bodes well.

        I also like the modified sense of awe in the presence of a Dynastic abbot, things such as being allowed to share tea with somebody of such high station, how she speaks to them in a language of commoners, and that for all of the accommodations they remain nervous. I enjoy the way that each of the representatives are taking different attitudes towards raising their grievances, with some more blunt than others (even if trying to dance around going too far in expressing defiance with regards to the Dynasty), and find the abbot’s initial responses in slight body language to be a fun bit of characterisation; one little touch that I find nice is how the peasants refer to only the Exalted, with no qualifiers, reinforcing the point that the Terrestrials will be the only Exalted they’ve ever known.

        Finally, I find the dynamics belying the abbot Mnemon Amalu’s direct responses to the peasants to be fascinating; the teasing out of what they’re actually trying to seek from her, the manner in which she presents a somewhat fearsome presence while not being so threatening that the petitioners don’t continue to speak their minds (perhaps even enhancing their doing so), and the manner in which she breaks down their grievances, combined with local concerns that they’re not asking about, in a manner that reinforces the state ideology while simultaneously inferring accommodations, in a manner that reinforces the awe of the peasants. I love the way that she’s implying a quid pro quo (especially one that refers to the greater emphasis the Order is being given in this Edition as a secret/cultural police), but one that doesn’t come across to me as cynical or nakedly corrupt; strictly speaking, neither the thing she’s asking for nor what she’s offering in return are bad things for the downtrodden. I think that people could do a lot with that image of the Order having more flexibility in being an authority that people can appeal to, while not losing its core values or character; certainly, such an image would do a lot for maintaining and enriching the credibility of the Order as a sincerely popular social force.

        I like how the accompanying artwork is a more abstract, stylized image, consisting of sharply contrasted shapes and a pyramidal arrangement of characters. I find the design of abbot Amalu to be good in terms of being an older, more natural looking woman who radiates an aura of serenity. And I find the shading to be reminiscent of a 70s Hong Kong martial arts movie, which is quite fitting to the subject matter on several counts.

        The chapter’s introduction covers the basics (I’m fond of the juxtaposition presented between the Order serving its purpose with a combination of patient instruction and effectively beating some people up), and then we’re given an outline of the Philosophy itself. That’s not exactly something new, but I find that it develops new interest in beginning with a series of agrarian analogies for the Perfected Hierarchy; I find the imagery compelling in its own right, and it makes sense as a narrative to communicate the theology in terms resonant with the majority of the Realm’s population. Something that I like in particular is how it references duties to one’s lesser as something that takes place throughout the Hierarchy, as I imagine that there’s value in cultivating the idea among farmers that the basic practical work that they need to do for their livelihood also contributes to the spiritual elevation of lesser beings. Still, for the benefit of loftier viewpoints, I also find the description of the Perfected Hierarchy in more directly theological terms to be useful; I also think it helps to clarify a couple of minor points that might have been a bit vague before, such as the exact perspectives on the Elemental Dragons, ultimate spiritual purpose, and the status of monks.

        Likewise, I think this description of the prayer calendar may be the most direct that the Immaculate approach to worship is; I think that there have been times in the past in which the calendar and the privilege of Dragon Blooded to worship were conflated, so I like being given the direct statement that each god’s prayer day has the mortal population engage in guided worship, while the Exalted are free to pray whenever. I also like the inclusion of how the calendar mostly differs across regions to focus populations on more local gods, which can account for variations in Cult size for different gods involved with the Order. On that note, I like the sidebar describing the manner in which gods who adhere to the Philosophy express that (the direct appearances at their own ceremonies and visibly engaging with the Dragon Blooded), and both of the motives for doing so; that some find the scripture compelling enough to regard it as a fine moral guide for the world is all well and good, but I also think a reminder that some may go above and beyond for the sake of keeping their overlords’ good graces is valuable. Also nice to see Gri-Fel referenced, with the promise of more attention later; I finally managed to get ahold of my Second Edition corebook for the first time in more than a year, and among other things it reminded me of my fondness for that fellow.

        People have often complained at a sense of uniformity in the Realm’s spirituality, so I’m interested to see what is said of heresy in it being brought up this early. For a start, I like how it’s presented as not just a deviation from orthodoxy, but a kind of expression of it; that one is not necessarily malicious, but an example of how life is born in ignorance. Hence, there’s a framework for monks and other pious adherents to treat outsiders with a more considered approach, rather than defaulting to contempt. It strikes a chord with my having recently reread a passage describing contemporary attitudes to so-called early Christian heresies such as Arianism (if not quite yet far enough to have the attitude of “there are multiple valid interpretations”).

        Anyway, the major heresies concerned with illicit worship and the Anathema. For the first one, I’m liking the way that this is structured; it gets the most obvious form of people worshipping gods out of order or keeping with Immaculate mores out of the way early (although adds some nuances in the form of how satrapies present the challenge of highly ingrained religions, and that there are occasional compromises of syncretism), and then uses the matter as the basis for varieties of heterodoxy within the Philosophy itself. The Pure Way obviously stands out, I like seeing the call back to the Five Insightful Criticisms, and I’m curious about what form heretical ghost veneration could take (possibly some system in which the idea of an ascending hierarchy of souls is leveraged into the idea that those that linger have some worthwhile insight, or are demonstrating a lack of need to reincarnate). The big thing is the heretical worship of the Immaculate Dragons; I think it makes the point that orthodoxy holds that the Dragons are to be emulated rather than worshipped much more strongly here when it details several of the forms and motives that such improper worship can take, rather than merely stating the distinction. Daana’d as a patron for the non-cisgender is grand, although I’m drawn a bit more by her being regarded as a sea goddess to some, and I find it interesting to present a scenario in which even the Dragon Blooded can slip into a heterodox view of a Dragon (in addition to the idea that they’re granted the unique permission of occasionally worshipping the five). I can’t fully recall if the Heresy of the Antitheses came up before, but I’m liking its presentation here of a kind of apocalyptic cult believing in the impending return of the Immaculate Dragons as their opposites; I would think that worshipping such holy devils would entail an enthusiasm for that return out of a perception that the world is in need of such punishment, but the idea of believing in it and praying for their own exclusion does speak to an interesting kind of fatalistic outlook. I suppose you don’t need to hope for the world’s end if you think things are bad enough that it’s impending anyway, and just wish to be spared.

        I like the sidebar addressing the question of what happens when the Dragon Blooded themselves end up as objects of worship, particularly the point about how a desire to not motivate it (either from piety or fear of censure) partially informs the loftiness of the Dynastic attitude. The idea that the Scarlet Empress was an object of secret worship is nothing new, but it’s developed nicely here with some details such as the notion that it’s enabled by veneration of her being naturally strewn through the idioms of the Blessed Isle’s languages, or essentially viewing her as a bodhisattva. It’s interesting to me that the more spiritual Dynasts don’t lend credence to the idea; I suppose that confronting it would create an awkward dilemma concerning whether or not punishing people for acting in a particular manner on Immaculate dogma describing the Empress is appropriate. I can imagine that on any occasion where a monk encountered such a thing directly, they’d sooner rationalise it as just people who are especially earnest in esteeming the Scarlet Empress, rather than make a public show of associating veneration for her with repression. And it looks helpful to lay down a simple little sidebar establishing Immaculate doctrine and general public perception of the other realms of existence; the substance of which is largely familiar, but I find the turn of phrase for the Underworld to adjust my perspective of how they see ghosts from “people erroneously holding on to life” to “people who fear to engage in their next life”, and the Wyld seems to downplay the sheer scope of it.

        The last I’ll look at for the moment is the part about Anathema heresies. First off, that picture; it seems to me that this would be less of a literal scene and more depicting something in the Immaculate perspective, with how sinister that Solar looks; even the jagged look of the anima strikes me as an exaggeration in keeping with Immaculate prejudices. The armour that he’s wearing is a different look to an Artifact, and I think it has interesting design possibilities for something like that to make the magical material prominent at the outlying components. Of the hunters, I question that Earth Aspect’s choice of attire, but I like her hateful expression and large club.

        I’m rather liking this contrast between the low-level pervasiveness and relative uniformity of heretical worship of divinities with the unique and varied dangers brought with the arrival of every new Anathema; the corebook already established the definition for that term that encompasses all inimical beings, but I find something striking in the focus here on them as sources of new kinds of heterodoxy. That’s nicely complemented by the point on how each Wyld Hunt needs to be equally mutable and adaptive, a line of thinking that feels most strongly informed by their primary focus over the centuries being the Lunar Exalted. I find the emphasis on the motivating factor for Dragon Blooded being strong enough to carry them over vast distances to be decent, and particularly like this notion that part of that motive is to reinforce their egos.

        Heh, I was just earlier thinking about how Ma-Ha-Suchi will have a certain prominence for making access to the Southeast difficult, so I like him being given as an example of the kind of danger of Anathema being allowed to grow into their power. That Wyld Hunts sometimes try to take their targets alive has been in the game before, but never quite seemed to be focused on in descriptions of the activity. Given how it’s a much more prominent element of this Edition, it’s sensible to refer to the matter at this point; I find the description of how and where they’ll lock such Anathema away to be especially sinister, and I like how such prisons have more to them than just the Imperial Manse. I think such an addition to the setting should be quite useful for anybody who ever wanted backstory to include a Lunar having a Tai Lung style prison break. I do wonder about these unkillable Anathema that might be taken so; would that refer to censured beings besides the Exalted, or the occasional Celestial who is too strong for attacking Terrestrials to bring down entirely.

        It would appear to me that there have been some adjustments to Immaculate perspective of Solars and Lunars even beyond the matter of recognising them as Exalted; I had gathered that it was always going to be a subject of deeper academia, but this would seem to say that monks understanding the truth of them is a matter of course. In any case, I find the statements here to do a lot to sell its credibility to any holdouts; the perspective that Solar and Lunar power is inevitably out of keeping with the Perfected Hierarchy, a seeming viewpoint that it can come whether one acted out of order or not and is more of a pitiable thing. I think it maintains consistency while still taking easy outs such as providing the Wyld Hunt with a convincing argument of their innate Exalted-ness off the table, and I’m amused at the statement that monks let the nuances of the distinctions lie for lay Immaculates lest they find the difference too confusing.

        Aaaand to just step ahead a bit, another handy sidebar concerning the subject of the Incarnae. The idea that the Immaculates won’t worship them despite assigning them an extremely high status (which… may not necessarily be an inaccurate one; the Elemental Dragons are a bit of a black box, after all) because they don’t really provide anything back into the world… that feels to me like a point of theology that would have originated among their own intellectuals rather than from the Sidereals. I couldn’t really see a motive for the Star Chosen to exclude the Incarnae from the prayer calendar, while it would seem to not be regarded as worth steering them away from when they made their own conclusions about it. It’s probably no skin off the noses of the Incarnae in any case, and I think the situation effectively sets up the likes of the Sun being forgotten in the Realm without needing any direct suppression.

        I have approximate knowledge of many things.

                    Okay, I’ve had a number of other concerns to attend to over the last several days (and indeed, over the entire period since the book was made available), but right now my intention is to have the thing finished by the end of the week.

                    So for all that the Immaculate Texts lie at the heart of the Philosophy, and thus have been frequently referenced throughout the history of the game, I feel as though it’s often been a bit vague on what the actual substance of them is and how they convey the tenets of the Philosophy. That’s something I found to be even harder to pin down when it included a premise of Texts coming from the First Age and being continuously added, even if it was a premise I embraced. I find the rendering here to clear up a lot of that confusion, particularly with the idea that the Texts are meant to provide narratives explaining and validating every social role within the Perfected Hierarchy, and I quite like the image of these narratives being supposed statements of the Immaculate Dragons or those who witnessed them. I find these details to provide an excellent framework for imagining some passage that might be quoted by characters in play, as well as ideas for the format of a monk’s preaching; if they should see people seemingly acting in a manner unbecoming of their station, and citing scripture that repudiates their actions. I’m also liking this added dimension to them as promoting the foreign and domestic policies of the Realm (especially as it might pertain to the future governed by a more devout House such as Cathak or Mnemon).

                    Yes, I’m getting the impression from the description of how the canon is expanded that for the most part the Immaculate Texts consist of pre-existing First Age manuals, tracts and essays of high quality that are largely just… arranged in a manner that creates a coherent image and with some modifications to the context or attribution to place them in the mouths of an Immaculate Dragon. Given the range of subjects the Texts cover, I think there’s a lot of potential in the idea of widely varied translations and readings. I’m also interested in the martial arts being contextualized as a valid method for spreading the faith and proving points of interpretation, as well as this forming a basis for duels to resolve doctrinal differences between monks.

                    Now, the matter of the Immaculate view of history was a prominent part of the preview given with What Fire Has Wrought, as well as a bit of discourse before that. So the subject of how there are different narratives for the populace at large and the more thoroughly educated monks has already been talked to death; to reiterate my stance, it’s not the first thing that would come to me, but I have no strong issues, and it does close off a few possible lines of contradiction. I still find it to be helped a lot by the point that the Philosophy has been constructed to require little repression to pass for true history, and the most fraudulent elements of it are things that are kind of esoteric mysteries anyway. I also like it being introduced with the subject of the Order being a major concentrated force of historical scholarship, both for characterising the organisation and creating archives that can have useful information, but no small amount of risk.

                    Oooh, the organisation section gets off to a very nice start; the contrast between the deep investment in the Realm itself with the missionaries transmitted with minimal support beyond the administrative reach, the image of what common monks do in the Blessed Isle’s minor villages on annual rounds in combination with their more sinister cultural police elements.

                    Just from an immediate overview of the organisational structure and accompanying sidebar, I find that a few revisions have been made to the Order’s inner workings. There’s a clearer sense of how it’s distributed around the Blessed Isle, and what it does in its holdings, things that I often found a bit ambiguous before. I rather appreciate this clear hierarchy of temples to missions to Directional missions to the Palace Sublime, the direct layout of the duties and activities of each and how resources and information flows up and down the ladder. I especially like the description of how temples interact with their satellite communities, the manner in which they serve as hubs of peasant spiritual activity and can foment personal and financial relationships with the monks; plus the way that they add one last point of character to the Blessed Isle’s culture, with a form of universal (if religiously managed) basic education. I also find monasteries to be interesting in the ways that they’re distinct from what I understand of most real life forms of monasticism that both fits them within the Order’s theology (they don’t exactly have conceptions of contemplating enlightenment or divinity, or praying for humanity separate from worldly distractions, while the scriptural focus gives them a large body of work to study in isolation), and provides incentives for outsiders to seek them out rather than leave them to their seclusion. The matter of Exalted monks retreating to communities dedicated to study of the Elemental Dragons for the sake of cultivating their Essence is particularly inviting, although I also like all of the little things that might compel a monk to join such a community (especially refuge from enemies).

                    And for the last of the distinctions in practices are the itinerant monks who do double duty as providing services for communities that are beyond the reach of temples (whether according to an assigned route or following their own wishes) and being secretive trouble shooters. This is about the same as it ever was, and a nice way to both extend the Order’s presence to smaller and more isolated communities and just add the interesting character of the lone monk who must act on their own initiative and conscience rather than being a functionary of a local temple. I honestly find it a bit cute how it makes a callback to Second Edition formalizing the role of spies and assassins into a dedicated order by making the term “celestially guided” into a colloquialism or euphemism for those who are assigned those duties, at the same time as I have a preference for it being a more versatile, ad hoc practice whose command structure is more distributed. I also like how the itinerants are ideally suited to the function of census taking for the Order as a whole, adding to its sense of sinister omnipresence.

                    Yes, when I was reading about the All-Seeing Eye, it made me think about various fictional depictions of the ur-authoritarian secret police, especially in properties such as Star Trek; the kind who are supposed to know what ever citizen has for breakfast each morning. I was already into the idea that the Immaculate Order had that role more (even if the Eye does have domestic intelligence purposes), and that feeling is sharpened by both having seen the restructured Eye, which is less suited to or interested in such minutiae, and getting a look at these instruments that the Order would have for acquiring such intel. I’m also interested in the idea that the Order might not be so severe with such lapses; that they can become aware that people have fallen into unsanctioned practices, but that their first instinct might be to summon such to the temples to be reprimanded and instructed before going to anything like disappearing them. I’ll see what more the chapter has to say on the subject.

                    The Mouth of Peace appears to have received a major overhaul; I do not believe that prior Editions stated that the role was definitively for women, and there’s a much stronger inspiration drawn from the Dalai Lama (although I feel some resonance with Arha from Earthsea, albeit for almost entirely superficial reasons). That’s not just in the basic concept of their spiritual leader being a reincarnation who needs to be identified from a young age (I like how they have Sidereal prognostication to give that some precision, but still need to backpedal in the event that the selection doesn’t even Exalt), but in the manner in which the question of succession/reincarnation has become politicized. Having her be concerned with the possibility that her absence might bring about civil war and trying to find a procedural argument for directly appointing the next in line is both a nice way of incorporating the position’s traditional role of counselling against war and giving the Order its own concern looking forward into a timeline for games. Glancing around a bit, it would also appear that the positions of Paragons of the Immaculate Dragons have also been eliminated, so it seems to drop the dichotomy of the Mouth of Peace’s involvement in political affairs being a necessarily sullying sacrifice and affords her a greater spiritual significance; maybe I was just annoyed at how Second Edition really hammered it in, but I prefer this alternative introduction as more of a figurehead position, in combination with being the Philosophy’s highest representative to secular institutions of power. It would appear that the angle of being virtually a puppet of Chejop Kejak has been dropped altogether, which was honestly something that I had begun developing reservations about, and I think there’s something more compelling in this dynamic in which she had free will but little power to contest the Sidereal’s policies, while the question of succession is leaving her with one last opportunity to make a mark. I also think that it makes for a stronger and more distinct drive than there being tension between the Mouth and her former House over the administrative distribution of the Order.

                    Speaking of the absence of the Paragons (an element that I never entirely cared for), we get what appears to be the most significant alteration to and expansion of the Order in the Breaths of the Immaculate Dragons. I’m always into subjects of administrative divisions and clearly stated institutional functions, so I’m all for something like this to both really hammer in what the Order might do on a day to day and larger policy making basis, and to give some texture for its plans and actions that can be engaged with in play. I also like the idea that administration of the Order is distributed along separate axes of location and purpose, particularly for the complexities that this adds to its internal hierarchies. Indeed, something that looks very interesting to me is the sense that, with the removal of the Paragons and the increased emphasis on the Mouth of Peace as a figure of public relations, that the leadership of the Immaculate Order is a bit more decentralized; even if temples have to report upwards, there’s not a strong commanding figure at the top of the pyramid, while it seems like the people who govern and implement the Order’s social projects are more dispersed and flexible (even if they have headquarters to give some coordination). I think it gives a capacity to represent Immaculate organs that are interacted with in play more autonomy and strength, at the same time as there’s a certain expendability to them. It’s a structure that I find very interesting in making the Order feel more like a system that transcends any of its individual members; it’s a bit reminiscent to me of how the book Northern Lights held that the Catholic Church had eliminated the Papacy centuries ago, and was currently governed by a tangle of courts, colleges, and councils.

                    As for the Breaths themselves, the first couple of them are a bit reminiscent of the portfolios of the Paragons, but not in any distracting way that prevents me from appreciating what they add to the activities of the Order and the Blessed Isle as a whole. From the Breath of Sextes Jylis, I’m beginning to suspect that a few changes have been made to the process of entering the Order, and I find something interesting in the manner in which they’re presented as internally policing; I also like the idea that one of the Order’s missions is to provide environmental sustainability, since it touches on questions I’ve had concerning things like forestry and depletion in the Realm. The construction focus of the Breath of Pasiap seems the most on the nose, but I find it enriched a bit with the idea that they sometimes contract their services out, and being the ones in charge of the Order’s financial management is a particularly intriguing combination (if construction would end up as one of the largest sources of costs and income); I like that the Order has a priority in such funding of not giving away to the laity precisely how wealthy the organisation as a whole is. Having the Breath of Mela as a dedicated division for combat training makes sense with the higher emphasis being placed on monks employing martial arts for instructive purposes, as well as how some of them engage in crusade (much more than old references to the idea that engaging in military ventures was something far off and theoretical), while I also think it effectively reins some of that sentiment in with the Breath having the smallest staff owing to most monks not wanting to learn much combat technique. When I started reading the Breath of Hesiesh, I was worried that it was going to be kind of dull and non-descript, but that was relieved when the general mandate of “whatever is not covered by procedure” was supplemented on a focus on handling the weird and the esoteric, and particularly with the commensurate tendency to draw in the more directionless or unusual members of the Order; I just like the idea that the monks who turn up to handle something particularly inauspicious might be rather quirky, or that the other Breaths in crisis might be supplemented with staff they’re not entirely sure they want. And I’d find the Breath of Daana’d being focused on community development and well-being to be a good reminded of how deeply involved the Order is under any circumstances, but think it’s an especially good fit with the idea that they’re liable to be the first to see unacceptable activity and the ones most conflicted about reporting it.

                    Between the Breaths and the things like temples and monasteries, I not only find this to be a strong expansion on the Immaculate Order, but another very good example for other kinds of comprehensive institution that can be added to a game, particularly ones that characters might develop in play.

                    Last for the time being are subjects concerning the Sidereals. First the sidebar concerned with the Wyld Hunt; I believe that in the past, astrology to locate Anathema was more of the All-Seeing Eye’s remit, but I prefer it being consolidated with the Order since they’re the ones who manage the Hunt and have the Sidereals. I like the way that it makes use of the altered presence of such thaumaturgy in the setting to both make a point about the Order having a more accommodating attitude to the development of such folk magic and being part of a general scheme to provide a plausible excuse for the discoveries that Sidereal magic can yield. I also quite like how it references not only the proper Wyld Hunt but several of these other components of the Order that have been introduced to create an impression that a discovered Anathema has a widely distributed net that can be closed around them; it’s a more complex structure than just finding them with omniscient magic, and I think that it can create for more tense and dramatic sessions and storylines in which one needs to identify and evade a network of observers and informants that will be on the lookout and both work to delay you and transmit your presence up the line. There can be a prelude of activities undertaken before the true and fully furnished Wyld Hunt attempts to descend upon you in overwhelming force. It’s a good perspective on the resources that the Realm was able to mobilize to ensure that its Hunts were the best in the business.

                    Finally there’s the focus on the Sidereals themselves. Like many other things brought over, this is a nice, lean description; the focus is on the presence of those Exalted within and their contributions to the Order, rather than giving a separate history lesson on them. I find the emphasis here to be less on the Sidereals as the secret masters of the Order; it feels to me more like they employ their capabilities more to supplement its initiatives and ensure it doesn’t stray from a beneficial line, as well as being the staging ground for Sidereal agents to work towards preserving the Realm as a whole. I’m personally gratified by this being a point in the game’s line in which it can come out and directly state that, until the resurgence of the Solars, the Fellowship was basically united in purpose (I’m guessing that the outliers would largely amount to ronin and those in league with Raken Thulio), and it’s only with recent events that the Gold Faction has become a thing again. I think there’s a subtle adjustment of tone to the matter of Chejop’s dwindling life, not so much impending disaster as a punctuation of the fact that the larger Bronze Faction has been losing some of its biggest players; the line given to followers knowing the history of successfully keeping Solars in check through effective application of force makes me think there’s an eye towards the idea of Sidereals planning around Chejop’s redundancy. And I quite like how it finishes off on the point that a major source of their current difficulty is in fact handling the suddenly very fractious and self-involved Dragon Blooded, since it maintains a focus on the significance of conflicts internal to the Realm and its reigning Exalted and the agency afforded to such. I find the line on Chejop trying to shore things up while preparing to harness the considerable resources of the Immaculate Order (which have just been effectively outlined for us) to be rather exciting; it creates an anticipation for the Sidereals making themselves involved in the conflict within the Realm in a big way, with a massive number of agents and adherents and assets, a powerful force along an axis separate from the usual politics of the Scarlet Dynasty.

                    I have approximate knowledge of many things.
