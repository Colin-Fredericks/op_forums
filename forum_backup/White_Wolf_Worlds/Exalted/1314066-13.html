                    Almost everything in the chapter up until now has been concerned with how the Immaculate Order do, and I find it to have accomplished that well, so it makes sense to now apply the same clarity to defining specifically what the Order does. I find it very effective to begin with the matter of how places where the Order holds sway (I like it being non-descript here as a reminder that there’s a bit more reach than just the Blessed Isle) they preside over every major event of a person’s lifetime; naming (implicitly still having that practice in which children can go years without a name), building layouts (very important in small communities to ensure they don’t infringe upon or otherwise disrupt each other), funerals (I find it interesting how the Philosophy doesn’t have a standard funeral rite, and so defers to whatever will be pleasing to the departed soul as defined by local custom). It’s nice to see a point of consistency brought in with the subject of marriage; it often felt weird to me that monks didn’t preside over Dynastic marriage but did for everybody else, so I like how their involvement here now consists of advising on a good time to marry rather than officiating. I think it really sells the ubiquity of the Order to refer to their place in the lives of everybody from the lowliest peasant to the House matriarch, and what those people respectively get from the arrangement. But the most important thing that I’m getting from this is that it’s putting the Order’s role as cultural police front and centre; that it views society as requiring constant vigilance even when seemingly compliant to ensure that the Perfected Hierarchy is adhered to. I like how that point of the group perceiving itself as needing to stand apart in order to fulfil that role places their modelling their entire clergy off of monasticism into context.

                    The section on the Order’s political activities reiterates the observation I made about how it presents a more nebulous and decentralized face, which matches nicely with how it’s described as not engaging in games of power and wealth. It’s an interesting contrast to a lot of real life hierarchical religious organisations, in which clerics might seek or need temporal power and sources of obligated income, to have the idea that most of what they have arises from donatives. Mind, in contrast with a lot of real religious organisations that I’m familiar with (primarily Christian), the Order does have a very close and cooperative tie with the centralised government and the monarchy; seeking land and business is probably less necessary when you can rely on the Empress to provide a lot of your money. As for the manner in which it does act politically, I especially like the idea that they publish literature to promote ideas on appropriate social conduct, as well as the way that they form networks of connections as an instrument to deal in rewards or punishments as appropriate for those around them in a more subtle manner. I think that historically, censure from religious authorities can be a bit of a dubious means of leveraging secular authorities, but I can buy that the Order makes itself popular enough among the majority that people will follow suit when monks scorn impious officials. And I’m quite intrigued by the idea that their missionaries and frontier bases take a very severe hand against the surrounding communities when they can; I imagine that the presence of Exalted amongst them helps out a lot in getting away with taking such a subversive role when abroad, in contrast with a lot of similar situations in reality where the missionaries tend to keep their heads down when adrift in a possibly hostile world. I thought before that Immaculate proselytizing constituted the most overt form of Realm cultural enforcement over imperial possessions, and I like the way it’s presented here, that the lack of Immaculate adherence is considered the basis for barbarianism more than forms of dress, language or custom, and thus obligates monks (and maybe other supporters) to oppose and sabotage such improper practice.

                    I’m presuming that the picture is of a monk advising officials; I like the colouring and brightness of the whole thing, particularly in the women’s hair, and the framing does a good job of giving the monk a central and imposing presence that is complemented by her softer and more accommodating expression and posture. I will note how she should technically be bald, though.

                    Mind, for all of the things that have given the Immaculate Order a softer air, I think it’s good to bring things on point with the way that their ultimate objective is phrased: to force the societies of Creation into adherence with its vision. That conviction, the idea that they employ social engineering techniques to enforce it, attributing every misfortune or unhappiness with deviation from the Perfected Hierarchy… the biggest thing that I see in this are the sources of contention with non-Realm Immaculates. Not to mention possibilities that might arise should Mnemon have her way. That goal, that methodology, and the focus on the matter of managing information, that’s really bringing the Dai Li elements being added or emphasized in the Order to the forefront, and I am here for it.

                    It’s an interesting idea, that the Order would be able to fold its role of censorship into the fact that they provide basic education to so many people; they cultivate the image of themselves as the only credible judges and distributors of permissible knowledge, and all in a form that maintains their public good will. The Order might restrict what literature it provides, but it’s still an organised endeavour towards providing literature to people at large (whom they also work to ensure are literate) in a world where they might otherwise have no such access. It’s a compelling image of a more subtle method by which the Realm maintains control, how the Sidereal provided support of the Order benefitted the power base of the Scarlet Empress, even if it lies atop a system in which they’ll engage in very public reprimands and destruction of unsanctioned material to reinforce their legitimacy and keep people in line. Having been told that the Realm produces literature for widespread consumption, I’d like to know what some of the substance of such might be, and the idea that monks commonly appear as a motif or device for the revelation of truth serves that purpose as well as elaborating on the general perception of the Order; I particularly like the way that this perception constitutes a more benign take on the monk in the rule of observer and inquisitor. However, for all that I’ve been referring to them as secret and/or cultural police, the fact does remain that they’re an independent organisation, and I think that point is effectively made by the fact that the group uses its own discretion in deciding what to do with the information it gathers, whether that be handing it to the authorities, using it as a means of developing its power networks, or keeping a lid on it and trying to bring wayward souls back into upright conduct.

                    I can see why the sidebar on the Philosophy’s aniconism would introduce an idea that the Order can employ a more flexible, even variable standard for what is acceptable; even if they ensure more widespread literacy, I can take the point raised by other people in the past that such a strict enforcement makes a lot of basic commerce impractical. If the purpose of aniconism is to discourage idolatry, it makes sense that the monks would preferably exert a judgement on what representative images actually cross that line, and reserve censure for those that do. I still like the idea that the baseline for art and decoration on the Blessed Isle prefers abstraction and geometry, and the phrasing around the permission for depicting Elemental and Immaculate Dragons suggests to me that it’s a more recent allowance, adding to the feeling that the Realm’s society has been an organic thing that differs over time. I neglected to bring it up before, but I do notice how there’s no inclusion whatsoever of a line to the effect of the Realm’s society having been more or less the same across its history, which honestly never really reflected how it was described even back then.

                    Now, one of the things I’ve been most intrigued by before now, the Order’s complicated relationship with social upheaval. One thing that I like here is acknowledgement that the Order itself can be a contributor or victim of disorder, offsetting other language that might create an impression of uniform integrity to the monks. I also think it’s good to set things in perspective; the Order might have the novel function of facilitating uprisings, but we’re also given a clear reminder that it is just as if not more often right there alongside the forces suppressing them. I see a little nod to practices of the Medieval Catholic Church in the line about the Order specifically not denying the authority of objectionable secular authorities (part of the point of excommunication of princes was that it was supposed to be a statement that people were no longer subject to them, and were free, even obligated, to overthrow them… even if that basically never happened), while I find the alternative in their own particular idiom to be quite evocative; the escalating series of censure that starts at monks publicly shunning, that rises through organising protest and arrives at unleashing the martial arts should the authorities try to suppress with force. It is interesting how the Empress, and apparently even the current Great Houses, took a fairly even hand in responding to such risings, with the allowance of some destruction to let off some steam before reprisals with surgical focus on the leadership while the movement at large is allowed to resume their normal lives and maybe even accomplish some things in the form of destroyed tax records and grievances against local officials resulting in their replacement; I find it particularly cute how rebel monks are publicly reprimanded in an inconsequential manner since they probably had actual approval from their superiors (it reminds me of the abbot from 36th Chamber of Shaolin). I will however offer one criticism of this section in that large sections of it do seem to be repeating verbatim things described earlier in the book, especially the account of Seven Rivers and the Unbroken Rushes Rebellion; this looks like it could have done with some tighter editing (maybe the fully released version went and did that?).

                    As for the part directly stating and elaborating upon the tenet in the Realm’s Immaculate Philosophy that the Dragon Blooded are the rightful rulers of all Creation… it divides me a little bit. To be sure, there’s some very interesting stuff in there, at the core of which is the distinct conceit that being Exalted is not merely a signifier of enlightenment through multiple lifetimes of correct action, but a reward for it that entails freedom to a level of privilege and indulgence that no mortal under Immaculate tenets is afforded. Sure, you still apparently get those more stoic Terrestrials who opt to conduct themselves with a bit more austerity, but the idea that the Order upholds that a measure of decadence is the earned right of those incarnated from many cycles of wisdom and adherence… it’s not just a very peculiar religious idea and the most overt element of the Philosophy being constructed with an eye towards enticing and empowering the Dragon Blooded, but it does put some of House Mnemon into perspective; how their piety is not at odds with them drawing upon a wide range of pleasure and artistic expressions. I can imagine some rather distinct theology reconciling the disparate expectations and allowances of the Philosophy. And it certainly creates an interesting framework for the upbringing of Dynastic children, and how religious tutelage may include preparation for enjoying the grandeur and power that comes with Exaltation.

                    My main reservation is about some of the particulars of how it describes the Exalted experience and relationships with mortals, buuttt… I can reconcile that partially with an idea that this does not constitute a 100% literal description of how things work as much as the general standards that the Order projects (and permits), and partially by getting over it a bit and taking the point that yes, maybe I could have more of an eye to how the powers and resources provided to the Exalted of the Dynasty might encourage them towards loftier goals and a perspective that they’re above the concerns of mortal life. Kind of a “calling for Diet Coke and one appears in your hand” situation.

                    Hmmm… leave it here for now, and then finish the chapter tomorrow. Maybe get a start on the next one.

                    I have approximate knowledge of many things.
                    Write up as I play Xenoblade Chronicles.

                        I often wonder what Immaculate missionaries are like. Probably well received in areas where the local deities are abusive but less so in other places. Taken as an objective whole, the Immaculate Faith lacks broad spectrum appeal outside of a society already indoctrinated to believe in its precepts(like the Realm and its more servile satrapies). The promise of DB privilege in the next life(false though it may be) probably provides incentive for the more cynical aristocratic converts while pragmatic concerns such as regular weather and harvests and the occasional monastic charity probably attract agrarian and urban working class types.                                     Oh dang, I forgot to comment on the sidebar about slavery. Nothing much to it, as slavery is considered to be little different than any other unfortunate incarnation in the Perfected Hierarchy, with the same standards of “mistreatment warrants an aided revolt”. Actually, there are a few old points that I’m surprised aren’t mentioned here, such as Dragon Blooded being considered the only ones properly enlightened for the responsibility of owning people, or the perspective that enslaving barbarians is seen as an efficient means of facilitating their education in the Philosophy.

                                    I think that in the past descriptions of the Immaculate Order have placed the experience of those within it first, and the activities afterwards. I find this reversal to be more engaging; playing monks or getting into their background might be a thing, but I feel as though the strongest presence of the Order in games is in the execution of their duties, so that’s the more important info. That and I feel as though the old arrangement left the matter of the Order’s conduct a bit truncated, and attention might have been exhausted by the preceding at that point anyway; in this form, I’m already informed about and invested in the Order, and the subject of monks’ lives is just a nice bonus.

                                    Anyway, the introduction of the subject is good, the matter of how becoming a monk is a hard lifelong commitment of service, and I particularly like that idea that they view those who enter but can’t hack it are still being instructed in the Perfected Hierarchy.

                                    So, the subject of entry; nice start in listing all of the sources and purposes that people might find in joining up, with a distinct point of hardly being founded on donated children at all; it would appear that even those who are put on a track to be trained towards monkhood from youth are intended to be voluntary, which might be fitting to how the Order has a much heavier workload than might be typical of monastic organisations. Of the backgrounds, I particularly like how the Order offers a refuge for marginalized and outlawed folks, and think the idea that Dragon Blooded might want an environment to cultivate deeper awareness of their Essence is the strongest insight yet into what motivates Dynastic Exalted towards them.

                                    A rather significant revision is the process of entry itself; where before, all postulants needed to present themselves at the temple in the heart of the Imperial City and be accepted by the Paragon of Sextes Jylis, here the system for admission is distributed at a much more local level. Quite apart from further emphasis on the decentralized structure, the old system never really made much sense; the Order was always presented as having an enormous number of monks, and the Blessed Isle is huge (not to mention restricting of travel for the lower social strata), the idea that they could maintain their numbers with such a concentrated recruitment system was quite ludicrous (although it’s fine for the Palace Sublime to be considered the only place suitable to assess Dynastic postulants). Even distributed, the substance of actually being a postulant remains… actually, it’s slightly modified, removing the flagellation by way of kneeling from the prior Editions (especially Second) to focus on the point that they’ll be tested by monks with commands and insults before being directly assessed by the monk in charge. Makes sense, the Immaculate Philosophy isn’t exactly big on corporeal mortification. Oh hey, and for the first time we’re actually given a layout of the process of training once postulants advance to acolytes, as opposed to First Edition’s “it happens” and Second’s “you’ve got to journey from the Imperial City to the Palace Sublime, and now you’re a monk”.

                                    I mentioned 36th Chamber of Shaolin before, and I’m finding that even more evident in this training process; the system of academic and martial instruction leading up to tiers of seasonal examination that need to be passed in set number before qualifying as a monk. I kind of like the way that washing out of the training at this stage isn’t necessarily a shameful thing, and can perhaps still carry a certain prestige of having made it even that far and having learned a few things before returning home, not to mention the way that the Order can still have uses for those who aren’t cut out to be full monks. Yess, I like the idea that oblates originate from failed acolytes whose services can still be accepted. I’m guessing that the difference between an acolyte and a monk who has not yet achieved the First Coil is that the latter cannot be dismissed for failure of tests, expected to brush themselves off should they be overcome by those extremely severe and elaborate physical, mental and spiritual trials and get back on the horse. I like it, if for nothing else than giving me pictures of grand training montages, and maybe some formative experiences that monk characters can refer back to. Finally, I like the continuation of a greater sense of how the Order is organised towards its missions in the way that those who achieve the First Coil consult with members of the Breath of Sextes Jylis to actually be given an assignment, since it emphasizes how they’re training towards being an active presence in the Realm rather than just being cloistered. I appreciate the levels of the Coils being described with brevity, and a focus on their functional responsibilities.

                                    There’s a picture, but… it’s just a repeat of an earlier one showing a peasant uprising. Indeed, that also accompanied the description of historical risings that I’ve found to be repeated exactly here…

                                    Let’s see, the details on monastic naming have been slightly modified, but the basic principle remains the same; entry level comes with being given a minor humbling name, and advancement grants the privilege of electing to go back to one’s original name or ask the archimandrite to confer a new ne focused on presenting one’s nature as a monk. I like to imagine the measure of camaraderie and informality in the idea that monks may call one another by diminutives when their full new names are a bit of a mouthful.

                                    Not much to say about the lifestyle of the monks, since it mostly remains the same as prior Editions (and probably about what one would expect from their presentation). Humble dress and personal styling, vows of celibacy and poverty, and dietary restrictions, the works. There are a few little elaborations that I like, such as noting how beards shouldn’t be styled, pointing out how there are values to one’s family in going childless, that part of the point of building lavish temples is to reflect well on the donations of the community, and the ways in which monks might make up for shortcomings in their appearance and eating habits. One thing that has my imagination is how monks are permitted to possess nicer, more elaborate robes for ceremonial occasions, largely because I happen to have looked at some pictures of Christian monks and priests wearing decorated vestments, so can see some very compelling images of such.

                                    Huh, so it would appear that the Immaculate Order regards hermits as an acceptable oddity rather than a fully established practice; I take it that some monks, either from their motives for joining in the first place or how they take to particular qualities of the Texts, find themselves unsatisfied with even monastic life, and feel driven towards complete solitude. I suppose that in the eyes of the Order, even if this constitutes a dereliction of their normal duties it’s not technically a violation of their vows, and maybe there are also some other monks who sympathise with their fellows wanting to be removed from distraction for deeper internal contemplation. In any case, it’s cute how such isolated figures become the subjects of rumour and myth among the lay populace, who take the refutations of the Order as just confirming the idea that there’s something strange and mystical to hermitage. I think a large number of pilgrims scouting out in search of those hermits would make for an interesting scene to engage with in some manner.

                                    Ah yes, that sidebar is clearly complementing the description of the Immaculate work ethic; how despite the expectations of outsiders of secluded contemplation, the activities of the Order are actually highly demanding on their time. I think that makes for another interesting insight into the relationship between the Order and lay populace, how they might work diligently on things like public benefits and ministering, but in a manner intended to be largely invisible. I suppose that some of that is administrative work that many people are liable to take for granted anyway, but that even for their more active involvements they might cultivate an image of effortlessness, encouraging people to not fully think about things such as, say, where all of those pamphlets come from. I can imagine that as not only adding to the mystique of the Immaculate Order, but aiding in their secret policework, which I suppose is an element of their mission not fully recognised by most citizens. It makes me think of a feeback loop in which people are conditioned to not develop awareness that the monks are watching them, such that when a monk publicly reprimands some inadequate commune or individual, that too has the air of an uncannily keen insight. Still, I also like how this whole section emphasizes the essential humanity of even monks, and that even they need vacations sometimes (albeit of a form that adheres to their vows and strictures). From that, I suspect that more than a few hermits consist of monks who took their sabbatical off in the wilderness and just found themselves unwilling to ever go back.

                                    Yes, personal connections looks like another aspect that is calibrated with a mind towards keeping the monks engaged with and present in society at large for story purposes. I think there are some clever ways in which such an agenda is folded into consistency with the Order’s overall mission, especially with Dragon Blooded monks and the Great Houses and the focus given on their secret police role (hence it being useful to have friends and family to observe and provide information). I get a kick out of how there’s a line on family loyalty being encouraged, but secondary to loyalty to the Order, considering that’s basically the opposite of the line given for monks who come from House Mnemon (which surely provides the most of them from the Dynasty by far). I’m particularly pleased with the emphasis given here for the Order’s view on Sworn Kinships, considering how that was such a powerfully enriched element from What Fire Has Wrought; Hearths with lay Exalted are all well and good for groups in which they need somebody to advocate Immaculate morals and represent the Order’s interests, but I’m personally more interested in the idea of Kinships consisting solely of Exalted monks (something that I’ve never really thought about before). Still, having a monk in a group of players is probably very useful for a Storyteller who wants to provide a group with hooks and the occasional prod.

                                    I can’t find very much to say about addressing the subject of monks who leave the Order, beyond the general appreciation for the subject being addressed. I do like the way that it entails a kind of reverse shunning, with the family that one might return to very studiously not addressing where one was and fellow monks acting as though you were never one of them; I suppose these standards are also in the interest of preserving the image of the immutability of the Order, since even creating a script for everybody to knowingly act within can shape outlook. Still, I find the idea of a monk who refuses to give up the vestments despite dismissal, and who preserves themselves through success in highly violent duels, to be a very compelling plot hook; I’m picturing something reminiscent of Burn Legend, of an especially vicious looking monk in a stormy wilderness with hardened bloody knuckles.

                                    And it’s brought to a close with the matter of perspectives on Exalted besides the Anathema by default, which is obviously a good deal more elaborate than ever before.

                                    First up are the Sidereals. Not much new here, it continues the core’s revised line about how they’ve set themselves up to be seen as servants of Heaven who aid in preserving the Perfected Hierarchy. I think it mostly just consolidates the information on who and how Terrestrials become aware of them. I get what the Bronze Faction is going for by starting to promote interpretations that paint the Gold Faction with the same brush as those they’re starting to work alongside, but it also feels like something that is increasing the risk of them giving themselves away. It’s a nice little hint of them starting to lose their handle on things.

                                    The Exigents section expands upon what was stated on Immaculate views of the Chosen of small gods in the core book (which, yes, was a thing, despite how many people seemed to miss it); that their existence constitutes improper congress between god and mortal in contravention of the Perfected Hierarchy, that overrides all other considerations, with the added point that (much like sorcerers and thaumaturgists) their capabilities can be valuable enough that they can get a head start on redemption by serving the Order itself. The core book said that the label of Anathema was reserved for those who actively worked against the supremacy of the Dragon Blooded, but the phrasing here seems to suggest that it can be enough to merely not properly acknowledge it. All of that done, we’re once more given some of my beloved examples. The characters given are all solid, a good cross section of various interactions between Exigents and the Order; I like how even the one declared Anathema still regards himself as a devotee, and there’s something particularly intriguing in the idea that respectable gods on the Blessed Isle might produce Exalted who will serve with the Order as a matter of course. And I especially like them as examples of Exigents specifically, with some very interesting gods to draw upon and unusual avenues or applications for their powers. I also like how one of them has a mortal lifespan, particularly for her being in an extremely dangerous and sensitive position; it would certainly be a poor lookout if the Time of Tumult entailed a bunch of forbidden Exigents going free because their jailor passed from old age.

                                    In reading the God-blooded sidebar, I need to remind myself that the majority of them will have no exceptional powers, and the most significant thing that the Order will be watching for will be continued improper relationships with divine parents. That idea of the Order taking responsibility for a bunch of children that a god accumulated in pursuit of power is also quite intriguing (although I imagine that they’d almost never be kept as a group while they’re being raised).

                                    Not much left now. Liminals are almost unknown, but inauspicious by default due to being undead, offset only by the observed tendency to drive other ghosts back from whence they came. The Order doesn’t have an intrinsic beef with Getimian Exalted, but here we’re given an interesting point in which their function as an extension of Sidereal interests ends with them directed towards purposes connected to the Star Chosen; still, at least the monks get fancy new meditation out of it. Abyssals can be straightforwardly designated as Anathema by default, and are apparently cause for some fresh speculation among monks as to the exact source of their power, from which I take it that the Deathlords and Neverborn are still a subject of limited awareness (I’m intrigued by the prospect of “bleak majesty of dead suns”). While the scant few Infernal Exalted remain an unknown quantity, associated with the Order solely by virtue of how the Sidereals will see to it that they’re regarded as Anathema once they’ve got a clearer picture of the situation; I’ll admit that the reference to Sidereals pursuing them with dread smacks a bit of some of the Second Edition hype, but under the circumstances I’m willing to look at it less in terms of there being something outstanding about them and more about them being an unnerving and unanticipated new permutation on the Solars.

                                    I’ll admit, I had my doubts about another book giving a chapter for describing the Immaculate Order, but it’s acquitted itself very well here. The Order feels like a more dynamic and engaging organisation than ever before, and the clearer picture of their structure, resources and personnel makes them look both easier to utilise practically and more inviting to do so. The Breaths and the expanding description of their training period in particular stand out to me. I wasn’t looking to care more about the Order as I was for the Thousand Scales, but the effect is the same nonetheless.

                                    I have approximate knowledge of many things.
