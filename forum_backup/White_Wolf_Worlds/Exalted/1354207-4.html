                    It can be, and often has been for better or worse, but the assertion that it must be is, if nothing else, kind of ignorant of the range or world views displayed by actual atheists.



                    Originally posted by Exthalion View Post
                    However, I think the mention of alchemy upthread is more useful for the vast majority of things. One of the fundamental differences between alchemy and chemistry is the importance of Will/consciousness. Transformation without are mirrored by transformations within. As such something like Willpower or Consciousness is a fundamental force or aspect of reality that is most certainly absent in standard RW scientific models.
                    I'm pretty sure that as real life practices of alchemy went, the mirroring of transformations was concerned with the idea that manipulation of substances was essential for the refinement of the self, not the other way around. The transformation of base metals into gold wasn't a case of "you have to really believe in it hard enough", it was the idea that if the correct, objectively functional formula for that transmutation could be discovered then engaging in the process would vastly elevate the spirit of the one doing so. As far as I'm aware, the point for dedicated alchemists was never about increasing stockpiles of gold as material wealth, it was about gold being pretty signifying it as a more rarefied mineral.

                    And I suppose that one could see the circumstances in which people first witness chemical reactions while lacking even a basic underlying knowledge of how they occur or much sense for the practical applications and be amazed enough by it to think it was spiritually revelatory. And as time goes by, experimentation with materials and reactions would lead to alchemists gaining real insights into chemical properties that gets used to produce commercially viable substances at least as a side line. The spiritual stuff does eventually get crowded out by things like changing world views, the commercial aspect becoming increasingly significant, and the fact that it becomes hard for alchemists to ignore that the direction aligning with rational materialism does seem to end up yielding more engaging results. I mean, at some point those folks could not help but recognise that lead was not a less pure version of gold, but you mix it with the correct solutions and it yields the most interesting crystals.

                    Turns out that the real way to convert lead into gold is to figure out ways of making marketable glass products from it.

                    I say all of this as establishing part of the basis for why I'm often a bit dissatisfied with fictional properties that approach alchemy as a kind of all-encompassing magical chemistry where it's like... the spiritual exploration aspects are absent and it just goes with something like "alchemy as the means of making whatever raw materials from what is at hand" or "alchemy as name for brewing outright potions" in a society that still seems to have limited access to or application of these things.

                    Now that being said, referring back to the world view underlying actual alchemy brings me to a point that another reason I could imagine rational materialism not having gained as much traction in Creation thus far is that it lends itself to having more directly empowered alternative world views and means of engaging with the world. I don't see that as meaning that rational materialism works less or is superseded or rendered unnecessary by magic, just an idea that systems pointing towards it never fully form for a variety of reasons, some of them being obstacles to the formations of those world views and social systems and some of them being people off on a number of other paths.

                    As alchemy in the setting goes, I like it forming the basis of a sorcerous initiation (and even the idea that some of these things would be beneficial even for people that aren't even sorcerers), but have a few reservations about it having shown up as a Craft in the Hundred Devils write-up for bats of all places.

                            In the most prosaic sense, I was referring to the fact that the mechanical resource of willpower must be invested in various sorcerous and thaumaturgical procedures, though an intense degree of focus and expenditure of mental energy might explain the same.
                            There is probably a great deal of merit in the notion that other world views also offer viable paths to power or prosperity such that there may never have been a point where any was clearly superior, if indeed they were not outright syncretized in an attempt to capture multiple benefits.

                            Tangentially, I am of the opinion that the collapse of civilization and the way that remnants were distributed may be another factor preventing the (re)emergence of a recognizable scientific tradition. While people have looked to reinvent the wheel, they generally don't. Solutions were already found to a great many of the material problems individuals and societies face in the past, and refined to a high degree by a civilization with time and resources to conduct large scale investment, research, and testing. Those with the natural talent, resources (including education), and supernatural to be best poised to make new discoveries and inventions will also be those most familiar with the old solutions and most able to at least approximate them.

                            The graduates of the Heptogram, among the richest and most intelligent cohorts you are likely to find in Creation, are also the ones with the best access to copies of the demonologies of the First Age which will contain information on the ideal demon to solve most of their problems.                             This is, I suppose, a subset of the lack of pressure to innovate. But I argue that situations of this type also... set the agenda? for intellection work. When innovation is required, there is an existing body of very useful techniques which will probably seem like the ideal seed from which to begin searching for a solution rather than starting wholly from scratch. If you need to pump water out of a mine, there are multiple solutions from summoned water elementals to bound pump demons to occult artifice which can do the job. If none of them exactly suit your particular mine, one of them seems like a more promising start than inventing the steam engine.

                                    You know, at times like this I kind of wonder exactly what people mean by science, and what their baseline for how fast its investigation is.

                                    I mean, prior to the development of large scale commercially viable production of electricity, people effectively figured out (with mathematical precision) the principles guiding bodies in motion, transmissions of force, sound and light, various aspects of fluid dynamics, several qualities of thermodynamics, ways to precisely measure chemical reactions and quantities of substances. Like, before people were even able to directly verify the existence of the atom, they figured out how to effectively measure its mass. Like, they could take two different substances and tell you the exact mass in kilograms of each that would contain the exact same number of atoms (that's what the mole is).

                                    Absolute Zero is something that a person accurately theorized about based on collecting data on volume contractions correlated to reduction in temperature and drawing a graph.

                                    And, like, various parts of astronomy, although that's at least one area where Creation absolutely doesn't play by the rules. You can't really figure out the motion of the planets from an objective framework, because they really are moving randomly.

                                    I'd also find statements to the effect of "it took thousands of years" are technically accurate but not quite assigning the blame correctly. Because again, a lot of this stuff could only effectively make progress during periods of social stability and prosperity.

                                    Still, I don't know, sometimes I feel as though people who talk a lot about science don't quite know a whole lot about its actual history or composition. At the very least, the they don't often seem to talk about specific things. Some of that makes me think it has less to do with the rate of scientific development and more with Newtonian physics, determining the mass of the mole and extrapolating absolute zero from graph paper lacks the sexiness of rocket launches and the electronic telegraph to most people.

                                            Science isn't stagnant in Creation. The issue is:

                                            1. During the Solar Deliberative era, a lot of technology and scientific understanding was derived from the Solars, since they had the best crafters.

                                            2. During the Shogunate, that Solar-centric technology and understanding declined, and while the Dragon-Blooded made new things to replace it, the Sidereals and Lunars were sometimes actively sabotaging them (for different reasons - the Sidereals wanted them to be easier to manage and less likely to blow themselves up, the Lunars just hated their guts.) Keep in mind that this was about 750 years, so you could still have seen eg. the level of real-world technological advancement from 500 BC to 250 AD or something. It also wasn't as noticeable because a lot of it was replacing stuff they previously had rather than totally new advancements. This handwavy sense of discrete obvious advancement along our history is absurdly silly and oversimplified, but it gets the idea across.

                                            3. The Contagion hit and everyone was fucked. 90% of everything died. Creation is literally post-apocalyptic. This isn't, like, the black death in the real world - society flat-out collapsed and a ton of stuff was lost.

                                            4. It's been another 750 years since then. Again, 750 years from the total collapse of civilization isn't long - going by the stupid oversimplification above, it *might* reach 1000 AD if we ignore the end of the world in between.

                                            5. That said, there is some genuine resistance to advancement. There are gods and exalted with reasons to take actions that prevent technological advancement, directly or indirectly. Look at what happened in real-world China around 1500 AD for an example, when the leaders of China increasingly turned it inwards because they feared (correctly) that trade was empowering people who could challenge their authority. There's no one central anti-technology group among the DBs (although there may be some of this among the Sidereals and Lunars), but there's a lot of DBs who will crush upstart mortal endeavors or redirect resources in nepotistic ways that make it hard for new ideas to thrive.

                                            6. Technology isn't, like, some sort of magic unified tech tree that everyone progresses along in the same way. But the main issue is that the sort of "advancement" you're thinking of requires that ideas be preserved and disseminated. Creation has mostly been bad at that and has had catastrophes interfering with it for the reasons listed above. Individual areas have their own technology and advancement, but something like an Industrial Revolution can't happen without a lot more stable exchange and preservation of ideas.

                                                    One thing is the difference between accumulating technical innovations, and actual science.

                                                    The East Asian, Indian, and Muslim civilization-complexes did accumulate technical innovations, but they never had a full Scientific Revolution. That only happened in Western Christian civilization. Why?

                                                    Well, one of the obvious candidates is that Western Christianity incorporated Aristotle into standard theology in the 13th Century under Aquinas, while the other major civilizations did not (either explicitly rejecting him like the Muslim theologians, or never even having him come to enough attention to consider in India and East Asia). Since Aristotle had engaged in rational inquiry into natural phenomena, the incorporation meant there was a certain derived respectability to doing the same which didn't happen in societies where religion-endorsed philosophy did not include investigation into natural phenomena.

                                                    Applying that to Creation, with gods and elementals directly involved in physical phenomena, it is not just somewhat harder to investigate; it is likely actively discouraged for mortals to poke their noses in. While that doesn't apply to the Dragon-Blooded, they're still going to be part of an overall culture that discourages that. Throw in how much knowledge was lost with the fall of the First Age and then with the Great Contagion, and the budding investigators among the Dragon-Blooded are probably going to divert themselves more into trying to recover old knowledge than making new.

                                                            Originally posted by Verzio View Post
                                                            Since Aristotle had engaged in rational inquiry into natural phenomena, the incorporation meant there was a certain derived respectability to doing the same which didn't happen in societies where religion-endorsed philosophy did not include investigation into natural phenomena.
                                                            Encouragement for investigation of natural phenomena has often been interpreted as being written right into the Qu'ran, and might be associated with the fact that the Islamic world has had, for instance, sophisticated treatises on the science of optics.

                                                            The science of optics being making and recording observations on the manner in which light is transmitted through materials to both make reasonable models for prediction and form a basis to speculate on what the fundamental nature of light is.

                                                            But again, not rocket science, so I guess the question of how it's investigated in Creation just kind of gets overlooked.

                                                            A reason that peer review is very important to modern scientific inquiry and it's useful for general economics combined with deliberate state endeavours to funnel larger numbers of people than ever before into scientific education is that the most accurate models are dependent on huge amounts of raw experimental data that can be collated to produce ever more precisely calibrated curves. It's the reason why a properly managed drug trial takes years before the thing is permitted on the open market; any given singular trial can only involve a few dozen, maybe a few hundred people, and you want to ensure as much uniformity in the variables as possible both to be sure that the thing genuinely works and all those people didn't show improvements as the result of a fluke, while both spreading it across as large a sample size as you can get to see how it interacts with several variables (to find out if certain specific interactions make it dangerous).

                                                            Scientific inquiry is always going to be slow when all of the experiments have to be conducted by lone eccentrics and their assistants, and all of the data needs to be stored on paper, and all the calculations done by hand, and the degree to which the educated can consult with one another is limited. I was told that the graph from which absolute zero was derived was massive, took up the floor space of a large room; that may have been an exaggeration, but it has a ring of truth from considering how many experimental observations of temperature and volume would have been necessary to form a sufficiently accurate curve, and the time that would have taken even before the graph is drawn out.

                                                            I don't know, what god is terribly concerned with you making observations that can be used to form precise models of how pressure works?
