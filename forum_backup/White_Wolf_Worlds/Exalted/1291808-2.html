                        Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post

                        You know how, when you're a subsistence farmer, and the only thing that you can really do with your baby is keep it in a bundle on your back?

                        Can't keep them in cribs, because you don't have any. Can't leave work to spend time with them, because nobody really will or can subsidize that. And getting too attached before they display much personality simply isn't practical, because there's something like a forty percent chance that they'll die of disease, exposure (even in your house, it can be cold) or malnutrition before they're two years old.

                        Or just being born weak and sickly for reasons including that the parent's diet wasn't exactly rich in vitamins.

                        And that's not even considering the documentation of fairly high historical incidences in which babies who were unwanted or inconvenient would just be left out in the wilderness to die. Birth control wasn't very reliable, abortion was likely a disaster and if you weren't really in proximity to a place that took unwanted children in... awful lot of situations in which children come along when they can't be cared for.
                        So, a lot of this is technically true, but it's also part of a once-popular historical theory that's been challenged and largely overturned. I looked at it a bit during my MA. I'm no expert, but I can find some sources. (I remember this because I had an argument with the other students about it!)

                        Yes, some people dumped their children because they couldn't care for them, and society often told parents that, for example, bastards should be handed to monasteries, the disabled should be abandoned, and if your baby died as a child suck it up, because children die all the time.

                        Historians Philippe Aries (Centuries of Childhood) and Lawrence Stone (Family, Sex and Marriage in England, 1500-1800) argued that essentially, the Medieval period had no real conception of childhood, and this developed with the creation of the nuclear family in the 18th century, which also encouraged parents to love and care for their babies and children rather than consider them in terms of help for work.
                        But these were both largely Early Modern historians, and a number of other Early Modernists and medievalists pushed back on this.

                        You can read one historians's review of Stone's work for free. Pages 5, 6 and 7 discuss parents not caring that much about their children. Besides history, he argues that sociological studies of families in societies with high infant mortality today show that, in fact, it's really upsetting to lose an infant.
                        http://www.alanmacfarlane.com/TEXTS/stonesex.pdf


                        Shulamith Shahar (Childhood in the Middle Ages), Barbara Hanawult (The Ties that Bound: Peasant Families in Medieval England) argue that most of the historical evidence actually shows that individuals, by and large, actually did really care about their babies, and were often devastated by their deaths*.

                        A more recent book I've heard good things about is Elisheva Baumgarten, Mothers and Children: Jewish Family Life in Medieval Europe, which also argues the same thing. One thing she found is that Jewish authories often complained of how many men were depressed and crying about their dead babies, and how they needed to get over it.
                        So there's a disconnect between the unemotional views of society (babies die all the time, it's not a big deal) and the individual experience.


                        *Though I suppose society had means of helping them cope that are not so available to families today; firstly, that most people had relatives who'd been through the same thing (I remember my mum found it really difficult losing two babies and then her mum partly because none of her friends had the same experience), and second, a strong belief in the afterlife.
                        Hildegard of Bremen, the famous medieval prophetess, received many letters from mothers asking if she had seen their dead babies in Heaven (and she often wrote back saying that, yes, don't worry, your baby is in Heaven). There's also the phenomon where midwives often told the parents of stillbirths that their child had been alive for 20 seconds... just long enough for an emergency baptism before dying, so again, at least they're in Heaven. (see Ronald Finucane "The Rescue of Innocents")

                        For the end, here's the epigraph of a five-year old Roman slave child. Older than a baby, admittedly, but still young enough to be a burden and have a high chance of death. Apparently the Latin really conveys the owner's (not even her real father!) sadness.
                        Here I commend to you, Fronto and Flaccilla,
                        your daughter, my joy and my delight,
                        Let young Erotion not be terrified by the black shades
                        and the gaping mouth of the Tartarian dog.
                        She would have completed six cold winters,
                        had she only lived but six more days.
                        Let her play happily between her aged parents,
                        and chatter my name with her lisping voice.
                        Let the turf covering her bones be soft and not hard,
                        and do not weigh heavily on that girl, Mother Earth,
                        For she was not heavy on you.


                        Tldr; people knew to expect high infant mortality, and authorities often told people to just buck up, but individuals were often greatly saddened by the early deaths of their children. Sure, some dumped them, but that doesn't mean most people weren't upset by it (and of course, people who dumped them were still often upset, even if they felt it was necessary; how many women today who give up babies for adoption feel entirely happy about it?).

                                    So, anyhow.

                                    What is Raksi's purpose in the setting?

                                    In one sense, she's the stereotype that the Realm points to; Anathema are bloodthirsty monsters, who literally eat babies.

                                    So she's important in allowing the Realm's system of oppression a bit more complex; when they say the Anathema are evil, it's not total rubbish.

                                    But villains are boring when they're utterly one-dimensional (and "she's evil because she's crazy!" is pretty one-dimensional); and they're also unbelievable. Most tyrants have support from at least some of the populace. I remember talking to a Syrian refugee (a guy who wouldn't go back to Syria because he'd be press-ganged into the army) who felt that Western media is very biased against Assad. Of course, Assad does bad things... but he protects people from ISIS, Al-Nusra, etc.

                                    Raksi controls a large territory, with many loyal tribes, human and beastmen. If they obeyed her purely out of fear, it might come off as a bit unrealistic, and of course it would be far too easy for DB PCs to foment an uprising.
                                    Even villains need true believers.

                                    So Raksi needs to be not 100% evil. So why do people support her?
                                    Originally posted by Isator Levi
                                    It puts me in mind of some interesting parallels; a person who, even going past all of the propaganda, probably did some really grotesque things, which has not detracted from an enduring image of being a national hero.

                                    It raises the question of whether a person who impales several thousand individuals (enemy soldiers, that) to deter possible invaders is doing it because they're cruel, or because it's seen as an effective deterrent under circumstances of limited resources.
                                    I think this is the most likely. Raksi makes her tribes strong, she defends them against their enemies, including the Realm (operating, I guess, from Greyfalls), which considers them heathens deserving of nothing but slavery, or Lookshy who considers them dangerous barbarians who threaten the Confederation of Rivers.

                                    Yes, they have to sacrifice a few babies to their terrifying goddess (though I wonder if many of the babies are stolen from other tribes), and that's painful, but it has to be done for the greater good. And no doubt their society has been influenced and structured over generations to reinforce this "honour" and "harsh necessity".

                                    I mentioned that's one place for her...

                                    But then, the Core book has an initiation for Solars where they learn dark magicks from a demon (and not even a simply alien demon, but a soul-sucking, doom-loving demon), and this is basically the Lunar equivalent. If you don't want to play a sorcerer who made dark pacts for their powers, talk to a nice ghost at sunset.

                                                        I personally read Raksi's ideology as being less focused on self-determination and more on a sense that holding all of Creation under a large, interconnected system makes it too vulnerable to large-scale collapse from something going wrong in a given sector, and she argues that greater provincial independence allows the system as a whole to be more robust against breakdowns or invasions of any given sector.

                                                        Mostly because I want there to be somebody in the setting who believes that. I'm not sure if there are any stray details in her associated write-ups that might contradict that.

                                                        One implication I think it would have would be Raksi being somewhat less concerned with their freedom than their responsibilities as she judges them; she wants them to govern independently from one another, but she also wants them to govern. Not necessarily confined indefinitely to their own principalities, but still reprimanded if they skip out on them.
