    Originally posted by JohnDoe244 View Post
    Immaculate Philosophy. (The Realm, page 86.)
    This might look like nit-picking, but the different terms refer to different versions of the religious practice. The Immaculate Philosophy is practiced in the Realm and its satrapies, while the Immaculate Faith (a more personal, less evangelical variant) is practiced in Lookshy. Other variants include the Intou Heresy (Immaculate beliefs + ancestor worship) and Prasad's Pure Way (Immaculate beliefs + Dragon-Blooded as physical gods worthy of direct worship).

                I suggest approaching the Realm's level of control over the people as a spectrum between tight supervision that rapidly arbitrates all disputes and curtails all deviations in the interests of uniformity and harmony and places that are practically the Wild West and of virtually no interest to government or Dynasty so long as their internal disputes don't spill out and they pay a reasonable amount of their taxes. It's a big country with a varied environment to provide different levels of access to those who enforce public order.

                Originally posted by Chausse
                Dynasts are forbidden to govern themselves, but I believe each Satrapies belong to a specific House ? Can't we say to an extent that the DB responsible in the House in charge of the Satrapy effectively govern it or am I missing something ?
                It is the privilege and duty of the House who is granted the satrapy's lease to ensure that it makes the tribute payments (of which it gets to take a cut) and otherwise remains amenable to the commercial and military interests of the Realm. The purpose of the satrap is to engage with the existing government to keep them informed of Realm wishes and lean on them to comply, to observe the populace for signs of dissent, and to manage the garrison (those troops also provided by the leasing House) for purposes of defence and occasionally directing towards problem elements if the troops of the local magnate prove insufficient to the task. In all other respects, the satrapy governs itself.

                    Another cool thing about the Realm: they have Judge Dee-esque Magistrates empowered to investigate crimes and enforce justice. The Magistrates were all appointed personally by the Empress, and many were outcastes, or convicts offered pardons if they took the job. As a result, Magistrates are a varied bunch of legally-sanctioned Dragon-Blooded vigilantes who are primarily loyal to the Empress. They assemble teams of useful mortals called Archons to help with their investigations and tip the Magistrates off to crimes that need investigating. Of course, with the Empress gone, nobody's choosing new Magistrates, and the remaining Magistrates don't have the Empress' protection any more.

                    I don't remember if this was specifically in the book, but knowing how the Empress does things, I'm sure that she chose many Magistrates based partially on who they'd most inconvenience. A disowned and resentful member of a troublesome family is extremely useful for keeping that family in check, for instance.

                                +1 on this. Magistrates are great, they offer tonnes of story hooks and slot into so many different types of games.

                                Think the passage Dex is referencing is this one from p.45 in The Realm:

                                Every magistrate is Dragon-Blooded. The Empress often chose influential rebels and heretics, relatively harmless criminals, and other social or political outcasts as magistrates — competent but disgraced Exalted who’d fallen victim to Dynastic machinations, or become scapegoats for grievous failures or personal transgressions against families and rivals. Primarily, the Empress required her magistrates to be savvy, tenacious, talented, concerned with justice, and dependent entirely on her favor for redemption.

                                --

                                Something I really, really like about the Realm in 3e is House Peleps’ ambitions for establishing the Imperial Western Protectorate. Essentially, their response to the Realm Civil War is likely to be forsaking the Blessed Isle entirely, striking out on their own and establishing a new empire in the West.

                                It just makes so much sense, in a world that saw Clans Burano and Ophris set a precedent with Prasad and given the fact that supporting the Imperial Navy is INSANELY costly, so much so the House is basically at a ‘go big or go home’ tipping point. It’s a daring move, one that might fail but one only they are in position to make (I’ve never quite understood why other Houses would be ok with Peleps having the manpower – marines especially – that the navy would require, but that’s by the by…) the type of high stakes gamble that screams story options and daring-do.

                                The fact it’s an ambition that is largely being held in check by loyalty and patriotism at the highest level just adds another great wrinkle (this on Peleps Lai, head of the Admiralty Board, from page 58 of WFHW, sums it up nicely: She still believes the Empress will return, and won’t entertain the notion of another monarch until convinced otherwise — and while she’s not as spry as she once was, her martial skills remain sufficient to trounce the occasional upstart who’d defy her. She supports the notion of a Western Empire, though, and dreams of personally presenting this new conquest to the Empress.)

                                The whole idea is further explored awesomely in Heirs.

                                I could waffle on about the way 3e has enriched the other Great Houses. It's great stuff.

                                            On reflection (if of interest to anyone) the Magistrates seem to stand a bit between A) the Chinese story tradition of morally "upright" heroic magistrate-detectives who investigate crimes and its actual historical experience (county magistrates appointed for short terms outside of their home region), and B) the more Western genre tradition of private investigators, "hardboiled PIs" or "gentleman detective", who are portrayed as less completely admirable - often eccentric and "picaresque" if not almost anti-heroic - and stand outside of the state and government to a degree. It's an interesting combination of styles!

                                            (A somewhat similar-in-spirit literary twist is Barry Hughart's Master Li, though that hews a bit more to the private detective template, possibly because Hughart appeared to have some kind of skepticism towards the Confucian social structure and officialdom).

                                                I'm curious myself as to why that particular aspect of the Realm military structure was originally written like that. Mind, I'm suddenly wondering if it might reflect on some oddities in the organisation of ancient world navies, of which I know little; the most solid point that I have is recollections of Sextus Pompey being able to hold out in Sicily against the Second Triumvirate for a long time on the basis of his independently powerful navy.

                                                If justifying character reasonings in the context of Third Edition, I'd say Peleps controlling the navy has long been acceptable on the basis of them being one of the oldest Houses, and that it's part and parcel with the sterling reputation they've cultivated. I also imagine that it helps for a navy to end up being a bit less powerful in their world than it is in ours, because Creation has a lot less coastline. And everybody else then gets to shirk the need to pay for the navy's upkeep.

                                                I'm pretty sure that marines are the kinds of troops that are well drilled for boarding and landing actions and not of much use for anything else. If we're trying to imagine how the setting works logically, I feel as though even the best marines are of little effect against a basically drilled land army.

                                                Suddenly I'm thinking about the First Punic War, of which I can recall little of the not much I ever examined in detail, seeing as it lacked the flashy features brought by Hannibal and Scipio. But I think it stands out as an example in which powerful navy versus the best land troops of the era was one that ultimately went to the latter. The Carthaginian navy could dominate the Mediterranean but couldn't inflict much grief on the rich heartlands of Italia, and in the end it was easier for Rome to catch up on naval capabilities than it was for their enemies to develop troops that could stand against legions.
