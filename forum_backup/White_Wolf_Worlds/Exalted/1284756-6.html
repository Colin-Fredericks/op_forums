            If it's just personal stuff then I can take a crack at it
            Pros:
            Beefed up charm trees: Okay let me get this out of the way with, I'm a freak and an outlier, I fully acknowledge this, and I would unironically leap to play an edition of exalted where every single ability had fifty charms. What drew me to exalted in the first place was the breadth of the mechanics, that in theory each ability had just as much investment opportunity as the next. And what kept me from ever really getting into either of the previous editions was the fact that that wasn't really true. The combat abilities had big meaty charm sets and everything else usually hovered at six or so. The fact that I can actually invest my character into being a spy or a sailing merchant or a scholar, rather then just a swordsman who also does some of those things on the side, is probably the single largest thing I like about the game and the reason it's still my favorite. I fully realize I'm at odds with a lot of other peoples perceptions of the game, but hey there it is

            Social system: Third has the best social system I've seen in just about anything. It manages to balance the problem most other games run into of either being to ineffectual to be worth it, to powerful, and still managing to at least somehow model how real life humans interact.

            Evocations: Another place I get to wallow in my crunchy mess, evocations fit the ideal of epic mythology much better, where each weapon is it's own unique existence that differs from all other. Far harder to write sure but as stated above, I'm the type of weirdo who likes that.

            Cons:
            The core book is cramped: It's no secret that there was some troubled history going on behind the core book. This compounds the fact that the core needed so much inside it already, setting info, the entirety of the beefed up base systems, an entire charm set, a collection of antagonists, all the item rules with a couple sample evocation sets, and multiple martial arts trees. They just didn't have room for everything, and some of the Troubles made things even more cramped. You can see frayed edges in a few places, incomplete mechanics around a couple of spots, so on.

            Schedule: Much related to the first one, these Troubles have kept things from ever hitting a solid pace, but the point I'm making here is more then just having to wait. Yeah that's a bummer no doubt, but I'm talking things beside that. The slow crawl has done noticeable things to the general mode surrounding the game, and means most of the material we have has already been walked all over by the time new things start to come out. Hopefully part of this gets alleviated when the ball actually gets rolling, but it won't change that the core got isolated on it's own for entire years counting the leaks and picked to pieces before we had anything else to pay attention to.

                            Originally posted by wastevens View Post

                            Could you elaborate on these two ideas a bit? Cause, uh... I don't understand why they strain credulity, as the former is historically accurate (in aggregate, there are exceptions, queue the Mongoltage, etcetc), and the later is both fundamentally true and seems immaterial when depiciting the consequences of imperial activity in a bronze/iron age setting.
                            Long tangent incoming:

                            Re; first point, the former depends on what we're defining as vastly different, vastly lower levels of wealth. My definition tends to be that a vast difference in wealth is something like the "developed" and "developing" world in 1960 or 1890, and I tend to think that's something like modern people's go to for the scales implied by that kind of phrasing.

                            But the economic argument is that it's never been possible to extract from poor countries to achieve that kind of gap, because there isn't enough money to transfer, and the logistics of doing so are impossible. Assume if country A is at $600 GDP and B is at $700 GDP, equal population size, then even transferring 50% of income from A to B is only going to put A at $1000 GDP. And of course transfers were waaaaay less than 50% in any case, very much lower; not every one in two, probably far less than every one in ten - most people are subsistence farmers after all (not people with much available wealth to spare in any form). Assuming equal population size, but doesn't change too much if population size is non-equal, particularly for a demographic juggernaut like the Realm (even in 3e's bigger world, I believe it still is?).

                            (E.g. Britain in India, where there is a smaller imperial power vs larger colonised territory, extracted tax rate about 7.5% of national income - https://tinyurl.com/yaewytjo. Most of which they would've had to spend in the country, on armies, administration, some nominal public works, etc.)

                            Look at the Inequality Possibility Frontier for practice - https://www.gc.cuny.edu/CUNY_GC/medi...13/WPS6449.pdf. Part of that argument is also that large extraction did take place, it would push many people below subsistence.

                            Even Spanish and Portuguese colonization of the Americas, probably an extreme of extraction, barely or did not at all push their national incomes up. There's no change in GDP/capita estimates - https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/m...ry=FRA+ESP+PRT

                            Some idea of what was possible for Rome is here: https://brilliantmaps.com/roman-empire-gdp/, and even then the lion's share of the difference is driven by the poor nature of the northern provinces (which is in turn primarily due to lower trade and stock of techniques and knowledge, and environment constraints).

                            In terms of the "wealth of nations" for empires, figuratively, through most of history, you're largely talking about two desperately poor beggars fighting in an alley, and one maybe gets a few pennies from the other, but they remain desperately poor. Or a rich thug rifling through the pockets of a poor man, but he's rich regardless of doing so. Rather than anyone getting rich from stealing. In a narrower scale, a few individuals can get rich and have marvelous estates, even then though they're often not the same ones bearing the cost of the empire, so looking at those in isolation in the context of the general poverty around them overstates things, and that nothing much has happened to the national income.

                            Of course, these relatively small amounts can matter to *them*! And it is still a sense some vast cultural outrage when treasures are dragged off to adorn a imperial administrator's house.

                            Re; second point, consensus usually is that the transition to modern economic growth in late 1700s and then 1800s both explains income gaps today and has nothing much to do with taxation / loot / plantations in colonies and empires. Rather changes in practices rooted in legal, economic, scientific changes that don't really have much to do with them.

                            Some econ historians allow some small measure of savings from colonial enterprises contributed to investment, but my impression is that this is mostly a sop, and that the most rigorous writers emphasise that growth in industries (seen across all industries and driving change) was mostly driven by savings driven investment and invention and shifting labour and had nothing really to do with colonial profits.

                            But that's all I'll do here, as that's an element that you can just search and pretty much any result on economic history on economic growth will tell you, so not much point me trying to reproduce here, risking getting some small element of the argument wrong (and then "de-convincing" anyone reading who wants to believe the opposite and is ill disposed to the way I've phrased things and their feel of any subtext they might think they find.).

                            I agree it shouldn't have much to do with the setting writing! I'm reacting a bit to an old dialogue about an impression I got that early 3e team wanted to use the Realm to say something about imperialism in the recent past and legacies in world today. But I don't get this impression from the current team, so I'm probably at risk of shading from useful (or at least thread relevant) points into a useless sort of "Ghosthead shouts at things that people who aren't even here now said, years ago, that probably have no relevance today, and that no one's talking about ITT!" here to go any further.

                            DrLoveMonkey, you're probably right about it being more of a player dependent element and I'm risking a bit straying into "Somebody, somewhere, not even in this thread, let alone in the books, is *wrong* on (or off) the internet" territory here.
