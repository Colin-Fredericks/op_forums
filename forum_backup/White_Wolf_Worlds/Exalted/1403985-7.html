    A few weeks ago, I came upon this blog called A Collection of Unmitigated Pedantry. It's this history professor who gives his own take on things related to pop culture depictions of ancient and Medieval history, both in terms of specific examples and general abstractions. It's pretty good, very detailed but not impenetrably so, and it often strikes a fair balance between pointing out what fiction gets wrong (and right) and acknowledgement that fiction has its own necessities.

    Probably his landmark thing are these multi-entry breakdowns of the siege of Minas Tirith and battle of the Hornburg in Lord of the Rings (including comparisons between their depictions in book and film). I also rather like his take down of myths for the Spartans. He's recently putting up the first in a series of entries about the historical manufacture of bread.

    It's a pretty good blog, I recommend it.

    Anyway, right now my perspective on how people might regard an oath underlying their induction as a deathknight is in his article about the composition and connotations of Medieval oaths.
    Collections: Oaths! How do they Work?
    https://acoup.blog/2019/06/28/collections-oaths-how-do-they-work/
    By popular request, today we’re going to talk about oaths. Oaths appear a lot in fantasy fiction – and even in historical fiction – and they are frequently done wrong. I remarked …


    It compels me to... interrogate certain perspectives that might seem to draw a distinction between what is real and what is ritual hokum in a manner that might be a tad too modern revisionist. It reminds me of when people lean on arguments about the Crusades that try to find more pragmatic, materialist reasons for why they happen, sceptical of the idea that people really would travel hundreds of miles and engage in warfare at great expense primarily for religious convictions.

    I have approximate knowledge of many things.
    Write up as I play Xenoblade Chronicles.

            Everything in that article is accurate, but I'm strongly influenced here by the works of James C. Scott and his take on peasant ethics and their position on their place in the social order, which is, uh, complex, but which for the purpose of this post I guess I'm going to sum up as "They take it as valid while also recognizing how bullshit it is enough to confabulate reasons for why they ought to be able to violate it when they need to, or why certain social obliations don't count when they become inconvenient for them personally or collectively." I also think things like priesthoods and knightly orders self-select for a degree of ideological devotion, but Deathlords looking for Deathknight candidates don't have that luxury, because they have to look for hypothetical Solar Exaltation candidates on the verge of death, which are going to be more evenly distributed across the population and thus you're going to get less of the sort of people who self select for going on crusade and more for people who subscribe to peasant ethics, which make up a substantially greater percentage of the population.

            I think you're probably going to get a lot of True Believers among the ranks of the Deathknights, but it's less about fully pledging themselves to the cause of the Neverborn at the moment of Exaltation as some sort of original sin you can judge Abyssal Exalted by collectively, and more the effects of subsequent training and indoctrination once they're taken under their patrons' tutelage.

            (Deathlords'll totes use "You were damned to kill the world the moment you took your Second Breath" as a talking point during that indoctrination, tho!)

                    The discussion has somewhat moved on, but in regard to the 3E form of elemental summoning: In Jainism, minute ensouled beings are constantly taking birth and dying. When you so much as strike a match you are bringing a conscious being into the world who will live and die for only a few seconds. While in Jain theology such a being has and will eventually be a human, Exalted isn't quite that inclusive in the cycle of souls. Still, it is relatively easy to construct an internally consistent moral position that conforms with both real world and in-setting morals which permits creating a being for the express purpose of performing a service for you as part of its cycle of spiritual growth.

                    Originally posted by Isator Levi View Post
                    Is the Abyssal bound by their oath? I think that needs to be considered in terms of a world where we might assume that people on the whole take oaths a bit more seriously. Christianity caused those features to atrophy because the guarantor was always God and the punishment was always eternal damnation. That which was theologically obvious could be left unstated.
