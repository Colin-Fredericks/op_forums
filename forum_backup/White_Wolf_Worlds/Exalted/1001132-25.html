        Can we try to knock down some of the more structurally unsound parts of the walls with catapults or sappers? If we can open one or more good entrances into Dun Nashuon, that would be preferable to trying to climb the walls.

        Overwhelmingly most of the walls are ruined, being little more than piles of cobbles and mounds of earth at this point. Masanori was attempting to rebuild them, but he only really managed to set up palisades on the most important bits: the areas around the gate, and where the slope is gentlest, which would be the western side of the fortress. The eastern and northern flanks are defended by the steeper slopes of the hill, meaning they need 'less' defenses, and he could afford to pay less attention to. He did, however, pile things like thornbushes and the like on top of the cobbles to form an impromptu defense, but it isn't particularly impressive or effective.

        The western side of the fortress, which is where the gentlest slope of the hill and the approach to the gate are located, is also where the most earthworks are located. They are, basically, berms and trenches in the side of the hill, designed to slow down attacking forces. There are fewer, even no, earthworks on the south, east, and north sides of the fortress, just the remains of the drystone wall and Masanori's palisade.

        Finally, Dun Nashuon isn't a castle in the Vanehani tradition. This is more along the lines of what it currently looks like. And, even in it's heyday, when it was used as the seat of a Tribal chief-kingdom, it was more of an oppidum (the diorama of the earthen wall is a very nice representation).

        Dun Nashuon's main defense, throughout its entire existence, was the fact that it was located on top of a hill, with uncontested fields of fire surrounding it. And that last bit is severely diminished in the present day. All the forests that are visible on the map were not present when Dun Nashuon was occupied by the Tribes, and would not be allowed to grow that close. Almost all of the land on the map, plus more off the edges, was farmland 700 years ago. If Kasai decides to move troops through the woods, they are going to trip over old stone walls.

        Said forests will provide excellent cover, both for troops and against missile fire.

        Finally..... sappers wouldn't really work, since Dun Nashuon is built on top of a hill. Any attempts to undermine the walls would lead the sappers right into the barrows under the fortress, and they would have a Bad Time.

        Also finally, the Hundred Kingdoms doesn't have a strongly-developed tradition of siege-engine-craft. Most fortifications are built on top of hills/rocky outcrops/mountains, and have walls made of sloping earthworks faced with stone, surmounted by wooden palisades covered in plaster. Siege engines won't really knock them down, and even if they did, the palisades are easy to repair.

        Don't get me wrong, there are catapults and the like in the Hundred Kingdoms, but they are small and mostly used as anti-personnel weapons as opposed to anti-fortification. Most castles are taken through starvation, disease, direct assault, or by fire. Almost every castle in the Hundred Kingdoms is built of wood covered in plaster/wattle-and-daub, and fire is always a fear.

        On top of that, Kasai doesn't have any siege-engines with him. There weren't any just laying around in Jianghu, and he doesn't have the engineering knowledge to build any.

                                            The bandits as a whole are generally not going to attack us. They might be disorganized, but that doesn't mean they are stupid. Even the slowest of dullards is capable of recognizing the defensive benefits of Dun Nashuon, and conversely, the likely effects of leaving it.

                                            1) The bandits, in general, are not actually that poorly trained. A decent proportion of them are actually deserted soldiers, and the bandits that weren't deserted soldiers learned quick, aka "swim or die"-type thing. In-game, a bandit battlegroup will have Poor drill not because, well, they are poorly drilled, but because they are not used to fighting in groups of any appreciable size and/or (usually 'and') lack effective means of coordination and communication between groups.

                                            For example: Kasai's army (and the overwhelming majority of the Hundred Kingdoms military) has systems like semaphore, bugle/drum/trumpet calls, or runners, meaning communication and the passing-along of orders can be, for the time and technological period, pretty damn fast and effective. Overwhelmingly most bandit groups don't have them, either because they work in small enough groups so they would be unnecessary, or they lack trained personnel.

                                            2) They are disorganized, and this is the main 'benefit' that "the law" (aka Kasai's army, or organized forces in general) have when fighting bandits.

                                            Even the freshest of Spear-Regiments is going to have an Oathsworn commander, and if a regiment is worth it's salt (and most are, outside of freshly-formed regiments), they will generally have a decent-to-damn-good sergeant/subofficer corps, made up of older, more experienced veterans. The Oathsworn commander is a semi-professional, and the sergeants/sub-officers in the regiment allow them to keep discipline in the ranks pretty well.

                                            Generally, Oathsworn officers are going to be throwing out (2 [War] + 2 [Charisma/Appearance] + 2 [sergeant/sub-officer bonus] + X [ Army commander Command roll successes] + X [Miscellaneous stunts, like having a battle-plan drawn up, etc]) dice on a command roll.

                                            It helps that Hundred-Kingdoms forces almost always have a single commander. In this case, it would be Kasai. The bandits .... do not. (If Masanori was still there, it would have been him, which would be terrifying.) Kasai can spread his Command Roll successes to effectively every unit in the army, while the bandits have 3,4,5 dudes jockeying for supremacy, and no Command Roll successes.

                                            On top of that, Kasai is going to come up with a plan-of-battle for the attack (ghostwritten and proofread by the good Sgt Maj Oxblood of course), which in game terms will give his forces a +2 dice stunt bonus to their rolls, so long as they follow the plan (and things go to plan, of course). The bandits plan is "don't die, you shits".

                                            Overwhelmingly-most bandit-groups stay under 100, or even 50 fighters in size. Logistics plays a part in that, but another part is ....... uh, "comparing dicks", to be crude. Chances are if you are a bandit and outlaw, you didn't play well with authority to begin with, and even if you were, living among others with issues-with-authority tends to rub off. Leaders of bandit-groups tend to rise either through coercion ('follow me or I'll kill you') or cooperation ('follow me and you can get some sweet shit!'), and they tend to jockey for position a lot. They spend as much time consolidating power within their own crews as they do between other crews. Generally, if a bandit-group gets too large, it will split (usually violently) into several smaller groups under their own leaders.

                                            Of course, the fact that Masanori managed to wrangle about 1500 individuals with authority-issues to work together, for a given sense of the term, means he is either terrifyingly charismatic, terrifying good at intimidating people, or terrifyingly good at leading forces in battle (the answer is "all of the above".)

                                            I'll be straight; I'm glad you folks decided to recruit him. This fight would have been much harder if you didn't. Probably harder than the Lunar fight, if you can compare massed-combat to individual. Now, it is going to be ........ easier.
