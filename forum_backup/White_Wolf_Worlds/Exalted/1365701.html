    How do you play and handle the Wyld ?

    02-06-2020, 08:46 AM
    So I've done a bit of Wyld in my campaign, and I had some trouble going in depth on the following questions :

    - What is it like to make players play in the Bordermarches, and in the deep Wyld ? In the Bordermarches I will usually depict Creation with a bit of a twist, but I'd appreciate some share thoughts. For the Deep Wyld, except by depicting the world like, from my last campaign example, a gigantic world with no skies nor ground, where titanesque creature travel all around wearing fair folk people on their back, I had no fucking clue, and it felt like I was going too far, the players had difficulty to relate. (For inspiration of what I'm trying to say, take "A Story About My Uncle", during the middle level in the open)

    - I'm rather ok with depicting Raksha to make them cool, but I feel like I'm lacking depth about them. What are they ? One of my first Fire Raksha was trying to build a new kingdom in a desert in creation because his dynasty was disowned in the Wyld, so he would conquer a part of creation and raise armies against the stealer of his throne. Another was a benevolent Wood Fey healing and helping injured people after a war and drought by giving them mutations to help them survive, rendering somewhat aliens for all outsiders. I had a core concept but I was lacking what they truly desired, I felt.

    - I have stronger problems with "smaller" fees, goblins and the like, I just don't know what to say about them. I don't want to fall into the DDesque "well they monsters so they monster stuff all day" I have no idea on how to depict them, and what they are essentially. Do they have real personalities ? If yes, how are they different the humans of creation, except by their looks.

    - What are the main and knows interactions of the Wyld and Creation ? I've seen a lot of Charm to protect from the influence of the Wyld, but I have no idea how to actually handle the influence of the Wyld. I tried giving mutations to players failing Intergity checks but it felt way too permanent for what was happening. Should the influence just be temporary changes, like a Marauder can do in Mage when he passes somewhere and will slowly change his surrounding to correspond to his vision of reality from Mage ? Have you had any simple systems and ideas too make a challenge about "the Wyld is doing stuff" that felt somewhat challenging or interesting for the players to interact with ? All my tries either were insignificant or too strong, and felt sort of unreal.

      I can’t say for sure how you should do it, but let me tell you about the last two times I involved Fair Folk/ the Wyld in my games…

      Okeanos Excerpt
      One game session of my Okeanos game had Barbus the Storm King venture into the Bordermarches to meet with the goddess Shepolpa, who sends storms carrying the Thousand Hungry Wings when she is wroth. The colors of the waters were more intense, and the lapping of the waves against his ship's hull had more of a rhythm to it. Sometimes his reflection was slightly out of sync with his motions.

      However, after he crossed into Middlemarches, he made the mistake of falling asleep, and sank into an enchanted slumber; his reflection, which had followed him into the Wyld, truly came to life, believing itself to be the real Barbus. (His player would go on to play Reflection Barbus, since RPing sleep is boring.) Reflection Barbus went into Shepolpa's domain in the Storm King's stead, and surprisingly made a pretty solid bargain.

      Reflection Barbus started the journey back to Okeanos, but as he sailed he felt himself getting more and more tired. He slept without dreaming, and when he awoke he was just as exhausted as when he'd started. (As he drew further away from the Wyld, his chaotic energies were ebbing.) He got into a tense fight with some critters, only to find out that his excellence had dimmed. (Reflection Barbus, being only a reflection, had no Charms.) More surprisingly, when a monster's teeth pierced his skin, he bled seawater. Realizing something was up, he sought out an agent of the Ocean Father in Creation, who told him that he'd become a thing of the Wyld. The Ocean Father agreed to purge Barbus of the Wyld taint, and proceeded to dissolve Reflection Barbus to death.

      At least the shock of it woke the real Barbus, who had been taken to the Strand of Sleepers in the Middlemarches. He found himself deep underwater, inside one of a long string of bubbles - like a titanic pearl necklace - extending as far up and as far down as he could see. The ones below him were occupied by people, sleeping or dead. Barbus carefully popped his own bubble, then seized that of the sleeper below him, and towed the entire strand to land.

      From there he fought some shark-headed goblins, and met a masked woman whose tribe were nomads of the Wyld; he observed that she had a masked cataphract servant, and when he asked her about him, she confided that she'd won his heart in a game. They bargained over transport into Creation for himself and the sleepers.

      During a stopover on another island for supplies, Barbus discovered curious hooved mammals, the likes of which he had never seen before - great wooly beasts with horns, bleating menacingly. He decided to hunt one for meat, only to find himself in the midst of an ambush by three more, intent on killing and eating him. (I used the stats for tigers.) He proceeded to trap the monsters in a massive cave; he rolled a boulder in front of the entrance, took his kill, and set sail again.

      On the way back, the masked woman observed that Barbus did not cast a reflection in the water, so she sewed a new one for him out of scraps of what seemed to be cloth, then had him stand over the water and throw it into the sea.

      Barbus did return to Creation after that, but not before agreeing to come and see the masked woman again. He was also pleased to learn that when the sleepers returned to Creation, the magic keeping them asleep was broken. It was a bit of a shock to the sleepers, mind, nor were they happy to see the masked pair at the helm of the ship.

      "Aaaaahh, Fair Folk!" some bawled.

      "Relax," the Storm King replied, "she's got him under her control. He won't do anything to you."

      "But what about her?" asked one.

      "Oh, she's just great, isn't she?" the Storm King said with a grin.


      Lunars Excerpt
      Our PCs left Chiaroscuro along one of the glass roads, and after some extended travel met a collection of Delzhan refugees. The refugees explained they were fleeing the predations of the Lion Folk; too many of their number had been lured to their dooms in the night, and tormented with nightmares of the hunt. The refugees warned them to not call themselves by their names in Lion Folk territory, lest they be lured outside to be hunted by their wicked magic. The PCs, in turn, alerted the refugees that there were jobs to be had on the docks at Chiaroscuro.

      They set out to the village the refugees had abandoned, where they discovered that a couple dozen half-starved Delzhan still cowered in terror in the otherwise-empty homes. They tried to flee the Lunar pack, but the PCs subdued them with tranquilizing drugs and ordered their retinue of elemental servants to care for them while the PCs set out to track down the Lion Folk.

      They discovered the tracks of lions and leonine men at the edges of the village, and began to follow them; shortly after, they came across a pair of tall, leonine beings with powerful muscles and wicked claws.

      These two Lion Folk, brother and sister, were lean and weary from hunger, and had followed their kin’s tracks to see if there were any fearful dreams for them to scavenge. The PCs explained that the villagers had fled at the predations of some Lion Folk much like themselves, and that those who remained were probably insufficient to slake their hunger. They asked the Lion Folk why they were ranging so far; the Lion Folk answered that they had been driven out of their homes, a cave on a savannah of glowing grasses, by a Solar when he threw down their leader in a duel. The hunger of their pride had formerly been mostly met by the latent energies of the demesne, but without it, they needed to hunt much more frequently. These two had not submitted to the leadership of the pride’s new head, Red Hands, so they were cast out.

      The PCs proposed journeying to the demesne, and asked the two Lion Folk to come with them. They discovered that the Solar was nowhere to be found, but that someone was still coming here regularly. They laid an ambush for the occupant’s return, only to find that it was Red Hands himself!

      Three Lunars and two Fair Folk were more than a match for one Fair Folk; they beat the shit out of him, and made him explain what he was doing there. Red Hands explained that the Solar didn’t actually take the demesne for himself; sure, he defeated their old leader, but he had no interest in keeping the demesne - he just wanted to avenge his sibling. But Red Hands found that if the pride thought they’d been driven out, he could harvest all the demesne’s energy himself, and use it to create a glamour army to expand the pride’s influence.

      The brother and sister forced him to step down, and took his place as the head of the pride; and thanked the PCs for their part in getting their home back.

          Possible consequences for failed integrity checks that are more interesting than mutating your PCs in no particular order.

          For context-
          I like the wyld as an existential threat, delight it putting the shits up my players and in my old age have started to push back against Raksha = fourth wall hijinks.

          -Minor intimacies (along with some corresponding memories) could be swapped between characters.
          -Food they're carrying rapidly spoils to a disgusting degree or does something so unnatural people go "I don't want to eat this" like a piece of dried and salted beef growing a horn.
          -Equipment and items of clothing could start to bond with people's skin in a way that needs to be cut away.
          -Any parasites the character might have could now be a serious problem.
          -Related to the above, did you know that a lot of seeds can safely pass through a human digestive tract and remain viable?
          -Carrying on this theme, Exalts aren't immune to diseases they're just rarely inconvenienced by them. But they're still there and viable to be mutated.
          -If everyone somehow fails an involuntary timeskip of hours or days, aside from that nothing else is wrong but your players don't need to know that.
          -As above, but someone/something is missing.

          Some areas of the wyld, particularly the bordermarches I see as being more 'stable' than others in that they follow the theme in how it mutates everything around it and if you already adhere to the theme it'll mostly leave you alone. For example a foresty area might have "trees" as an underlying theme, the locals might dye their hair green and wear clothes made from plant material to avoid the worst of it.

                              So my current story arc has involved a mighty journey from just north of Iscomay, to the Haslanti League (by wooden-fae-whale-living-submarine), and then (by Airship) across the great Northern Waste, into the Bordermarches, through the Middle Marches, and then to the Elemental Pole of Air (which itself shouldn't be very Wyld-y, really).

                              And before that, the game was set in a land very far north, beyond Iscomay, near the Northern Bordermarches.
                              (See pic here: http://forum.theonyxpath.com/forum/m...hapter-fiction)

                              So I thought a fair bit about how best to portray the Wyld, Raksha, etc...

                              And then I thought, well, what's the point of it? The point of the Wyld isn't that it's where Creation meets the Deep Wyld, per-se. It's kind of there to be where Raksha live, but obviously you can have Raksha live in Creation if you really want.

                              The point of the Wyld is that it's where your PCs can go on adventures in an utterly bizarre land that doesn't have to follow the rules of everyday reality. It's great for horror, it's great for wonder, it's great for putting in cool/stupid stuff.

                              I wanted to have Ice Giants who lived in a ridiculously large castle, so I did. I wanted to have a coven of hags who bathe in blood, live in a spiky black castle, and have armies of berzerk slave-soldiers and twisted barghests, so I did. I wanted the PCs to travel in a flying ship which honestly makes no sense for the technological skill of the setting, so I did (it's made of magic light steel, you see, "feathersteel" they call it). I wanted to have floating islands, so I did. I wanted to have a giant storm full of Storm Serpents, so I did. I wanted the PCs to have to sail through an asteroid field-esque area of giant islands crashing into each other, so I did (though as the PCs can't sail and so are employing a Dragonblooded captain to sail, it maybe didn't have much impact). I wanted them to fly beyond the edge of the world so that there's no land beneath, above, to the side, or in front of them, so I did. I wanted that void to have a huge floating ice castle, and a battle outside between skyships and a giant flying demon, so I did. And it's the Wyld, so it makes sense.
                              As long as it's themed towards the right element, great*.

                              I mean, I threw in random (Air-themed) bizarrity; owlmen, deer with two heads that split in half, various ice animals, a floating castle of Cloud People, old men made of wind whizzing about, etc. But really that's just background decoration.

                              *I would recommend using the elemental theme a lot, or a different theme as long as its distinct, because as Lioness said, it can just feel silly if it's chaotic. I mean, even Warhammer's Chaos Wastes actually come off as having a theme, even if the theme is "eeeevil+hooves+tentacles" + either blood+dogs+red+horns or tongues+crab claws+pink+intersex sexy sexiness or rot+disease+green+one eye or birds+blue+the kind of mutants you see in post-nuke 80s pulp sci-fi.

                                  In terms of mutations...
                                  Well, first of all, I ought to explain that I love permanent mutations. I played a Casteless Lunar in 2nd ed, and I'd frequently get mutations, quest them off in the Wyld but in doing so get more mutations, etc, etc. My DBT form had no connection with my totem form, it was a huge clawed rock-monster.

                                  Once my character's daughter bullied him into getting Tattoos. But later he sacrificed them for sorcery.

                                  Once I rolled 54 dice against myself for mutations. Gave myself permanent retrograde amnesia. It was great. Would have been more interesting if it had been a physical Abomination, but oh well.

                                  In Dark Heresy, etc, I love Corruption Points, with PCs eventually getting... mutations! Brilliant.

                                  I am, of course, aware that my players may not feel the same way....


                                  So, anyway. For the Wyld, I just based it on the 2nd ed rules, with some small changes, for example rolling Stamina+Integrity rather than Willpower+Essence (I think it makes more sense, and then PCs can use excellencies, etc).
                                  In the Bordermarches, once a month roll Stamina+Integrity vs Difficulty 1.
                                  In the Middle Marches, once a week roll Stamina+Integrity vs Difficulty 3.
                                  In the Deep Wyld, once a day roll Stamina+Integrity vs Difficulty 5.
                                  If you fail, you get Mutation points equal to the amount you failed by, (which obviously caps the level of mutation you get, Bordermarches are only going to be 1). Botch means you get two related ones (like Extra Limbs+Centaur, or Poisonous and Toxin Sacs).
                                  Mutations will be appropriate to the Element of the area a majority of the time.
                                  There's a 10% chance you get a Derangement instead. A Derangement that of course, has in no way to fit the way human psychology really works.

                                  Wearing cold iron amulets or something like might give you a bonus.

                                  So, when the PCs travelled in their ship for 3 weeks through the Bordermarches and 2 weeks through the Middle Marches, they had to roll twice at Difficulty 3.
                                  The Lunar PCs either passed, bounced it off their Silver Tattoos (I didn't roll a Derangement for anyone), or were protected by the Wyld Migration charm.
                                  The Dragonblood captain NPC passed.
                                  Of the two Solars on their way to kill Mela, Tarhunzas was fine, Indra got the ability to shoot lightning from her hands.
                                  The crew... well, one has owl eyes and webbed feet, one has a white lion's mane, one has blue hair and his skin exudes a kind of slime, one has white rabbit ears and speaks only in a growl, one is a polar-bear-centaur, and one is 7 foot tall (but weirdly proportioned) and made of living ice.
                                  The cold iron amulets that the No Moon made helped... but not enough.
                                  Luckily, the Full Moon and Changing Moon got tons of successes to convince them to stop freaking out from body horror and instead be proud of their awesome magic bodies.

                                                      I haven't actually brought the Wyld, per se, into my current 3e game, so this is all theoretical "how would I handle this". But, nevertheless:

                                                      Bordermarches are basically "Creation, but a little weird". They still have landscapes, animals, plants, and people, and so on. Fair Folk can live there indefinitely, though, without fearing calcification, and strangeness does happen - there might be a river that flows normally, but all the fish in it are furry, rather than scaly, for example. Or the animals in the river might be normal, but the river flows backwards, uphill. Things are still recognizable, but they're often a little strange. Also, things become a bit more tied to story and narrative - dramatic coincidences become a bit more likely, things become just a bit more likely to work out in ways that resemble standard stories, that kind of thing. Mutations in the Bordermarches aren't really a risk unless you spend an entire story, or at least the majority of it, within them.

                                                      Middlemarches are more strange, though they still tend to make some sense. This is where the majority of "true" Fair Folk actually live, in the sense of making their homes there. Most Middlemarches, as a matter of fact, are the domain of some Fair Folk noble or another, and they shape the region more than anything. To enter a raksha noble's territory is to enter their story, and that shapes everything. The Princess of Shadowed Lilies transforms her region of the Middlemarches into a dark forest with strange beasts, that leads all travellers into its heart to find her twisted tower of black glass and ivory, while the Cavalier of the Dust shapes the Wyld into a sea of sand where everyone either sails across it in desert boats and has swashbuckling adventures, or sinks and drowns. If an area of Middlemarches isn't claimed by a raksha, it's more crazy and chaotic, though usually in a semi-stable way - it might be a place where all rivers are made of blood, the animals are made of wood and the trees of meat, and the wind always sounds like a choir of children singing, but it will probably stay that way all the time you're there (such landscapes generally change after a story, but not during it). Narrative becomes even more powerful: in Fair Folk domains, the personal story of the ruler of the domain takes precedence, while in unclaimed areas, the Wyld is almost desperate to generate a story, and will latch on to whatever tale travellers seem to fit into. Mutations in the Middlemarches depend on where you are. in a Fair Folk domain, they're a matter of whether they fit the story. "Bit part" characters, beasts, and so forth will be mutated to fit the dominant narrative, or simply barred entry, while "main characters" will tend to be left alone to serve their role in the tale, unless being warped or twisted is part of that.

                                                      The Deep Wyld is madness, without fixed form or pattern. Few Fair Folk make their homes here, because even they find it difficult to shape the chaos. The Deep Wyld has no fixed form, and whatever shape it takes will shift. It takes new forms every scene, and these cannot be easily predicted (though there is usually an elemental "theme" to them, depending on how close they are to a given Elemental Pole). This deep in the Wyld, story still rules, but tales are no longer coherent. Each scene will start a new story, based on whatever travelers was doing at that moment, with little rhyme or reason. Mutations are common here, as the world tries to reshape everything along with it. There is a risk of mutation every scene, and sometimes more frequently, depending on exactly how chaotic this section of Deep Wyld is.


                                                      As for what raksha/Fair Folk actually "are", I do find the concept of "living stories" to be a useful one. Basically, every true Fair Folk is somehow central to some kind of story. Now, note that that may not be as a protagonist. A raksha noble might decide that it's the nemesis of one or all of the PC Circle, for example, and suddenly start showing up everywhere to bedevil and thwart them. It doesn't have a "real" reason for this, because a nemesis doesn't need that - it's just what a nemesis does. The "lesser" creatures of the Wyld, on the other hand, hobgoblins and such, are either very, very heavily mutated humans or animals, creatures that have been so warped by the Wyld that they can only survive there for long, where the rules of Creation don't totally apply, or else they're creations of Fair Folk nobles, in which case they're literally side characters. In the former case, they tend to have more realistic motivations - hunger, shelter, desire, etc. - but their mutations might transform those into some very, very strange things. A buck-ogre, for example, is probably hungry, and wants to eat human flesh when it gets hungry, then sleep in a warm place. Horrible, but understandable. In the latter case of creations of the Fair Folk, though, I don't think they really have much of an internal life at all. They aren't "people", they just exist to be their roles in a story. The hobgoblins in a raksha noble's domain don't have personalities, they're just there because a great noble Dark Lord should be served by an army of crawling minions, that's just how things work. They'll always be doing "Dark Lord's servants" stuff, and if you try to talk to one, it will have an extremely limited ability to converse.

                                                          Originally posted by Lanic View Post
                                                          What I was more interested in, is whether people think that what is shown on the map is all stable creation or whether if you go, say, far East of Scavenger Lands, the reality starts to be not so stable much before you reach that edge (e.g. the area around Lost Zarlath or even east to Rathess tends to already be quite bordermarch-y).
                                                          Personally, I tend to assume that any settlements notable enough to be marked on the map are within Creation proper. There might be Bordermarch areas near them, but they'll be in Creation. Ascension is the closest settlement to the border on the 3e large map, and it's conveniently close to the center of the map's east-west axis too, so I'd use that as the guideline for where the Wyld tends to start - about 500 miles away from the edge of the map, where it's nearest the Elemental Poles. At the corners, though, the borders might come in much further. I'd estimate that the borders could come in about 2000 miles from the corners of the 3e map and not really overlap any marked settlements, to be consistent with what I said earlier.
