                    I would tend to have an intuition on abolitionism, in a strict sense, and as a movement, as unlikely in the Realm, as this would require staking out a moral absolutist position that is at odds with how the Realm's religion works. As a structurally hierarchical form without radical commitments to too many further unambiguous, moral imperatives and religious laws (as opposed rather to interpretable aphorism and metaphor) which would constrict Dragonblooded and Sidereal freedom to act in whatever means they deem justify the ends.

                    Abolitionism in our world came from a combination of religious imperatives to treat all co-believers in egalitarian "brotherhood" and civic imperatives under the influence of popular democracy to establish universal rights of a citizen and human being within a territory. This is really happening most recently in the 18th century and overrunning into the 19th, but more generally in particular sorts of societies at particular sorts of times. Without this, slavery can become distasteful and economically impractical and so marginal (in New World slavery, there are frequent examples where slavery was about to or did become a marginal mode of labour). But it is hard to move from this to legal commitments that is should never happen, rather than commitments which moderate the practice if they speak on it at all (although usually only as social convention!). (Even in these religions that stress a brotherhood of the faith, these can even include measures of accommodation in religious law and orthodoxy which practically would prevent a rise of much of a belief in abolition, as in Islam).

                        I generally agree with you, but I could actually see the religious mandate of the Realm pushing some (not all) believes to oppose slavery, because they believe that slavers are not doing what they're supposed to; ie converting them.

                        I'm thinking of some of the stuff that happened with native American slaves in Spanish and Portugese colonies. The justification was that it was okay to enslave locals in order to teach them Christianity. But a lot of slavers weren't that fussed about teaching their slaves Christianity, partly because they, well, just didn't care, and couldn't be bothered, but also because that might mean in the future that their slaves were Christians and then they'd have to treat them better, or even let them go. Sometimes they even made it harder for their slaves to learn about Christianity.

                        Then you had things like how slavers would turn up at a village to enslave everyone, and the locals would say "Gloria Dei! Maria! Jesus!" and do the sign of the cross (because they'd learnt from other indigenous people that this was the way to not get enslaved). And sometimes it'd work, and sometimes slavers would just enslave them anyway, and pretend they didn't see any Christian stuff, which would really piss off the Jesuit who'd been through their last week and believed (truly or not) he'd converted the locals.

                        So, in the end, the Pope just decided the whole thing was a mess, and that it was actually getting in the way of converting America, so he declared that all slavery of the indigenous people of North+South America was forbidden. (So they enslaved Africans instead. There were other reasons, some more important, but that was one.)

                        Anyway, I can imagine some Monks deciding that Cynis has well and truly failed in their attempts to convert the slaves to the Immaculate Order, that slaving had interfered with missionaries' attempts to convert the Threshold (as the barbarians keep insisting that the Dynasty who kidnapped their relatives are not, in fact, the good guys), that slavery is out of place in the Perfected Hierarchy, and so the whole thing should be scrapped.
                        Not that I expect this to be a common view, but it could definitely be the view of some people.

We see slaves' uprisings, we see Christians, we see ecstatic cult from the east, this all through upper-class Roman lens, but we do not really see abolitionists in the sense discussed here. They were clearly biased in their presentation, but it definitely wasn't a common practice to just ignore important issues, the way XXth century propaganda did it. Instead, we see Marcus Aurelius, an emperor-philosopher who decided to choose ascetic live despite enormous wealth and heavily tried to replace gladiator fights with non-lethal sports out of ethical concern, but without a slight hint of abolitionism in his writings.

                                    However, the major bias in survival of ancient literature is that it was more likely for christian literature to be copied and this gives us the other end of the spectrum. Christianity was treated with contempt in most preserved Roman sources, int. al. as "religion of slaves and women." Christian literature brought to us some writings of people from lower classes, writings giving a glimpse into their lives, or a prison diary of an upper-middle-class woman Perpetua. In this literature, we can read much about slavery, but we still do not find slavery condemned as an institution. We can read that masters should be good to their slaves, that they shouldn't be cruel, that they shouldn't be unjust, that definitely one shouldn't watch gladiator's fights, that slaves are definitely people, but we also read that the fact that a slave and master both became Christians doesn't mean that the said slave shouldn't stay obedient to the master. Really, they lived in a completely different mindset.

                                    My point was mostly this: when reading about abolitionism in the Realm in this and other threads, I think that most people depicted abolitionism in such a distant culture as an obvious stance that any sane person would jump into. And yet, we can see that a whole millennia-spanning civilisation can never really think about it more deeply.
