    Historically, aristocracy has looked down on merchants pretty heavily. Since their wealth was classically tied to land incomes, aristocrats lived in an economic environment fundamentally different than that of a merchant, for one, that meant their income was usually fixed while merchant income fluctuated(this became a serious problem for them as trade became more profitable and land incomes depreciated in value). Participating in trade meant actually working to achieve a living, something aristocrats viewed as crass and lowbrow, since the expectation was that their own fief based incomes would be adequate to fund their lavish lifestyles. Merchants upset this worldview by being able to acquire enviable wealth by interacting with the working class, thus jeopardizing the aristocracy's sense of superiority. The way the two groups handled money was different too, merchants tended towards frugality(and displays of opulence were generally those that their funds could handle) while nobles were constantly socially encouraged to spend without regard for cost(worrying about costs being a thing for peasants). This had the effect of leaving many nobles drowning in debt (you can see this with the Great Houses still spending money on lavish parties and taking out compromising loans from House Ragara even when they're approaching civil war, most Dynasts just don't know how to dial back spending).

                            All right, time to get into this in earnest. First off, I’d say the quotation used to open the book is quite fitting and sets the appropriate mood; I’m sure that every single person who sees it will take its meaning and its essential truth to heart, and have no defensive arguments or nitpicks whatsoever.

                            That said, I do think there’s something worthwhile for the starting paragraph declaring that peasants of the Blessed Isle have typically had better lives than their fellows across the sea; it speaks to the security of the heart of the Realm, if nothing else. I think that the opening sets a good picture for how the Realm is so deeply rooted and the focus of so many agendas within and without that its downfall would not be a smooth transition. After Fangs, I like how Lunars are given a place of prominence here, at the same time as I find the phrasing for putting Solars last to make them not so much an afterthought as an unknown quantity with way too much potential to influence the situation.

                            Just going to skip over the chapter descriptions and have a glance at the lexicon… it’s interesting to me how barbarian is now inducted as formal terminology for the Realm, which also ties in nicely to Fangs. I’d been wondering how the terminology for social strata works now, so it’s interesting that citizen is a term encompassing peasant, patrician and Dynast together, rather than being its own middle level; I take this as final confirmation of my long supposition that peasant is not a term that applies even to, say, urban merchants and manufacturers, unless the implication is supposed to be that such people do not exist and those ranks are filled with patricians. Either possibility is curious to me, but I’m ready to let the book make its case.
                            Let’s see, other new terminology… martial orders and their oblates are new, though I have an inkling that they’re primarily concerned with the Caul. Nice to see the new significance of the Merchant Fleet reiterated. I had been wondering about attitudes towards the Old Realm for… reasons that I cannot recall, but that question seems reasonably resolved by a statement that a local term for the First Age is the Realm Before, so that’s fine. The rest is all familiar territory.

                            The inspirations seem about what one would expect, even if the specific works aren’t all familiar; a Chinese classic on intrigues among a sizable ruling clan (although the idea of it including the relationships of servants intrigues me), fantasy fiction about an empire amidst civil war of supernatural powers (whose name I’m sure will prompt no acrimony), non-fiction about colonialism and imperial relations, aaaand… Heh, of course that old chestnut of Grabowski’s (fairly accurate) critique of Hero would find its way in; I like the way it’s referenced as inspiration for the Realm and Dynast outlook on the Empress (as likely cultivated by her) rather than using its stylized elements straight up. They look like a good selection to me, and I meant want to sample a few of them if I can.

                            Now, a fiction piece concerning a small cross-section of the Realm’s citizens being accosted by bandits; they kill all the guards, take the point of view character hostage and even profane a sacred statue, and no comeuppance comes upon them. It’s an interesting way to start, establishing a tone not of the empire’s power, but of the vacuum left as it’s beginning to fall apart. I like how the ways in which it places women first reinforces the culture of the Realm, as well as the little bit ascribing some vague but enormous dimensions to the Imperial Mountain at the start, both for setting the scene and giving an idea for how to portray it. I’m liking the bandit leader’s design in the picture, with how she’s practically dressed and has a rough face, but still a bit of ornamentation in things like her braid and lipstick.

                            So, chapter one, History and Life in the Realm; this is something that I’ve been particularly looking forward to, to get a full sense of what has been adjusted for the changes in What Fire Has Wrought, what has remained the same because it was good, and maybe consolidating a few favoured details scattered through old source books.

                            The choice to start the description of the Realm’s beginning from the point of view of somebody on the ground level is really good, for a number of reasons. For one, it’s an avenue for some really evocative language of living through the eschaton’s onset and abrupt cancellation. It sets a strong emotional tone for the Realm’s beginning, a sense of the terrified, confused and cathartic feelings of the moment in which it would be easy to capitalise and establish such a polity; the kind of thing that cuts through a clinical detachment and scepticism. And it’s a reminder that there are people subjected to events who aren’t getting the full picture, and that there’s significance to their outlook.

                            I’m glad that when it starts getting more into the history of things, it maintains brevity (no sense in devoting too much attention for something so removed) and a dash of mythic tone, as that both keeps the scenario from feeling mundane and would logically be how things are experienced in a setting such as this. It’s also a good opportunity to provide some flowery description for the Fair Folk, which I’m generally for; I like calling them the gentry of imaginary worlds in particular.

                            The origin story of the Scarlet Empress remains the same in general outline, but the details are adjusted mildly; the tone is more literary than biographical. The thing that stands out to me the most is not going into detail of her journey into the manse, particularly not adding anything so specific as her having needed to personally sacrifice some Hearth member. I suspect that some people might view this as sanitising a bit, but my recollections place such a backstory as having been too rigid and provocative, and I think that it’s better for the overall mystique of the character for the details of her quest to be mysterious both in and out of character. I also like things such as the sense that her drive towards the weapons was overcoming a certain scepticism of her era, as well as language giving a sense of it more as an interconnected arsenal than a singular device.

                            The Seven Tigers story is still here, and I appreciate how concise it is; it gets across the point of how the immediate aftermath had some opportunists whose ambitions could have been disastrous, and that the Empress demonstrated a willingness to turn her power upon fellows and Creation in general, without needing to devote too many words to it. That’s not to say that it lacks flair, just that it’s efficient and has a good sense of proportion.

                            Ohh, now this is something particularly clever; Chejop Kejak is still present in this backstory, but with the twist of presenting him in the terms that he would be recognised in popular legend and official biography of the Realm, as an anonymous monk who followed astrology to meet with her and declare that she had been divinely selected to revive the Realm and rule the world, pledging the support of the Immaculate Order to give some structure to her nascent government. I like describing him in these terms for how it gives the more widespread perspective and keeps more of the prominence to her, and think it has just enough hints for a newcomer to put together that he’s a Sidereal Exalt. Overall, I find this way of writing the history to be engaging, getting across the important details in short order and having a kind of smooth flavour to it; you get a feel for tone and emotion in the events more than if they were recounted at length.

                            Oh dang; yeah, the image of the Scarlet Empress proclaiming her sovereignty is definitely something that always ought to have been presented in full colour. I also like how the frame is oriented upwards from the perspective of the peasants, increasing her sense of scope and dominance, and reinforcing how that gesture would have impacted its witnesses.

                            I’ve generally enjoyed the backstory to the Imperial City, but I’ll admit to a few details that were a little bit incongruous; the version in Exalted: the Dragon Blooded being slightly at odds with describing it as coming from a thorough ruined city, and the description of the Empress’ early activities as having a certain fickle quality which seemed a bit out of sorts with the gravitas suggested only shortly before. I think this part keeps the essentials of that story while tidying up some of the details. I even find the manner in which it describes her seemingly unknowable standards for whom she would admit entry to lend itself both to her mystery, and convey a sense of practicality. The adjustment in motives for those not called upon to “leaving will be a grave insult” to “what if we’re called upon right after we leave” is one I like, purely because it speaks to me directly. The image of encampments moving to occupy and reconstruct ruins is compelling to me, and the description of the sequence of dependent industries that develops it from a boom town to a fully operational city captures a quality I find attractive in how systems and communities form connections. Overall, I like how this section conveys the ad hoc nature of the Imperial City, right down to the lack of official name, and how it sets a contrast between that and it having become a centre for wealth and governance across Creation.

                            Araka Jeresh’s story is still here, but makes a few slight adjustments; the description of his original overture conveyed an implicit misogyny to the character that never fully made sense to me, so I’m glad that’s out while retaining his general presumption of offering to share the burden of her authority. Likewise, I’ve often kind of thought that her so quickly taking him as a lover was a bit misogynistic on the part of the writing, so I like it stating here that he was taken on as a messenger first, and a relationship came after. This would also be a spot where I find it okay to display the more playful quality of the Empress in the idea that extreme backtracking amused her enough to recruit somebody. This section in general, of the games of trying to impress and bedazzle the Empress and the manner in which she would respond, reminds me a lot of a time that Lea made a point of how the character needs to walk a fine line between having genuine sexual agency and the sexual agency of powerful women just winding up as something to titillate a male audience. In my opinion, it manages to walk that line, as I find the descriptions of the elaborate gestures made to win her favour as placing a lot of the authority with her. And with her motives to take on lovers… I can’t quite nail it down, but my immediate emotional reaction is that it’s doing a good job. It feels… like a combination of actual considerations in dynastic politics with pulp fantasy stylings (in which things are a bit more lurid) and some things that have become baked into Exalted (a general sense of meaningful intimacy not needing to come from fully committed relationships, and that there can be political exchanges in such things due to that sense). And it’s not making too big a deal out of it, so it can pass by as mundane in the eyes of the setting; in the absence of overall misogyny in the surrounding culture, there wouldn’t be any inherent slut shaming that comes from implying political support by openly entering a relationship, never mind the attitudes to the mere rumour of such faced by the likes of Elizabeth I and Catherine the Great. In contrast to the prior sections building up her mystique, I also find it to be rather humanising for the Scarlet Empress. I think it’s a good job.

                            Oh, there actually is a sidebar clarifying that the emerald-clad monk was Chejop Kejak; this is the kind of thing I get for processing the books in this manner. Normally I’d be a bit critical of a move such as this, but the sidebar wins me over with its choice of language. Things like the contrast between the official story of a humble monk proclaiming and entreating her with a shrewd sage who functions as a full founding partner in the empire, and that the Empress proved a lot more firm than he expected or liked. I also get a good sense out of the description of one of Chejop’s benefits giving him an established government in which to give Sidereals plausible covers in travelling and anonymous officials. Yyeesss, being clever ultimately seems less valuable than providing details on what the Empress and Sidereals get out of their partnership, and it thankfully still does so in a manner that is to the point and a bit sharp-witted.

                            All right, the establishment of the imperial service, a premise of which I’ve always been fond. For a start, I like that idea that she was initially only willing to personally meet with military commanders, presenting a challenge for having agents to enact her will without relocating officers from areas in which they were needed and suited; it not only gives me a picture of having to adapt her style on the fly and without a proper apparatus to call upon, but a starting bias towards familiar colleagues that she needed to grow out of in order to actually govern. In this way, we seem to get not only the origins of the All-Seeing Eye, but the general policy of governing by continuously keeping subordinate powers off-balance and in competition, striking me as not so much an elaborate calculation as being initially her only means and understanding of how to manage the powers that have clustered themselves beneath her. That idea that, lacking a formal apparatus to exercise power, her means of keeping all of these wealthy and experienced subordinates in line is to collect and distribute information that ensures they’re constantly churning against one another. As somebody with a fondness of grand institutions having ignoble origins, I like the All-Seeing Eye emerging from the informants keeping that mess going, and I enjoy the image of her participation being offset by a public persona of holding herself loftily above their intrigues.

                            The ministries strike me as comparatively prosaic, which is honestly what you’d want in a bureaucracy (or “paperwork junta” as the book colourfully describes it). What I like here is maintaining the point that these early days are still pretty wild, as the lack of formality makes things like tax collection amount to sending people out to seize property, and offsetting those already in possession of power and connections by giving them responsibilities that occupy their attention and drain their resources. I’ve always trying to keep an eye out for the writing giving ideas for areas to apply the Bureaucracy Ability, and I think this is one that would serve as inspiration for games in which players are establishing their own services. My tastes also go to the description of how she could secure service and loyalty from mortal leaders by means of their desire to have official cover to interfere with Exalted superiors. And it’s good to have another sense of the transition in governing style and structure, not only from early Realm to familiar Realm (i.e. extracting the military from civil governance), but away from the Shogunate in things such as subordinating powerful landlords to a centralised bureaucracy. So it comes out as a display of the Realm’s origin that is less abrupt, has more sense of history, while also having some practical play benefits.

                            Similar goes with the origins given to the magistrates, who an initial dependence on personally trusted individuals became not only an opportunity to cultivate the legend and popularity of her enforcers being grand and charismatic figures of justice, thus allowing her to spread her own cult of personality, but a means of quietly eliminating challenges to her autocracy and concentrating vacant powers in herself, further giving the Shogunate a sense of presence by its deliberated absence.

                            Thus we’re given an elaborate background for what the Realm’s law and custom is, and why these are seen to derive solely from the Scarlet Empress. It at once makes the Blessed Isle feel a lot more diverse and messy, and gives a sense of credibility to the weight of her presence and the amount of power she’s managed to personally exercise.

                            The legions appear to be the branch of the imperial service whose origins have changed the least from prior Editions; the basic outline of a pre-existing military that she didn’t want to make anxious with threats of a purge, and revived with things like benefits given to private enterprises that would finance and furnish them is all still there. If there’s any difference, I would find it to be things that make the early legions seem a lot stronger, give more of a sense that they came through the Contagion in better shape, and that part of her process for both projecting her own power and directing their attention externally was through things like grand parades. It covers a few of the points that would interest me such as how the military is being paid for, gives it a basis for some impressive pageantry, and lays the groundwork for the Realm’s militarism and expansionism. It also conveys an area in which the authority of the Empress might have become less absolute than it was in other areas, having to deal more carefully with something that might have had the power to destroy her.

                            So we’ve got the establishment for all of the parts of the Imperial Service, including the practical considerations for how and why they operate and both the accuracies and perceptions of the power held over them by the Scarlet Empress. What Fire Has Wrought gave us the picture of the power distributed through the Great Houses, but all of this is the structure of the imperial apparatus that clearly and meaningfully operates apart from and above them.

                            This section has all been very good. It breathes life and personality and history into the origins of the Realm. It gives meaningful information about how these governing institutions might originate and operate, but keeps it from being too dry and excessively detailed; it is densely packed in the best way. The best part is the way that it continuously gives the Empress a sense of presence, a few strategically placed peeks behind the curtain, providing an origin for her stature over her empire that feels organically developed as well as not being as absolute as it would appear. And, as I kept saying before, being presented in this lean, tight, personable manner really feels like it could serve as a blueprint for nation-building activities undertaken in play, so it has practical value in addition to enhancing the Realm by giving a sense of reality to its beginning. I feel as though it would be a difficult balance to provide information like this in a manner both educating and entertaining (while also not being overwritten), and it’s managed beautifully here.

                            This seems like a natural spot to take a break, but I do not think it shall be a very long one, as I’m eager for more.

                                                Now, with the establishment of the government out of the way, it’s on to the subject of the masses. Beginning with the point that, while the Empress was concerned with establishing authority over the elites, the countryside was still tumultuous in a manner that local authorities found difficult to manage. We’re given an early picture of legions inefficiently deployed to put down full uprisings, around the early patrician class needing to curtail them with shows of force or the occasional payoff. All of this to give a picture of why the Immaculate Order was integral to the development of the Realm; their system of matching exhortation that Dragon Blooded are rightful rulers with insistence that this is conditional on them displaying proper virtue and nobility is framed as a means of not merely reinforcing the desired hierarchies, but giving peasants a mechanism to protest. So we’re given a picture of the state religion and class relations that is a bit more reciprocal and gives the workers a bit more agency, even if it’s possibly a bit nominal for being filtered through authorities that serve the interests of the state. What’s particularly interesting to me is the idea that the Empress finds the occasional peasant revolt to be an acceptable outlet for their frustrations, being generally neither a real nor rhetorical danger to her; I guess that’s one of the benefits of being the literal saviour of the world (even if capitalised on by other development for her cult). The Unbroken Rushes Movement is still here, but in this adjusted milieu it stands out with the idea that the unknown monk leading it took the step of questioning the legitimacy of the Empress herself.

                                                I like how there’s a description of the Order’s role in widespread education, both for the means of projecting imperial propaganda and for presenting a beneficial amenity of the Blessed Isle. What’s particularly interesting is that idea that there are time when the deft hand of the Empress deals with rebellion by pardoning and incorporating the good will of the rebel leaders, which not only maintains her majesty among the peasants but provides a means for her to reassign territories to the detriment of Dynasts. I find this true to life on a couple of counts, both of the monarchy that finds it advantageous to provide some level of support to the workers over the nobility (as the latter is often a more direct threat), and having read some accounts of nascent rebels against Qing emperors who ended up being won over as ardent supporters.

                                                Right, good, a sidebar on the subject of exactly how land ownership works; this is something I’ve been particularly waiting for some word on. Yes, maintains the basic line from the original book still holds, that all land belongs to the Empress and its distribution is based on a series of leases connected to her. I will admit that I would have liked a bit more clarity on how the properties of Dynast and peasant intersect, but presuming that doesn’t come up further in the book that’s a question for later. The key point remains that Houses are increasing rents to acquire more revenue in the short term.

                                                For relations with the Threshold, I think some of the events have been adjusted slightly, placing the earliest drive for conquest a bit earlier than in prior Editions. I think the motives are changed a bit as well, with the focus on a perceived need for foreign resources. And it puts the satrapy system into the context of needing to govern despite limited staffing or infrastructure.

                                                Let’s see, it states that the earliest to acquiesce were those harmed by the Seven Tigers; I was thinking that meant concentrated in the South, but looking back those generals aren’t explicitly tied to that Direction this time. I like the description of the Empress inviting sovereigns to take audience with her, as it recalls an image I’ve always found attractive, of Napoleon demonstrating his power by conducting audience surrounded by the subdued princes of old European domains. The impression I’m getting here is that the familiar satrapy system was something initially established subtly; of special trade incentives that evolved into full tribute, and defence of vassals transitioning into conquest of neighbours. I like that the employment of diplomats is referenced here, as I’ve often felt that they were an under-realised quality in portraying Realm foreign policy, and I think it does a good job of tying things like Immaculate proselytizing to serving practical local needs (and vice versa with regards to reconstruction efforts to better facilitate extraction of resources for the Blessed Isle).

                                                The whole thing comes off in a very sinister light which I’m enjoying, which adds to the credibility of the Empress becoming very good at this kind of management. I particularly like that idea that, in times when the Realm had neighbours who were better suited to warfare on even terms, the preference was to conquer neighbours and leave them isolated against the natural dangers of this setting rather than engage in costly direct war. Her approach to imperialism puts me in mind of the Simpsons’ depiction of Bill Gates.

                                                Before going to the subject of the Anathema, I want to say that I find all of this to complement the picture given in Fangs at the Gate; they’re both written with a slant inclined towards their respective subjects, and between the two one can probably get a more complete and complex picture. On that note, it’s interesting how the small number of free Solars are presented as a comparatively more manageable threat than the Silver Pact. It certainly makes sense, given how the latter more uniformly had capabilities that would be suited for combatting such a foe, and were vastly more numerous. Fangs already did an exceptional job for Lunars, but it’s helped along by presenting them from the perspective of their enemies, this idea that their dominions are hellish places to try and campaign in and that the Empress has needed to personally survive the occasional assassin come in the guise of a petitioner. It also reinforces the value that the Order and the Realm draw from one another, the former providing the more mobile and expert Wyld Hunts and the latter enabling them with provisions and official sanction. Description of the borders of the Threshold being defined by a game of cat and mouse in particular strengthen my perception that, historically, the greatest success of the Wyld Hunt would be in presenting a threat Lunars would be obligated to withdraw from.

                                                All right, going to give the timeline a quick perusal; I’ll start with an appreciation for it being presented contiguously, rather than divided with sections of text. Let’s see… I like being able to put numbers to descriptions of the process of establishing the magistracy and bring the military under the throne’s control. Tepet’s failed siege would be expected, but it’s interesting to include the exile of Cevis Ghandarva. Scavenger Lands invasions are about the same… as is founding of the Deliberative, but for once we’re getting more solid timelines for the development of Great Houses (interesting that it took more than a century). I’ve always been very fond of House Jerah and Eyem (oracle-engine is an effective term in this altered milieu) and their understated role in the backstory of Ragara, so it’s nice to see them placed here; interesting that Ragara was only 29 when he acquired his House. The conquest of Prasad is much earlier than I would have expected. Like how Bagrash Kol’s achievements are attributed to use of a “primeval relic”.

                                                Let’s see, what else… finally given a firm date for the reappearance of the Caul. Oh, and the beginning of current trade in the West; the fact that it’s qualified as mortal presents an intriguing picture of what the previous situation might have been. Ah, and House Iselsi’s assassination attempt is still preceded by a spectacular failure to invade the East, it’s just that both events have been moved forward significantly rather than the House languishing for some four hundred years.

                                                It’s a leaner timeline than in First Edition, and in most respects I’d say it benefits from that. It drops a few extraneous details in favour of ones that provide a stronger sense for relevant history i.e. the relative ages of different Great Houses, and it effectively incorporates new details such as the dissolution of Shogunate gentes. The one area in which I’d say the First Edition version has it beat would be in how that one used the opportunity to reference a few strange and intriguing events, but I’ll grant that the approach to designing the setting in this Edition lends itself to doing the same thing elsewhere.

                                                Moving on, there’s a bit more detailing for those enemies that have presented a meaningful challenge to the Realm as a whole. I like the placement of Bagrash Kol’s empire as something that cemented the imperialism by presenting a threat that could overcome isolationist sentiments, and it’s good to include more subtle and weird dangers such as a heresy promoted by Lunar infiltrator (again building heat for them by giving them operations on the Blessed Isle itself) and a sorcerous plague. Given how it’s been discussed in the past, I’d find that giving the Realm more vulnerabilities actually strengthens its presentation, because it can cultivate the idea that it’s continuously proven the strengths of the Dragon Blooded rather than them resting on their laurels.
                                                Speaking of Dragon Blooded achievements, there’s then the matter of Lookshy. I’m a bit uncertain about returning with the idea that using the Defence Grid inflicts backlash on the Isle, but that’s offset by the neat idea that reluctance to fire it at the Scavenger Lands was for fear of relics buried throughout rather than just the arsenals of Lookshy. I’m kind of imagining that the framing of the three invasions as a battle with the city is meant to capture the Realm perspective specifically; that they would frame it as warfare against a defiant Shogunate legion and downplay the rest of the River Province, whereas the other side might still have more to say for the range of heroes and contributions provided by the likes of Sijan and Nexus. In any case, I think it presents a fun new dynamic to their relationship to have the idea that they both get more capital from facing off across the Inland Sea and spooking those behind them than they do from actually resolving the conflict.

                                                Now, the section introducing the Dynasty as part of the greater scope of the Realm begins with the point that despite innumerable lovers over the centuries (including the fact that they were women when they weren’t well-bred Exalted), the Scarlet Empress has had few official marriages, Rawar being the first. It’s interesting to me how this is folded into a bit of background for Ragara’s ambitions; he’s probably no less of a dick, but there’s at least some motive behind it when there’s a whole thing of the Empress having several children via lovers who held equal status to one born from her official consort. Hmm, the description of Sesus as a brother seems to be in error, probably ought to be corrected. In any case, it looks to me like a good way of establishing the Realm’s concept of a line of succession (and the measures somebody might go to trying to secure it in earlier centuries) by way of anecdote.

                                                Description for the Thousand Mazy Paths ties nicely into all of the historical setup, with an especially good contrast against the original attempts to prevent outright civil war. The particulars given sound pretty good, that idea in which nobody has a clear shot at the top because there are too many obstacles and rivals, and I find it to give a reasonable basis for why Ragara was unimpeded before being suddenly stopped. It’s also good for it to state right out that this was all something that made the state less effective even while it ensured the security of the autocrat. I think prior Editions were always a bit too coy concerning the hows and whys of Houses being raised up and cast down, so good on them for including a reference to that procedure; I believe that twelve fallen Houses might be a larger number than before, but it fits with making the Realm’s history a bit more dynamic and giving the Houses more distinction and variety. I’m glad to see the idea of her announcing a date for selecting an official successor being given a bit more focus, and think it’s cute to point out that her disappearance is still very far off of her projection.

                                                I like the way that the description of the prior disappearances of the Empress aren’t phrased in terms that offer a truth to precisely what she did as much as different perceptions shaped by the level of society the speculator comes from; it makes possibilities for what became of her a bit more open ended. The Council of the Empty Throne has a nice bit of grit to its membership and motives; I get a slight impression that the only people who could be considered trustworthy for participating in that decision were some of the oddities, somewhat on the periphery of the factions that they represented. Likewise the description of the manner in which the empire is breaking down manages to be both informative of the finer details and evocative with some nasty, grinding imagery. The sense I get from this is not quite that the Realm is ignominiously collapsing as it being a repository of vast power that is turning in on itself, and that would carry both the promise of interesting times for the participants and the threat that even in its death throes it might inflict disaster on the surroundings.

                                                Yes, I think this final section on the organization and distribution of power in the… the Scarlet Realm, that was the term that raised a query from me about accepted terms for the First Age. Anyway, this is a sensible place to stop. The whole subject has been well-handled in my eyes, with a real sense of terrible strength and far too much complexity and a feel for the presence of mighty, decadent, scheming mandarins even when it’s not addressing them all by name. It’s all sex and blood and money, painted in the picture of a historical treatise for an overly bureaucratised state. It’s good stuff there.

                                                Mind, I would be interested to see if the lifestyle section winds it down a bit, focuses a bit more on the personable side.
