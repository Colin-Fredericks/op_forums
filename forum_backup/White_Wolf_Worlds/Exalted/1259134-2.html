                            The ideas that girls and boys receive different nutrition and incentives when young and that accounts for different strength differences is pretty easy to test. Just track strength in boys and girls from birth to the end of adolescence. If you get a differentiation between the sexes that just starts at puberty, then it's probably not any kind of nurture phenomenon, because it would be odd for parents to nourish and incentivize children exactly equally from birth to adolescence, then suddenly decide to encourage sharply different behavior when they become more autonomous adolescents. Just not the parsimonious solution.

                            And on that, I believe divergence does tends to hit at 12, e.g. https://adc.bmj.com/content/59/5/453 or https://www.semanticscholar.org/pape...c54047db97d367 for the graphics. (A couple other studies point to divergence at 12: https://journals.lww.com/jpo-b/Abstr...ldren_.17.aspx and https://www.researchgate.net/figure/...fig3_308909501). Some small residual differences sometimes present; may be from multiple causes (boys choose more exercise / encouraged more exercise / purely biological, etc.)

                            These are modern Western samples, so it could be the case that this is sharply different to a more nutritionally restricted context where dimorphism emerges from differential nutrition.... though I have to caveat that from the few skeletal bioarchaeology bits I've read in my past, height and size reduction from malnutrition tends to be a "hits both sexes equally" kind of thing, in the main, and to be honest, may be less of a deal than disease burden size reduction that of course does not discriminate. (E.g. https://www.researchgate.net/publica...tosh_AAPA_2015 - height difference between male and female, with a noisy sample, averages about 5-6 inches from Mesolithic hunter gatherers through to medieval Europeans, despite huge changes in subsistence economy). Also reasonably stable difference same difference in measured differences in height in present day societies at different places in nutrition landscape - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_o...ight_worldwide

                            Though it's not crazy to think that differing nutrition for boys and girls matters, and may have had some statistically measurable effect. But it looks to me more the amount of room between "Actually we don't have enough food, so we feed boys and girls both little enough for them to survive and they get more or less equally close to their peak nutrition" and "We have enough food; we feed boys and girls alike enough", is just not very large for any cultural practice in distribution of food to matter much as a proportion of whole dimorphism (and if anything, where it has mattered, feeding girls not enough is more likely to sadly result in higher female mortality rates).

                            I don't say any of this definitive though! But certainly something you can solve with reason and evidence.

                                            Originally posted by Saur Ops Specialist View Post

                                            Without an actual control, that would just get you more of the same. And the corresponding control, for reasons noted above, does not exist.
                                            I don't think you have to fabricate an entire parallel world and alien society (the "control") in order to test your hypotheses, really.

                                            Your hypothesis has physical mediating factors (exercise, food intake), so you'd just measure how those explain intrapopulation variation in the whole Male+Female young population and then whether they can explain the difference. (It's not a "I chant to my crops so they grow!" hypothesis, or worse a "The crops grow depending on the gods unknowable moods!" hypothesis. There are measurable physical factors at play.) There would be a bit of work removing some circularity in nutrition of course, so you'd have to show that nutrition increases were exogenous and not just driven by differing appetite.

                                            Ideally you'd also quantify and define the social factors that are supposed to be upstream of the physical factors, in a falsifiable and non-subjective way, and show that they are upstream.
                                            It's a pretty weighty hypothesis, so hard to argue should be the null, default hypothesis and the burden is on proponents to measure the factors to show that's true, opposed to a simpler hormonal development hypothesis.

                                            The absence of differences between boys and girls between 12, when social factors and downstream physical correlates might be expected to hold (unless they suddenly start at 12 for some reason), seems to me like fairly strong evidence against (or even more complexity).
