    Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
    ~11 was me accidentally doing 6 specifically not 6+.

    In either case, the point isn't 5 vs. 6 in terms of value, but that either alone isn't actually "...a resting parry so high, no mortal and most supernatural enemies can't even hit them..." The char-gen aspect doesn't help matters, but it's not the only thing that matters either. I think it's a very fair point to make that Charms matter a hell of a lot more.
    Okay but...

    Dex 5
    Melee 5 (Or Martial Arts 5)
    Specialy
    Medium Weapon (like a Hook Sword)
    Stunt

    Gets you to Parry 8 for 0m. Which is about a 4% chance to hit on 8 dice with no Charms (worse than needing a Natural 20 in D&D). And that's a problem. Because you can be mounted, full defence, and up your stunt on top of that for Parry 14. And now you layer on Charms (say, Crane Form, to get scene long +1 Parry and let you Counter Attack everyone who attacks you). (And if you just wanted to flex, Willpower Full Excellency for Parry 20.)

    Whereas:

    Dex 3
    Melee 3
    Specialty
    Medium Weapon

    Puts you on Parry 5. Now if you have Dipping Swallow Defence and Hail Shattering Practice, 8 dice mortals are going to have a bad time. ~63% of their attacks are going to miss Parry 5 and grant the Solar 1i. Of the 37% that hit, about half will have at least one "1" for Hail Shattering, which is going to roughly cut that hit rate down to ~25%... which is still, like, five times higher than the Solar stacking all the non-magical bonuses.

    Sure that's not an exact apples-to-apples comparison there but there's a difference between "1 in 3" attacks hitting and "1 in 20" attacks hitting. And this is before you look at builds like Death Kitty who is getting hit "1 in 1000" attacks at 8 dice (before she uses Agile Beast Defence).

    1 in 20 (or 1 in 1000) might technically be non-zero odds of hitting, but they're bad enough to make it farcical to roll out a combat.

    Which is fine if you are the combat focused character. Your mighty Dawn, legendary warrior, champion of the sun, is supposed to stride invincible through swaths of skilled mortals. But if a mortal only has a 1 in 20 chance of hitting your weakest party member... that's not a scene worth running.

    ****

    There's 8 Essence 1 Charms you can take with Melee 3 (and you can reach for HSP or some other E2 charm with the Glory Stone). You're not exactly starved for options if you start with your base stats a little lower for your characters who have combat as a secondary focus. But using those options is fun. You have to spend resources and actually try to win the fights. It's worthwhile for your opponent to roll because they might hit you. They probably won't hit you, but if they don't hit you then you can use your Charms to gain bonuses for them missing you!

    You can have a back-and-forth. You might get Crashed, but that lets you Initative Shift!

    Your 3/3 Exalt will not lose a fight with an 8 dice mortal.

    Folks want to use their Charms in fights. Winning a fight because you have sun-magic and your enemy doesn't is a very Solar thing. I'm not against Solars winning because their Charms are awesome. But I want them to try. I want them to use their powers. To make decisions. To spend resources.

    And even if we wipe all of my strawmanning and reframing the argument away, and go back to your original numbers (40% to hit Parry 5, 20% to hit Parry 6 for equally invested Lunars and Solars vs 8 dice mortals) -- is the 20% fight worth running?

    Sure, I get your wider point -- with Charms we can drop that chance well below 20% (you could FBS/DSD/HSP/GSS/EM that down to, what, 0.4% for 5.5m - lose a mote every other round with 5m regen). But my point is that folks want to use their Charms, but that's kinda pointless if the Charms don't actually help them.

    The 40% fight, I think, is worth running. That Lunar might spend some motes. They might use their cool Lunar powers.
    The 20% fight... that needs a lot more ST support to make the anatagonist usable.
    The 10% fight (which is what it drops to the moment the Solar picks up a medium weapon - like an offhand shield)... is a lot of bookwork for me as an ST. I can make it work, but it's a lot of work.
    The 5% fight (which is what happens when the Solar starts stunting their defence with their shield)... is what I'm complaining about in this thread. Functionally, that's a resting parry so high mortals can't hit them. Which is fine, if it's one player whom I can't challenge with mortal soldiers in direct combat, but not fine when I can't use any 8 dice opponent against any member of the Circle.

    This is where Exalted really starts to fall apart. If this was the highest level of resting Parry, the DFO Anton Raid, the hyper-twinked, mega-optimized, spreadsheet-baseball stats, version of the game that the top 5% of optimizers play, then I wouldn't have a complaint. But it's not. It's Dex 5, 2BP on Melee and grabbing a shield (or any other medium weapon). Walk into any Exalted community and a chorus of voices will tell you to build your character like this. Heck, I made a thread on this forum telling people to build their characters like this. This is where the vast majority of folks play. It's where you had to play to make 2E work. But it doesn't work in 3E. (Unless your ST takes a massive role with homebrew or doesn't mind you mindlessly walking over all opposition -- which is fine if you're having fun, but doing this for a year seriously bummed me out and has left me never wanting to run Exalted again.)

    The 0.1% fight (which is the optimized build like Death Kitty)... is something the rules of the game lets you do, and the reason why it's important to have a Session 0. I love Death Kitty: I think it's the perfect Lunar. This is what system mastery looks like -- it's fifty times better than the casual playstyle above (using this arbitary 8 dice attack metric). And, sure, I guess I'm complaining about this as well and I admit that I've gotten sloppy in my examples and blurred the lines between this level and the 5% level. But this isn't the level of gameplay I'm primarily talking about. I mention it only in recognition of Heavy Arm's point that, yes, laying Charms on top of your base abilties makes you much, much, much more powerful. But my entire argument is that you don't need this power - the game is already unbalanced way before this point. (And this is Death Kitty's resting Parry. She can Agile Beast Defence/Dexterity Excellency better defences, sure, just why would you bother?)

                                                    Some general comments on this stuff:
                                                    Exalted presents a big world, with Solars near the top. Solars are, by design, like dropping Goku into Berserk, and other Celestials get there by Ess 2 or 3. The answer for "why have so much space devoted to stuff that Solars clown on" is twofold: first, to make Solars actually feel like god mode, and second, so that the game still functions if you want to play Dragon-Blooded, or God-Blooded, or even mortals. (I agree that mortals could use some more widgets -- I'd have cut at least half the fiddly bits Exalted get and given more options for baseline. But the system works fine with mortals and correctly depicts the world the game is selling you, it's just rather bland.) I think the game needed a sidebar that explains this; it's in the lore, but they needed to step out of narrative voice somewhere and be like "yes, really." I keep seeing people running an Ess 4 Solar circle with multiple combat-optimized characters and complaining that nothing's challenging, but that's intentional! This is the game that people pitch by saying you can suplex Cthulhu; in the past, when the numbers prevented you from actually doing that, people were not happy!

                                                    The game gives you the power to design the kind of character you want, but it also gives you the responsibility to ensure that you actually want what you're making. It's not their ST or the game's responsibility to nerf players' characters enough that fights are challenging, it's their responsibility. Most of the problems I've seen come from people not taking that responsibility seriously enough. If you're having this problem with your group, here's my advice: emphasize that the descriptions of what different ratings mean are correct, and then tell players to come up with an Exalt-scale character concept and then give them the Attributes, Abilities, and Merits that suit that concept, without limitations, making clear that it's fine to have a bunch of 5s if that suits the premise. This is the correct mindset to be in, and helps to fix the thing where players are like "oh, I'll just take Melee 5 because it's so cheap!" If there's a player who still wants Dex 5, Melee 5 on Gandalf, you should make sure they understand what that means, but at that point it's not your responsibility to make armies or big monsters challenging to them, and if they can't enjoy the game because they clown on everything, you should be offering to let them retire that character or giving them a plot reason to retool their stats, not trying to change the entire setting. Customers who order steak get steak, even if they really wanted chicken; adding a houserule that you can't order steak is kind of missing the point.

                                                    Though I hesitate to invoke the dread GNS Theory, Exalted and especially 3e has more of a simulationist mentality than a gamist one. The mechanics are meant to represent what the world is like and fit your character in where the lore says they should fit, which is perhaps the only real way to handle a game with this many build options. This can be a massive hassle for GMs, but it's also where the game's breadth and versatility comes from. One of the things I like best about Exalted is that it feels like a world, and the more the mechanics seem tailored to provide a "play experience" first, the harder that is to maintain, so I can understand why they do this even though it makes "what do I actually do here" a real problem when running games.

                                                    In a system that gives you a wide breadth of options and power levels in character creation, you're stuck with either 80% of builds as newbie traps, or having the upper 20% be unusually strong. You need to have this mindset going into the game, because in 3e, pretty much everything is viable. If you have a player thinking "I want to be a decent fighter, so obviously 2/1/4 physical tertiary isn't viable", they're going to misjudge the power level of what they're actually creating, and the game material is aimed at people who do not misjudge it.

                                                    Heavy Arms is correct that the charm prerequisites are too high and send mixed messages. My advice is to houserule all Ess1 charms as capping out at Ability 3, and Ess2 at Ability 4. This is mainly an issue with Solars, though -- DBs and Lunars seem a lot more subdued.

                                                    I've mostly been framing this issue as a criticism of players who don't engage with the game on its own terms, and I think that's true, but this is also one of the fundamental problems with 3e's mechanical depth: if you're a Solar who goes pretty deep on your Supernal, you almost always win automatically at that thing. Which is fine, but it means that 80% of your character's mechanical complexity is concentrated on the thing where you win automatically, and so that complexity feels ill-used. It's like having a Rube Goldberg machine to press a win button; combining five Charms to get some ludicrous gigantic number is fun the first time you do it, but that's not enough to justify how fiddly the high-end stuff is.
                                                    God that was long! Sorry, I had a lot of pent-up frustration about "my Solars keep clowning on everybody" complaints.

                                                    One last thing, about the Octavian white-rooming: Elenian is correct that, while assuming a roll scores EV is OK for eyeballing a single interaction, it is not a suitable way to test a fight unless every roll has a small impact on the outcome, which is seldom the case. It's analogous to white-rooming a single roll by saying "the average d10 result is a 5.5, which is a failure, so I'll assume these ten dice are all failures." When you iterate many rolls each of which can have a significant impact, you get the same dynamic at a higher level. Assuming all rolls do EV doesn't just fail to tell you that a fight has a 30% chance of TPK, it may fail to tell you that there's a 90% chance. This is particularly an issue with enemies like Octavian, who have stuff that just completely obliterates people on a good roll.
