    Im pretty sure Chausse is right, you can lie in a persuade action. If you want somebody to join you on your quest to fight the evil witch of the plains, you can lie to a man and say the witch killed his wife, even though she just died of regular old disease. You leverage his intimacy of love for his wife to persuade him to seek vengeance, just like how if she had actually done the murder you could convince him to go honestly.

    You still actually need an intimacy to leverage off of though. For example if he'd hated his wife that wouldn't work. In the case of getting yourself off the hook from a crime though, what intimacy did he leverage? If he was caught red handed he can't just go "uhhh persuade action to let me go leveraging their...love for their city...because...they don't want somebody as cruel as I am even in their prisons?"

    If he persuade actioned them to let him go based on them believing his innocence they need an intimacy based on his innocence, like "This guy is not guilty of x crime." Or "this guy would never commit a crime." Which IS something he could instill and raise to defining, but that takes a long time usually.

    EDIT: You can never persuade somebody to believe something. Beleifs are intimacies and they can only be instilled, or form naturally through roleplaying. If they saw him do the crime then they have an intimacy for it, and him likely if it was bad enough, and that actually works against him there. Persuade gets people to do things, instil gets them to believe things.

                I think the social system on the whole was written on the assumption that people would recognise its intent and engage with it in good faith, and so if addressing the circumstance of arbitrating an action based on something that the targeted character could assuredly never believe in was considered at all, it seems like it could be a high priority to chop when word count is at a premium.

                In the spirit of the described unacceptable orders and the Orichalcum Rule, I think it's perfectly reasonable to conclude that something a person simply could not believe, like the premise of a physical impossibility, simply fails outright. Barring circumstances that would reasonably introduce doubts, a person doesn't need Willpower or Decision Points to avoid being convinced to act on something from the assumption that they can fly.

                Still, I think it's also worth considering that between people sometimes having a surprisingly malleable sense of the truth (particularly as might reinforce existing prejudices) and genre conceits, I think the range of things that are functional lies in Exalted still ends up being fairly broad.

                It's hard to really assess the validity of the scenario that has started this tangent without knowing the details.

                    To me "I love my wife" is not a valid Intimacy to leverage for the Persuade action "Kill the Witch".                     Personally I'd do it this way : first you lie to instill the Minor belief that his wife did not die of illness. Then you instill to transform your lie to a Major belief that she died from evil sorcery. And then you instill to transform your lie to a Defining belief that the Witch killed her. Once you're there, then you can Persuade the poor sap, at no penalty even! But getting there will take you as much work as honest influence.

                    EDIT : If your Persuade action only makes sense if the target has formed a new Intimacy in the process of being persuaded, then it's not a Persuade Action. Rather, it's any number of Instills plus a Persuade action in the same stunt.

                    Note that this doesn't mean you can't lie in Persuade actions, you're free to misrepresent what the target is agreeing to. For instance, you might convince someone to go with you to kill the Witch together, when what you're actually planning to do is to use him as a ritual sacrifice to open the Witch's inner sanctum once you both get there.

                        I don't think you can change the nature of an intimacy as it goes up in level, can you?

                        Also persuading somebody to do something by honest influence doesn't require an intimacy which completely supports the action. Like you don't need to convince somebody with a defining tie of hatred for the Realm to come join your Realm resistance group. You might need to convince somebody with a defining tie of love for their ancestors that the Realm invasion needs to be fought to preserve their memory.

                        Of course you could also convince them that the Realm needs to not be fought, for their retribution will be swift and destructive, crumbling all that person's ancestors built and letting their legacy sink into the mud.

                        Neither is a lie, but depending on your exploitation of it you can make them do opposite things. You could do the same thing with a lie, but you still need to find out their intimacies first. For example you could say that their respected ancestor's ghost actually came to you and told you that buried beneath one of their streets is an old ancient tomb that holds relics they hold dear. Which is a complete lie, you don't even know if their ancestor's ghosts have moved on to Lethe or not. If you're a Solar trickster-god though it's something you should be able to convince people of.

                        It's risky though, because if whatever you're getting them to dig for isn't a tomb, or the actual ghost shows up, then you've been made. Now everyone knows you as a liar and they probably want your head besides. It's a much safer bet to instill the intimacies you want slowly, building up until they have deep seated beliefs about you and then exploit those, that way if you're revealed to be a liar you have defenses. The ancestor ghost shows up to oust you, but they've a defining principle that says you're a prophet of the ancestors, so you might be able to convince them that this newcomer is a demon sent to trick them or something. Might anyway.


                        I'd also remind everyone about the orichalcum rule. If the rules suggest something that's dumb or bad for the story, the rules are wrong and the story is right, and this rule supercedes all rules.
