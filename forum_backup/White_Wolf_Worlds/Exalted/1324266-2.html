                Other suggestions to keep combat challenging for high essence Solars:

                Split them up. Always a good tactic to put the pressure on the not-so-combat-optimized part of the group, without having them face the same opponent which can challenge the Dawn.

                Place the fight in the Shadowlands or some special Manse where there is no mote regen, or a heavily reduced one. Makes the above attrition idea much more feasible.

                The above attrition tactic is more effective than it was given credit for. Two combats which are challenging enough to get the players to put up all those scene-longs and force mote expenditures, and which are not the same scene? Well, trust me, that second fight will be tight. Running on mote drip while fighting the real Wild Hunt after they softened you up with a bunch of demons and elements and you thought you got away will not be the Circle's best day.

                NPC's do not have to obey the same rules of PC's. Think raid boss in mmorgps, or D&D4e solo's. Want to give some demon/behemoth/elemental/whatever 100 health-levels, powers with cooldowns instead of mote cost and go through different 'stages', resetting its initiative every time it moves to the next stage and multiple actions/initiative tracks? Well, go for it! Our ST in the chronicle we just finished last weekend gave us some really nice fights like this.

                Unless you use the above method one, big, bad opponent is not going to cut it. Action economy alone will mean it loses. Use plenty of opposition.

                Terrain. Make it dangerous. Collapsing temples, volcanoes going boom, sinking ships, amidst a mega-fauna stampede, fortresses which are on fire, toxic fumes from braziers, shambling hordes of undead which are trivial combatants but are plague-carriers or in a city which is the target of a Rain of Doom.

                Put in other goals beyond reducing the other side to the incapacitated health level. The PC's might be really tough, but they're allies/loved ones/innocent citizens/person who knows what they need to learn is not. Or perhaps close the portal to Malfeas., boot up the Reality Engine or open up those Essence Vents before the Manse overloads and explodes. Count-down timers can really make them sweat.

                Anyway, think D&D4e, not 3.X or 5e. Your challenging fights should be big set-pieces, which, yes, will take some prep-time, but done right can be very rewarding. Throw-away fights are to make the PC's feel bad-ass, or can be resolved with simple checks and narrating.

                            I'd like to take a step back here, and question one of the fundamental premises of the thread - do essence 3 characters, or indeed any Exalted at all, actually need to be immediately challenged in every encounter for there to be drama? I'd argue that, in fact, they don't.

                            To unpack this a bit, I'll first define "challenge". What I think people usually mean when they describe something as "challenging" or "a challenge" is that there's a meaningful chance of failure, and that that chance depends at least in part on the decisions the players make during it. I do think a lot of roleplaying games use this definition, and most traditional RPGs are, in an abstract sense, asking the question of "how will you accomplish your goals?". Exalted, I think, is actually asking a different question. Exalted, by design, starts characters at a high level of competence, one where (in their areas of specialty, at least) they're typically able of taking on some pretty extreme opponents or situations with quite good odds of success. This is particularly true in 3rd Edition, at least for Solars, who start with up-to-Essence-5 charm access in one Ability, their Supernal. I think that's intentional - Exalted has decided that to make it so that the answer to the question of "can you accomplish your goals" is "yes, pretty easily", right out of the gate. By getting that out of the way, it can move on to the questions it's really interested in, which are "how will you choose your goals?" and "if you do accomplish your goals, what are the consequences?"

                            These questions are fundamentally about the ethics of power. That, I'd argue, is one of the central themes of Exalted, reinforced in numerous ways. Exalted, for example, has no alignment system a la Dungeons and Dragons - there's no cosmic morality that the characters can point to and say "See, I'm doing Good!". And charms may allow you to do various things very well, but they don't help you choose the right goals. A Solar with high Bureaucracy is just as capable of running their state efficiently and achieving her desired ends if she's creating a brutal tyranny or an enlightened utopia. And these sorts of questions are certainly capable of creating drama! Plenty of works in other mediums ask questions like these as their main source of drama. Consider Macbeth, for example - the question of "can I kill the King?" is never something that really seems to trouble Macbeth. The driving question, instead, is "should I have killed the King?".

                            So, when you're planning for Exalted games, I think an important step is to always think about the questions I outlined above. That means, when you're presenting stuff to the players, make sure they have multiple viable routes forward, with distinct consequences and rewards, so that they can make meaningful choices. And, once the players have picked a path, consider the possible consequences of that choice, both the obvious and the unexpected. For example, in the original post, Epitome mentioned that the entire circle could summon 2nd Circle demons. So, is that the only option? Are there other things the circle could do to accomplish what they want? And will choosing that accomplish the goals in the way they want? If you summon Octavian, for example, he's going to fight in a certain way (brutally). Do you want your enemy mashed into an absolute pulp? Or if there's an earth elemental in the area, Octavian might ignore, or at least do only a very desultory job of handing his given target, in favor of going off to beat up the object of his hatred. And then there are consequences to consider - obviously, as I've already mentioned, Octavian might not fight the opponent in exactly the way you want. But summoning 2nd Circle demons should also draw some attention to the circle that they might not want. I personally see the Loom of Fate as having certain warnings built in for when things like demons pop in - it might not draw instant Sidereal attention, but they'd probably put it on a list. And who else wanted to summon Octavian that month, and now can't? Will they be interested in the party now? What about the reaction of the average person on the street? Now that the circle is known to summon demons at the drop of a hat, are people going to be comfortable working with them?

                            I'd say that if you make sure that there's always interesting choices to be made, goal-wise, and that every path taken has some consequences, your game will still have plenty of narrative potential, even if any given challenge can be fairly easily overcome.

                                I have the simple ST philosophy, that when my players get too much power, then make the game about how well they can wield that power.

                                Did they just defeat the evil king badguy? Ok, now they're kings. Rule. How will you rule?

                                For an easy primer on what kind of challenges a new kingdom (or one being restored from a bunch of exalts rampaging through it) just go play Tropico 5 and take some inspiration.

                                Or play tropico 4 (if you can stomach the graphics) and look at the political factions there: It was part of what I loved the most about that game. The factions there were simple, but clearly had different goals and demands of the government:
                                - the green faction in Exalted could be a group representing the country's farmers, hunters and anyone else who works the land. They'll be against anything that polutes their workplace, and will require good terms with the gods for good harvests and hunts. Perhaps they'll require the players deal with cruel forest gods that demand unreasonable sacrifices? Protip: Don't piss off the people producing the food for your population.
                                - the nationalists in Exalted could be what amounts to the exalt's "fanclub" in the kingdom: They think the exalts rock, but only as long as they make the country better than the neighbouring countries. Failing that, they'll be pissed and might want the old regime back.
                                - the religious faction in Exalted doesn't need much explanation. Perhaps the kingdom the exalts conqured/liberated/whatnot has a lot of people who worship the immaculate dragons. How are they going to make peace with those people? Diplomacy? Appeasement? mind control? Is one temple demanding favorable treatment over another?
                                - the communists, in Exalted, would be representatives for the workers in the cities. They don't own any land, and perhaps they were worked to the bone by the old king to produce weapons or goods for export. They want better working and living conditions, more pay, all that jazz... and perhaps the wealth of the country depends on their industrial output, so... better not piss them off, right? but perhaps their demands will eat too much into your profits?
                                - the aristocracy. You might have taken over from the evil king, but what about the nobles of the land? Do they trust you as the new ruler? Will they switch aligeance to neighboring kings or warlords if you come off as too weak? who knows
                                - the merchants. as rulers, how will you deal with the guild? You need them to buy goods your country produces, but they want special tax exemptions, and you like taxing things now. remember: the guild always plays dirty, but do you dare anger them by calling them on their bullshit? Only a foolish warlord makes an enemy of the guild, for suddenly your enemies will be able to buy weapons, horses and mercenaries at great discounts, while drug dealers will start appearing all over your territories.
                                - if the players just delegate power to a governor who rules in their place... remember: a ruler who is never there, perhaps always off on adventure, might not recognize what he or she comes back to one day. Perhaps you're not even in charge when you return home one day.

                                Each of these are a potential plot hook for some kind of adventure. Perhaps there are other secret organizations operating in the kingdom that later reveal themselves, now that its the players who are in charge?

                                oh well

                                TLR - if you are unsure of how to deal with players who have gained great power.... then go see how they wield that power, and make the game about that for a bit. Will they become tyrants? Will they become selfless champions of the people? Cult leaders?

                                            I appreciate the concept of having the method and the consequences being more the focus than the means of victory, but I don’t think the two are mutually exclusive. In fact I think one can even help facilitate the other. For example if you’re summoning Octavian and the Princes, that could label you unfavourably as women and men who consort with the fel powers powers of Malfeas. That’s only really a choice to be made if the task at hand is so difficult or dangerous that not summoning them would risk failure. I think it could also add weight to otherwise interesting choices. Like let’s say your circle battles long and hard through the freezing tundra of a northern winter, trying to recover a stone that will purify any water it’s submerged in, so that you can bring it home, clean your defiled lake and save your people from having to migrate elsewhere. Unfortunately, when you get there you discover a hidden little civilization nestled around an otherwise toxic volcanic geyser, using the stone to purify it for their crops and drinking. Now that’s already an interesting choice, but it’s even weightier if the PC have suffered for months to get here, and likely will for months on the journey back either way, and what’s all that for? For nothing? And not even because the legends were wrong, their salvation is right there, they’d just be condemning these people to death by taking it.


                                            Also there can be a bit of an issue with Solars being so powerful that the consequences of their actions also are easily taken care of. For example if you’re labeled demon summoners and your zenith just says “wait guys, we’re actually still cool” and backs that up with solar magic, then that problem just goes away. Not to mention with have multiple big complex systems for social interaction, combat, crafting ect. Which kind of implies that we’re supposed to be using them in interesting ways.

                                                    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey View Post
                                                    I appreciate the concept of having the method and the consequences being more the focus than the means of victory, but I don’t think the two are mutually exclusive.
                                                    I definitely agree, and never meant to suggest otherwise! There are absolutely times when a serious challenge is appropriate, and can add drama. My point was more that the questions of consequences and uses of power should generally be the larger source of dramatic tension, not the only source.

                                                    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey
                                                    Also there can be a bit of an issue with Solars being so powerful that the consequences of their actions also are easily taken care of. For example if you’re labeled demon summoners and your zenith just says “wait guys, we’re actually still cool” and backs that up with solar magic, then that problem just goes away.
                                                    Perhaps, but I feel 3e has actually largely addressed this issue by cutting out a lot of the "you just win" or "affect giant swathes of Creation" charms that 2e had. Now, you generally do have to get in there and engage with individual NPCs to solve a lot of your problems. For example, if the circle has gained a rep as demon summoners, even someone with Supernal Presence or Socialize can't just wave their hands and say "Actually, everyone feels we're still good". They would have to get out and actually talk to all those people, or, at very least, send competent minions to do so. And that has consequences of its own - every minute spent convincing the common people that you're one of the nice demon summoners is time not spent talking to the envoy from the neighboring queendom who wants a trade deal oh and by the way, what's all this about demon worship? And successfully convincing the people that you're nice demon summoners has knock-on effects too - it makes it easier for someone else to come in and claim that they're nice demon summoners too, or even that demon worship in general isn't such a bad thing.

                                                    Originally posted by DrLoveMonkey
                                                    Not to mention with have multiple big complex systems for social interaction, combat, crafting ect. Which kind of implies that we’re supposed to be using them in interesting ways.
                                                    Sure, and I'm not advocating for dispensing with those systems. What I'm suggesting, however, is that expecting most interactions with such systems to result in success, often extreme success, on the part of the players should be the default, and that trying to source all your drama and tension on the assumption that they won't succeed is going to be disappointing, because Exalted isn't really built that way.

                                                        will the guild dare send merchant caravans to their lands - knowing that the population are mind-slaves, and that their merchants might suffer the same fate? That can ruin your economy as foreign trade dries up

                                                        that kind of mass brain-washing is sure to attract a ton of attention from neighboring warlords who might start to get really worried. And of course it'll provoke a MASSIVE wyld hunt! Perhaps a whole realm legion! As useless as the realm regent is, then he knows damn well that nothing unites the realm as well as a good ol' common enemy - and sending a legion or two towards a kingdom overtaken by anathema?

What does the gold faction think of the player's actions? Do they still think they're redeemable? Or are they just going to 'feed' the circle to the bronze faction and let them send dream-assassins or something similarly impossible to defend against.


                                                        This is part of what I love about Exalted: It doesn't matter how powerful you get - there will always be something more powerful that can be dug up from the lore.

                                                        Perhaps Mahasuchi hears of the players and finds them a threat - how well can the players defend a highly intelligent lunar warlord using guerrilla warfare against them? A solar with kickass powers is still only able to be in one place at a time... five attacks coordinated to take place at the same time - can't save everything. What will the players prioritize to protect? The farming community? (if not, enjoy the food shortages in three months) The plantation producing stuff they need for industry? (if not, enjoy the material shortfalls soon) The mine? (I hope you don't need iron for your weapons industry) The capital city outskirts (just how terrified do you want your population?)

                                                        just make sure that there are options and ways for your players to actually come out on top - this should NEVER be structured purely as punishment to the players. This is exalted after all, rule of cool and whatnot - but you don't have to tell your players that. If they've gotten so paradigm locked into thinking that only them as a team of adventurers can solve things, without delegating... then show them that they can't be everywhere at once - teach them to delegate - or conversely if they rely too much on delegation, then remind them that sometimes you need to handle things personally.


                                                        And if the players are heavily into mind-controling their population - then consider how robust or weak the mind control is. How exactly is the mind control done? What'll happen if a clever guild merchant shows up somewhere in their nation and convincingly claims to represent the players, demanding all kinds of goods and money turned over to him. Will the players realise that a population without free will is an open invitation for exploitation? Or perhaps the mind control makes the population highly xenophobic? Or maybe the mind control has conflicting orders baked into it - making people's minds lock up on occation.


                                                        Hell, if the players become tyrants... maybe they'll attract another circle of solars who want to save the kingdom from the evil tyrants - and thus the story can come full circle, especially the players end up going "wait... are we the baddies?"
