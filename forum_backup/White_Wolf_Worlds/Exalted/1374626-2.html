                                            Speaking mostly from 2E info: The Sun has two options if he decides to get personally involved. One is going mano-a-mano against the fey hoards which is unlikely to be particularly effective since he can still only be in one place at a time. (Beating Balor's face into the ground being a useful and enjoyable exceptional). The other is to fire up the daystar and start rains of sunfire. Given how far the Fey had penetrated that would likely leave most of Creation that isn't the Blessed Isle in ashes.

                                            Between the two extremes, he could deploy the armies and open the stockpiles of Heaven. Gods are more powerful than mortals, but I highly doubt most of them are martially inclined. Having a lot of panicked socialites on the battlefield would do more harm than good. Yu-Shan may be entirely settled, but it also has enormous estates and massively high standards of living and so lots of space for individuals (who aren't living in their office or on their boss' couch). So their armies may be in the millions, but the Fair Folk were an endless hoard that was attacking the entire border of Creation at once and had only gotten more concentrated as they melted a bunch of it.

                                            With all his Exalts in a box, his only "middle way" option might have been to personally take command of the Heavenly armies, or assign Mars to do so, try to marshal as much of the Elemental and Terrestrial courts as still existed, and try to get the squabbling, panicking Terrestrial Armies to fall into formation.

                                            It is possible that the Old Realm had more conventional (between personal combat and end the world level) military power than Heaven did. (The Solars did seem to love their doomsday weapons.) The Usurpation depleted or destroy most of that war material, and reduced the Terrestrial population immensely.

                                            That would not be an enviable position to be in. Three separate forces that had never worked together, the strongest remaining one having not seen combat in 6k years, the other two massively in disarray, and almost no cnc infrastructure.

                                            Not insurmountable problems, but it looks an awful lot like exactly what was not supposed to happen when the Mandate of Heaven was given.

                                                    Originally posted by Epicurus
                                                    Is God willing to prevent evil, but not able? Then he is not omnipotent.

                                                    Is he able, but not willing? Then he is malevolent.

                                                    Is he both able and willing? Then whence cometh evil?

                                                    Is he neither able nor willing? Then why call him God?
                                                    Epicurus' petulant arguments work better for the Unconquered Sun, who is neither omnipotent or purely good, than they do for God.

                                                    The Unconquered Sun didn't create humanity, nor reality, and thus cannot be a perfect moral arbiter for either.

                                                    He didn't grant humanity free will. Though one could make the argument that he did the next best thing by giving them Exaltation and ownership of Creation, instead of enslaving their species to serve the gods.

                                                    In that sense, he might come close to the idea of a God who granted humanity the freedom to love or reject him. The freedom to defy his will, and go their own way, and to suffer the consequences of doing so.

                                                    Rather like how Creation suffered from the Solar Deliberative, the Usurpation, the Great Contagion, the Fair Folk Invasion, etc.

                                                    But unlike God, the Unconquered Sun isn't allowing these things to happen according to a plan. He doesn't have a perfect afterlife awaiting everyone who obeyed his commands even at the expense of their lives. He isn't planning to eventually end the moral rebellion and set the world back on a harmonious course. He isn't keeping a meticulous record of the crimes of those who, until the end, would rather place themselves before others.

                                                    In the end, the Unconquered Sun is imagined as a man with the powers of a god, rather than a God who designed man.
