    Originally posted by Grod_the_giant View Post
    I think there's no possible way a sourcebook could help with a campaign like that. The entire point of jumping a couple decades into the future is to see the effects of choices the players made. Everything, as much as possible, should ripple out from there-- a broad &quot;this is what happened over the next century&quot; would almost certainly contradict what happened on your personal game, at which point why even bother?

            There's a lot of RPGs that are based on existing franchises that struggle with similar problems to metaplot. Star Wars RPGs, for example, have to contend with the fact that new Star Wars stuff is constantly being made, what is and isn't canon has shifted repeatedly, and so on.

            They tend to very much skew towards characters that aren't going to fundamentally change the setting to help with this. This doesn't mean weak characters though. The RPGs of the big comic book universes tend to use the comic book convention to return to normal (even through massive asspulls and retcons) to justify how the setting never really changes. You don't have to worry about your PC group of Batman characters taking down an alliance of rogues, because they'll just escape Arkham again for whatever module/etc. uses them. Hulk punched Omega Red so hard the villain exploded? [Technobabble] held him together just enough he survived it so his healing powers could kick in, and he can show up later despite people thinking the threat was gone.

            But Exalted is designed towards the exact opposite approach to what the PCs should be capable of.

                    Just some thoughts.

                    Metaplot works in WoD due to the fact that players are supposed to be chumps (I say that endearingly I love the World of Darkness) who are in over their heads while ancient elders engage in a vast Jyhad. In fact the Jyhad or War of Ages that elder vampires were engaged in was one of the central concepts of VtM. And part of the fun of the gameline was following the metaplot and looking for clues to the vast conspiracy to figure out who's doing what and when. Ultimately though when a methuselah kills off a Prince or an Antedluvian wakes up and eats his clan, there's not much that VtM PCs will be able to do about it. For the most part, VtM is a "sink or swim" setting (and to a degree all of the World of Darkness games are too). When the Technocracy obliterates Ravnos with orbital laser beams, that just happens and while that event might alter the course of a campaign, it's not something the PCs would be capable of participating in.

                    In fact WoD metaplot adventures that had PC participation (like the Baba Yaga adventure where the PC's basically sit there and watch a bunch of badass elders do badass things but can't really interfere A) because they're not nearly powerful enough and B) would mess with the metaplot) were generally pretty un-fun. The adventures end up feeling very much on-the-rails where you get to look but not interfere. It makes for cool reading but not nearly as cool gaming.

                    With Exalted, the players are big damn heroes who are capable of easily changing or altering fate. Metaplot falls on its face when the the new book covering the metaplot says that the Bull of the North just conquered the Linnowan and took over Halta, but in your campaign the PC's murdered him and burned Halta to the ground. The metaplot in WoD would fall aprt too if your PC's could just deflect the Technocracy's space lasers and slap Ravnos back into sanity. That's the sort of thing you can do in Exalted. Ultimately Exalted is designed to be a sandbox game where you have a rich and detailed setting, but the setting is largely frozen in time so that the PCs can do what they want and focus on what they want.

                    For the most part I find that as the capabilities of the PC's increase, it begins to cause more and more issues with metaplot. In D&D I ran into this a few times when they would release big metaplot expansions to change stuff around, but I was running a campaign for Epic PCs who would be able to just no-sell those metaplot developments. So I'd have to try to figure out how to have those metaplots happen but not allow the PC's to do anything about it (which the players generally didn't like) or just ignore the metaplot, which is normally what I would do.

                                            Originally posted by KnightFenrirWulfhart View Post
                                            ...
                                            Also, I absolutely love when games have clues and things that are later revealed in metaplot and you're like "I was right?!" Or when a character you hated has had something happen off page and comes back slightly different and you actually get really invested in them. *Chef kiss* ...
                                            So in context of RPGs, this point here is actually a big reason a lot of them don't do metaplot, and why Exalted specifcally is trying to avoid it, especially in 3e. Those clues aren't there for fiction writers to fill in and folks to guess what the writers will do. They're there as plot hooks, that shoudl be used by GMs and players to build on for their own campaigns. RPGs aren't really meant to be a mystery novel that you try to outwit the author. They should provide material to allowf or better games. When a plot hook like that is created and then answered later on by the gameline, it in effect "closes off" that plot hook to many tables. While it is intersting to read and youc an ignore it, if it is soemthing that by being open it wasn't talked about, foten when it is closed it gets talked about in ways that make later supplements and books less useful to people in general unless they follow that particular plot line.

                                            A lot of folks read RPGs for ficiton, and that's fine, but I think folks often forget RPGs should in general probably gear to what's useful for tables. A mantra I remember the current and previous devs had is that if you close off/solve a setting hook, you should introduce soemthing else at least as cool to make up for it. And oftentimes, metaplot is not written in that way, as it treats plot hooks as something later authors can solve, rather than something for tables to use.

                                                Originally posted by Penelope View Post
                                                [USER="741"]I’m a (relative) novice to serious RPG play (as opposed to games in middle school and high school that were more about just hanging out) so I didn’t understand that the power level of the PCs made a difference.
                                                Just remember that "power level" here is directly proportional to the power to change the default state of the setting, rather than how powerful a character is compared to a normal human IRL.

                                                While there's a correlation between "power" and "power to change the world," they're not the same thing.

                                                If you could bring Bruce Wayne into the real world for the purposes of changing the world for the better, but you had to pick Bruce Wayne billionaire CEO, or Batman, but not both? You'd take Bruce Wayne. Bruce's wealth and social influence is far more potent in the real world than Batman's fighting and detective skills will ever be. Bruce's in-universe obsession with being Batman makes him exceedingly more effective at stopping individual instance of bad, but horrible at addressing systemic bad; which has the narrative benefit of meaning Gotham never really changes for long and Batman constantly has enemies new and old to fight.

                                                A lot of RPGs don't really pay attention to "soft power." D&D uses gold primarily as a form of XP (though different editions vary this considerably). Early adventures pay out small amounts of gold, limiting your ability to get awesome/magical gear so your stuff stays roughly in line with your Level. As you progress, the challenges get harder, and the payouts bigger, and what stuff you can buy gets more potent. At the same time though, the price of goods, land, and so on doesn't actually change. If the PCs stopped investing in cool gear, and realized their level 18 adventure netted them enough gold to basically buy a kingdom and live comfortable off of taxes, they can start impacting the setting in very drastic ways the default D&D play-styles don't really account for (and the ones that do generally just treat this as the end-state of the campaign).

                                                Since so many RPGs have PCs gain soft power almost incidentally while focusing on PCs amassing hard power, there's a tendency in RPG talk to treat them as the same (since they will be in a lot of rule sets).

                                                        Originally posted by Penelope View Post
                                                        Wow. I never thought of it that way. Thanks. I guess in Exalted PCs have a lot more soft power than in more traditional RPGs.
                                                        A lot of the Exalted do indeed start with a lot more soft power - the Dragon-Blooded, for instance, while mechanically the "weakest" of the Exalted, have pretty huge cultural power built in. Even a Dragon-Blood who doesn't put a single dot into things like Influence, Backing, or any of the rest of social merits, still has the baseline power of being a Dragon-Blood, which many people in the setting will defer to, or have positive feelings towards, by default. And other Dragon-Blooded are more likely to be willing to work with them, compared to any other type of Exalt, and there are many more Dragon-Blooded with actual positions of power and authority in the setting.

                                                        In contrast, the Solars probably have the least soft power of any type of Exalt at the start, having no strong cultural traditions of respect and authority to call upon, and a lot of people have active traditions considering them dangerous and untrustworthy. But one of the things that makes Solars (and Abyssals and Infernals) so dangerous to the Dragon-Blooded hegemony is the Solars' skill at very quickly building up their soft power. It rarely takes long for a Solar to make a place for themselves in whatever community, and overcome the traditions working against them through their own force of personality and incredible skills with handling people.
