                            I really don't like the uncritical use of "product of their time." Everyone is a product of their time, and everyone also has the ability to think for themselves. Being a product of his times explains why he couched things in terms of bioessentialism, but it doesn't change that bioessentialism is retread eugenics with all the racism and sexism that implies. It doesn't excuse not seeing it for what it was like lots of people at the time did.

                            It also doesn't matter to your question. It's not just "Twitter mob" stuff. Regardless of Gygax's birthday, he pushed heavily for D&D to include bioessentialism successfully, and thus modern RPGs were saddled with racist coded monsters from the beginning.

                            His knowledge of history makes this worse, not better. He was asked, publicly, about what many people see as a paradox in how D&D is constructed: creatures with low Intelligence are True Neutral because they lack the capacity more moral thought. Playable races had enough intelligence for moral thought and could fall along the spectra of alignments. Monster races could be intelligent enough for for mortal thought, but where hard coded as Evil. Should they be "soft coded" as Evil and if you raised an Orc in a Good society they'd be Good despite the books saying Orcs are always Evil? No, because to a bioessentialist that isn't a paradox. You can't raise Orcs to be Good, it's in their fundamental nature (biological essence) to be Evil despite their intelligence.

                            And to drive home the point about this not being fantasy but related to real world views, Gygax referenced a Confederate officer and Indian hunter on the need to kill Native American children to ensure their inherent savagery would be eliminated from the world. So, Gygax himself, said Lawful Good D&D characters should think like a slave owning proponent of genocide; and that's morally acceptable because bioessentialism means some races are just irredeemable like that. He knew his history and referenced that figure specifically. Nobody pulled a "gotcha" on him. He chose to say it.

                                            Originally posted by Lysander View Post

                                            I've never really seen it. When Gygax was making D&D, he basically just lifted everything out of Lord of the Rings. D&D was "wargaming in Middle Earth". Of course, over time Gygax started separating D&D from Lord of the Rings, but it's pretty clear LotR was the primary foundation for D&D. And Tolkien never intended for orcs to be stand-ins for black people. In Lord of the Rings there were some vague real-life inspirations for a few groups, like Hobbits represented British people who wanted to keep their heads down and avoid conflict, but for the most part there aren't very many strong parallels between racial groups in Lord of the Ring and racial groups in real life. There have been people who written that Tolkien was racist and such, but I don't feel that orcs were ever intended to be stand-ins for black people (or Asians or any other real-life racial group) in his writing.

                                            Now in early D&D, one of the basic concepts was "natural evil" that is, races (like orcs) were naturally born evil and even via nurture they could never rise above that. And Gygax made comparisons of orcs to Native Americans, in the idea that they raid and attack towns and such. But that was something that quickly became outdated and even by early 2nd edition D&D you were starting to see exceptions to that concept and seeing the occasional member of an evil race that was of neutral or good alignment. And then towards the end of 2nd edition edition you were beginning to see that those exceptions weren't very exceptional.

                                            These days, of course, any links between real-world groups and fantasy groups in D&D are gone, and most all of the "traditionally evil" races have large non-evil subgroups, and the gameline goes out of its way to make it clear that nobody is "born evil" but rather it's due to societies (usually under the sway of evil gods) that cause certain races to be largely evil, so those concepts no longer exist within the gameline.

                                                While Tolkien didn't intend for orcs to be black, the framing wasn't great. That said, there is a lot of intentional coding in the LotR. Tolkien might not have meant it as derogatory, but it always suffers from what happens when a person with zero connection to minority or foreign cultures tries to include them as fantasy parallels: you get stereotypes that are insulting even if the intent was a compliment. It's pretty hard to escape things like Tolkien saying that his dwarves were meant to be coded as Jews. His decision to make the Christian coded peoples of the West (elves, hobbits, some men) as being more resistant to Sauron's influence because of their spiritual connections to Eru Iluvatar, while Southrons (aka men from Middle Earth's equivalent to North African/Middle Eastern peoples) are easily corrupted by Sauron by rejecting the Valar, is not something you can just handwave away as innocent. You can try to repeat one of the countless arguments against the simple facts of the matter, but it's easier to just accept that LotR definitely promotes European Christians as correct and thus other peoples and cultures are inferior and should be ruled over by the divinely ordained true Numenorians.

                                                ------------

                                                The problem with, "and now the old links are gone," is that they aren't gone, just finally more history than current reality. The more people push to just have inclusive fantasy because it's fantasy and who cares if you want to have more diversity in characters, the more there's push back to return to the "proper days" of RPGs where things like Gygax's bioessentialism was normal. This links are like persistent weeds: you need to keep tending the garden and pulling the weeds up before they spread, or unfortunately there's more than enough people out there that will gladly have weeds to make the garden too ugly for others.

                                                        I'm not sure that really goes with what Tolkien's writings imply... though he waffled a lot about the Fourth Age and whether he wanted a cyclical myth or the Fifth Age and later to shift from the mythic past of Middle Earth into the modern history of our world.

                                                        He never really presented the Numenoreans as not having a divine right to rule (Aragon was made king for exactly this reason, and he and his son alone ruled for as much of the Fourth Age as Tolkien ever decided on as "proper" noble rulers that reunited the Numenorean kingdoms and shepherded in an age of prosperity and peace for the hobbits and middle men of the west), just that they were still flawed, and many mistook imperialism for their duty towards true nobility. The Kin-Strife happened well before the events of LotR, and Aragorn's ascension and ideal dynasty kinda refute the idea that Numenor was best forgotten.

                                                        I don't think the eugenics critique works in the slightest... royals have been picky about their bloodlines for thousands and thousands of years in reality for the simple reasons of practical inheritance and the more ephemeral narratives used to justify absolute hereditary monarchy. Eugenics isn't about ensuring the royals and noble aristocrats have their bloodlines in order, but the entire society needs to be "pure."

                                                        It still doesn't really escape that the Westrons via the Numenoreans got the objectively correct religion of Eru, and were purposefully coded by Tolkien to be the secret mythological ancestors of the Germanic peoples of Europe. Who would then find the right religion again, and the British Empire would become (by most metrics) the largest empire in non-mythological history just in time for Tolkien to be alive. I don't think his writings and known comments really adamantly state that he really meant that to be the overt message in all this, but at the same time I really don't think he did much to avoid the implications of the narrative he set up. However much he was public in his disgust at folks like Wagner for using mythology to base German propaganda and well know stories, Tolkien was at best very blind to how much his own work played into British propaganda. As you said, the perspectives of those that experienced European colonization of their lands are going to view the work in a very different light than the people that benefited from colonialism.
