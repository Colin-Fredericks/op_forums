    I don't have a good title here

    04-16-2015, 10:12 PM
    My dad just died.

    I was at work. I had just clocked out a little late; I usually get overtime on Thursdays because of my work schedule. He called my sister's girlfriend and told her he was having chest pains and wanted to go to the hospital, but not right away because he needed to change his shirt. It was soaked through with sweat, he said. She told him to chew some aspirin and be waiting outside for her. She got the children into the car as fast as she could and got over there; I doubt he waited six whole minutes. When she got there, he was pale and indeed drenched. She's an RN, so she knows what's what.

    "Tell my kids I love them," Dad said, and got into the car. As best I can figure, this is the moment when my sister called me. I was already driving home, but it wasn't a big deal to turn around and get to the ER.

    They tried to keep him talking during the drive. He explained he'd been out gardening, planting some strawberries. He chatted as best he could, but about two miles away from the hospital, he stopped. My sister's girlfriend stopped the car, pulled him out onto the shoulder of the road, and began administering CPR while my niece shrieked and wept and panicked. My sister's girlfriend called 911 and got an automated loop telling her to remain on the line and an operator would be with her shortly. She tried flagging down every car that passed, including two sheriff's cars, but the only one that stopped was a fairly useless (though good-hearted, for which I honor him) man who really couldn't assist but was willing to listen to the 911 busy signal until a dispatcher finally picked up.

    I didn't know the details, but my sister and I knew this was happening because we'd been waiting at the ER for far too long and any attempts to call her girlfriend went straight to voicemail. In that situation, there's only one scenario that really makes sense: She wasn't there because she was pulled over, and she wasn't answering her phone because she was on her phone. To pass the time, my sister called her doctor friends and her nurse friends, and they came down to help.

    An ambulance was dispatched. The EMTs gave him CPR the entire way over. My sister's girlfriend arrived and gave her car keys to one of my sister's nurse friends so she could drive the car somewhere else where the kids wouldn't have to see the scene. Dad was wheeled into the ER, and they worked on him for twenty minutes before calling it.

    They took us back to see him. Death diminished my dad so much. He was very tall when he was alive, but short on the gurney. I don't know how that works. Half of his skin was purple from bruising and the other half was yellow from blood pooling. His eyes were partially open and had nothing there, just glass orbs staring out. It was the most horrible thing I'd ever seen until we closed his eyes, which was worse somehow. I don't know how so don't ask. I touched him on the head and felt nothing moving in there; if you've ever touched a corpse, you know how there's an imperceptible amount of movement in a living thing that shakes and rattles the body. Dad was still, and touching him was like touching a warm but rapidly cooling lump of clay.

    My sister wept. I tried to cry and couldn't really because that's not how it works for me. My sister's girlfriend kept apologizing for not saving him, and I tried to be kind to her, because there's nothing she could have done differently and I don't blame her for anything.

    After a while we left the ER. We went to my sister's house. I stopped first at my dad's to get his dog and his keys so we could lock the place up. I hate that house so much. It is a very Dad house, and I dread the thought of going back to put paid to it.

    I'm going to stop telling this story now.

                      When I held my dog in her last moments, as they injected her, I saw the transition from living being to just matter. It was perceptible-even if I hadn't been holding her, I could certainly describe the invasive stillness.

                      When my father died, I hadn't seen him for a while before hand. Didn't see the corpse for a few days more. So, when I saw him, I was more prepared to see an object, and I saw just that. I'm glad, in retrospect, that no one allowed me to touch it.

                      I've meditated on the difference a lot since the end of February, and I've come to a conclusion:

                      No one should have to see that transition in anyone, particularly family. The sight of the immediate loss of elan is too harsh a thing to see, I think, and I feel sorry for anyone who is broken by that.

                      I'm sorry, Jim. I'm sorry that you saw that transition, and I'm sorry that you have to go through this and more, and I'm sorry that sorrow can't do more here.
