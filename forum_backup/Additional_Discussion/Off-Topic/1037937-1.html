    Solar and wind power require a lot of land space, and wind turbines harm birds. They're also dependent on weather patterns. I love to see more clean power being generated, but solar and wind are still far from perfect from an environmental perspective.

    I would like to see more investment in, and studies done on, liquid-fluoride thorium reactors. Thorium has a significantly higher melting point than uranium (the most common nuclear fuel) which not only reduces the risk of meltdown in and of itself but allows for the fuel chamber to be designed with a plug that melts before the thorium does--in the event that the reactor gets too hot, the plug melts, the fuel drains and the heat dissipates.                 A lot of the nuke power plant tech has to do with waste, and potential waste products, and the rest tends to be the lack of improvement due to multiple reasons...

                Bear with me, Nuclear energy, the north american power grid and alternative energy systems are something of a hobby horse topic of mine.

                One of the big reasons the majority of currently used plants in the US generate so much radioactive waste is the specific designs chosen, in large part due to political concerns over the years. See there are a lot of more efficient designs, but most of them (including most of the low-pressure molten salt reactors) have the *potential* to theoretically result in material that could be utilized in weapons... There was a lot of political concern about un-monitored weapon production many decades ago. So there's a drive to stay with the proven no-legal-headaches designs. That got changed a bit as some laws got tweaked in the Obama administration.

                On the low-pressure liquid thorium (molten salt) reactors they do indeed have a LOT of potential and have since the 60's when they were invented, trialed, and even scoped/evaluated for the power source for military bombers. Unfortunately the ideas while going through an uptick right now, have been rather dormant for quite a while. As a result the problem with them is not a need to do research... we've built research reactors from the designs, even ones that are quite small. There are two catches, first is that they're in that technically-possibly-this-might-be-able-to-make-trace-amounts-of-enriched-weapon-material band... bearing in mind, this is one of those situations where *technically* it happens, and *technically* if someone wanted to go to the effort to make a way to filter out plutonium from the running molten core, they could get material that with a lot of enrichment effort could make a weapon... but it's not like you could turn a tap and it goes kind of thing, it's a handful of atoms transform and exist for a duration measured in hours before they're changed again. The second catch, and by far the bigger one, is that there are no questions about the basic physics or functionality, but there ARE concerns and questions about long term durability, what sort of repair/replacement is needed over a long period of time and that sort of thing. Molten salt is particularly rough on components, but the rest of the system has questions as well. It's not that there's a *problem* with that in itself, but what it means is that the only real way to get solid data on it is to build a commercial scale MSR or ten, study them aggressively for up to 20 years and produce the charts of risks and operational procedures.

                And that's where the problem comes in. Because it's such a high capital investment to get the data, nobody really wants to jump in and start building commercial test plants.


                And it's unfortunately not just Nuclear power that has those questions raised once the plants start running. The latest big example of that is the Ivanpah* solar power plant. The last time I checked up on it (a year or two ago now I think) they had just significantly increased the amount of natural gas they could burn there for the second time... To the point where if they burned all the natural gas they were allowed to purchase in a somewhat clean modern natural gas power plant it would match the projected output of the solar plant.

                And yeah, there's the lesser problems with wind turbines and birds and bats and so on. Every form of electrical generation has problems. My personal favorite "whoops" example was when they tried to install tidal generators on the bay of Fundy about 7-8 years ago. The turbines lasted I think something like 2 weeks before the blades were torn apart from the tidal forces. Again, a case of the tests all working out, the engineering seeming sound but when it hits commercial production... problems.


                *-If you're not familiar with it, it is a giant solar power plant. It operates by using mirrors to focus light on a tower to heat a liquid salt material, which is then used to heat water, and do all the normal power plant things. The catch is you need to keep the salt molten and flowing even when there's not light from the focused sunlight to heat it... like all night. A feat they achieve by heating it with natural gas.

                        "CANDU advantages
                        CANDU stands for CANada Deuterium Uranium, because it was invented in Canada, uses deuterium oxide (also known as heavy water) as a moderator, and uranium as a fuel.
                        CANDU fuel bundle
                        CANDU fuel bundle. Source: Nuclear Waste Management Organization.
                        CANDU reactors are unique in that they use natural, unenriched uranium as a fuel; with some modification, they can also use enriched uranium, mixed fuels, and even thorium. Thus, CANDU reactors are ideally suited for using material from decommissioned nuclear weapons as fuel, helping to reduce global arsenals.
                        CANDU reactors can be refueled while operating at full power, while most other designs must be shut down for refueling. Moreover, because natural uranium does not require enrichment, fuel costs for CANDU reactors are very low.
                        CANDU reactors are exceptionally safe. The safety systems are independent from the rest of the plant, and each key safety component has three backups. Not only does this redundancy increase the overall safety of the system, but it also makes it possible to test the safety system while the reactor is operating under full power."

                            Originally posted by Omegaphallic View Post
                            "I had nearly forgotten CANDU reactors can use Thorium.
                            Yep, we made quite a tidy sum of money selling them around the world several decades ago. Unfortunately they have that same issue I mentioned above in that the design can be used to manufacture weapons grade plutonium (considerably more easily than a thorium MSR)... there was a lot of political tension when we were setting them up in... Turkey I think? Anyway, it's a solid (if a bit old now) design. They are still a high-pressure design with the risks that entails... If a low pressure MSR has failure, even one as bad as say a massive break in the core, pretty much the worst that can happen is the molten core leaks out, cools down and turns to a moderately radioactive lump. Even if someone is actively trying to destroy one, and piles massive amounts of explosives around the core... you're going to get moderately radioactive lumps scattered around, but again... that's about it. Easy to clean up, and the worst-case-accident is able to be cleaned up with a few dump trucks. If a high pressure system has a catastrophic failure you get a lot of lightly radioactive material scattered very far. Fukushima for example.

                            But the Candu's are cool. I actually ended up running some rough numbers on them for power plant consumption and output for a World of Darkness style game at one point on them. (Story-teller time mode activated)

                            See, one of the things about sea water is that it has uranium in it. Quite a lot. We can filter it out if we want (and have done so in trials) but generally there's no reason to bother because Uranium is not expensive. So I wanted something that would work for a Pentax project... you know the kind, pointlessly evil and environmentally destructive and profit generating for no particularly good reasons. So I settled on Pentex getting the rights to build desalination plants around New York City, doing a whole big publicity launch complete with a water drop corporate mascot. The whole new jobs, more employment, wonderful things for the city, cleaner water for the masses all that jazz. Of course what they *actually* built were completely automated plants that stripped the uranium from the seawater, used it to power reactors (on the same site) and used the power generated to desalinate the water, all in a fully automated system so that nobody but automated security would normally be in any of the plants. Being Pentex of course, they had secondary plans to use the water to slip drugs to the whole of the city. And in order to make sure that the whole of the city was consuming the desalinated water from their plants, well of course the incoming water supply lines to New York City had to have a "structural failure".

                            Still the whole point is that the interesting math I worked out was that with the amount of uranium extracted from seawater as it runs through a desalination plant is about three times the power needed for the most power-hungry of desalination plants.

                            Kinda makes me want to see if it would actually be possible to build something like that in real life, an automated power plant and desalination plant that also powers the local town.
