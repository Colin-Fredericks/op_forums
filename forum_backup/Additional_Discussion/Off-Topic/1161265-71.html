    Originally posted by nofather View Post

    That's an unfair take on the drow situation. Just like elves and any other group that was on top, when you're there, you to cut out opposition. Because aggression and control of resources equals a win, whatever alignment you are. But this isn't even 'just' an evil thing (though it leans towards it, based on D&D's description). The fact that Vhaeraun and Ghuanadaur worship is rising and Lolth's grasp of things is diminishing doesn't seem to support the idea that 'evil groups don't try' because these are both evil groups, just like the Lolth priesthood was. But even beyond that it's clear that the various drow peoples in the Underdark have at least attempted to make things better for themselves, by conquering others and trade, just like the sun and eladrin elves did. And it's gotta be harder to live underground like that than it is on the surface, even if you don't have humans out-competing you.

    But even without talking about technology you don't have that much difference in the status of 'good' elf races on the surface and 'evil' ones in the underdark beyond them acting within their alignment. Are you just taking the view that 'good alignment = progress'?

    No, no. My point was that the *current* status of the drow in the Realms does make sense, because you have nuance. Vhaeraunites are evil (and not all of them are, actually) but reasonable, and they're trying to improve the life of their people. Eilistraee and her followers are good, and they're building a bright future for their people. You have entire subcultures of drow that show a different take on life and the world.

    My point also was that the FR drow would start to show their cracks if they only had Lolth, a deity that has explicitly (The Drow of the Underdark, by Ed Greenwood) been described as limiting the potential of the drow and hampering their attempts at improving their conditions (because she doesn't want them to develop, she wants them to be her puppets). A deity whose dogma reads like a bunch of edgy nonsense based on denying fundamental aspects of life. By merely following her edicts, the drow would show exactly what I said: absolute lack of attempts at improving their conditions. They would merely follow an endless downward spiral of conflict, hatred and stagnation.

    In short, I was arguing that in order to make a race like the drow feel compelling, there's a need to add that kind of nuance that deities like Eilistraee and Vhaeraun, or factions like Bregan d'Aerthe bring. You can't rely just on a handful of super dudes who are magically born different to make races like this compelling.

    A different point could be made for evil races that are not self-destructive and whose people can generally expect a good life. In those cases, it'd be more a matter of individuals than groups or subcultures, because in those cases it would be more a matter of morality than achieving a decent, satisfying life. There wouldn't be a dramatic condition that people would need to escape, like the condition of so many drow under Lolth, so there wouldn't be the need to add various dissident groups that would inevitably form because of such a condition. An Evil Empire (TM) that doesn't abuse its citizens would be an example.
    When I said that the Lolthite drow never really attempted to improve their conditions I was pointing that they don't make a whole lot of sense from a world-building PoV because they mostly stayed there, stagnating, while they had all the reasons to seek different paths. Every time such a different path was sought in the Realms, it happened by getting further and further away from Lolth, and that is why I said the drow would feel less and less compelling if they only consisted of Lolth&her followers.

    That said, "good alignment" tends to provide a lot of the things that the drow miss in their life, but I've explained it in my first post about Eilistraee.

                I mostly eschewed the idea of innate alignment the first time it became limiting (which is to say when I was like, 13) and it takes little effort overall to do so, but it remains frustrating to still be having to fight against that grain after I've been playing D&D for more years of my life than I haven't been.

                My feeling is that if you're going to have most of a "race" have an innate alignment or predilection towards the same then you have to do something with that information. What are the implications of that? What does it mean? I guarantee that the answer will always be more interesting than the setting as presented. A good example is a game that I played in where the party was confronted by the dilemma of helping a kingdom that concentrated power in the hands of very few and was pretty openly racist. They justified their absolutist aristocracy and explicitly racist policies on the grounds that the group within the kingdom most active in exerting pressure for a more inclusive and egalitarian system were the lower nobility primarily made up of Yuan-Ti. The Yuan-Ti in this case were almost entirely evil and pretty transparent about their intention to exploit and subvert any social progress or erosion of absolutism in the kingdom to do corruption and push their evil agendas. The party was forced to grapple with the fact that the noble we were helping were awful... but also technically not wrong, and that there was no way for us to prevent the Yuan-Ti from perverting any positive change we actually managed. It was a good time.

                        Originally posted by atamajakki View Post
                        I have an idea for some easy discussion: what are you unpopular D&D opinions?

                        I made a Tumblr post with mine and have gotten remarkably little pushback, but I’m curious to see all of yours before sharing mine here.
                        I feel like a lot of mine have kicked off multi-page arguments here. But to reiterate:
                        - 4e was the best edition of D&D to date.
                        - Alignment can work, but only if it’s applied more strictly than traditionally presented.
                        - There is absolutely nothing wrong with meta gaming, and most of the perceived problems with it are the DM’s fault.
                        - There is absolutely nothing wrong with linear adventures, and most of the perceived problems with them are the fault of other flaws in the adventures’ design.
                        - Most “sandbox” adventures are terrible, and designing one well would most likely get you accused of railroading.
                        - A game’s design being “Videogamey” is a good thing.
                        - “Realism” doesn’t matter.
                        - You can win D&D, and the game functions best when the players are trying to do so.
                        - Most DMs should call for a lot fewer dice rolls. The vast majority of actions can and should be resolved without a roll.
                        - Passive skills are great.
                        - Critical hits kind of suck critical failures are even worse. Checks being binary pass/fail actually works really well.
                        - You should tell the player the DC before they roll.
                        - Just, in general, you should give the player more information. They need it to make informed, meaningful decisions, which is what the game is all about.

                        There are probably a lot more, but that’s what I’ve got for now.

                        EDIT: Oh! I remembered a few more.

                        - The Abilities don’t mean anything anymore, and players shouldn’t be expected to “roleplay their Ability Scores.”
                        - The game would be better off without Intelligence, Wisdom, and Charisma.

                            Quoting the ones I share with Charlaquin on at least a basic level even if I'd phrase/frame them differently:
                            Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post

                            - There is absolutely nothing wrong with meta gaming, and most of the perceived problems with it are the DM’s fault.
                            - There is absolutely nothing wrong with linear adventures, and most of the perceived problems with them are the fault of other flaws in the adventures’ design.
                            - Most “sandbox” adventures are terrible, and designing one well would most likely get you accused of railroading.
                            - A game’s design being “Videogamey” is a good thing.
                            - “Realism” doesn’t matter.
                            - You can win D&D, and the game functions best when the players are trying to do so.
                            - Most DMs should call for a lot fewer dice rolls. The vast majority of actions can and should be resolved without a roll.
                            - Passive skills are great.
                            - Critical hits kind of suck critical failures are even worse. Checks being binary pass/fail actually works really well.
                            - You should tell the player the DC before they roll.
                            - Just, in general, you should give the player more information. They need it to make informed, meaningful decisions, which is what the game is all about.
                            - The Abilities don’t mean anything anymore, and players shouldn’t be expected to “roleplay their Ability Scores.”
                            - The game would be better off without Intelligence, Wisdom, and Charisma.
                            Though I have to specify a couple of caveats to the above to make them exactly my opinion; namely that D&D can be "won" but the win conditions aren't exactly what most people expect when hearing someone say that (since it's not either the players win or the DM wins, but either everyone wins or no one does), and that Intelligence, Wisdom, and Charisma being absent would be a smoother transition if there were a 'magic' attribute added in their place so that spell-casting characters don't get an automatic boost via their removal.

                            And now to add ones that I often get a lot of push-back when I mention:
                            - Being a DM is not something that requires some special thing; anyone can do it, and even get good at it, even if they dive right in from not knowing anything about playing table-top RPGs before reading their first rule-book - you don't have to be a master of the rules, or some kind of prodigy, or the best storyteller anyone in your group knows or anything like that, you just have to want to be the DM, and then continue to be the DM (hopefully because you enjoy doing so).
                            - Banning player character options because you don't personally like them is horse-shit. For specific example, there is no reason to say "no one can ever play a gnome" because you don't like gnomes, as you can just choose not to portray any gnomes of your own and make it clear to your player that wants to play one "I'm not a fan of gnomes, so besides your character there aren't going to be many/any of them in play, and we're not going to be doing any storylines or adventures that play up gnomishness."
                            - (this ties into the above) Being DM doesn't make your vote any stronger than your players, no matter what part of the game is being voted on - all the people sitting at the table to play the game are equals, and should be treated as such despite taking different roles to facilitate game-play (so while the DM does break any tied vote, the DM's vote does not outweigh everyone else's vote just because they are willing to DM).

                            Not so noble anymore.
