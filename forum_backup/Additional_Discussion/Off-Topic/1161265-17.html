    Originally posted by Morty View Post

    Then everyone is stuck with the bare-bones systems for non-magical skills and combat. There's really not much to D&D once you remove magic, especially 5E D&D.

    Besides, a martial character past level 7 or so has more combat power than anyone in Lord of the Rings, just by the virtue of how many hit points they have and how much damage they can deal.
    To be fair, a lot of people already play spell-less classes like Barbarians, Rogues, Fighters, Monks, etc, even in the standard magic-heavy D&D system. They even have fun doing it. In all honesty, I've found that D&D tends to run better in low-magic environments, when everyone isn't walking around with +8 magical swords, battles aren't routinely ended by two castings of Fireball, and travel doesn't just become, "We fly there," or "We teleport there."

    So my preference for D&D is running exactly those sorts of low-magic games since I find that once you begin taking out some of the higher-powered magic options the game tends to run a bit more smoothly and be a bit more interesting fun.

        Just two things.

        About bounded accuracy: I liked the idea at first, then, I had the nasty experience of a rogue character of mine failing most of his stealth checks in a game, despite having 17 dex, proficiency and expertise and sometimes even advantage... Yeah, I didn't like it. Still I think in a high magic setting, magic items are somewhat expected to become a kind of technology and constraining them into plot devices and solely under the DM's supervision never made much sense to me. I get it in a low-magic setting, where every magic item is an unique and special artifact (and I totally get why others like that more). In FR, or Golarion? Nay, I don't think so.

                Was your DM setting DCs appropriately? At level 1 with a +3 Dex and Expertise in stealth you should have a 90% chance of succeeding on a moderate difficulty stealth check and a 65% chance of succeeding on a hard difficulty stealth check. Advantage increases that to 99% and 88% respectively. Granted, Stealth DCs are usually set by the passive Perception of the creature(s) you’re trying to sneak past rather than a flat difficulty, but you shouldn’t be encountering a whole lot of monsters with higher than 15 passive perception. That requires either a 20 in wisdom or 18 wisdom and proficieny in Perception, and in either case a 17 Dex Stealth Expertise Rogue should be having slightly better than even odds of slipping past it. High passive perceptions become more common at higher levels, but at that point you’re getting more out of that expertise.

                Don’t get me wrong, if you don’t like 5e’s flatter bonus progression (Truly bounded accuracy didn’t really end up happening), that’s totally fine, you do you. Just this particular experience seems like either a case of a string of unlucky rolls (which can happen in any system) or a DM setting DCs too high (which makes it an inaccurate depiction of the way the system is intended to work).

                                                    Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post

That kind of thing seems to have gone by the wayside in favor of making endgame play pretty much like early game play, but with bigger numbers and more dramatic stakes.                                                     Trades person => Adventurer => Lord => Conqueror/Emperor=> God.

                                                    Lord being initially obtained ~9th level, but fully by 14th. Conqueror coming in at 25th and God at 36th.

                                                    D&D itself had five editions:
                                                    1. The original in 1974
                                                    2. Holmes Basic
                                                    3. Moldvay/Cook's Basic and Expert
