            Size matters not to a Jedi. Size matters very much to the object the Jedi is interacting with. An X-wing is a huge, heavy machine that's only meant to move in certain limited configurations; if you don't distribute its weight evenly and very carefully while lifting it, it will fall apart under its own weight. It would be like how a car should behave when Superman lifts it over his head: Yeah, I'll grant that Superman is strong enough to lift a car over his head, but the car is not structurally sound enough to bear all of its weight on two points as wide as his palms, and it should fall down his body like a hula hoop if it even lasts long enough to get over his head in the first place.

            I believe that is why Yoda had to lift it out of the water so carefully, because more than using the Force to lift something up, he also had to be very aware of the X-wing's stress points and make sure he wasn't picking it up by its wings (which would break them off) or by the body (which would also break the wings off), but rather creating a molded pillow of energy to support every part of the ship according to its structural needs. If he didn't need to be careful with it, if the Force is just magic and can lift cars over Superman's head because fuck it, then Yoda was kind of being a prick by moving it so slowly and show-offishly.

                            There appears to be a lot stretching back through this thread, and I have little interest or patience for it, but one simple thing stands out to me: I find the assertion that we are supposed to see Luke's success at the Death Star as a display of telekinetic manipulation to be a tremendous stretch, barely implied by the film at all. Given that by that point in the film we've seen little show of telekinesis as something that Jedi are actually capable of (Vader has his choking thing, but apart from being distinct to him at that point, it would seem to exist more as an expression of character than a general utility), it would seem to be something both irrelevant and unintuitive as an explanation for his achievement. By that point, the Force has been communicated to Luke in largely spiritual terms, as a subject of perception and intuition and strength over the mind; to make a precise shot without the aid of an inaccurate computer is building upon the scene in which he blocks blaster shots without the aid of his deceitful eyes, not a scene for an unrelated character choking somebody from a distance. Whether or not such an achievement constitutes him being more powerful than Obi-Wan, well... when your experience of the Force so far amounts to matters of certainty, and awareness of a larger world, I would say that being able to step unhesitatingly into death so thoroughly that you completely transcend your crude matter to become a more luminous being is still a few steps beyond making an extremely difficult shot well, and neither are really something that a collection of video game superpowers hold a candle to.
