    Originally posted by monteparnas View Post
    Good mechanics for social interaction isn't the same as being best or worse for RP.
    A big old second to this.

    The ultimate hypothetical RPG seamlessly meshes playing a character (RP) with play the system (G). Any mechanical complexity runs the risk of pulling people out of RP to focus on all the G of the complexity. It doesn't matter if it's fighting, talking, building a castle, or inventing a functional way to start a Mars colony. It's easy to run into the trap of designing fun and engaging systems for these activities that are still G focused and don't do anything to actually make RP better (or worse, but that can get pretty subjective as different people have different needs when it comes to things like immersion for RP and how their sense of immersion handles breaks for mechanics).

    D&D having less social traits in comparison to the WoD doesn't actually mean the WoD has better RP tools - even social RP tools - than D&D does. The pre-V5 the WoD social rules are rather lacking considering how much you can invest your character in being a socially adept individual. I've seen people make solid (if not necessarily compelling to me) arguments that D&D's lower stress on social traits helps RP in D&D because it helps reinforce the focus on the game so you know what kind of characters you're going to be playing than a more open ended system does. They argue that the WoD's plethora of social traits is actually a trap that makes you think it's even return on investment to invest on social things as physical things, when the WoD doesn't actually do that, while D&D's obvious skew towards physical traits is telling you what's the most important thing to invest in for the game side of things rather than getting bogged down on stuff that looks like it will matter to role-playing when it actually doesn't do much in that regard. Keeping in mind that this argument isn't really arguing either system is good for encouraging RP, just that D&D gets out of the way better.

    As noted, more recent systems have done a lot of work to try to actually mesh RP and G more fully. None of them do it perfectly, and frequently many are built to very specific tastes rather than trying to have a broader appeal, but design continues to iterate and innovate new tools.

    But to me at least, listening to debates about whether any D&D or classic WoD mechanics are better for RP is like listening to people trying to debate whether professional baseball players or professional cricket players would make for better professional American football players while both sides refuse to even acknowledge that rugby exists.

Player announced what they rolled, and the DM used their THAC0 including hit tables to see if the player rolled high enough.

            AD&D 2e, 2e Revised, and BECMI, actually moved THAC0 to a player facing mechanic.

Let alone offer a counter that allows for the exploration of why the original hypothesis failed and how to build up a strong one in its place. Which is, lets recall, the goal of the Socratic method: to refine philosophic hypotheses until the questioner has reached a point of inability to successfully challenge the current hypothesis to refine it further.

                            Originally posted by Thoth View Post
                            Socratic method involves asking questions, rather than just doing the equivalent of saying "you're wrong" and sticking ones tongue out.
                            While the Socratic method does rely on asking questions, the point is not to simply poke holes and nitpick. Proper Socratic Method should not just be engaging in JAQing off style rhetoric where questions are used to disguise the intended meaning of, "you're wrong," or sea-lioning, or other such ways in which the end result is simply causing annoyance and aggravation in those you're engaging with. Even Socrates took it too far and ended up being forced to drink poison for pissing people off instead of helping them by failing to temper his questions to his audience.

                            Again, the point of the Socratic method is ultimately constructive. Socratic dialogues are meant to strengthen the original statement by helping to refine weak points in its premises, phrasing, and logic. An ideal outcome is that the defending interlocutor feels fulfilled by the experience because the process resulted in their original statement being stronger and their own beliefs more firmly held now that they can be expressed with greater nuance; their life is no longer unexamined. A good challenging interlocutor needs to be able to ask extremely purposeful questions because finding flaws is the means, not the end. Finding flaws is to eliminate the flaws, and thus refine the opening hypothesis. They also need to be able to offer alternative hypotheses or premises to address the issues at hand in a different way.

                            There's a reason (mostly Aristotle) that the scientific method draws heavily from the Socratic method in its base philosophic concept of how to arrive at the best answer - that is the least flawed answer of the current theory we are best capable of supporting, with scientific consensus replacing aporia - rather than necessarily aiming for perfect truths. Einsteinian physics replaced Newtonian physics not just by asking a question Newtonian physics couldn't address, but also addressing why Newtonian physics seemed right for so long (namely that Newtonian physics are right at the scale human senses normal inhabit and its only after leaving those limits do Einsteinian physics really start to differ).

                            In other words if you disagree about something, you ask about a point of contention to make sure you are understanding correctly, rather than default prepping high horses and or soap boxes.
                            That's not Socratic method, that's just asking for clarity. Conflating the two is bad rhetoric. While asking for clarity is certainly something people engaging in Socratic dialogue should do, it is not, by itself, sufficient to call your rhetoric Socratic method. Yet again, questions in the Socratic method have an explicit end goal to why you're pursuing a line of questioning.

                            Person 1: My subjective opinion
                            Person 2: Interesting, how do you account for A, B, and C?
                            Person 1: A is countered by D and B isn't an issue with this technique, C hasn't really come up so off the top of my head something along the lines of E
                            Person 2: Ah but what about G?
                            This is an excellent example of poor Socratic dialogue, and a good way towards hemlock instead of aporia. Most of the Socratic school of philosophers came to the opinion that it's better to not get into a Socratic dialogue and just accept premature aporia (or as we'd say today, "just agree to disagree"), than to get so wrapped up in asking questions that one loses sight of the goal of the method ("Ah but what about G?" is a strong sign Person 2 is not engaging in Socratic thinking and is instead caught up in the urge to tear down without the imperative to rebuild).

                                            Originally posted by Saur Ops Specialist View Post
                                            Rather, as D&D is fundamentally a hot patch on a 1970s wargame, I would like for it to stop acting like it isn't, because trying otherwise is just an awful lot of "How do you do, fellow children?"
                                            The problem is it is distinctly possible for D&D to be both a hot patch to a 70s wargame, and something more than that. D&D isn't pretending to be something it isn't. It's trying to be the most of the thing that came out of it's origins. I could point to countless examples of different sort of games that started as a hotpatch/mod/whatever of some other game and grew into their own things. They benefit from focusing on what that new thing that made them special and stand out from ever other "we tried out this different thing based on this existing engine" attempt out there in the first place.

                                            What made D&D stand out from other "hotpatches" to wargames was moving from playing the "general" that orders units of largely nameless individuals around the map in military conflicts, to players having one champion that they play specifically. Chainmail "hotpached" 1:20 scale combat by adding in 1:1 combat and a fantasy supplement.... but those alone didn't turn it isn't a different thing. It just gave your rules for two champion units facing off in a one-on-one duel instead of large scale mass combat, and then gave you rules for fantasy creatures on top of your standard Medieval wargame fare. When the original D&D team realized that there was a distinct desire to play original characters over extended periods of time in 1:1 scale, they made a game that centered around just that and dumped the 1:20 stuff for the most part. Thus we got D&D, which become more than just a hotpatch to Chainmail, as it became its own thing after a few iterations. Just like Counter Strike and Team Fortress aren't just Half-Life mods any more. D&D developers might benefit from remembering how it got started, butt hat doesn't mean going back to 1:20 holdovers in the original D&D books, it means focusing on why people wanted to play their own unique characters in a wargame based system in the first place. Team Fortress isn't wrong for getting away from beating up headcrabs with a crowbar to iterate into a greater and greater focus on each class having a unique role in a team based multiplayer FPS. That's what makes Team Fortress fun (and why it's been copied repeatedly by multiple huge FPS games), and forcing everyone to go back to smacking headcrabs with a crowbar to earn their class would be a huge mistake.

                                            Or, in other words, RP is what made D&D stand out, and RP meshed with wargame is what it should be striving to do. Not striving to go back to being Chainmail with OC champion units for 1:1 scale fights.

                                            ...whereas most people wouldn't go to D&D if it weren't about dungeon fantasy fights.
                                            The plethora of RPGs that aren't dungeon fantasy fights indicates otherwise. People go to D&D because D&D is the biggest name in RPGs, so it seems like the obvious place to start. D&D is so dominant that a mediocre sci-fi game based on D&D can easily compete with if not outsell some sci-fi games lots of gamers (even D&D fans) would agree is better at doing sci-fi stuff.

                                            People go to D&D because they want to try RPGs, and D&D seems like the place to start.

                                            If people only went to D&D for the dungeon fantasy fights, the OGL boom wouldn't have happened. It happened because everyone was trying to shove every genre possible into the d20 system that was the most well known system (and the essentials of it still are) in the world. The boom's glut was horrible because you could take almost anything, kludge it into d20 shaped box, slap a d20 logo on it,and sell more then enough copies to make a profit. Even the oWW was guilty of it. Nobody would have made MC's WoD, or the d20 Engel, or d20 Trinity-verse games, if d20's only appeal was dungeon fights.

                                            4e recognized this, and seems better for it.
                                            Except 4e's legacy is basically, the most mechanically sound D&D game, with the best GM tools, and being completely divisive over whether or not it captured the "feel" of D&D or not.

                                            I've never seen anyone hype 4e as the "roleplayer's edition of D&D." It's the, "GMing combat is actually fun for the GM edition of D&D." Focusing on those wargame roots made for some great systems, but failed to capture that spark that made D&D standout in the first place, and you don't really see WotC regretting 5e moving back to closer to 3e in how it runs for it.

                                            When I look at what games like Lancer from Masif Press are doing with the 4e combat engine and better non-combat mechanics, it's clear 4e should be considered a great game. But that doesn't mean it's a great D&D game. As an edition of D&D I just don't see the argument for how it was better for focusing on the wargame stuff and not on the "I want to play my original character" stuff successfully.

That's part of why the first two editions aren't really losing out to 5e in the RP department, either. Secondary Skills are also not to be discounted as a Background-alike; they may lean hard on the DM to flesh them out, but, as noted, that's not really any less of a lean than what 5e is doing about everything, including keeping track of who has Inspiration.
                                            As RP tools, I can't think of any times Themes or Secondary Skills actually encouraged fun RP at the table. Inspiration was handled poorly in 5e too, but Mojo in X-Crawl (which game first but works similarly) and Fortune from Pugmire are directly linked to some of my favorite D&D/d20 base RP moments of my entire gaming life. It is definitely not about the ability to add some flavor and uniqueness to your character with some mildly useful side abilities. It's about a system that actually had incentives to RP your character doing cool things instead of mechanically optimal things.

D&D developers might benefit from remembering how it got started, butt hat doesn't mean going back to 1:20 holdovers in the original D&D books, it means focusing on why people wanted to play their own unique characters in a wargame based system in the first place. Team Fortress isn't wrong for getting away from beating up headcrabs with a crowbar to iterate into a greater and greater focus on each class having a unique role in a team based multiplayer FPS. That's what makes Team Fortress fun (and why it's been copied repeatedly by multiple huge FPS games), and forcing everyone to go back to smacking headcrabs with a crowbar to earn their class would be a huge mistake.

                                                        Or, in other words, RP is what made D&D stand out, and RP meshed with wargame is what it should be striving to do. Not striving to go back to being Chainmail with OC champion units for 1:1 scale fights.
                                                        And that RP probably isn't hampered or enabled one way or another across editions because it, yes, still very much leans on combat rules. RP gets treated as a potential way to avoid them, the part of the game with the most word count dedicated to it.

                                                        The plethora of RPGs that aren't dungeon fantasy fights indicates otherwise. People go to D&D because D&D is the biggest name in RPGs, so it seems like the obvious place to start. D&D is so dominant that a mediocre sci-fi game based on D&D can easily compete with if not outsell some sci-fi games lots of gamers (even D&D fans) would agree is better at doing sci-fi stuff.

                                                        People go to D&D because they want to try RPGs, and D&D seems like the place to start.
                                                        Because of monopolistic business practices, yes. Unlike Xerox, holders of D&D never took many pains to try and note that D&D wasn't all RPGs, but they've never suffered for it the way that Xerox would have if it didn't do that.

                                                        If people only went to D&D for the dungeon fantasy fights, the OGL boom wouldn't have happened. It happened because everyone was trying to shove every genre possible into the d20 system that was the most well known system (and the essentials of it still are) in the world. The boom's glut was horrible because you could take almost anything, kludge it into d20 shaped box, slap a d20 logo on it,and sell more then enough copies to make a profit. Even the oWW was guilty of it. Nobody would have made MC's WoD, or the d20 Engel, or d20 Trinity-verse games, if d20's only appeal was dungeon fights.
                                                        The OGL boom that was followed by a tremendous flood of shovelware that tanked the market, further cementing the aforementioned monopolistic business practices.

                                                        Except 4e's legacy is basically, the most mechanically sound D&D game, with the best GM tools, and being completely divisive over whether or not it captured the "feel" of D&D or not.
                                                        And in mechanical soundness, you can sit back and not worry about things that might otherwise get in the way. Clerics don't have to be healbots, fighters don't have to suck in perpetuity, and the ranger actually has a coherent theme. And if people want to say that isn't keeping the feeling of D&D, perhaps they'd like to have their paladin character fall and get turned into a fighter the moment that they breathe in a way that their code doesn't approve of, which apparently, according to Gygax's John Milton Chivington cheerleading, does not prevent them from massacring orc non-combatants.

                                                        I've never seen anyone hype 4e as the "roleplayer's edition of D&D." It's the, "GMing combat is actually fun for the GM edition of D&D." Focusing on those wargame roots made for some great systems, but failed to capture that spark that made D&D standout in the first place, and you don't really see WotC regretting 5e moving back to closer to 3e in how it runs for it.
                                                        Except that in a game that leans so heavily on combat, the approach to combat is part of the RP, is it not? At the very least, it's a kind of character building touch to have even the basic attacks tailored to an OC as tricks that they've presumably picked up to defend themselves.

                                                        When I look at what games like Lancer from Masif Press are doing with the 4e combat engine and better non-combat mechanics, it's clear 4e should be considered a great game. But that doesn't mean it's a great D&D game. As an edition of D&D I just don't see the argument for how it was better for focusing on the wargame stuff and not on the "I want to play my original character" stuff successfully.
                                                        It had so many more options, not just in spinning Primal off to not be Divine by another name, or by letting Martial shine all around, but in the ritual casting that 5e gutted for no good reason. 5e didn't have to throw those out, but the grognards demanded a purge, so it seems.

                                                        As RP tools, I can't think of any times Themes or Secondary Skills actually encouraged fun RP at the table. Inspiration was handled poorly in 5e too, but Mojo in X-Crawl (which game first but works similarly) and Fortune from Pugmire are directly linked to some of my favorite D&D/d20 base RP moments of my entire gaming life. It is definitely not about the ability to add some flavor and uniqueness to your character with some mildly useful side abilities. It's about a system that actually had incentives to RP your character doing cool things instead of mechanically optimal things.
                                                        If they let someone play something they wanted to but otherwise might not be able to, is that not enabling RP? That's why the Oath of the Ancients is akin to a dump taken on someone's dinner plate - it's not a warden, will never be anything like a warden, yet often gets sold as the closest thing to it. Ditto for the Avenger and Oath of Vengeance paladin. And then there's the conspicuous lack of the warlord/marshal... and no, the batlle master fighter isn't one.
