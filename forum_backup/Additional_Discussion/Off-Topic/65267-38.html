        Originally posted by Dawngreeter View Post
        So. Comics. I finally decided to try and get into them.

        Which isn't to say I have never read comics. Hell, I was even writing one for a time. I loved Sandman, Transmetropolitan, Watchmen, Preacher (never read it through, though), Dark Knight Returns, stuff like that. Classics would be the best way to describe them, I guess. Walking Dead too, though that's not yet a classic. But I never really kept track of the ongoing superhero stuff.

        Back when New 52 came out, I tried getting into it. Felt like a good jumping-on point. Animal Man was FANTASTIC, read about 10-15 issues and then kinda drifted away. Detective Comics seemed really awesome with the face-getting-cut-off Joker storyline but after a short while the story seemed to completely forget about that and I lost interest. And that's about it. Now, Marvel is resetting their stuff and I looked into Doctor Strange first - read just the first issue and I love it. There's something in there that shares Animal Man's DNA, I think. Which is similar to Image's Witch Doctor DNA. I'll stick with it, they're doing something awesome there.

        So, hopefully I'll start keeping track of the superhero stuff a lot more but I'm looking for some pointers from knowledgeable people. There's a crapload of comics out there and it ain't cheap buying all of 'em (though significantly easier to track down now, thanks to Comixology) so any help finding worthwhile ones would be much appreciated. I'm mainly interested in new Marvel and post-New 52 DC (though I have no idea what's going on with them after the initial #1 issues started appearing).
        Iron Man Extremis is a 6 issue run that is pretty good as a jumping in point, plus Iron Man is awesome as a character. Give it a look, it's easy enough to get.

        Alias is also very solid. Not a superhero comic per se, it follows ex superhero Jessica Jones as she tries to make a living as a private detective. I'm told it's similar to the Netflix series, but I haven't seen it so I can't comment. Check out the first couple issues, see what you think.

        She-Hulk is good for a laugh and a pretty interesting character, but YMMV how long you want to stick with it. Slott's run is well worth a look (I think it's called Single Green Female, but not certain. Ran in 2004).

        Comicvine is immensely useful as a resource with following what comes after what, which can be difficult - just in case you don't already know it.

        Hope that's helpful :-)

                                        I was as unimpressed by Sanderson's lectures about magic as I am by his novels, but if anyone's interested there's videos and blogs.

                                        The problem with Dr. Strange is really one that extends to all magic heroes and, occasionally, villains. JimB has it right, for plot reasons a magician can't instantly solve a problem, but once the problem is mostly explored and the reader sees it for what it is, they happen to have a spell that allows them to do it. What bugs me is that while this is seen as an issue with magic characters, it's really something that comes up in all comics. Things like super strength and durability and even speed vary depending upon the writer, with someone like Spider-man able to lift a bus, but his punches not being able to utterly destroy a human in a costume in one shot. How often has a villain pointed out their physical superiority at the end only to have the hero say something like, 'Yeah, but now it's personal' or 'Now I'm mad,' leading to a trouncing of said villain. And technology heroes can be awful about it, with their science often just being magic with pseudo-scientific terms, and that last-minute gadget they've whipped up or 'reversing the polarity' being the equivalent of that spell Dr. Strange or Dr. Fate picks up.

                                        Still, interesting stories are told with these other characters, while somehow it becomes a roadblock with magic heroes.

And technology heroes can be awful about it, with their science often just being magic with pseudo-scientific terms, and that last-minute gadget they've whipped up or 'reversing the polarity' being the equivalent of that spell Dr. Strange or Dr. Fate picks up.

                                            Still, interesting stories are told with these other characters, while somehow it becomes a roadblock with magic heroes.
                                            I think it's particularly egregious in how Marvel handles mystical characters. Doctor Strange is typically used in one of two ways. He's either a deus ex machina where his powers suit whatever is convenient for the story, or he's essentially just another superhero with levitation subbing for flight, arcane bolts subbing for laser eyes, etc. Other characters like Scarlet Witch have the same problem. I think it can be seen in more tech based characters as well, but writers are getting better at making it natural within the context of the setting. There tend to be more underlying roles to Marvel pseudo-science than Marvel mysticism.

                                            I don't think it has to be that way though. DC has (possibly had?) a pretty robust mystical setting in part because of Vertigo's popularity in the 80s and 90s and in part because they continued to foster that little corner of the universe (Michael Moorcock famously wrote a "magic bible" that was the anchor for their core line mystic books), and the result is a lot of interlocking and interesting mythologies. I think the problem is that Marvel has never really supported many books with magical characters.

                                            I mean, regular readers can generally understand the style and some of the underlying roles of Tony Stark tech or Reed Richards tech, but I don't think Doc Strange has had the space to develop a signature to his magic.

                                            tl;dr: Whether you're talking about magic or pseudo-science, a setting is only as developed as the space it's given, and it takes time and effort to develop the internal logic of a character's world. Strange unfortunately doesn't have that.

                                                Yeah, I think the past has shown that you can have sort of ruleless or rules-lite magic characters who are both engaging and not walking deux ex machinas. And I've read interviews where the editors have said their big hesitance in making another Dr. Strange comic is they haven't found a 'magic system' they like.

                                                Another problem with Strange is that he's the Sorcerer Supreme, basically the most powerful magical force in the entire dimension, and his duty is, more or less, to defend it. In the case of Dr. Strange losing, the entire dimension ends or at least changes so drastically you wouldn't recognize it. The X-men have this problem with Apocalypse, whose 'win condition' requires an entire spinoff alternate universe. And while Age of Apocalypse is cool, because it's the only thing that could happen if Apocalypse wins you know when he's showing up he's not usually going to conquer the entire world, enslave humanity and pit mutants against one another. Just like you know when Dr. Strange goes up against Shuma-Gorath or Dormammu, Dr. Strange isn't going to lose and turn the Marvel Universe into the Dormammu Universe. Which is a meta way of thinking, I admit, but Hellblazer focused on John Constantine and really got readers engaged in his flaws and weaknesses, and Sandman and Lucifer focused on characters with boundless power but the stories were told on a mythic scale. Dr. Strange really hits a weird medium for that, because he's got his flaws, but he's also the Sorcerer Supreme so he has some level of purity about him. And he's really powerful, but as a heroic figure he can't go around just wrecking peoples lives.

                                                And the support network. Dr. Strange has some allies, but they exist in a weird untouchable state as well. Wong the eternal manservant, even when suffering illness of his own. Clea, another Sorcerer Supreme. They've tried getting Strange hooked up with plucky girl apprentices, something that, looking back, seems to be weirdly common for magic comics, but they stop at really engaging them fully with Dr. Strange, and they usually don't last. I'm a big believer in support and background characters doing a lot of the work in fleshing out the world of the main character. Paul Dini is great for that.

                                                All that said, it's why he's pretty good as a back-up in other comics. During Civil War and afterwards he was a tired mentor who had a lot of impact on the Marvel Universe while, as far as I can recall, not even having his own comic. He supported Captain America's side and kept their Avengers concealed from Capekiller squads, he used forbidden magics to try and put down the Hulk in World War Hulk, lost the title of Sorcerer Supreme and guided the Avengers against Dormammu's acolytes, acted as an adviser to Magik and Pixie. He gives characters some room for development with a sense of the Marvel Universe at large, while not just zipping in and solving all their problems.

                                                Maybe this current run will manage it, the first issue didn't really interest me much but I'll try again once the first couple story arcs are complete. I always want to like magic comics.

While Strange tends to fight inhuman - usually demonic - entities, which apart from often being Chaotic Evil more or less by definition, just don't carry the same moral weight with readers. If Spider-man killed Doc Ock, then that would be crossing a moral line that just isn't the case if Strange were to kill Dormammu. Also, the undefined nature of the magic comes into play: Spider-man can't really do much if he can't physically touch the opponent, while Strange just has so many more options that it starts to strain belief that he can't figure out a way to bind Dormammu or Nightmare to a rock and keep it in his attic.

                                                        Well-done magic characters like Constantine I think work because they emphasise the cost of doing certain things, or the limitations of the character. Constantine saves the world in the short-term, but in the long term is arguably doing more harm, and his means aren't exactly moral either, so there's an edge, a conflict there. While Strange (based on very limited reading) basically waves his hands, maybe says an incantation, and problem fixed, with no greater cost than maybe he's a little tired. Maybe. It's just really, really hard to get engaged with a character who is fairly bland as characters go, and is so rarely challenged.

                                                        That's my view, anyway.
