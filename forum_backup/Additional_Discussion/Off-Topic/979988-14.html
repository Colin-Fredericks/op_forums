                        Originally posted by Sergeant Brother View Post
                        As for the possibility that some aspect of home schooling might lead a child to less success than some ore option - that may be true. Then again, who gets to define a successful life?
                        This question is essential to the root of why homeschooling is as successful in the US as it is: US educational policy lacks a clear principled concept of why we educate children, and thus public education is constantly a mess as different philosophies of education are haphazardly smashed together in the mix of local, state, and federal directives.

                        Since we, as a country, have no idea what we want schools to accomplish, private enterprises from home-schooling to expensive private schools perform well because they can set their own educational agendas.

                        I get this can seem a funny thing (a lot of people respond to "what's the point of school?" with, "teach kids stuff!" without ever really digging into what we're trying to teach them), but defining success is something we don't really do as a country; which fails students of all types of education.

                        Without a social guiding principle to measure to, my best answer to this though is "success" in primary and secondary education is optimizing the potential of the child to pursue their own careers and lives after they are educated. I know that's vague, but it's unavoidable. I do think this goes beyond basic skills like arithmetic and reading though. Civics is a good example. As members of a democracy, all adults should understand how their democracy works so they can advocate for their political beliefs.

                        I don't trust the government to decide what the absolute truth is to the degree that it should force it on every child in the nation, I see that as highly dangerous. So if somebody wants to teach their children that the earth is 6000 years old, I may not like it and I intend to teach my kids evolution, but I wouldn't think of forcing evolution in their children just as I wouldn't want those parents forcing young earth creationism on my children.
                        Phrasing this as a matter of "truth" is extremely problematic to me. Again, I went to a religious school. I was taught my faith's dogma on the creation of Earth and life in one class, and proper scientific method in another. How to reconcile these things were not "facts" but discussions and invitations to study and experiment with personal philosophies.

                        The government doesn't mandate schools teach "absolute truth" but rather teach our best understanding of science in science classes. Teaching children that the Earth is 6000 years old as a matter of scientifically valid "fact," its as bad as teaching a child that "2+2 = 13" as a matter of mathematically valid "fact." It's making that child scientifically illiterate. Teaching your child the Earth is 6000 years old as a theological position is a different matter, and teaching your child how to approach conflicts between science and faith is an additional matter.


                        Originally posted by Totentanz View Post
                        We've got one party that wants to keep loving adult couples from being married, and another that wants to force people to bake cakes.
                        This sort of false equivalence is extremely counter productive. Nobody wants to created forced baker camps. The bakers are going to be making those cakes anyway, that's already what they voluntarily do for their profession. The only thing being forced on them is that sexual identity doesn't constitute a valid reason to deny service (and in some states assumed sexual identity since the laws are phrased that denial of service is allowed based on belief not fact).

                        This is also something most people have long since considered settled case law. The same exact thing was tried in response to desegregation of private businesses. White business people would claim it was their right to deny service on any reasoning they saw fit in order to refuse service to black patrons. That argument didn't fly. "Public accommodations" even fully privately owned ones have been held to strict standards of anti-discrimination regulations for nearly 50 years.

                        When you go into a business that qualifies as a public accommodation, you are going into a business that requires you to set aside your personal beliefs if they contradict the publicly demanded anti-discrimination regulations those businesses operate under.

                        What the left wants in this case (and frankly plenty of right-leaning people as well) is that we recognize sexual and gender identities in the same class as ethnic identities; that the law shouldn't mandate you can't discriminate against black people in your bakery, and then not mandate the same for LGBTQ individuals.

                        Basically, we already "force" people to bake cakes, the fight is over expanding who you "have" to bake cakes for.

                                    Testing is important. The problem is the concept of using testing to punish schools. Low test scores should be used to identify schools were we spend more resources to find out why they're failing and what we can do about it, instead of ensuring that underfunded schools stay underfunded by cutting their resources so they can't adequately teach.

                                    That's the thing about testing. The point is to give the test-giver and the test-taker feedback on how well things are going. If a kid fails a test, you don't give them a suspension, you look for ways for them to get more/better/etc. education so they don't fail again.

                                    Test making... isn't actually that profitable. The test makers rake it in on test prep courses for well off students that can afford having the extra tutoring with materials written by the test makers. It's like video games. Consoles don't make money, the games do, but you need people to buy your console to get them to play the games. Test makers need the tests in schools so they can make their money writing study programs for passing the tests.

                                    Administrators are definitely a bigger issue. Money is part of it, but not the totality of it. Distribution of them is a big deal. A rural district with one high school gets the roughly same amount of administrative staff as an urban district with four high schools. This of course is idiotic from an economic stand point, but it also drastically impacts how useful administrators can be. School Boards are an entirely different beast though, because most board members are not paid, have no idea about education, and have insane control over how schools operate.

                                    Politicians... education is a football. I don't think it really makes any of them anything. They all promise to fix it, none of them do, and at this point it's just a standard campaign promise made because you have to make it, with exceedingly few politicians actually working on it as a serious problem.

                                                        Charter schools tend to work best as a supplement to traditional public schools. They're capable of focusing more completely on forms of education that serve a more niche group (such as online education for students that would have any of a number of issues with attending a traditional physical school) and/or be the cutting edge of new styles of education since it is much easier for charter schools to incorporate new research into education compared to traditional schools.

                                                        Charter schools tend to be less effective when they're used to create "public" religious school options (since there's already a lot of competition there with traditional private religious education, they have to compete by cutting corners leading to the above example of a lapse in accreditation and credit transfer), or when they're attempts to privatize pubic schools which leads to a lot of corruption in the system as profits trump effective education.

                                                        This is, again, a concept that can work just fine. There are European public school systems that balance these things well, so it can be done, Implementing it in the existing US system has proven a very mixed bag. In an ideal world, we could revamp the system and use these things well since it's clear these are tools that could make things better. Of course... well... the world is far from ideal.
