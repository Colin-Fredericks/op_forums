        For me it really depends on how I experience the media itself.

        When it's things like books, I always want to have a physical copy if I can even if I use a digital copy 99% of the time when I'm just looking something up when it comes to my TTRPG collection. I find reading a physical book more enjoyable when I want to actually sit down and spend some time getting into it. I'm much more likely to find tasty ideas when physically reading that I tend to skip when it's digital (most people here are very familiar with some of my more common typos... I have a friend that didn't even know this account was me but figured it out from those alone, and that comes from a bit of a mental disconnect I have with digital text where I don't process it as thoroughly as I do printed or written text).

        For things like music, TV, and films, I used to be more physically inclined, but I've largely shifting to digital. These days I only worry about physical media for these if either I know it's something I want to be able to experience with a group (my home TV is basically a mini-theater for when friends come over and we want to watch a movie together at this point), or it's explicitly a choose to support specific artists/creatives by upgrading my purchase.

        For video games, I've moved rather strongly towards digital. I mean, it's already a digital media in the first place, the physical media only started as a simple way to get a digital file from the distributor to our homes. While we mentioned the storage issues of optical discs earlier... SSDs keep growing to pace. You can get a 4 TB thumb drive, while the current largest game on the market if you include all of the add-ons and DLC hits ~500 GB. Though SSDs can't replace physical media for storage (files generally degrade within a year if not plugged in, and most SSDs will start to crap out in about a decade outside of highly optimized conditions), if your collection is growing and you're keeping up with storage needs you're highly unlikely to lose data permanently unless something happens that's a much bigger problem than archiving your collection.

        That all said, I really wish there was more energy towards digital archiving. Way too much is being handled by enthusiasts and not large scale organizations. Which means there's minimal R&D going into better forms of digital storage meant for archival purposes. The fact the the sun could just fart out a CME that happens to be big enough and gets close enough to Earth and boom... decades of human creativity gets erased... seems to not be a concern to nearly enough people. I know governments and businesses aren't run on altruism, but they all use extensive databases that aren't backed up in a fashion that can withstand a sudden high energy EMP (whether natural or something humanity ends up inflicting on itself). These are institutions that need records, and more and more those records are getting too big to back up on paper alone. Not funding solutions to long term digital storage is incredibly silly... but warning them hasn't worked for decades... so I don't have a lot of hope it'll change before something bad happens.

                                            While I think we're currently living in an era with a lot of perverse incentives when it comes to how manufacturers can maximize profits that leads to consistently negative consumer outcomes, there's a point where it stops being cutting corners and just becomes accepting that reality doesn't do perfection. There's always going to be a tipping point where the diminishing returns of more quality assurance ends up increasing prices higher than people could afford anyway. It's always going to be some level of balancing act.

                                            That's why it's better for everyone if we go in the direction monteparnas pointed: get as many people off of reliance on single modes of transportation. If it's viable to cycle/e-bike, drive, take a bus, or take light rail to do all of your regular travel, not only do you have choices so your normal method suites your lifestyle, you have backups if something goes wrong and you're not stuck without any options until you address whatever happened. Redundancy is generally the most efficient counter to inevitable breaks in the system.

                                                Originally posted by Lysander View Post
                                                In a ideal world Montparnasse, all vehicles would be built for long term reliability with no corners cut in making of automobiles.
                                                There are lots of reasons why modern cars are built how they are, not all of them about cutting corners. Actually there's a bunch of brands that don't.

                                                In part, modern cars are made to break easily for a very serious reason: that makes accidents more survivable. I lived in the time when cars were made of durable materials.

                                                Between the greater energy transfer to the passengers and the survivors getting stuck in a distorted mass of steel, even with lower speed car crashes were way more lethal.

                                                Cars are still lethal. On top of the things Heavy Arms said, how it's important to have options, especially low cost, public options, I simply find the very notion of car as a personal right an absurd.

                                                We're talking a heavy machine that's intensely resource-heavy at every point of its lifetime: a lot of resources to make, a lot of resources on fuel, a lot more on maintenance, then road maintenance, garages, because cars don't just consume a lot, they require infrastructure that isn't a joke. And with power to transport five people plus heavy baggage at considerable speeds.

                                                All so people could feel free and each have one to drive alone, leave unused most of the day, conducting without much care because it is so ubiquitous and necessary it is unfeasible to actually demand from every one the level of caution it actually demands? At no point this was sustainable, and no one thought it would be when the automobile revolution was planned and executed.

                                                Problem is, none of those problems is solvable, because they are a direct result of the proposal of this machine: It will always be big and fast, so it'll always be more resource-heavy and dangerous than the alternatives.

                                                Same reason we don't have flying cars: we do, actually, had for a very long time. They're called helicopters and are hugely expensive, harder to pilot, harder to park and way noisier. That's not failure. That's the price of flying. The more we improve the tech, the more both get better, so the difference isn't really going anywhere.

                                                Now we live in a world where the problems of too many cars compounded to an extreme. It's reaching the topping point or already went over it. Fortunately younger generations are starting to see how ridiculous the cost is and seeking other options more readily.

                                                Of course it's hard for generations who grew up hearing that cars are freedom, rite of passage, basic adult right and absolute necessity to wrap the head around all those things being false. But they are... Except for one: thanks to a lot of work done on planning cities around cars and some countries like the US dilapidating the public transport system to appease lobbyists, it is indeed a necessity for many who have no choice. And a very real disability for others that can't afford one.
