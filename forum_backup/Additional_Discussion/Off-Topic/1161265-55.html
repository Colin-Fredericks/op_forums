                        And notably, I didn’t call them innovative. I said I was happy to see them innovating, and things like the 3-Action economy are certainly innovations, if small ones.

                        Originally posted by Morty View Post
                        I actually had something of an epiphany about the ranger class, lately, namely that there's no good reason for it to exist, unless it focuses on the animal companion, spirits, nature magic... or basically anything else that's not conceptually covered by a fighter or rogue with a good proficiency in survival.
                        And animal companions, spirits, and nature magic are pretty well covered by Druids.

                        This is what I was debating with Pmark about earlier, regarding design rationale. The ranger doesn’t have a design rationale, beyond continuing to try to capture the increasingly nebulous “feel” of the ranger of previous editions, which gets harder to do the more conceptually broad the fighter, rogue, and druid become. And the ranger is far from the only class to suffer this problem, it’s just the most glaring example of it. But the Bard has the same problem, as does the Paladin, and to a certain extent, the Sorcerer.

                        The root of problem is that Class no longer means anything. There’s no clear design constraints behind what makes something a Class. So we’re left with some Classes defined by incredibly broad concepts like “guy who fights with weapons,” “guy who casts spells,” and “guy who casts spells, but more religious-themed than the other guy who casts spells”, some Classes defined by nebulous feelings like “guy who is kinda like Aragorn, but also is kinda like Drizt” and “Guy who uses magic and weapons and is also kind of a generalist skill user, but most importantly he plays music,” some Classes that used to be defined by broad archetypes but have picked up feeling baggage along the way like “sneaky guy, and also guy who’s best at whatever this version of the rules calls a skill,” some Classes defined by absurdly specific concepts like “guy who fights with magic and a sword and casts spells through his sword”. There is no consistency in the design of Classes, so every Class both can and cannot be said to have no reason to be a Class.

                                                    Said that, why I see realism as important, because, basically, it increases immersion to me. That simple. Counting ammunition, keeping track of the weight of stuff I carry (and not to be able to carry 3 full plate armors and 7 spears...), having different critical ranges and multipliers and things like armor check penalty, all those things are good in my eyes, because they're making sense, from a realistic standpoint and in the end, are not too much of a hassle.

                                                    That's one of the reasons why I don't really like too rules light games. After a point, abstraction actively decreases my level of immersion.

Also, D&D has a certain genre, epic high fantasy thus some concessions are needed, like characters being able to take hits and not dying for the first arrow.
