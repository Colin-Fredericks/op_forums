    Small tangent, but I've long found it irritating that a piece of fiction - most often movies - will give the villain a chance to monologue about their ideology and reasons for doing evil, but the hero's only response is to punch them in the face. You so seldom get moments where the protagonist deconstructs the argument and refutes their point, like "no, sir, you're wrong, and here's all the reasons why".

    Partly, this is a direct outgrowth of modern media's persistent pattern of Anti-Intellectualism. Wherein only bad guys "overthink things", and it requires a no-nonsense person of action to "cut through the bullshit" and "get things done". And where even non-villainous intellectuals are either reduced to ineffectual "eggheads", are the supporting characters to protagonists-of-action, or are themselves so action-y that their intelligence is "made up for" by being so capable. (For more on this, see the Renegade Cut episode on James Bond.)

    However, the other reason I dislike this trend of setting up the villain's philosophy and then not deconstructing it is because it's too easy for an audience member who WANTS to agree with the villain (or who is merely receptive to the villain's ideas) to do so. Just look at how many people genuinely agreed with Thanos from Infinity War, about how overpopulation is a problem and that we need a purge. The Avengers et al., from what I understand from Endgame (I haven't watched it), don't ever really counter Thanos's ideas or point out the flaws. They just get a bunch of their people together and beat him up. Leaving the ideological question unanswered.

    (Of course, while Thanos's Neo-Malthusian ideas are wrong, what gets lost in the shuffle when it comes to discourse about the movie is that it was chosen, probably, out of a corporate desire to paint Environmentalism as villainous. A trend that we ALSO see in Hollywood. Just go back and consider how many villains in recent years were citing environmental concerns, or had an ecological bent to their ideology. This is not unintentional, I think. Big Business has a vested interest in demonizing opposition to Capitalist exploitation of the environment, even while at the same time corporations try to court ecologically-minded consumers with the impression of the companies also being ecologically-minded.)

    Returning to my point, it is very easy for a piece of media to convey or at least foster a message that has unfortunate consequences. An in-aptly told narrative or text can do so, even if the creators did not intend for it (let alone when they do). Which makes the trend of setting up a villain's philosophy and then not addressing it in the narrative - just assuming the audience can be properly horrified and not need correcting - quite problematic. Not necessarily because audiences are dumb (although the state of media and textual literacy is, itself, a problem), but rather because ideas can be latched onto quite easily with how influential media can be. The above-mentioned Infinity War has almost certainly created a whole new generation of Neo-Malthusians, for example, let alone what effects works like American History X have on the cultural landscape.

    To reiterate a detail that might get lost, the author does not have to intend a reading for it to be accepted by some portion of the audience. Milton certainly did not intend Lucifer to be seen in a positive light - being a good Christian writer - but that's a popular interpretation of Paradise Lost, much to Milton's chagrin.

                        Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post
                        Not sure how this could be construed as elitism.
                        Because of the word "capable."

                        Things like taste, preference, or whatever are not matters of capability. You might not like Picasso, but I believe that you could at least study his work well enough to understand why he's considered a master on an intellectual level even if such study doesn't lead to you liking his work. Saying you're incapable of understanding Picasso would be negative comment on your intellect.

                        Originally posted by Bluecho View Post
                        Small tangent, but I've long found it irritating that a piece of fiction - most often movies - will give the villain a chance to monologue about their ideology and reasons for doing evil, but the hero's only response is to punch them in the face. You so seldom get moments where the protagonist deconstructs the argument and refutes their point, like "no, sir, you're wrong, and here's all the reasons why".
                        I don't disagree with the main thrust of this, but I think there's some points unaddressed.

                        First, there's a lot of movies that (at least attempt) to have the heroes show their response through their actions rather than through speech. The end of Captain America: the Winter Soldier doesn't have Natasha do a big speech to take deconstruct Pierce's arguments, but her actions to sacrifice SHIELD's secrets in order to expose Hydra, even to great personal expense, is a retort of it's own even if Pierce isn't around to hear it. Thor of all people is actually very prone to try to talk Loki into seeing reason rather than having to fight.

                        Second there's also just a genre convention issue. Action movies don't leave a lot of narrative space for everyone to give big speeches. Unless there's purposeful deconstruction going on (Rorschach and Doctor Manhattan both get to say their piece in contrast to Ozymanius in Watchmen), you don't see it as much. It's fairly common in more traditional sci-fi (Star Trek does this enough TVTropes names it after Kirk, Star Wars does this a few times, the Atredies of Dune love doing this, and while I hate to give Card too much credit Ender certain gets one hell of a scene with this).

                        Last, I think that some creators feel that the villain's speech is self-defeating enough that it doesn't need rebuttal. They don't need to have someone explain why Thanos's plan is nonsense because it's obviously nonsense... or at least it feels like the writers thought it would be. Going back a bit, you don't see people complaining that Loki's "humans suck" speech in Avengers didn't get more than pithy responses from an old guy and Cap, because it's clearly not a sound philosophy as performed. Going back to the original Kingman movie, there's a general sense that engaging with Valentine is kinda pointless on this level. He's right about climate change, but he's so far gone into the extremity of his solution, you're never going to talk him out of it. Having someone sit there an pick apart how wrong headed he is would feel redundant.

                            My two cents on the narrative of "the Marvel movies don't do enough to refute Thanos' conclusions or motivations":

                            One, they do more than nothing in having the charger of Gamorra, who probably had the deepest insight into his world view of anybody, when she calls it something insane and unfalsifiable. That scene also depicts the futility of doing so, as Thanos is impervious to such criticism with his certainty that he is not only in the right, but the only one capable of appreciating that and acting upon it. I will grant though that people aren't necessarily arguing that such a rebuttal would be for the benefit of him, rather than the audience.

                            That leads to the second point of how the film didn't proactively affirm that his world view is correct. Maybe it being wrong is not in the text, but the subtext that the villain who is totally insistent that he's right and shrugs off any assertions that he isn't is not a credible advocate isn't exactly buried deep.

                            Last is that Thanos isn't a political agitator, he's basically a terrorist. You don't really oppose people trying to implement their politics with violence by having a cordial debate with them, because it wouldn't accomplish anything anyway.

                            I do get that the dramatic conventions of a movie, especially a comic book movie, can bend away from reality to have opposing characters express their viewpoints, but I think in a case like this it would still validate Thanos more than he deserves. Really, I think it says a lot more when heroic characters hear his motives and basically just roll their eyes at him.

But I see this as just a facet in the larger context of such films having a tendency to present the villain as the one with the proactive, transformative vision and the hero being somebody that is provoked to stop them and maintain the status quo. We don't really get much media that reflects the reality of villains as people who already sit in privilege at the top of unjust systems who act severely against folks who are proactively trying to change it for the better, and that probably is as a result of a motive to not alienate audiences (including those who might be on the side of the heroes but be frustrated at such narratives close to reality) as well as just general inertia in creative trends. I'd kind of blame the latter a bit more myself.
