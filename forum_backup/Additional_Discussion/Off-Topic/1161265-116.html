                        If you want some help DMing - in 5e in particular, but also just in general - I recommend the videos of Matt Colville, a game designer who has been DMing games of all kinds for decades. He's got a playlist for his series of Running The Game videos, where he talks about various topics concerning the science and art of running a campaign. The first few episodes revolve entirely around designing a mini-dungeon he made for 1st level characters in 5e, which I think is a good place to start.

                        If I had any complaint, it's that I personally wouldn't throw a Bugbear as the boss of a dungeon for 1st level characters, since they can be pretty deadly. I would substitute a Goblin Boss instead; same CR, but less likely to outright kill (not just knock to 0) a 1st level character of any class. But both Matt Colville AND the WotC team made a Bugbear for a 1st level dungeon (Matt in his dungeon, WotC in The Mines of Phandelver). So what do I know?

                        Back on point, I like Matt Colville because he's really good at making you WANT to DM, when before you might have just wanted to run a PC. He made ME want to DM. And his videos are very informative.

                        Obviously you should also read the actual 5e books, too. There's no getting around that. But check out Matt's work if you have time to spare. You won't be disappointed.

                                        A short non-sequiter observation, but I was skimming through Curse of Strahd to find the Guardian Portrait stat block. Because of reasons.

                                        I typed the wrong page number on my pdf, though, and ended up on a random page about the setting a Barovia. So, while I was there, I decided to read a few passages, and tried the "Alterations to Magic" section. I noted that spells meant to contact parties on another plane are known to Strahd, and that he can choose to become the recipient of that spell instead.

                                        Almost immediately, I thought of the spell Augury, which is all about contacting one's deity in order to receive an answer about a question. The response being either "Weal", "Woe", or some combination of both or neither.

                                        And it occurred to me: given the nature of the spell, the caster gets no response BUT Weals and Woes. Nor any verification of where the result comes from (it's an article of faith that it's their god talking). Meaning, if Strahd became aware that someone was casting Augury, and he decided to interpose himself and answer the query...the caster would have no way of knowing he'd done it, would they? So Strahd could, out of the blue, receive information about something a party in his domain wanted to know, and selectively answer in a way that would guide that party towards an outcome of his own desiring.

                                                I've had this nugget of an idea for a while now. What if you ran a DnD game where all the basic rules remain functionally the same...but everything is scaled down?

                                                A game where "Tiny" sized creatures are considered "Medium", and you scale everything else accordingly? Basically, what if you ran a DnD: Borrowers Edition?

                                                Playable races would be as follows:
                                                -Lilliputians: The aforementioned Borrower type people, who use the Human stats. Size: Medium.
                                                -Pixies: Basically Elves, but tinier. Hell, you could just MAKE them "Elves", but in the sense of pre-Tolkein elves that make shoes or cookies. Size: Medium.
                                                -Mice: Sapient mice, ala Redwall, Mouse Guard, or The Secret of Nimh. Thinking of using some of the Halfling stats. Size: Small.
                                                -Rats: Like Mice, but bigger, and probably with different abilities to differentiate them, according to popular conceptions of them. Size: Medium.
                                                -Sprites: Like Pixies, but with wings. All their racial traits are going into purchasing flight, with Ability scores geared towards spellcasting. Size: Medium.
                                                -Birds: Common small birds, because BIRBS. Might use Aaracokra stats. Size: Medium.
                                                -Frogs/Toads: Because I know some folks like Arnold Lobel's books about Frog and Toad. Imagining frogs and toads will be two subraces of the same creature. May or may not tap the Grung stats for this. Size: Small or Medium.
                                                -Toy: Humanoid children's toys, brought to life. Because we have to have someone fight the rats above, for Nutcracker-style antics. Size: Medium

                                                From there, it's just a matter of skinning existing mechanics to fit the smaller scale of everything. Monster stat blocks will employ logical conversions, like using Giant sized animal stats to represent normal sized critters. A Human being is just a Giant, to a mouse or Lilliputian. Nor did anyone say a Beholder needed to be measured in feet, rather than inches. Really, though, thinking about the various player races and what influences they draw from made me consider that this kind of campaign would draw A LOT from children's stories. Borrowers, Frog and Toad, Redwall, The Nutcracker, etc. Seems like I'm already running off a stylistic trend, which may bias tone, mood, and story.

                                                Then again, no one ever said some of these children's books weren't frightening and intense. And besides, DnD has never been one to shy away from action fantasy, which many of these works are or can be geared towards.

                                                Because of the scale, an entire campaign could be run in and around a single house. The interior walls and floors - depending on the nature of construction - could easily serve as an enormous and sprawling dungeon, complete with interesting use of verticality. Traveling down a human-sized street could be a big journey. And, given the tech level available to such small characters, it could technically be set in any time period. A borrower is going to swing around a sword (or rather a repurposed pen knife or sewing needle) to fight, whether he's in a medieval cottage or a modern urban apartment complex. The kind of vermin that will be their bread and butter opponents also don't change overmuch when industrialization kicks in. It's only really when folks start getting computers and cell phones that a tiny character like that is significantly straying from the level of power they can use. If only because a borrower who gets a smart phone could conceivably wreck some havoc on the macro scale as easily as a human with the same device.

                                                Thoughts and feelings?

                                                            Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post
                                                            Premise for a D&D campaign:

                                                            The PCs are all retired adventurers, hanging out in a tavern, reminiscing about their past adventures. All in-character talk is first-person, past-tense. The player of the character currently telling the story acts as DM. Instead of giving Advantage on a roll, spending Inspiration allows you to interject with “that’s not how I remember it!”
                                                            So, I’m going to run my next campaign this way, minus the rotating DM aspect, and I won’t be a stickler about the tense of in-character dialogue. But I love the retired adventures reliving past glories framing device, so I’ve decided to use it to glue the dungeons in Tales from the Yawning Portal together. If characters die during the flashback adventures, it’s assumed the rest of the party was able to resurrect them before the next dungeon. I also really hope at least one character does die though, because my plan after exhausting TFTYP is to continue on with the same group to Tomb of Annihilation. My hope is that Acererak as the villain will have more personal relevance to the players, since they will have recently played through Tomb of Horrors, framed in what is effectively their characters’ backstories as the most difficult adventure they had ever been on together. And, if any of them died in any flashback, the death curse will have a direct impact on them.

                                                            I also think it will be a pretty cool twist for the players to finish the final flashback adventure, just in time to get caught up in one final adventure that takes place in their characters’ present, against the greatest enemy of their past, where death is permanent for the first time in the campaign.

                                                            I kind of want to weave Acererak’s influence into the other Yawning Portal adventures. Any ideas for how he might be involved in Sunless Citadel, Forge of Fury, Hidden Shrine of Tamoachan, White Plume Mountain, Dead in Thay, or Against the Giants?
