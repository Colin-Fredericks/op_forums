    Keep on Poppin', Hunie: a Few Words on HuniePop

    06-08-2015, 09:01 PM
    I'm going to follow the time-honoured off-topic tradition to create a post simply because I can. Deal with it. *shades* If you want to play it and haven't yet, there might be spoilers, so beware.

    When the pornographic match-three dating sim HuniePop was released on Steam in January this year, there was quite a bit of commotion around it. The point of HuniePop is to date an assortment of different girls, collect information about them, talk to them and give them gifts and Candy Crush your way into their hearts and panties. Since it dealt with sex, it was probably unavoidable that someone's feathers got rustled; sex tends to do that in video games, particularly after the recent years of turmoil around similar issues, what with Sarkeesian and GamerGate and such. It's understandable that the game would draw attention. After the first hubbub about the themes and aesthetics dies down, many seem to have settled on saying that the mechanics are pretty solid, and chosen to treat the framing of the game with an eye-roll and a shrug.

    I would agree that the match-three gameplay is really good and entertaining enough to carry the game even if you care not one bit about the context it's put into. However, after having played the game to completion (by, which I mean, full, 100%, all-the-things-you-can-collect-gotten completion) I kind of need to voice a few thoughts about that same context.

    Now, when you first look at the game, there are some things that stand out that can be easy jump on. Some things in it which are really pretty creepy; for example, many react to the "HunieBee", a device that's an in-game representation of the menu screen which have a function to track the location of girls you've talked to... and also any other girl that happens to be around you. Of course, in dating games you could pretty much always choose to meet any person you liked for some reason, but pointing that out as an in-universe thing makes you think about it more. And that's... kind of a thing I like about it. Yes, the HunieBee is pretty much a stalking device, but it also makes you think about how this convention of the genre is often not even thought about. I would not call HuniePop a deconstruction or a parody of a dating game - it has humour but is clearly committed to the genre - but it does riff pretty hard on some meta observations about its own medium from time to time (Kyu in particular is lovely that way). By daring to point out silly conventions that it then continues to rely on, the game comes across as both committed and tongue-in-cheek.

    And it also dares to have some subject matter in it that's not very accepted in games today. Except for the obvious (SEX!), there are multiple uncensored swear words and some quite harsh language (my favourite being "What's wrong? Did I hurt your whore feelings?") and the character Audrey's personal gifts that make you gain more points for talking to her are all drug-related. Few games would probably dare to reward you for essentially furthering a high-risk behaviour like drug use, and there are no drawbacks from it either (even though those drawbacks may have been more interesting, but I'll get to that later). It just feels like HuniePop does things that other games doesn't quite dare, without making it feel like it's doing it "just because" (looking at you, Hatred). Getting a bit shocked and taken out of your comfort zone isn't a bad thing, it's been a quality lauded in literature for ages. Being challenged this way is healthy.

    On the Steam page, it says that the game is "a unique sim experience for PC, Mac and Linux. It's a gameplay first approach that's part dating sim, part puzzle game, with light RPG elements, a visual novel style of presentation, an abrasive western writing style and plenty of "plot"." This descriptions mentions the word "sim", that is, "simulation" twice. I wouldn't say HuniePop is a dating sim. It's a dating game, but in my mind, a simulator puts effort into making the game simulate reality to the best of its ability. Pilots train in simulators, because it's close to reality. HuniePop, in the other hand, abstracts the dating experience. No date I know of actually plays out in a match-three window. Strictly speaking, they could use the same mechanic for a fantasy dungeon crawling game or a kung fu tournament game instead. The gameplay is actually pretty far from what it's a metaphor for. And this is something that I actually feel might come across as a bit problematic. The subject matter of the game is a pretty big one: love and the struggle for closeness and and satisfaction in life. By gamifying it to this extent, it can come across as cheapened or distanced; it becomes possible for the player to detach the context and focus on merely the gameplay, while in other dating games the context and the gameplay might be so close as to being undetatchable. I'm not saying this is bad or harmful, as I said I like gameplay, but it does create a situation where the attachment to the girl your dating might not be as strong as it might otherwise be, and here the question of objectification comes in.

    A game like this would naturally draw questions about objectification to it. It's an old, but also pretty broad concept with quite a few different meanings. There are theories arguing about the usefulness about the concept, given the different circumstances now compared to when it was invented in the 40s. When I use it, I'm going to use it as meaning the process of viewing a person or character as a means to an end, rather than as a someone you're interested in in their own right. So are the girls in HuniePop objectified?

    Yes.

    It's pretty obvious to me that they are. Sure, we get to know things about them, they are very distinct from one another, but it's all very formulaic. You ask a question, you get an answer, when she ask about what she said you have to answer correction. This is robotic, it doesn't breed interest. It also doesn't help that there is no narrative happening at all. Once the premise is set an the girls introduced, there is no change in that state ever. They only ever interact with each other once, when they're introduced, and after that they're entirely focused on you. Even when you've slept with them, they don't acknowledge this in any way. Furthermore, the only context you ever meet the girls in are in terms of chatting or dating. You never do anything else with them. In my mind, they're clearly objects; characterised, but shallow, uncomplicated and never shown interacting with anyone but you.

    There are a few exceptions to this, though (SPOILERS!). After you've slept with all the girls and hand over their panties (don't ask) to active the endless Alpha Mode, you get to know from Kyu that Audrey was a virgin before you. That's a bit of development, if through analepsis. There's also some deeper involvement between Tiffany and Jesse, since they're mother and daughter (And yes, you do bang them both) which lends a bit more character depth, I felt.

    I think earlier, the game developer intended to have a more interactions between girls, or at least more things to do than just dating, and possibly a bit of a plot surrounding the girls as well. It would have been a different game, and in terms of character representation, I think it would have been better. I can see the value in focusing on the puzzle gameplay, however, and I respect that choice.

    Despite what I wrote above, I still think the sex parts are treated very nicely. The game is clearly pornographic, but in terms of smut/minute, it's really very tame. It takes until the third date until you even spot any naughty bits at all, which is ages compared to many other erotic games. The girls never come across as pushed or bullied. Also, the final sex "scene", which is constituted by other puzzle wher eyou have to make matches fast and sustained instead of on a max points per move basis, is one of the best things about the game, I feel. Here, the gameplay works very tightly as a metaphor for intercourse, in that you have to keep making moves, all the time, until you reach the end. Also, and this I was really surprised by, it's entirely focused on your partner's pleasure. It's her body that blushes, it's her moans that echo, and it's her orgasm that fades out at the end (yes, it can sometimes get awkward). So much focus on a female orgasm is rarely seen in media, and I think it's a refreshing thing.

    Well, that was a wall of text and a half. If you read all that, good on you. Any opinions on HuniePop? Want to beat me other the head with a shovel? Feel free to reply. I sort of just needed to write that, either way.
