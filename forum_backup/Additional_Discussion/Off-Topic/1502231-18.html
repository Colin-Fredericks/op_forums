        I'm currently reading "A Tainted Cup" by Robert Jackson Bennett and that author once again produced an interesting setting with an interesting genre-mix. It's this setting where technology is all plant-based and people "enhance" themselves via genetic modification. And so you have thise empire of all these humans modifying themselves and the environment around them set against the threat of Kaiju-style monsters trying to invade that empire. And it's only because of that threat that the empire grew so quickly because it was only due to these plant-based inhuman improvements that human civilization was able to put up a defense against these Kaiju-monsters that are coming from the ocean.

        That's the setting but the genre of the story the novel is a Sherlockian Whodunnit-mystery that starts with the bizarre murder of a famous general (since it's plant-based, the bizarre murder-weapon is a supernatural seed that made a small tree grow out of the general's head within seconds).

        So far, it seems like yet another really fun novel from Robert Jackson Bennett.

                                                        I finished reading "A Swim In A Pond In The Rain" by George Saunders this weekend and that had been more pleasant and accessible than I expected. It's the lecture of George Saunders (famous short-story-author) doing a lecture on Russian short-stories and this is supposed to be the book-version of that. May favorite was the short-story from Tolstoy called "Master And Man". Such a great story that highlights storytelling which is all about "show, don't tell".

                                                        I also read the short Sci-Fi-novel from Nick Harkaway called Titanium Noir. It's more detective-fiction. Just pulled that from my TBR-2023-pile after enjoying the mystery-stuff in A Tainted Cup by Robert Jackson Bennett. It's still a novel with a great sci-fi-concept: There's a miracle-drug which heals even the biggest injury and it reverses aging - the downside...? You grow bigger every time you use it. And so there's a wealthy elite in this setting which are literal giants. But it also highlights that it isn't pleasant being these supposedly immortal giants. But I think the biggest strength isn't that sci-fi-conceit but the writing itself. I listened to the audiobook-version and I think the writing is especially strong if someone reads it out loud. There's a rhythm to the curt dialogues the detective-main-character has with people. And having someone read it out loud to you really emphasizes the rhythm/metric of the text.
