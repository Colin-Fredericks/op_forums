        To be clear, I do like D&D. A lot. There are just a lot of little things about it that are frustrating because they are clearly only still around because they have always been (which is, in my opinion, the worst possible reason to keep something around).

        Take, for example, the 6 ability scores. When the game was first created, they existed to do very specific things. They modified results on very specific dice rolls, most of them using their own individual subsystems, and the names made sense for those handful of specific things that they did. Then 3e came along and unified all the various subsystems for resolving different tasks into one simple mechanic, specifically rolling a d20, adding a modifier based on the appropriate ability score, and potentially adding other modifiers for specialized training or miscellaneous circumstantial advantages or disadvantages. This was great for the game because it made actions much simpler to resolve. But, because basically every conceivable action used that system, it meant that every conceivable action also had to be able to be tied into one of those six abilities. And the abilities just weren't designed to handle that. Certain tasks that previously hadn't been related to any ability had to be crammed into abilities they didn't really fit with, which is how we've ended up with Dexterity as the god-stat, Wisdom being this bizzare mishmash of perception, intuition, resistance to magical mental coercion, Charisma covering any and all social interaction with a single score, etc. The only reason they have stuck around despite being a poor fit for the game's core resolution mechanic is because they've always been there. From a design standpoint, the game would be far better off completely re-thinking the ability categories. But, because it doesn't "feel like D&D" without Strength, Dexterity, Constitution, Intelligence, Wisdom, and Charisma, that will never happen.

        What's worse is that, because D&D is so iconic, it influences how other games are designed. Tons and tons of games copy D&D's ability categories, sometimes with different names, often with the same ones, but generally falling into the same broad concepts. Even when those games would be better served by dividing up their core attributes in different ways.

        Not to come across as an Angry GM fanatic, but he wrote another fantastic article on this subject, if you're interested in reading about it in more detail.
        Fanservice BS: I Hate Ability Scores (In D&D 5E) | The Angry GM
        http://theangrygm.com/i-hate-ability-scores/
        The Angry GM delivers advice to players and dungeon masters of fantasy role-playing games with humor, snark, and attitude. Game masters and players are sure to find something of use, whether they are playing AD&D, D&D 3.5, D&D 4E, 5E, Pathfinder, D&D Next, or any other role-playing game.

So even there, I was still using my sneak attack as a method of fighting.
                                                            That is the root of the problem, yes. Health in D&D scales upwards so sharply that you need to keep up. Simply attacking with a weapon stops mattering fairly soon. In D&D as it is, a brainy or social non-magical class basically needs a sneak attack equivalent. Like the PF investigator's studied strike, or something to buff party members, and such. It really does narrow down possible design.
