    Regarding I'Cath, I think it was one of those prime examples of the biggest problem with early Ravenloft, especially the Islands of Terror, where too many domains were essentially just elaborate dungeons the PCs would wander into randomly and then have little to do there outside the designed adventure. This is one of the reasons I enjoyed the addition of the Clusters, so that some of them could be expanded into something closer to a functioning society.
    Although, the Verdurous Land/Steaming Land wasn't a very good one, as the only real commonality between Saragossa, Sri Raji, and the Wildlands is that they are hot and humid. In hindsight, I think Sri Raji might've worked OK if attached to the Core as a little Sri Lankan-like island in the Nocturnal Sea. (This would come with the idea that the realm should almost explicitly be the dark equivalent of Ravana and his island fortress of Lanka from Ramayana.) The Saragossa I'd likely make the warmer southern extension of the Sea of Sorrows, with Souragne as an island on its edge.

                    That article made me claw at my face a bit as far as my issues with the whole soulless construct citizens of Ravenloft, I'm going to be honest.

                    "We want people to feel like the people of the domains have their own agency and internally valid culture and are not just a battleground for adventures by outsiders. But also, we're going to stress that the majority of the people of Ravenloft are soulless p-zombies with no agency. Also, the domains are going to all be barely connected floating pocket dimensions within the Shadowfell, not even within a single demiplane, and the more usual references are going to be to them as places that the mists yank you into."

                    There are good things in that hardcover, but they largely just make the bad things that much more exasperating. And knowing the reasoning behind some of them makes me want to cry out as to why the developers could not just pick a lane, as it were.

                    edit: This particularly, as far as an actual quote

                    This can very easily be [a story about] your home. [Your character] can imagine a world where there are not these horrors, and it’s up to you to make them better.
                    "We have straight up published an adventure that states the domain will just reset to factory settings eventually if Strahd is killed. We've also said in this book that is a regular thing that happens with domains if you take out the darklord."

                    I'd be all over the idea that people of a domain could achieve some measure of heroism, rise to the challenges before them, and through some campaign of harrowing and terrifying adventures manage to overcome the darklord of the land. That's great. That's the stuff of adventures. That's a solid compromise between D&D and Horror as it were, which is the line Ravenloft has always had to tread.

                    If Van Richten's is wanting to send that message, it is telling it in the most muddled, self undermining and self contradictory way it could have chosen.

                            Originally posted by No One of Consequence View Post

                            I think that may have been the Dark Matter setting for Alternity. Which was a really cool setting, but not quite the same thing.

                            I think in my mind I have this idea of a generic "every city" (in my case, North American, but it could manifest as anything), where every urban horror story, real or imagined, you've ever heard likely takes place. Giant man eating alligators in the sewer system. Ghost trains in the subways. Serial killers. Dangerous gang lords. People above the law. Murder hotels. Lost 13th floors of buildings. Apartments full of Satanic cultists. Undead being kept alive by refrigeration. Vampires. Haunted mansions. Mad science experiments at the university. Maniac cops. All of it. The suburbs around it are full of ghosts, dream-haunting killers, teen witches, Stepford Wives, rabid dogs, and murder clowns. Even the roads and highways are full of vanishing hitchhikers, killer cars, and haunted roadside bars.
                            I really, really like this concept. A 5e Modern campaign set in an urban horror Domain of Dread.

                            As I thought about it, an extra complication to the concept crept into my head: that of a veneer of normalcy.

                            The concept is that the inhabitants - who, as is the case with other Domains, are soulless constructs - are on at least some level aware that something is very wrong with their world. But they're both ignorant and incurious, and will latch onto any non-supernatural explanation of events. They don't believe in monsters and don't want to. Except they still kind of do, they just don't want to admit it. The inhabitants vacillate between superstitious paranoia and dogmatic skepticism, depending on which way the wind is blowing.

                            This is to the benefit of the monsters that secretly control the Domain, who may or may not be the Darklord and their agents. Like Vampire: The Masquerade, a screen of secrecy, misinformation, and cover-ups serves to maintain the illusion that everything is fine. That there are no monsters, no magic, and certainly no predators preying on the citizens. A fiction said citizens cling to, in between bouts of spreading rumors of monsters and occasionally being whipped up into paranoid mobs.

                            The PCs in such a campaign might be contacted very early on by agents of the Darklord, informing them in no uncertain terms that they'll be allowed to deal with the random monsters, serial killers, and evil cults they encounter. They might even enjoy strings being pulled to keep them out of trouble with the law (who are, of course, horribly corrupt and partially stocked with monsters themselves; so, no change there). With the caveat that the PCs need to stay quiet about it all, or they'll be crushed, either by assassins or by the systems that bend to the Darklord's whims. While the party is free - encouraged, even - to clean up small evils in the margins of society, they cannot be permitted to tackle the rot at the center of it.

                            At least, not until the PCs become high enough level that they can start fighting back.

                            The very first idea I had when I thought of this conceit was that of a Scooby Doo style band of teen sleuths and their intelligent animal mascot, who go around solving mysteries. To muddie the waters, the Domain would be filled with perfectly mundane criminals who dress up as monsters, to accomplish some mundane crime. The Scoobies go around foiling their plans, gaining notoriety for their efforts. Though they don't know it, they serve the interests of the Powers That Be/Darklord, by reinforcing the idea that everything that seems supernatural has a mundane explanation. The teens, for their part, are unaware of this, being largely benign in and of themselves. While some of their number are credulous enough to entertain the idea of monsters in the moment, they're ultimately confident, from experience, that monsters aren't real. The masks always come off in the end.

                            Which can lead to the amusing situation where the PCs need to follow the sleuths around, protecting them from real monsters. All while being pressured into not enlightening the sleuths, even if the party have ample evidence of it.

                            Magestuck: The Ascension
