                        So, despite one of my unpopular D&D opinions being that critical hits and critical failures suck, I actually really like what PF2 is doing with crits and fumbles. And that’s because they’re designing the game around having four degrees of success. Where D&D has traditionally had a more or less binary task resolution system with crits as this small, random chance of a result outside of that binary, PF2 is starting from the assumption that degrees of success matter and designing to take advantage of them. I love that saves vs. AoE damage will be double damage on a fumble, full damage on a fail, half damage on a success, no damage on a crit. And I love that crits and fumbles are going to be much more common. Because it’s giving crits and fumbles a place in the system.

                            We've been talking about the core classes her often, so I figured I'd throw something together a list of what I think should be done to them.

                            Fighter - do what 4e did and make it a martial defender class, instead of a generalist. Focus on being tough, durable and keeping enemies in place. Plenty of room for different archetypes and ways of doing that.

                            Barbarian - rename it as "vanguard" and make it an aggressive, mobile class that can push past its limits. Which may mean a berserker. But also a skirmisher, duelist, dervish... not sure if shwasbuckling types should be here as well. It's one of those cases where the "warrior" and "rogue" archetypes blur. I think that if a character type engages enemies directly, rather than try to use subterfuge or tricks to get the upper hand, it should go here. It's a blurry line.

                            Rogue - I've really come to detest this class. It's supposed to be a well-rounded skill specialist, but it's still shackled to the old-school thief kit. I'm not sure what to do here. First question we need to ask is - do we need a class specializing in non-combat skills? Maybe we don't. Maybe the rogue can just be a canny skirmisher who exploits weakness for massive damage, and we can let people pick whatever non-combat skills they want.

                            Still, I feel like there's space for characters who are better at non-combat, non-magical skills than others. But it shouldn't be one class. I think the rogue class should stay as a physically-oriented expert class, with another one taking up the cerebral and/or social aspects. Like the factotum, except it works. Or the PF investigator. Though the problem remains that it's very hard to contribute in combat if you can't keep up with the merciless HP bloat.

                            Ranger - Either cut it or give it a proper focus. There's two options here. Make it a beast-master class, or merge it with druid. I've seen homebrew that did the latter and I think it can work.

                            Paladin - I think 5e's take on it isn't bad. The 3e version was a trainwreck, but in 5e it's martial zealot focusing on oaths and ideals, rather than deities. Lots to work with here.

                            Monk - Cut it. Make it a non-core psionic class or subtype of one of the martial classes.

                            Bard - Cut it. Like with ranger, there's just too many bits and pieces here.

                            Wizard, Cleric & Sorcerer - Now obviously, wizards aren't going anywhere. Neither are clerics. Arcane and divine magic is part of what makes D&D what it is. But what about sorcerers? They were introduced as a way to play an arcane caster without being a bookish scholar. Since then, they've mostly desperately tried to be relevant.

                            So what do we do here? Do we roll wizard and sorcerer into one arcane spellcaster class and make them distinct subclasses? Or do we try to make sorcerer truly different and give it mechanics to match its purpose? I think both are viable, but the second option appeals to me more.

                            I had this idea of dividing them based on how they acquire their power. Studied magic, granted magic and innate magic. "Granted" magic would cover clerics and warlocks. But that's a lot less evocative.

                            Druid - It's a mess of a class, but probably worth keeping in some measure. A nature-oriented magician is a very strong archetype. The issue with the class has always been that each of its main features could make for a whole class in itself. 5e at least takes away their animal companions. So we've got shapeshifting nature-casters.

                            I don't think druid needs a full spell list, honestly. I feel like something akin to paladin's powers, except focused on shamanism, witchcraft and elemental magic could work better. Then again, slinging fire and lighting around with abandon is already handled by sorcerers. I've once seen a homebrew class list that folded rangers and druids into one class - but it was a list that heavily emphasized subclasses. So there was one "naturalist" class, and ranger was a subclass that emphasized using its magic for martial combat.

                            If we cut monks and bards, we're left with two spots for other classes, assuming we don't add any more. One is occupied by the new "brainy rogue" class, and I think the third one should be a warlord type. It's an archetype that's missing from any edition except 4e, and it neatly rounds off the purely martial classes.

                            Do we need a dedicated gish class, or is it better handled by multiclassing/archetypes? It's a hard question to answer without context.

                            Another question is: what about ranged weapons? If we give fighter an actual job and cut/transform rangers, where do we go if we want to have a straightforward ranged attacker? Do we have to create a dedicated class for it, or can vanguard serve as one? The reworked rogue class can do it too, but it would presumably focus more on trickery and stealth, not directly wiping out enemies with a barrage of shots, Legolas-style.

                                                        Originally posted by Caitiff Primogen View Post
                                                        I find the evolution of the Rogue/Thief to be interesting because early on its playstyle was clearly meant to avoid combat at all costs unless it could line up a perfect backstab. There's a lot of people who want to play a Rogue-type but don't want to feel locked out of combat and so we keep trying to make Rogue work as a martial class when it just isn't really in its DNA.
                                                        I would argue that the changes between the Thief and the Rogue are a direct result of the shift in the focus of D&D. In AD&D, you get experience for securing treasure. It didn't actually matter if you killed the monsters, as long as you got the treasure they were guarding. It wasn't just the Thief that wanted to avoid combat, everyone did, because the combat was dangerous and you could get just as much XP without putting yourself in danger by avoiding direct confrontation. In that kind of game, it's fine to have a character who is specifically designed to discourage you from getting involved in combat, that rewarded sneaking past the monsters, stealing their stuff, and that could really only deal with monsters by catching them unaware, but did very poorly in direct conflict. The Thief was a class focused on exploiting the most efficient tactic.

                                                        As D&D evolved, the focus began to shift away from going into dungeons and extracting treasure from them towards going on quests to save the kingdom. The characters moved away from being treasure hunters and towards being fantasy heroes. By 3e, you no longer got XP for treasure, you got it for killing monsters or otherwise overcoming encounters involving monsters, which meant the most efficient strategy changed from avoiding combat to fighting everything. D&D has always had a heavy combat focus, but that shift was where combat became the primary activity in most D&D games. And in that style of game, it doesn't really work to have a class built specifically to be unable to meaningfully participate in direct combat. At around the same time, the Assassin class was merged with the Thief and became the rogue.
