                    My main gripe with that word, and its more blaise/agressive/tumblr usage is that it confuses a descriptive term with a prescriptive term. Or, more insidiously, it uses heteronormative where heteronormal is the proper expression.

                    "Normal", fundamentally, means nothing more than average, or mean, or center of the distribution. Heteronormal, as a descriptive term, is perfectly fine. A relative majority of people in the world are heterosexual, so if you take a grand average over all those people, the norm will be, not surprisingly, hetero, and barring massive changes, this is not likely to move. Perhaps a couple notches down the Kinsey scale, but not to the middle or the other side.

                    Normative, on the other hand, carries massive connotations of active use, of doing, of trying to move a situation towards that. One, could, for example, attempt to make society more or less heteronormative.

                    Sadly, most uses I have come across use the word "normative", with all its negative connotations, when they ought to use normal.

                                    I think there's a disconnect there between academic and general language.

                                    In an academic/demographic sense, our species is heteronormal: the majority of humans are heterosexual or heterosexual leaning along that spectrum.

                                    In general conversation though, people don't mean the statistical definition of normal. Normal is usually closer to, "expected," rather than, "average." Nobody - in casual conversation - is going to call out people outside of various statistically average demographics "abnormal," as long as they're still expected. Most people don't even know though the distributions of, say, blood types to know what's average or not. People know some combinations are more rare than others, but if you're sitting around chatting and it comes up that someone in your group is A- you're not going to comment on how abnormal they are (A- is ~3% of the human population). It's just a fact about them. You might be a rarity to a hematologist, but even most of them aren't going to be weird about it.

                                    When people want to push for inclusive language, that's the goal. For being whatever identity you have to be part of the "normal" in the sense of the expected. A trans-man is no more noteworthy to what is normal by society's standards than having A- blood (maybe less so due to the need for blood donations as a practical matter) is the goal.

                                    That's why those of us knowledgeable about the academic lingo need to be aware of how people who aren't are going to interpret things. As I said before, trying to correct common language doesn't work. Common language beats academic language in societal importance and always will. It doesn't matter if heteronormal is a perfectly neutral academic term (so is retarded as a different example). Using such words needs to be done with care for precisely the reason Eric said: people will read them under the context of common language not academic neutral. And in common language making one thing normal, means something else is abnormal; and abnormal things are vastly more likely to be the subject of discrimination.

                                    Though this little aside feels like it's getting away from the point of the thread, I'll try to tie it back by saying that this is all core to a lot of current slang in the communities in question. Though that qualifier is also important. There isn't one LGBTQ community with one slang. Slang varies with location and so on. Terms that might be cool in Miami aren't necessarily terms that are cool in NYC. But there is still a common cultural pressure behind those slang variations. There's a desire for both group identity that goes with any sub-culture, and the desire for acceptance by the greater culture around them.
