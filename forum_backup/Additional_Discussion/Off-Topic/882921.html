    05-05-2016, 12:28 PM
    This is a general question, and I didn't know where to put it. How does White Wolf/Onyx Path plan to approach the short lifespan of electronic documents? I'm not really asking for a specific business plan or anything, obviously that wouldn't be forthcoming. However, since electronic files don't last anywhere near as long as printed files, is this a concern for how to handle product releases?

    I wonder about this as the gaming industry starts to really promote electronic documents as the way forward. Just curious.

          There are issues with the lack of permanence of digital files... but that's not really a everyday person problem. That's a large scale institutional problem over decades.

          Historians are worried about this sort of thing (esp. as old files are not archived in accessible formats) because it is very possible that we're going to find ourselves with massive holes in the historical record on account of poor digital archiving and/or any sort of EMP bursts over certain storage facility heavy areas and/or purposeful digital attacks. There's too much data, in too many formats, to hope to maintain it all in the current fashion we do on a societal level.

          But, like I said, this is meaningless for something like RPG ebooks. Such products are essentially (if in a very niche fashion) high priority for being maintained because they're not things like record archives meant for storage. They're products actively being sold.

                  At this point, RPG products are in as good as position as possible to avoid any issues with being lost into the digital record keeping whole. They exist in multiple device agnostic forms (Onyx Path as copies of their books in text-only manuscript formats and multiple forms of the final documents), and print form (since Rich gets test copies of the print books to make sure they're correct).

                  There's really not much more that can be done.

                  I would argue that this is an issue that factors into people's lives on an everyday basis. Consider the evolution of technology just from when White Wolf began publishing, how much money has been spent on tech to keep digital files current, versus one hardcover book?
                  This seems a bit blind to the reality of publishing in the 90s. White Wolf was virtually always working on books in a digital format because by that point that was the primary method of layout and printing. While hard copy formats were part of record keeping as well, failures to keep digital files current has actually cost them money because turning a hardcopy into a new digital file is extremely time intensive/expensive compared to maintaining the digital files.

                  Keeping digital files current is far less expensive than turning a hardcopy into a new digital copy years later; if there's a continual demand for the product.

                  From one day to the next a DVD collection essentially became junk.
                  There's a difference between them losing monetary value, being more expensive to maintain, and being outright junk. I have a rather extensive VHS and DVD collection - along with other fun things lik 3.5 floppies - they all still work just fine. For the ones where I have access to higher quality videos via things like streaming services... yeah, they're kind of useless, but plenty of them aren't available like that.

                  I have the impression that digital publishing is in a good position to lead the way for device agnostic file formats that could alleviate a lot of the problems digital files cause with regards to their overall permanence. I have no idea what that would look like, but it would definitely be advantageous for RPGs to be involved.
                  The majority of digital RPG products are released in as agnostic formats as possible (keeping in mind that there's no universal format right now because device manufactures don't want there to be).

                                                          The thing is that the record/tape/floppy/DVD series of storage, is that it while it did lead to costs to the consumers, we didn't really lose anything. The problem with sustainable digital storage isn't that storage formats change. The entertainment industry has a vested interest in transferring product onto new storage formats. If they don't, they risk losing rights over old products. Every time that an album,movie, game, etc. gets updated, it helps them extend their copyright protections instead of those things going public domain.

                                                          Institutional systems don't have the same pressure.
