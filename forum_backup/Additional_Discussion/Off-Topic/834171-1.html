                                Something that always kept me away from the old World of Darkness was the sense that it was too confined. And something that will always keep me from being able to consider it realistic was its simplification of the world. Almost every problem has an easy-to-point at cause, usually some sort of supernatural. Vampire is notorious for this, where one can literally trace your lineage back to basically the beginning of humankind in thirteen easy steps. You can look through the books and read about certain characters and see why they did certain things. Whereas if you were to look at a history book you can read about certain real people and see that they did certain things, and if you dig deeper you can make some educated guesses particularly if you have access to their correspondences with others and personal viewpoints, but as you go farther and farther back in history it becomes pretty blurry, especially if you wander away from the more thoroughly studied civilizations that might have had records. But even then, most of it's going to be a mystery, tainted by the cynicism of the reader or the teller.

                                This is why I thought old World of Darkness was too much of a 'story' to be considered realistic. It has a rich history, but it's like Lord of the Rings or Game of Thrones, everyone is accounted for in neat appendices, to the point that people might actually get angry if you start doing things not directly referenced in the books. Which is of course the point, it's not a bad thing, but you're part of a Storytelling collective. Your character can fight alongside Lucita de Aragorn or take direct orders from Sascha. And I think what the guy in control of White Wolf is doing is trying to revitalize that by getting the IP out there so more creative people can have more characters and events for you to play with.

                                One of the reasons I liked new World of Darkness was that it felt more grand. There's an entire world out there and even the books can't cover it all. Vampire politics are different from one county to another, let alone city, state, country and continent. Since record-keeping is verboten and records destroyed by protectors and enemies alike, it always struck me as making more sense that there would be massive gaps in knowing exactly what's happened to the history of one splat. Even the metaplot, something I found to be a weak point in World of Darkness, was more appealing to me in new. I don't know every mekhet in the world, but I know Norris and grew to like him and the way he personified the shadowy spymaster stereotype of his clan before being diablerized by his friend and Prince. One of my favorite books, Belial's Brood, was the first non-fiction book to have a pretty detailed in-world narrative, with a character trying to find out the background of the group for typical vampire reasons and perhaps finding too much. And the vampire clan books, also in-game were specifically about a mysterious agent trying to find the history of their clans, and only finding deceit and further mystery, and not a character with an encyclopedic memory who will reliably tell you everything that happened to them since they were born.

                                So, I certainly found new to be more realistic.

                                The style difference, I wouldn't necessarily say it's 90s, but that's going to be far more of a personal thing to decide whether you like it or not. Aside from one game about being part of a larger narrative and the other about making your own, the classic game was much more about fighting an eternal struggle against unbeatable odds. Whether they were the Technocrats, the Wyrm, the Antediluvians, they were something that as a character you were meant to struggle against in a grey vs black showdown that would end in a typically gothic fashion. Make the most of the now, because tomorrow may never come. I really liked that they opened it up more as the books came along and especially in the 20th Anniversary editions but that's neither here nor there. Chronicles was much more about your character doing their own thing, and in some ways it can lack a lot of motivation, and place more of a workload on the player to find something for their character to want to do, or the storyteller to give them something to do and a reason to do it fast. In this way I suppose it's more like the open ended games of the post-d20 world like Eclipse Phase, or in video games a sandbox or MMO. I even build my stories emulating sandbox games.

                                    I'm very split on this as I think each has major aspects that embrace the (potential) reality of the situation and others that seem stylistic conventions.

                                    For examples:
                                    The x-splats (Clan, Auspice, Path, etc) of CoD seem more realistic than the various racial stereotypes of the WoD. Yes, they are archetypes, but it comes across as if they created the archetypes because they existed that way. The Lord of the Night vampires, the Fear of the Darkness werewolves, the Channel of Higher Power mages, the Unearthly Beauty prometheans, the Living Elemental changelings. Their existence formatted the archetypes and continues to cement them into the collective unconscious. Conversely, the y-splats (Covenants, Tribes, Orders) feel very forced and not as any kind of organic out-growth of the world these creatures would live in. While I can see many of those groups existing within those societies, they don't seem to be broadly encompassing enough to have enough influence to be the real driving points behind their lives. They make for fine religious-philosophical schools of thought and organizations to belong to, but they lack to broad scope to become major political influences. But this also keeps the major violent conflicts to a minimum. When you only have 15 vamps in your Covenant, you're not going to "go to war" against the other Covenant that has 16 vamps. 5 Packs of Uratha might get into it, but we're still talking less than 20 combatants on each side. That's going to make much less noise.

                                    The x-splats of the WoD (Clan, Tribe, Tradition, etc) seem to less realistic to me. All these very specific cultural and regional stereotypes seem too narrow to support large enough populations for them to be a driving force behind their lives. However, the y-splats (Cam/Sabbat/Anarchs, Garou/Wyrm/Fera, Council/Technocracy/Crafts) are large and opposing philosophical differences. Significant enough to create long running conflicts and major divisions among the societies while remaining broad enough to sustain large populations on each side. But then the on-going conflicts between these large groups seems to be where the realism breaks down. The all-out warfare discussed in various books seems to be something that would be very difficult to keep hidden for long, even with all the mind-manipulating powers available. The Concordia War is the one that really stood out for me. One story was going on about a battle between two factions on either side of a bridge. Even with much of it taking place in Chimerical reality, it would have looked like all-out gang warfare. But we're expected to believe that there were no authorities that interfered? No reporters that told the story? I know the Mists cover a lot, but even the mundane version of what happened would have been newsworthy. The WoD supernatural world is hidden, but it seems just barely and only by having a lot of in-place, automatic magical cover-up solutions (Delirium, Mists, Veil).

For me, realism begins and ends with the fact that the world presented in both lines is a work of fiction, in which supernatural creatures exist. CofD tries to maintain this sense of realism, by presenting a more or less uneventful hidden world. That's my major issue with the line. It is not realistic for a world inhabited by monsters.

                                        Other than that, I agree with you. It's just that, as I'm typing this, I'm reading about Masquerade's clans. But the way monsters are presented in CofD sets up a very promising backdrop that fails to deliver. Like I said, uneventful and bland. Nothing any CofD character does can matter in the long run. That's where I feel the fourth wall coming tumbling down. Everything that's ever happened in its history is very here and now, with the exception of Requiem for Rome.

                                        As for the x-splats of WoD, I can see that in some clans, but I still think they encompass a more global view on traditions, cultural customs, and myths, then the vampires of Requiem which are bound by literary representations of vampires (the much praised archetypes). Also, I'd like to point out that Mage the Ascension's x-splats were spot on realistic (for those interested in the real-life occult).

                                        Sure, it's not bad, but there original intention under Achilli was to encompass archetypes found in Masquerade. Subgroups would offer a much needed variety (and I'm not just talking about bloodlines which became painstakingly narrow). So what you got, in a noir genre analogy, a grouping of femme fatales with the added option of playing femme fatales with an obsession with bees, the dead, and so on. Groups should have that, and so much more. The Malkavians are more than just lunatics, they are also seers, pranksters, psycho killers, share a hive mind, etc. I'm not saying these are all good, but they offer players a huge variety and a great selection of concepts.

                                        Personally, as I have said before, loving both CofD and WoD, I can't help but mix and match. In the end, I have my own ST'ing style and eventually plan to move on to a homebrewed game of supernatural horror and occult mystery that accommodates exactly the kind of stories I like to run. So it's probably not an issue with either worlds, but just a matter of personal taste and style.

                                        Can't wait to see how Requiem 2nd edition shapes up to be, as a lot of my (loving) criticism is aimed at 1e.
                                        VtM just happens to be the new and shiny thing for me (after 12 years of Requiem), and has a very immersive atmosphere to it.

                                        We'll see.
