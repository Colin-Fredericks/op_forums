    I wouldn't say streamlined. Some parts are (esp. Awakening 2e over 1e), but other parts aren't. What it is, is more mechanically rigorous. That means there's more charts to consult and variables to figure out, to make up for a lot less of the ST just picking a number based on their best interpretation of the game. Some newbies are going to be OK with the extra steps and math involved, others are going to find it abstract and hard to turn the numbers into characters compared to Ascension's approach. There's a lot of preference and taste involved in how people feel about these sorts of priority shifts. The CofD in general also tends to rely on systems that work best when the players are actively pushing for their use. Some players are very into player-facing systems and others do better with ones that leave more in the hands of the ST. Awakening is pretty friendly for people that are already familiar with TTRPG play, but I'm not sure I'd recommend it for a brand new player or even someone that's dipped their toes into D&D and is looking to try something else.

    Both editions of Awakening allow for mages to either contain Paradox (generally that means eating damage) or letting the backlash happen as it will. Releasing a backlash doesn't mean you get ot point at someone else and they get to deal with your problem. It means handing the ST a suite of options to make your character's life "interesting" that you have no further say in what the outcome will look like; which will also earn you enemies even if a mix of directly (like summoning Abyssal entities) or indirectly (like having the Guardians up your ass because they really hate people that feed the Abyss frequently). But yes, it is on purpose designed to reward the players for engaging with the obsessions and hubris of mages by making it mechanically fun (and usually some sort of reward) to let cool but bad things happen

                                                    Novels don't have more information or details than films, they just wildly differ in what kinds of things they communicate effectively.

                                                    You don't put the details of how a character's plan works in regards to dozens of other setting details in a blockbuster action adventure film... because people don't go to action adventure films to get a huge chunk of time in the movie having deep setting lore explained to them. Novels are far better for world building and adding lots of details the fans will pour over because novels have more time to be character studies since you're never going to have a duel in print that is as spectacular as it looks on screen; and thus there's more time to drop details as characters are doing things.

                                                    There's a reason why heist films and who-do-its have some very standard storytelling techniques to help do exposition to explain the schemes in a fashion that doesn't put the audience to sleep. The top crook narrates the plan over the action of it actually taking place. The detective narrates the true events as you see a replay of events but in a new light. These convey tons of information, but how they do it is very different from novels in the same genre because of the pros and cons of medium choice.

                                                    That said, novels still have to employ their own methods well. We had Palpatine resurrect himself as a clone back in the Legacy era without any need to do deep dives into the melding of science and the Force. He just did it via hording cloning tech for himself, clones of Force users tended to result in Force user clones as something that was already established, and hand-wave Dark Side power to allow his Force ghost to take over a clone body. The newer novels only need to explain so much because Lucas added a bunch of extraneous details that made it harder to suspend disbelief when the sequel movies opted to copy the resurrection storyline. One fan's deep lore enjoyment is another fan's convoluted mess that takes them out of the story.
