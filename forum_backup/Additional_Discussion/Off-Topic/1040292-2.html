                        My own thoughts/observations.

                        The Young Turks, and the person specifically boosting this plan have managed/run a Superpac in the past specifically to support democratic candidates... so they've clearly got no illusions that the biggest money bag wins the election. This makes me wary of that whole side of things.

                        Likewise, the Young Turks have an established history of biased reporting, usually with a pro-democrat slant. The whole "See the anarchists smashing stuff?!!? They're anti-trump protesters! See how horrible The Other Side is?!!?" that was done by the conservative media? The Young Turks were doing *exactly* the same thing 8 years ago. So they're not Shining Warriors of Justice And Correctness, they're just as manipulative... just with a different slant, targeting a different audience.

                        I would argue that Sanders did spend a LOT of time on the universal healthcare push, since he was a major backer of Clinton's push back when her husband was in office. Not as much as Clinton I will agree, but that's also the difference between the political risks a senator can take vs the first lady.

                        I do agree that health care needs further reform, a single payer system would be grand, but likewise agree that repealing the ACA is NOT in any way step in that direction... the ACA added certain key protections that if removed will have significant problems, like the pre-existing condition clauses. If anyone wants a breakdown of the actual problems faced by the US health care systems I believe John Green has done several videos on youtube that cover them at a basic level.

                        On the example of Tank construction for useless instantly mothballed tanks... It's actually something of a double-edged problem. It's not JUST keeping people employed, the problem is that it would be *more* expensive to fire the people than keep them on. The problem there is that in order to maintain a ready capacity to manufacture military vehicles you need to keep people around that know HOW to do it. Like many jobs there's a lot of knowledge that can't be simply dumped into a training manual that someone can read and do the job perfectly just from the manual. So they reduce the staff to a minimum production level, keeping just enough people around that you're not losing critical knowledge and you've got people that can train others if a need for production arises. If you *don't* do that, and you suddenly need to train people from nothing, with them learning elements not documented or clearly teachable as they go, you get a much higher ramp up time.

                        The other thing to consider with that "military industrial complex" spending is that it's not like companies are taking all the money, pocketing it and doing nothing. Granted in many cases things can be streamlined, but ultimately those companies are employing workers skilled and semi-skilled, to do things. And since they're tied to the military, that means it's american citizens (or green-card holders) doing the work, most typically in the US itself. In other words, cutting that "pork barrel spending" means cutting middle class jobs. A lot of them. So it's something you want to ease into rather than hack and slash.

                        Cutting out things so that something better can be built on top of it doesn't work in politics. You want an example of this? I've got one. Dan Quayle, former vice president of the US best known for incorrectly correcting a kid in a spelling bee on spelling the potato(e) actually did something else while in office. The paperwork reduction act of 1995. It was a simple and lovely idea, take a large amount of paperwork, convoluted regulations, put in newer, cleaner more straight forward rules and cut out the old. This eliminated some largely pointless things like the requirement to structurally test ashtrays in government buildings (where smoking wasn't allowed anymore). Something that helps everyone right? The end result was that the new regulations went into effect, and then it got reconned that the old regulations could not be removed. Because politics. Short of open revolution and rebuilding a system of law and government from scratch, government systems do NOT get more straightforward, they only get more convoluted. And even then, that doesn't usually happen. As an example I offer up one of the collection of laws that's causing headache these days... Civil Asset Forfeiture. This is the fun thing where the police can claim your property (money, home, vehicle etc) is suspect of being involved in a crime and take it. You know what the federal root of those laws are? England. They're English laws that after the american revolution the newly independent nation opted to keep because hey, it's already there and it's useful. Of course it has drifted since then, been applied to the state level and then abused/changed to deal with issues of the day, but that's where it came from.

                        As for the Canadian vs the American politics, they have one major thing in common... it sucks. Major parties want power, they'll always try to appeal to as many people as they can swing, and this will happen until they do something to cause it to all come tumbling down.
