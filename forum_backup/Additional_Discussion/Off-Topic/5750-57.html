                I managed to get up with the others and see a trio of horror movies a couple weekends ago that were good examples of budget being inversely proportionate to quality.

                The first was Cooties. I've been trying to find budget numbers on it but have failed. I get the feeling the company, Lionsgate, has recognized it for what it is as it only had a limited release before its direct to video and streaming services releases. Still, there's some semi-big names in the cast. Elijah Wood, Rainn Wilson, Jack McBrayer and Nasim Pedrad, Jorge Garcia, and a number of the writers doubled as actors. Long story short, even though I know it's too late for that, a small town has poorly treated chickens, serves them to schools, and apparently has same-day shipping because it infects the entire United States school system throughout the course of the day. Kids who eat this turn into fast zombies. There must be another cause of the outbreak because barely any kids managed to get out of the school before the whole town was overtaken, somehow. There's a lot of bad jokes, including a man with a speech impediment solely for the words 'dual rear wheel,' over the top characters you can't really connect with, and some good special effects. Horrible movie, I don't recommend it to anyone.

                Then there was Hellions, something I've actually been looking forward to. Grainier film, which I equate to less quality, it's about a teenage girl who just found out she's pregnant with the bad boy she fools around with. In what's a common, but not bad, trope nowadays, it tries to tread the boundaries of whether it's all in her head, representative of the turmoil she's in, or whether it's actually happening. What is 'it?' Well, it's Halloween night, and her mom is out with her little brother, leaving her to man the door. Unfortunately the only trick or treaters are strange little kids in strange masks, who make it clear they have designs on her baby. Robert Patrick was in it, as a protective sheriff with a past that shares some parallels to her current situation. Decent movie, good acting all around, nice special effects, there's some poor dialogue and the script is weak in places but it's not bad like Cooties was.

                Finally was Last Shift. A gem and the best movie I saw that night. Officer Jessica Loren has always wanted to be a cop, like her father. And we join her on her first night on the job, unfortunately on the one year anniversary of her father's on-the-job death. She's got an easy first assignment. The old station is shutting down, they still have some 'hazardous materials' to ship out. Act as a security guard until the truck comes to pick it up. Shift ends in the morning. No problem, right? Well you've seen movies before and you can probably figure out there's more to it and probably get some clues as to what just based on what I've briefly said about it. There's good acting all around, the special effects are sparse but well handled. It all takes place in the old station, which you really get a feel for as the movie goes on and pieces start being put together. Like Hellions there was a lot of, 'is this supernatural? or just in her mind?' but unlike Dora, the star of Hellions, Jessica is a trained policewoman and not just a damsel-in-distress. I appreciate not everyone likes the minimalist kind of thing in movies, but I really dig it, and would recommend the movie. It does have its cheesy parts, but it's good.

                        The Hive just came out on Steam for an unreasonable $9.99. Still, it's a good movie with a nice meshing of horror and sci-fi told in a unique manner.
                        Basically the protagonist wakes up with amnesia in a boarded up room with warnings for him all around and a pleading note to, 'REMEMBER.' He tries to remember, and we see the flashback sequence, but it's clearly not any of his memories he's...remembering. Anyways what unfolds is a not-quite zombie outbreak, told from the perspective of the protagonist who is in touch with the hive mind that's taking over things. It was a really nice twist on storytelling, I thought, as he puts together what happened to him through pieces of other peoples memories, as well as his own. The not-zombies were pretty well done. I'm a fan of minimalism and really the most notable feature was a shifting of the eyes that seriously conveys a creepy vibe. As well as black vomit.

That was another one of the film's structural problems; it wants to create this elaborate background to the effect of Blackbeard having created some kind of society by having some kind of network of kidnappers and human traffickers for hundreds of years, and all it does is just create weird questions about where any of these people (of whom there have to be thousands, maybe tens of thousands) come from and how that affects them. I mean, is Hook supposed to have been brought there as an orphaned child as well? Or Smee? Are you gonna give any of these people personalities that might be conductive with the idea that they've been working in mines their whole lives? I'm not saying these should be things the movie tells me, just that it should either not be a precept in the first place or else at least not vaguely dance around the issue. Also, you know... why are there no women in their society? Hundreds of years and Blackbeard kidnaps (presumably) one woman, ever? You'd think that at some point he'd think his ridiculous steampunk society could be sustained more easily by having people reproduce, especially around the time his flying pirate ships are doing slave runs through aerial warzones.
