                    So, to perhaps more seriously address that:

                    It's worth remembering that in the US, the Baby Boomer and older Generation X workforce existing in a place where good health benefits were one of the major perks decades of labor activism had fought for. In an economy where a decent education meant you could get a job that you could have for decades, that would pay you enough to raise a family, get a house, have modest luxury and leisure items, health care was one of the big fights for what employers should offer their employees, and that became part of the "deal" for what good (aka middle class) jobs meant.

                    Job security (and the pensions that went along with it) and good employer based healthcare were the two big indicators of "making it." They were the status symbols that separated "real" careers from jobs "for teens" in how becoming a middle class American was defined.

                    That's why the big health aspects of the social safety net were things like Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP, and so on: People that couldn't get into those jobs needed help, not people that had good jobs.

                    That mentality is a huge part of why the US health system is so biased against universal healthcare. It's not just some weird aversion to the idea of government programs, but a reaction to a healthcare system that redefines what "success" means; which successful people tend to not like.

                    The growing push towards the US finally getting on a proper universal healthcare system is, of course, that the younger Gen Xers, Millennials, and Zoomers don't live in that America. That America doesn't exist (as much as it ever actually did since a lot of people never had access to it even during the time) That concept of success is long gone, because access to that success is so hard to find.
