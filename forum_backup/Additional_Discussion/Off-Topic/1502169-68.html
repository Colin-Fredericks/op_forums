                                                The problem is that while certainly someone is going to get thrown under the bus for this, it's invariably going to be the wrong person/people.

                                                1) The fires are a regional event over multiple jurisdictions. No matter how well prepared, there's invariably time lost in managing responses across nearly a dozen different groups. I'm sure lots of people are going to try to weigh in on the LAFD and city budget negotiations, but neither the LAFD, the LA city council, or mayor Bass seem to think the yearly budget adjustments would have made a significant difference; calls for accountability though are unlikely to care about that.

                                                2) The fires growing so large are a product of multiple problems that have been getting worse for decades if not centuries all hitting at the same time. There's limits on what you can prepare for when it comes to natural disasters in general, and even less you can do when you've been handed the shit-end of a stick that combines multiple facets of climate change, underfunded forest management, and flat out racist laws that nobody paid much attention to until recently as California fires have been getting worse (specifically in the 1800s the state outlawed the controlled burning methods used by indigenous peoples of the area for "scientific" methods proposed by colonial settlers, which has be quietly making that controlled burns the state does less and less effective over time).

                                                3) While I don't want to come over as overly crass here, that's a lot of people that might not have realized it, but voted for this. People voted against controlled burns in their neighborhoods because they didn't want the smoke and air quality bad days, because fires started in other places so why should they deal with it (because it still meant lots of fuel was in their neighborhoods and fire doesn't give a shit about city lines). People voted against ordinances to reduce or remove certain ornamental plants that look nice but also explode during intense fires spreading the fire even faster. Etc. etc. etc.

                                                And that gets us to the problem: we needed generations of southern Californian residents to be doing their part to keep the area resistant to fires (because fires have been happening there for thousands of years, and people have been living there for thousands of years, so it's not like we don't understand how to live there). Nobody alive today could have done enough to undo all the damage of generations of neglect of problems that could have been addressed before they got this bad. They could have started the process, they could have done some to make the damage less intense, but this was going to be a bad fire for reasons beyond any living individuals' control.

                                                IMO, people looking for heads to roll are wasting their time. Trying to pin the blame on a small group of individuals and seeing them lose their jobs doesn't fix all of the issues that will make the next round of bad fire so bad. Those issues are still going to be there, waiting for the right winds, and humidity, and increased winter heat levels, and improperly managed brush overgrowth, and so on until people focus on fixing all of that.

                                                            It's worth remembering that the vast majority of people living in the path of natural disasters do not have the kind of agency you're ascribing. I get caring is hard, but there's no magical place on the planet to just move to where there's work, affordable living, and no natural disasters to worry about. People are also interconnected, that's why caring matters. That's why we need some level of government to let communities manage the infrastructure necessary to make living somewhere comfortable; it's not a job that can be done on an individual basis.

                                                            You bring up earthquakes... but that's a perfect example of what happens when people keep caring and aim their efforts in the right way: In the past 25 years ten people have died in earthquakes in California. The last major earthquake before that killed 57 people in one quake. California's fault lines are still active, powerful quakes that do lots of damage happen regularly... but we don't hear about them because after a bunch of bad ones in the 90s, California majorly revamped building codes and that's lead to more and more quake resiliency over time. California buildings are now more like crumple zones in cars: there's still plenty of damaged structure to clean up and repair after a big quake, but people are protected. Being worried about earthquakes in California (as long as nobody does something stupid like lessen the building code restrictions because earthquakes don't kill as many people as they used to) is kinda silly because as far as natural disasters go... things are pretty well in hand.

                                                            Fires are harder, because there's less of a "one-size-fits-all" solution. You need lots of people, doing different things, in different places, to not just protect themselves, but also make sure fires are more easily contained so that they don't jump to other places. Breaks in that chain negatively impact everyone, not just the people failing to do their part. Your town can do all the fire prep, and do it right, and strong enough Santa Ana winds will make it all pointless if the next town over didn't. The people that didn't most certainly need to shut up and sit down when discussions of accountability and rebuilding with better infrastructure take place, but they're not a majority of people impacted by all this either.
