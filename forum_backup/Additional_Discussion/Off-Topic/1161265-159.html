                                        Originally posted by ArcaneArts View Post
                                        In fairness to the argument outside of the context*, so long as the ways stakes are established and maintained are in place, it is functionally unimportant to the narrative one could live, without interference, to the end of days. It's also easy to note how the age thing is sort of a formality in DnD in particular, where agelessness often ends up a purview of the PC's by virtue of what dat-darn-adventurin'-done-get-ya if they don't die early on.
                                        This is why I don't mind as much the wildly varying lifespans of DnD races. At least as a function of game design. The only timescale that matters in a campaign is the campaign itself; the period during which the PCs are out adventuring. As in theater, PCs in RPGs are born when the player starts running them, and they die when the player stops. Lifespan can affect roleplaying, but for all intents and purposes it's window dressing. Games like VtM show that you can run characters who are functionally immortal, and not have it break anything.

                                        Indeed, having characters who are naturally long-lived is a useful tool in the DM's arsenal. Who is more qualified to exposite about things that happened 500 years ago than a person who actually lived during that period? Yes, it can cause world-building issues (although stay tuned on that), I wouldn't want to remove it for precisely the reason I just explained.

                                        Originally posted by ArcaneArts
                                        There are games where stuff like that would break it for me, but DnD has such a long history of being medieval just to be such that I'm honestly numb to the setting breaks.
                                        While you aren't wrong in thinking that, on this point I must contest. DnD isn't just about medieval settings. It's about post-apocalypse settings. Just not in the way we tend to use the term these days (like Mad Max).

                                        We established earlier in the thread a theme of this world being a pale echo of an earlier, more vibrant age. That was a big thing with Tolkien; the civilization and struggles of Middle Earth were small potatoes compared to those of the past. DnD is much the same, not least because it cribbed from Tolkien, and from Jack Vance's Dying Earth series, and from post-Atlantis Conan stories, and from...well, from Real Life. The medieval era as we know it was the post-apocalypse of the fall of Rome.

                                        Not, of course, that Rome "falling" actually killed "civilization". Only the Western Roman Empire fell, which just happened to include the city proper. The Eastern Roman Empire (what we call Byzantium) continued on for many centuries after. Constantinople was hardly less advanced, prosperous, or glorious than Rome.

                                        What we think of as a "Medieval Age" is a sanded-down, simplified genre view of a Real Life age that was far more complicated than is depicted. Places like rural England, France, and Germany may have been pretty feudal - and continued to be so well into the 1500s - but you could travel a few hundred miles and step into what seemed like a completely different setting. Constantinople, Venice, Islamic and post-Islamic Spain, and other locations were vastly different. The "advance of progress" did not happen uniformly across the geographic board, because that's not how civilizations work.

                                        Why, then, should it be different in DnD? Having Elves be this race that lives hundreds of years doesn't "break" the medieval setting, any more than the presence of magic and monsters does. If elven longevity changes society, those changes probably center squarely on where elves live, while the humans who live one country over live more or less unchanged.

                                        The DnD setting I've been working on explicitly makes it so where you are geographically dictates what kind of campaign you'd run there. Anglia is a nation depleted by war and full of monsters who grew strong while the populace was out fighting and dying. Bordeau is the home of the Order of the Iron Ward, unilaterally trying to control magic from the shadows (they are NOT the "Magic Police" with backing from the crown), all against the backdrop of political unrest that threatens to explode into revolution. Porfieri is a Venetian canal city with as much going on below the waterline as above it, where swashbuckling intersects with court intrigue. The Metallic Principalities are a collection of landlocked states, on the cusp of war over who will take control and revive the Old Iron Empire. The unnamed continental shelf has an entire political ecosystem of merfolk kingdoms and city states, and the strait between Anglia and Bordeau is the quasi-frontier of efforts to reclaim that seabed space from the undead and monsters that drove merfolk out in the first place (and is also the site of numerous ruins, both merfolk and of a Doggerland-style formerly abovewater kingdom). All that is just on (or around) the one continent.

                                        While it's entirely reasonable to get bored with medieval fantasy, that's only because the fantasy is always hitting the same notes. It's like how people got sick of WWII shooters; it's not because the setting is lacking in material, but because all the WWII games fixated on aping Saving Private Ryan. A swords-and-sorcery setting can be a lot of things, if one is willing to explore beyond the stock genre conventions.

                                        Magestuck: The Ascension

                                                Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post
                                                I can't remember where I heard this, but I think in one of the settings, divine magic doesn't actually come directly from the gods. Rather, the gods simply teach their followers how to use the same magic that they themselves wield.

                                                Which has some intriguing implications, if that's true!
                                                This is how I prefer to look at divine magic in DnD. Granted, as a theist and a mythology nerd, I prefer deities to have an existence of their own. (I also don't much care for the idea that gods require worship; it paints them as little more than divine parasites, which doesn't sit well with me. Others may like this conceit and some great works - like Gaiman's American Gods - have come of it, but it's not how I prefer to conceive of divinity.)

                                                But there's a utility both to keeping the existence and nature of gods ambiguous, and in the idea that their worshipers don't necessarily derive "Power" from them, so much as the gods inspire mortals to transcendent understanding. Clerics use Wisdom as their casting stat for the same reason Druids (and to a certain extent Monks) do; they are actually Wise, and that Wisdom grants them the ability to effect change on the world. While Wizards understand magic intellectually, Clerics, Druids, and Monks understand it intuitively, in a way that words have difficulty expressing but that the character nonetheless understands on some level. It takes more than just faith in a god to get Cleric power, but rather exploring higher spiritual truths embodied by their god and spread by their faith. (This is why not all priests are Clerics; their faith may be strong, but understanding is a higher bar to reach).

                                                Instead of gods being the source of divine magic, they are teachers conveying a Logos that exists beyond the ken of "mere" deities.

                                                There's something objective about divine magic. Through this, a character doesn't necessarily need to worship a god in order to grasp those transcendental truths. They might be atheist, or merely agnostic, but subscribe to some philosophy or even institution (like the Azorius Senate on Ravnica) through which they explore spiritual matters.

                                                Nor, indeed, does what a character worships need to BE a god for them to inspire awe and understanding. A Spirit Naga, like the villain from the module Against the Cult of the Reptile God, has Clerics who serve her and have power, yet she only passes herself off as a deity, when in fact she's "only" an immortal. Another example are the Kuo Toa; the prevailing wisdom among some is that they create gods through some mechanism unique to them. But I think it's just that their fictional gods are their way of expressing and exploring greater spiritual ideas, and through this they achieve Cleric magic despite just worshiping idols. This framework is useful for justifying cults, both good and bad, that might not otherwise have divine backing in the sense we usually think of.

                                                To some extent, we have a bit of this with Charisma casters as well, though I have my own ideas about most of them. (Bards, for instance, do magic through the power of their artistry, being so intense that it taps into the primal aspects of creation). Warlocks might have a similar relationship with their Patrons as Clerics do their gods; the Patrons are teachers, not masses of "magical power" that the Warlock siphons "magical energy" from (if you can't tell, I'm kind of disenchanted with the Energy model of magic that dominates so much of media). This is how Warlocks can gain power from things like Hags or Unicorns, despite them not being that powerful. While the truths are more Arcane than Divine, they still have a "knowing" of magic, that they are able to convey to those who make pacts with them.

                                                Paladins, meanwhile, "tap into" divine truths more...indirectly. The Paladin doesn't necessarily need to understand transcendental matters (they don't need a high Wisdom), they just need to be totally committed to following them. Their Oaths give power through Charisma because the Paladin has the Will to live according to the precepts of that Oath (or, in the case of Oathbreakers, to completely commit to their transgression; a Paladin who lacked that conviction wouldn't become an Oathbreaker in class features when breaking their Oath, but would just turn into a shitty Fighter). A Paladin is a living conduit for the truths that Oath represents, so their thoughts and deeds channel the transcendental truths regardless.

                                                            I wasn't referring specifically to DnD, but the trope in general.

                                                            Plus, my preferred method of handling divinities in games is to have them less as "people" in the way we usually understand it, and more as personifications of abstract concepts. Essentially, self-aware Platonic Forms of concepts like Death, War, Order, Love, etc. Their identities as discreet entities being masks they assume based on what mortal worshipers expect to see in the rare cases where they directly meet (a vision of) their gods. It's less that belief makes gods, and more that the gods wear mortal conceptions of them like clothing, "changing outfits" as the need arises when interacting with different cultures or responding to theological drift.

                                                            The gods bother with the charade at all because 1) their true forms (like that of the One that stands at the center of creation) are simply too much for any mortal to bear, and 2) because while the stories and traits ascribed to them might not be literally true, they are useful teaching tools to allow mortals to grasp those transcendent truths I was talking about earlier. Their names can change - indeed, divisions between gods is far more fluid than one would think - but what they have to teach is the same. Those messages being different depending on which god (or perhaps which "Domain") it is; they are as much abstractions as beings, and wish to promote their area of expertise and the understanding thereof. (War teaches how to make war, regardless of right or wrong, because that's what War does, and because it has wisdom to impart, albeit specific to its purview).

                                                            Under this framework, being believed in is unimportant, save in the sense that faith in something is a positive force and a means of teaching mortals.
