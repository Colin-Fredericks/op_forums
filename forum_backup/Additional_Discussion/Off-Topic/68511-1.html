        I was being lazy. I actually do have a law degree and the quote I gave is more valid than your advice.

        Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
        It's worth nothing that the person with the liability in a lawsuit stemming from the tattoo of a copyrighted work would be the tattoo artist, not the customer. Theoretically the courts could order the tattoo altered/removed, but CCP (as the IP owners, they'd have to be the ones that sue you) wouldn't be able to sue a person with a WOD image tattooed on their body directly.
        No. The courts would be hard pressed to order the removal of a tattoo (meaning disfigurement and scarring). That would constitute a "cruel and unusual punishment" (see English Bill of Rights, the Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution, Article Five of the Declaration of Human Right, et al.). There is also the Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990 (VARA) that gives artists a number of rights, chief of this point is artistic integrity, prohibiting works of art from being mutilated regardless of physical ownership or copyright.

        This is exemplified in Carter v. Helmsley-Spear, Inc.

        "A bench trial was subsequently held in June and July 1994, at the conclusion of which the trial court granted the artists the permanent injunction prohibiting defendants from distorting, mutilating, modifying, destroying and removing plaintiffs' artwork."

        "The right of integrity allows the author to prevent any deforming or mutilating changes to his work, even after title in the work has been transferred."

        "Article 6bis of the Berne Convention protects attribution and integrity, stating in relevant part: Independently of the author's economic rights, and even after the transfer of the said rights, the author shall have the right to claim authorship of the work and to object to any distortion, mutilation or other modification of, or other derogatory action in relation to, the said work, which would be prejudicial to his honor or reputation."

        "Because they are personal to the artist, moral rights exist independently of an artist's copyright in his or her work."

        link: http://cyber.law.harvard.edu/metasch...es/carter.html

        ​
        Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
        Second, they'd have to beat back a derivative use defense. As tattooing is an art, the artist could claim that while the image looks the same, making it look the same on the contours of a human body combined with the person flair of the artist's own skills are sufficient derivation that it doesn't count as infringement. Essentially the argument would be that tattoos like this would fall under the same protections as pop art (like Warhol's Campbell's soup series).
        A 'derivative use defense' does not exist.

        Subject to sections 107 through 122, the owner of copyright under this title has the exclusive rights to do and to authorize any of the following:
        (1) to reproduce the copyrighted work in copies or phonorecords;
        (2) to prepare derivative works based upon the copyrighted work;
        (3) to distribute copies or phonorecords of the copyrighted work to the public by sale or other transfer of ownership, or by rental, lease, or lending;
        (4) in the case of literary, musical, dramatic, and choreographic works, pantomimes, and motion pictures and other audiovisual works, to perform the copyrighted work publicly;
        (5) in the case of literary, musical, dramatic, and choreographic works, pantomimes, and pictorial, graphic, or sculptural works, including the individual images of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, to display the copyrighted work publicly; and
        (6) in the case of sound recordings, to perform the copyrighted work publicly by means of a digital audio transmission.
        If it is not authorised, it is automatically an unlawful derivative work.

        link: http://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html

        Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
        Third, even if the derivative use argument isn't accepted by the court, the tattoo artist could still claim it as fair use. In other words, it would fall into the category of copyright infringements we allow as a society as part of the balancing act of protecting the monetary value of copyrighted work to the creators so they can make a profit and the cultural value of the works to society at large. However, since fair use is inherently infringement, it isn't on CCP to prove the tattoo is not fair use (though it behooves them to make a strong case and not trust that the person they're suing can't make a good one), it is up to the tattoo artist to make the case that the tattoo is fair use. This is a big fat question mark though, since US law sets very little hard precedent here and judges individual cases by a set of criteria.                                                             That's not quite the issue here though. There is a safe, no guessing, method: ask the IP-holder. I have no idea how easy it would be to ask CCP, but it seems like contacting Rich about the stuff that Onyx Path owns outright would be simple enough. If you get a yes, then there is no legal issue what-so-ever.

                                                            Outside of that though, this is less of a "it is only illegal if you get caught," and more of a, "you can't know if it will be ruled illegal or not unless you get caught." There isn't a huge stack of consistent case-law to guide anyone here.

                                                            Imagine if speed limits weren't hard lines, but guidelines that the police and the general public can apply some judgement on. So driving 10 mph over the limit on a clear day, one a easy stretch of road, while nobody is around for miles isn't inherently illegal because there's really no risk of harm. But going 1 mph below the limit in a blizzard with 5 foot visibility and horrible traction on even the straightest of roads could be considered unsafe driving and ticket-worthy.

                                                            On one hand, this system allows for motorists and the police to make cases more situational and not have fines levied over an arbitrary number. On the other hand, it means that should there be a dispute about the safety of the speed someone was traveling, it becomes very sticky on what was, or was not, legal. An officer could pull you over for doing +15 mph over the suggested but in court both sides would be arguing over whether or not that was really dangerous in the situation or not.

                                                            That is, basically, where IP law tends to be. The latitude that allows for cases to be handled so that each one is examined in its unique context also creates a huge amount of uncertainty over the legality of any edge cases.
