    About magic items: burn me, but I actually like the 3e/PF model better. Yes, I could see how it made magic items "mundane". Still I think in a high magic setting, magic items are somewhat expected to become a kind of technology and constraining them into plot devices and solely under the DM's supervision never made much sense to me. I get it in a low-magic setting, where every magic item is an unique and special artifact (and I totally get why others like that more). In FR, or Golarion? Nay, I don't think so.
    One thing that I'm surprised that was never officially implemented in D&D for weapons and equipment was material grade: the idea that weapons and armor made of a certain material was overall better then others.

    I see this happen a lot in video games where there are various material grades where your character(s) are chasing equipment grades to upgrade from. Like, starting out with bronze equipment, then chasing for iron weapons, then moving up to steel weapons, etc. It wouldn't completely invalidate magic items, in fact it would make magic items seem even more special, but it's not the only path of equipment progression. That a player would be just as excited as getting, say, an adamantine broadsword or a mithral shield as they would enchanted versions. It would also show off player status: your guy would have to be a badass adventurer who went through a ton of shit to be all decked out in mithral gear versus the bandits lugging around iron gear.

    Plus we'd have weapons just be referred to as the more diagetic "iron sword" or "steel sword" versus immersion breaking "+1 sword" or "+2 sword".

        Ah yes, indeed AD&D, at least 1E and 2E (I'm the inverse of yourself, never played 3.0 or 4.0, only getting back into the game with 5E) had such strongholds which could be founded, however D&D had explicit rules for building them, managing them and mass combat for defending them.

        The true endgame was Apotheosis (made explicit in the 1974 edition, referred to coyly in Mentzer's era as becoming "Immortal" to avoid trouble with Christian groups). Other differences between the games are things such as the focus being on removing treasure from dungeons rather than combat and adventuring. Also monsters being rational creatures who may be persuaded or simply be non-antagonistic being the default.

        If you want an classic D&D experience with 5E, in the DMG I'd use the Chapter 9's "Gritty Realism" variant and Chapter 8's Milestones variant, using treasure to define milestones. If you want to get even closer ignore Skills, Backgrounds and Feats. However that latter set only matter for strict emulation (reducing the rules), "Gritty Realism" and Milestones capture the feel without losing 5E's nice character system.

            Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post

            My dream campaign would be one where the PCs spend the first tier of play (1st-4th level) just scraping to get by. Taking jobs to pay off their lifestyle expenses, supply them with arms, armor, ammunition, and spell components, and saving what they can. By the second tier (5th-10th), they’ve made enough of a name for themselves to start taking on bigger jobs, amassing more wealth and fame until they reach 3rd tier (11th-15th). At that point, they would start investing the wealth they had earned and leveraging their fame as heroes and building some sort of lasting institution. At that point (the point where most campaigns peeter out), I’d put those characters on hold. While they’re off building their strongholds and establishing their churches, start some new characters, maybe apprentices of the higher level ones. Play them through those early adventuring levels. Every once in a while, pop back in on the more experiences characters. Have them deal with a major threat to the realm, or if they’re the more self-interested type, whatever institution they founded. Go back and forth like this for a while, until the new characters get to third tier and the more experienced ones break through to fourth tier (16th-20th). The original characters become kings, queens, archmages, world-famous thieves, religious leaders, what have you. The apprentice characters start to found something of their own. Start some new first or second tier characters to carry on the torch. Repeat the same process, only this time you have the original characters occasionally deal with world-shaking, maybe even multiverse-shaking events. Theoretically this process could go on as long as you wanted, just retiring characters who make it all the way to 20th level. But yeah, I love that concept of not discarding old characters when you want to start a new campaign, but just putting them on the sidelines for a bit, there to play a higher-stakes adventure with from time to time when you need a change of pace from the newbies.

            That kind of... Got away from the original point a bit... but what I’m trying to get at is, third and fourth tier characters should have plenty to spend their gold on, but D&D hasn’t really supported high tier play since the TSR days. And there’s sort of this vicious cycle where high level play isn’t well supported, so no one plays it, so WotC doesn’t see any need to make support for it.
            If you wanted to drift back to 3.5 or Pathfinder for a bit, some of us (your's truely included) covered the low-level part of this in a fan made adventure on Planewalker.com around 2007 to 2008. We made an introductory adventure for Planescape called Desire and the Dead that spans levels 3 to 5 where the PCs start of as a community watch group in Sigil. You can still find the PDF online.

                                                    Originally posted by Morty View Post
                                                    Every time I hear about old D&D's high-level options, they feel arbitrary and stemming mostly from the franchise's inherent inability to deal with the power levels it allows for.
                                                    My personal understanding is that they come more from the origin of the hobby. In the sense that when you got to "name level" or became a lord, you essentially became the commanding unit of an army. That is, the game progressed sort of into a wargame. In the original 1974 rules that wargame took the form of Chainmail, a previous product by TSR, in the later Mentzer rules it was the WarMachine system. You sort of ended up roleplaying the day-to-day life of a commander, but mechanically became an elite unit in a wargame.

                                                    First off, why would a high-level character retire? They've got more reason than ever to go out and personally deal with things, since there's increasingly few things in the world that can threaten them. Powerful monsters and other high-level individuals, yes, but they can more or less laugh off the efforts of a small mortal nation to stop them.
                                                    Although in AD&D characters do become "superhero" in strength, in D&D I don't think they do outside of possibly the magic user. In D&D a level 14 Fighter is basically identical to a level 3 fighter, he just has more health and better gear and the gear list was quite small, ~15 weapons and ~4 types of armour. This feeds into why the character would become a Lord/Stronghold owner. By Level 14 there was nothing more to do. RPGs were new, so there weren't feats or any real customization options. The rules don't say you have to retire, just that that becomes an option. I think it's just something Gygax and Arneson thought might be a cool progression rather than something being dictated to follow.

                                                    Also D&D had some enemies, not found in AD&D, like the colossal dragons from the Companion volume, that can really only be taken down by a party + their armies, not just the party.

                                                    Another thing is that early D&D sort of simulates the kind of fantasy found in Vance's "The Dying Earth", Lieber's "Lankhmar" and Anderson's "Three Hearts and Three Lions". These three series of novels are the origin of most elements of D&D Magic works like "Mazirian the Magician" from "The Dying Earth". Thieves have guilds and operate like characters in Lankhmar. Clerics, the alignment concept and many monsters are from "Three Hearts and Three Lions".

                                                    In all these pulp sci-fi fantasy series, powerful characters are Lords or Tower owners, who send their armies or creations against their enemies. The assumption at the time was that the most powerful fantasy characters were basically Aragorn, rather than a superhero. They didn't anticipate the one man army super characters in fantasy.

                                                    Giving every fighter a keep and every thief a literal thieves' guild is also needlessly restrictive. There are many ways both classes/archetypes can extend their power beyond the reach of their weapons.
                                                    Again this isn't the form it had to take, just an example. Even the classes are just examples, rather than the only ones. A note in the 1974 edition says that characters can be anything even a dragon or any monster race. The classes supplied are just for illustration.
