    But related to something Nobody mentioned, what with the police armed up with military hardware as a standard thing these days, I got curious and did a spot of research. See, apart from a slight spike back up in the last few years (economic woes tend to cause that) violent crime has been on a steady decline for the last 2 generations. Reporting is WAY up, but violence is down.

    But the ultimate gauge of serious issue is death, so I looked to police deaths in the line of duty. And there are good and bad years, but the thing is the US has had between 100-200 deaths per year in the line of duty for the last CENTURY.

    So while the population has increased 4 times, officer deaths have remained the same. Currently about 0.3% of the population at large is a law enforcement officer one one variety or another (prison, federal, state, local), and unfortunately I can't find statistics for the country going back more than a decade or two on that front, but the simple population grown vs decreasing deaths speak to it becoming it a safer job. But let's put this into some standard numbers, we have roughly 1,000,000 police in the US and 300 million people, with 100-200 deaths per year in the force, giving us 10-20 deaths per hundred thousand. The reason for this rather odd number is standardization. For comparison, independent contractors, the realm that usually falls to the construction workers of various sorts and freelancers in general have a number of 12.7 per 100k. For comparison Firefighters have a death rate of 2.33, and the average is in 3.5 range.

    So even before the police started getting military hardware things were steadily getting better, implying at least that it's not the presence of assault weapons and body armor that render police safer.

            Originally posted by Nobody View Post
            Probably what needs to happen in the US is that we stop the military from selling its excess hardware to local law enforcement. However, this creates a problem of then what do we do with the excess hardware? That's a lot of shit to go to waste.
            Well the traditional method is to sell/gift the stuff to other countries, preferably with nice and expensive maintenance contracts.

            Part of the problem is also of supply, demand, storage, and changing tech.

            The M1 Abrams tank is a good example of this. The factory that manufactures them is exceeding demand in the US. They are quite literally rolling off the line and into storage against the assumption of another order in 3 years or so. The argument was raised, why not shut it down then? The problem is that it costs more to do that. To shut the line down and fire all the people is fine and will save money for at least the next 3 years. The problem being that to fire up the line again should an order come in 3 years hence, to find people able to use the equipment and meet the requirements and certifications, will cost *more* than just running the plant pointlessly for 3 years.

            Nature of manufacturing, we have reached a point with technology and manufacturing that we have a minimum volume produced.

            Demand, well that's a straight forward one. Unsurprisingly when you're not active horrific ground wars, equipment isn't being destroyed and doesn't need to be replaced. This is doubly true when you're going through force size reductions.

            Storage... surprisingly you need some fairly specific conditions to successfully store goods for long periods of time, which are difficult and expensive to set up and maintain. So that's a headache right there.

            Finally the big one, changing tech. Even if we had say a huge old salt mine that was re-purposed to military storage and the option was chosen to simply store all the extra guns, tanks, planes and weapons against future need in future wars... it wouldn't matter. technology continually updates and military hardware is one of those big drivers. So dumping a bunch of extra equipment into storage, even if that storage were to exist, isn't actually going to be terribly helpful for the future because it'll be vastly outdated by the time it's needed.


            So we rapidly fall into a situation where because of the need to keep up on the newest hardware and have minimum manufacturing volumes, there is going to be a large volume of surplus equipment, be it a generation old or just not in current demand.

            Spinning off from that thought, there are a lot of amusing things from the end of WWII when a more significant equipment glut existed, in some cases (such as the artificial deep reef in the pacific) it was simply tossed overboard, in some cases it was just distributed via returning troops (my grandfather was discharged with his side arm, rifle and main weapon which would be disturbing if his main weapon wasn't a PIAT gun), but frankly that solution can't really be applied now. Besides the increasing gun control measures, dumping several hundred thousand surplus military grade weapons on the general market would be a disaster.


            But to jump back into the problem, odd as it sounds technology *might* have a solution of sorts for the minimum manufacturing volume issue... If the work force is replaced exclusively with robotic systems, those systems are a lot easier to re-purpose and change around as needed. Unfortunately that's not likely to happen because of the "American Jobs" angle.
