              Bernie's certainly a real candidate. He's the only current serious contender to HRC; the rest of the Democratic Party candidates really don't have enough driving force.

              He's also ahead in one poll in New Hampshire. Which basically means squat (almost all the polls at this point basically mean squat outside of the weird thing the RNC is doing with their debates to handle their massive 17 candidate field), because it's a single poll, in a single state (favorable to Bernie in general), ages before anyone votes on anything. Keep in mind the first time New Hampshire voters will cast a ballot on this is in February.

              There's actually a fairly significant backlash amongst the US polling organizations at how poorly the US media is using the polling data. Polling at this point in US presidential races is almost universally insignificant. It's just laying the foundations for a solid polling data set in a few months.

              I like Bernie, though I'm not sure he's the best choice from a general election perspective. The current state of things leaves the Democrats with a huge electoral advantage, but that means a candidate that can pull swing states is all the more important. The real test for Bernie's viability isn't Iowa and New Hampshire, it's places like Ohio and Virginia. Because those are the states that are actually going to decide the election in the end (in terms of statistical uncertainty).

              That said, I think Bernie's actually a lot like Biden: a really good Vice President in the making. He's extremely experienced in DC and really does know how to make the system work. He'd be a hell of an operative for pushing the general liberal/progressive Democratic platform in the Congress.

                                  It does speak kind of poorly for him when the loudest things are not, "Here's why Bernie's awesome," but rather, "Here's why I'm not voting for Hillary."

                                  Though I never did get the whole, "dynasty," thing. It's a democracy. We should select people based on their merits, not their family. Which also means, "they're related to a past president," shouldn't matter either. FDR was related to eleven past presidents. Good thing nobody held that against him... And exceedingly few presidents didn't come from political families; even if that didn't mean a lot of presidents per family.

                                  The e-mails are also a pretty much non-scandal. You know what happens to the vast majority of such things? They get deleted, either on purpose, or by neglect, because the government generates a vast amount of records, and doesn't employ nearly enough archivists to actually do anything with them. So most of them get dumped somewhere, and never get found because nobody actually cares about them.

                                  Even if deleting them would be something that you could consider a crime... bringing legal action does... nothing. Because there's no case. Innocent until proven guilty remember? How do you prove she deleted something she shouldn't have, instead of something she was within her rights to delete... if she deleted them thoroughly enough that they can't be recovered (and if they could be recovered, they could be turned over...). It was certainly a stupid thing for her to do, but if she committed a crime in doing it there's no way to prove it. So bringing charges is just a waste of time and money because there's no case to build in an actual court. As such, it's only valuable as a political attack against her in the court of public opinion where suspicious is all you need compared to actual proof.

Which also means, "they're related to a past president," shouldn't matter either. FDR was related to eleven past presidents. Good thing nobody held that against him... And exceedingly few presidents didn't come from political families; even if that didn't mean a lot of presidents per family.
                                                  Where I see the bigger headache coming in is fame and association with fame.

                                                  People hear a name, recognise it, it fits into some shapes in their mind, and the contexts associated with that shape decide level of support. This is why actors have a major leg up, people know "Oh hey he's that famous actor guy that does action movies or was it cowboy movies?". This is why presidents have a significant advantage in second term elections. This is why people link families together. You are supporting a Bush, so he's probably going to be like that previous Bush and the Bush before him, or you're supporting a Kennedy, well he'll probably be like the other bits of the Kennedy family elected.

                                                  It's one of those fuzzy lines for the US... there are a lot of powerful, rich, political families. They have all the advantages that increase the odds of them getting elected if they go into politics. They have reasons to go into politics. And that makes some people uncomfortable since your leaders coming from largely family lines... well, it's that whole US and kings and lords thing.
