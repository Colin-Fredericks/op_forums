                    Oh shoot, I forgot something...
                    Originally posted by Charlequin
                    What ever happened to "show, don't tell"?
                    Okay, so the thing about 'show, don't tell' is that it undermines the credibility of a character/scenario and is just lazy writing besides when there's something talking at length about some quality that never actually gets depicted (note; we see Poe come back for piloting on Swamp Bar planet, which is the film's moment to show us how good he is, and I at least think it's effective). It stands out especially when a writer doesn't know how to depict the talent, leaving it as a hole in the story, or something that gets shown and bungles it. So yeah, you've got to depict characters as exhibiting the traits we're supposed to see them as having, whether or not they're talked about. That does not mean that they can be depicted as doing basically anything without any reference to context. 'Show don't tell' does not automatically mean that showing a thing is superior storytelling, especially if the thing doesn't make sense; when people see a thing that doesn't match the surroundings, it increases a sense of artificiality. On top of that, appealing to an unseen or mentioned background is kind of the opposite of showing, and implicitly acknowledges that the film has not depicted a thing that is necessary to preserving its own credibility.

                                                Originally posted by LokiRavenSpeak View Post

                                                I always heard the term applied as unisex. Remember first reading the term on discussions about sasuke from naruto back when i liked anime.
                                                It is sometimes used for male characters, yeah. The term actually originally referred to a very specific archetype of fan fiction OC, which happened to be female in the iconic example but could technically be any gender. It has evolved into a much more general term for characters who are "too perfect" or sometimes for characters that serve as stand-ins for the author (where the original meaning was both). Some folks use the term for characters of all genders, while other people use Gary Stu or Marty Stu (depending on their preference between rhyming and alliteration) for male characters. Either way, the "this character's positive traits are undeserved" complaint, what ever name is used for it, seems to me to be made about female leads more often than male leads.
