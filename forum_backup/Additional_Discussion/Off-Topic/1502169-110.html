                        This is not new. They're just repeating an old trend of clinging to a chosen source rather than making searches.

                        At work we have a search engine at hand just for the company normative. It's gigantic, and since we're a Brazilian bank they're not guidelines, they're serious law covering every procedure. Reading it is important, disobeying can have heavy consequences, and they are regularly changing.

                        95% of workers rely on others to know what to do instead of reading, go by memory until the procedure they remember outright fails before asking if it changed, and get really angry when someone tells them to read the normative, even if this person tells exactly what to look for.

                        I've seen people be fired as consequence and even then nothing changed.

                        LLMs are worsening this due to easy access, but the problem is actually way older. They are content with any answer, regardless if it is correct, and will frequently set up their brains to not only believe, but fiercely defend anything they heard as undeniable truth. And the cause: this is instinct to save energy, because thinking about it is literally harder.

                        And yeah, bootlicking also helps a lot, which is sad, but just look in the corporate world how much sycophants always have it easier. Even when it's just their nature, I'm not even cynical enough to say they're always doing it in bad faith or are always incompetent. But the effect is depressingly effective.

                        But I also disagree that verifying is getting harder, I find it actually easier. We have more sources, and if you know how to reign in their stupidity LLMs are excellent to accelerate the search and curation of sources.

                        It just keeps not being automatic. You have to know how to verify and put the effort, and that's what most people won't do. Also the internet multiplied non curated sources, which means you don't have at least a baseline for where people get most of their disinformation. TV gave us a common ground, but also a hard to verify source that could and did, nonetheless, lie. The internet made the source of their lie harder to pinpoint, and AI is empowering people to lie to themselves more effectively than ever.

                        Still I think human made lies have a tendency to be more dangerous.

                        Videos haven't been trustworthy ever. Taking things out of context or manipulating them is as old as the record technologies themselves. And without a decent legal framework to crack down on fake news and their production process, I'd say we're already too saturated by them to say AI is making much of a difference.

                        I'd say even the opposite: if we pass law making invisible watermarks and similar mandatory, AI would make way easier to track fake news to the source. Sure, it would still be possible to bypass, but it would require enough effort to get us back to Photoshop.
