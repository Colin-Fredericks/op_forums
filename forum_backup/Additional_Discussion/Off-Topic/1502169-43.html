            A different perspective: people aren't sick and tired of superhero movies, they're tired of companies milking the whole "cinematic universe" concept.

            There's a few key factors:

            1) Volume of content. This is really the biggest problem. Watching the entire MCU film catalog (so not even getting into all the streaming shows) is ~80 hrs. Phase 4 alone is ~50 hrs. That is why people burnt out in a nutshell. It takes less time to watch all the Phase 1-3 films than it does to watch just the Phase 4 ones. If you wanted to watch every last bit of MCU film and TV show right now, it would take you ~50 days without any breaks. To give some perspective on this, Sazae-san, the longest running anime ever (and still in production so that title isn't going anywhere) with a whopping 2,640 episodes (at normal Japanese format for a 30 minute show with ads) would take under 38 days to watch all of. The MCU has more content than a children's show that's been ongoing for 55 years. And most of that content has come recently, all in a big wave of releases.

            2) Streaming fragmentation. This is something that's not unique to the concept of cinematic universes, but it impacts them hard. Esp, with the Sony-verse and MCU crossing over, and the X-Men/Deadpool vs. possibly coming to the MCU, the giant heft of watching all of this stuff is compounded by it not all being in one place. Loved seeing the three live-action Spider-men teaming up and want to go back and watch the older movies? Well... which streaming services do you have? Loved the Kingpin and want to go back and see his earlier introduction.... well.... hmmm... how's Netflix feeling about unlocking those again? How about Agents of SHIELD? Woo boy. Of course this leads into:

            3) Licensing! One of the biggest problems is that Disney can't get everything under one roof. It's kinda ridiculous, but considering how much we're seeing companies just wipe shows/movies from services and locking them up in digital vaults, there's clearly more money in owning and not showing something than showing it,or licensing it to someone that will. Even WB with the DC franchise films more solidly locked up in ownership still can't seem to get its house in order on what to watch where.

            4) Superheroes are still doing well when companies play to the strengths of the genre. The Boys got successful spinoffs. Invincible did well. The Marvel and DC animated stuff continues to be high quality and well loved by fans. The audience is there. The want is there. But this goes right back to 1: the time isn't there for what the big studios are trying to do.

                                Reshoots have been part of cinema since the beginning. "We'll fix it in post" has been part of the policy of pretty much every major studio for over a century. There's nothing new to doing reshoots. There's nothing new to reshoots ballooning budgets. There's nothing particularly unique to Disney or Marvel here either (Justice League and Suicide Squad....).

                                Reshoots are one of those things that the general public only really notices or cares about when trying to explain why something is wrong with a movie. Nobody sits around and says, "man, Back to the Future really blew it by doing three weeks of reshoots," because the end result was seen by everyone as for the better (Eric Stoltz probably wasn't happy about it at the time, but he doesn't seem bitter about it) and the movie made a ton of movie. Speaking of Disney, Rogue One had a huge amount of reshoots by their standards, and is considered one of the better Disney era Star Wars films.

                                They're just parts of the business. Everyone does them, they're not controversial on their own. Most of the time you don't even notice them (esp. when everything is still within budget), sometimes they are vast improvements, sometimes they manage to make a bad movie worse. Extensive reshoots can make a bleh movie good, but cost too much to net a profit despite increased sales for having a better movie, and they can flip a movie that was going to bomb to at least do OK and be worth the expense.

                                Focusing on reshoots is, like focusing on superheroes to me missing the core problem. The core is the "cinematic universe" because it's a balancing act between creative teams with a strong identity and a cohesive franchise that all of those different creatives need to be making at the same time. The more projects, the harder it is for the people at the top to work with each team individually. Extensive reshoots are just a symptom of trying to keep the "cinematic universe" cohesive when you can't ensure producers are as involved on each project as much as they should be. It gets to the point where it's easier to "fix" things later than try to manage four different $100+ million projects hands-on.

                                            If this was unique to Marvel/Disney, I'd consider this more important. But as it's not, I don't see the point.

                                            Also the "Marvel Method" is a comic book scripting scheme Stan Lee came up with decades ago. While nobody claims it's the "end-all-be-all" it's the core "character" to Marvel properties and is actually important to translating Marvel properties to other media because if you don't understand the Marvel Method you don't understand how the fan favored characters and plots were constructed in the first place; which you need to do if you want to adapt them well.

                                            The Marvel Method is also pretty useful for film. What made it so revolutionary was trusting artists to do their jobs. Before Stan Lee implemented it, the industry standard was "Full Script." The writers/editors of the comic companies would hand the artists a complete script with action direction and page layout demands, and the artists were just there to make it happen on page. The Marvel Method was to just give them a broad outline of major story beats (either visual or text) that needed to be hit, and let more get filled in collaboratively by the people actually putting it to paper.

                                            This approach to collaborative story making helped Marvel stand out as not being nearly as cookie-cutter as DC was at the time, and allowed a lot of break out characters that were created to fill various plot needs to have room to get turned into future major icons of the company. The whole point is to avoid things feeling like they're designed by a bunch of executives and let the creative flair of individuals shine through to help give titles their own character and feel... because the executive in question literally didn't have time to keep up the Full Script method for all the books Marvel was trying to make at the time and had to cut his oversight and trust in his creatives more.

                                            It might have some major issues translated to film,but at the same time, who's done a massive film franchise better exactly? Just because the MCU started down a bad path to keeping up what it created, doesn't mean other methods have produced a better approach to a massive cinematic franchise yet. The biggest comparison I can think of is the James Bond franchise, but what they didn't do is content bloat. People constantly question if James Bond should just finally be retired, new eras come in. There's high points. There's low points. But the argument to not make another James Bond movie never seems to be correct.
