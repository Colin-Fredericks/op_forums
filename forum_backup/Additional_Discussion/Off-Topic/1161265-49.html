                Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post
                Exactly. I'm not saying a character is forced to behave in accordance with their alignment every time (otherwise, how could they fall / grow?). But the alignment system provides a glimpse into the character's psychology and how they would normally behave in accordance with the situation.
                Pretty much, Alignment is simply a reflection of someone's actions over time. When you look at alignment, it's a reflection of the types of actions that the character typically engages in. This is where you can get some interesting things like the nobleman who has deluded himself into believing he's a good person but his alignment is Neutral Evil. What does that say about the kinds of actions he's engaged in?

                Really, it's not much different from the "Good/Evil" bars you sometimes see in video games (Mass Effect, Star Wars, etc). If you tend to engage in a lot of actions that fall into one of the alignments, then that's what your alignment is. If you start engaging in other actions outside of those, then your alignment will slowly shift. In general D&D is more interested in dealing with the immediate present, so it's never had huge, in-depth rules about changing alignment, just leaving it up to the players and the GM to change a PC's or NPC's alignment if necessary (with the understanding that they will give out warnings to players, letting them know that certain actions could have repercussions on alignment).

                When an NPC is lawful neutral, they're going to deal with a stressful situation much differently than someone who is a chaotic evil, and it's nice to have alignment as a short-hand reflection for determining how NPCs are likely going to act in a given situation.

                    Originally posted by Morty View Post
                    But they don't necessarily have anything in common with any of those. They might, but they might not.
                    Not necessarily, but they might, and it says something about the world they exist in whether they do or do not. Both are valid choices that inform the setting.

                    Originally posted by Morty View Post
                    And do we really need a spell that protects against an entire arbitrarily-decided category, or is it just because we've always had it? The last thing D&D has ever suffered a shortage of is spells.
                    Do we need it? Of course not. We don’t need to have each creature slapped with a type identifier like “beast”, “humanoid”, “fiend”, “fey”, “giant”, etc. Doing so merely gives you flags for the mechanics to look for, which can be useful depending on how you use them. Alignment, from a design perspective, is just another such flag that can be used. Or not used. If it’s not used, there’s really no point in it being there. If it is used, it is useful. Don’t mistake distaste for how it has been used as actual lack of utility.

                    If you don’t like alignment, there’s absolutely nothing wrong with removing it from the game. But it isn’t useless.

                    Originally posted by Morty View Post
                    And yes, it works best as a guideline, it should be broad... and then you realize you've watered it down so much it no longer means anything or is anything. Why not just use something you don't need to continually ignore? Something that outlines your character's values without bringing objective morality into it. Like... Virtue/Vice, Intimacies, motivations, story arc or any other of the myriad systems other games use for it?
                    See, you’re describing here why it doesn’t work best as a guideline. If you’re going to use alignment as a mere outline of a character’s values then it is practically useless. That role is better served by deeper and more nuanced systems like CofD’s Virtue/Vice, Fate’s Aspects, etc. like you say. I’m arguing that it’s value is more akin to color in Magic the Gathering (as an identifier separate from supertype, type, and subtype). And in order to work that way it needs clear definitions.

                    To be perfectly clear: I’m not saying alignment needs to be rigid because morality systems should be rigid. Alignment makes for a poor morality system. I’m not saying alignment needs to be rigid so it can be used to force players to roleplay a certain way for fear of punishment. That just leads to antagonistic group dynamics. I am saying that there is potentially value in a system of flags to put on characters, on a separate axis from creature type, and in order to be useful for that purpose, each such flag needs clear definitions.
