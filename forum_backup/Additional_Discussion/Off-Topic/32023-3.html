                                I've dabbled in 2E and 4E, and played 3-3.5E pretty heavily. I used to think that I could tell whatever story I like regardless of the system I'm using. Now I realize that's bunk, and the Story will be shaped by the rules that govern it, even if that influence manifests in the ST having to rig up a bunch of house rules to support the type of story he and his players actually wanted.

                                I find D&D valuable, as a frequent GM/ST, because each edition tends to be very blatant about the different ways the system will shape the stories you'll use it to tell. Further, the stories the systems are geared to direct you towards tend to match the stories the fluff text in the books suggest. A lot of other systems are full of hidden dangers that result in the play experience being very different from the sort of stories the books, on the surface, seem to suggest. Anyone who tried to run a CWoD game using the rules strictly as written knows what I'm talking about. Every time I try another RPG system, or think about including a peripheral supplement to a system I already know, I have to spend time thinking about what those new rules will actually do when my players interact with them, and what kind of play those rules will encourage. That's rarely a problem with core D&D stuff. It's a bit less certain with the enormous sprawl of peripherals that each edition accumulates, but having a well-defined foundation helps a lot when trying to figure out how those expansions will actually modify it.

                                Is a game or two of D&D worth the experience? Yes.
                                Are you less of a "true gamer" if you don't ever get around to it? No.
                                Are you more of a "true gamer" if you shun it? Hell no.

                                Just remember the Mantra of Nerd Harmony - It's OK for different people to like things that you dislike, and dislike things that you like. It's not OK to be an asshole about it, though.

                                            I hope people don't mind I'm bringing this thread back.

                                            My impression of DnD is, it is a RPG with training wheels (my experience is with versions 3.0/3.5 and a bit of pathfinder). It is difficult to make mistakes bad enough to wreck a character to the point of being unplayable (you would probably need to know what you are doing). The single most important choice you can make in the game is the character class you pick for your character. Your class determines nearly everything that your character can do, what special things it can do, what general things it can do well, and what things it does poorly at. Your character class is mostly set in stone. For instance, at level 5 all fighters have a +5 bonus to attack rolls because of their class, at that same level all wizards can get access to level 3 spells.

                                            Some classes are more sensitive to poor customization in other details (like ability scores such as strength) than other classes. A character with poor stats in strength, dexterity, and constitution probably shouldn't be a fighter; a wizard with poor stats in intelligence would be limited in the level of spells it can cast (or might be unable to cast spells at all). Other things might add to your abilities or improve performance of what you can do, but these are often things you can do without.

                                            The problem I have with such a system is it is difficult to break the mold. The classes are put together in ways that they are balanced with each other, but if you wanted to play something different than what was offered, it would be difficult to do the proper balance (the game didn't offer much support for that). Of course, moving to other game systems that are not class based but are point buy based, one comes to learn that it is easy to make broken characters. One would need some understanding of the rules, know what works and what doesn't work, and maybe played a few damaged characters to learn from experience, in order to make good characters. Example characters do help plenty, as does discussing what works for the game.

                                            Overall I prefer other games systems than DnD, even though they are simpler to set up a game for one (dungeon crawls are easy to setup).

                                                    I wouldn't describe DnD as an RPG with training wheels.

                                                    Well okay, roleplay wise, yes. It's not an immensely flexible thing(although more flexible than I think even the Wizards team realize), but once race and class are picked, there's a certain amount of railroading happening. On top of that, it plays with motifs and archetypes so engrained into the Western consciousness that it doesn't really take much explanation to get someone into their role (contrast that even with either Mages from WoD, where you'd think the idea of who and what you are would really be intrinsic but requires more education than you'd imagine). In terms of concept, it is very quick to pick up and play.

                                                    But DnD-any edition-are all somewhere on the simulationist side of the roleplaying mechanics spectrum. There's a lot of information to memorize in order to run your game, and the nature of the math of the game is far from intuitive. Also, 4th Edition might have been the most dumbed down the game ever got, and it still quite often required multiple rolls to determine is something actually worked. Earlier editions were even more ridiculous in the nature of some of their demands. This frequently means that a DM either has to do frequent heavy lifting or fudgery, or a player has to read the book just to get an idea of how they can do anything. Contrast this, again, with at least WoD, which while not ridiculously easy to understand is still fairly quick to pick up and understand and execute, with players needing to read to understand the depths of what they can do.

                                                    Yeah, DnD is a beginner game in some ways, but not enough so that I feel I could ever describe it as having training wheels.
