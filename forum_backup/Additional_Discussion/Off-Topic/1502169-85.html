    Very true indeed.

    I didn't expected many people to know Santos Dumont*. He's famous in Brazil and France, and I think that's about it unless you're seriously into aviation history. My beef is with the Wright Brothers and how they actually harmed aviation far more than helped it due to their greed. I wouldn't be as pissed-off if people just thought aviation was a collective endeavor without any particular hero, or praised one of the other names that could claim the first "valid" flight, like Traian Vuia, Clément Ader, or even Karl Jatho.

    To be honest, the "first airplane" thing is mostly a marketing stunt. A lot of people did a lot of "flights" that both contributed something to the field, yet weren't really practical airplanes, and both the 14-Bis and the Wright Flyer were exactly that. Nice shows and good proofs of concept, but nothing else. The very concept of an aviation institution was actually older, the Aéro-Club de France was organizing regular events since 1898. Actual, viable airplanes for something other than stunts would only appear in 1909, by a bunch of people (including Dumont and the Wright Brothers, but with newer models).

    Romanticizing science heroes is, unfortunately, a common thing. I just find it harder to swallow for a few cases than others, and the Wright Brothers are in this camp (together with Edison) because of how they actually harmed the field instead of improving it. That's my problem right there. They made the thing, but also made it into a personal mission to jeopardize development as much as they could, in many ways pioneering corporate takes on IP.

    * For whatever it's worth, the - (hyphen) doesn't exist in Dumont's name in Portuguese. It's present in his name in some languages, including English, Japanese and French, it isn't in many others, I have no idea where it came from. But in Portuguese you don't hyphenate composite surnames, having more than one surname is normal for us as we get both paternal and maternal surnames, so having two surnames is a standard in most Portuguese-speaking countries, and we can have up to six surnames before it becomes really weird.

        I thought Dumont started to use the hyphen when he was doing his work in France because it was easier in various forms as the composite surnames aren't as much of a thing in continental culture. Basically he had to register a lot of his craft as the "Santos-Dumont [No. X]" and originally didn't use it when just signing his name, but then started adding it to avoid confusion. I think most US aviation history enthusiasts see it with the hyphen because we read a lot of stuff in translation from French.

        In the US itself more broadly, a hyphen or not is very dependent on cultural/ethnic background and how much you're willing to put up with slow-to-be updated government systems.

                As I said, the not-knowing-Dumont part is actually pretty expected. Most Brazilians never heard of the Wright Brothers, either. My particular problem with their reputation is because of the harm their did to the field.

                If you want to research on him, though, it's a very interesting figure, one who really contrasted to the Wrights because he was "obscenely nice". But the early history of aviation is really full of curious people and experiments, to the point that the Wright Flyer and the 14-Bis are straight-up boring in comparison to many other things being done. A lot of important design innovations that went in their wings, for example, had been developed for Daedalus-style gliders mimicking bats, pigeons, what-have-you, by people that actually mustered the courage to jump from high places on those contraptions.

                Oh, and it's also important for the history of clocks, specifically because of wristwatches! The first wristwatch was from 1810, but it was a unique piece of jewelry for a queen, and for almost 100 years those time pieces were just novelty jewels without practical purpose. Santos Dumont asked Louis Cartier for a watch that were more practical, because pocket watches were a pain in the ass during a flight, so in 1904 Cartier made for him the first modern wristwatch, practical and meant for men (which was important back then), a model called Cartier Santos Wristwatch. Cartier still produces this line. The reason wristwatches got traction was basically because they took the aviation community by storm, and then the military, specially by the start of the Great War.

                Seriously, the history of aviation is crazy.​

                            If one looks to the history of innovations in mass communication? Probably not. People lamented TV, cinema, radio, newsprint, and even printed books on the same grounds.

                            The Internet is just technology. It doesn't cause harm or benefit on its own. It's how we use it that determines that.

                            For me, there are two big questions the Internet poses:

                            First, is if the Internet represents a technology that we can't fully adapt to the new reality of. Past technologies came with an inherent limitation that meant we had a lot of time for societies to adapt to new things and transition more smoothly (though rarely perfectly: planting kids in front of the TV to keep them busy was certainly an issue). The Internet has grown incredibly quickly and is still changing on a rapid basis. Laws meant to regulate the Internet to combat this are usually out of date before they're written, and they're usually written by people that don't really understand what the second and higher level impacts are going to be. In fifty years, I highly doubt history will judge the Internet are harmful technology, but most likely our struggles to adapt to it aren't going to be taken seriously and the blame will be on us for not seeing the obvious ways to do it "right" that later generations will consider common sense and normal.

                            Second is the ephemeral nature of digital information. The Internet understandably wants digital information, but digital information has a very short shelf life. With so much cultural expression happening in the digital world, we're already at the point where one disaster could cause a loss of information not seen ever in human history.

                                It's also worth mentioning that a lot of the problems ascribed to the internet existed way before it. And while we have some evidence of things getting worse, we also have the problem that the internet, by its own nature, makes the problem more public, so this very evidence is biased to some degree. Are we seeing more problems because there are more problems, or because the internet makes the problems more visible?

                                Most people nowadays think, for example, that child kidnapping is on the rise, as we have more news than ever of such kidnappings. But the fact is that precisely because it became far less common, it also turned into relevant news. When I was young, posters of lost children were ubiquitous, they were everywhere, and in places like police departments there were huge murals with photographs of lost/kidnapped children. From the previous month alone, and all local. Simply put, no newspaper would waste time with a child kidnapping.
