        S5E5 - Tanks For Everything

        Wow, what a pick-up in an otherwise pretty mellow season start.

        To begin with, the overarching storyline is cute as hell. I love how they show the softer side of Rainbow Dash, which isn't really on display too much, and even then isn't particularly... soft. Casting any other pony in this role would have been much worse, which is perfect as she just happens to be the one with the newest pet as well and it's totally in character for her not to read up about the fiddly details in advance. Then, when she's confronted with this problem, she's just all action, all the time, as usual. Naturally, a plan like stopping winter will fail, but the sequence in Cloudsdale is dynamic and fast-paced and entertaining despite the outcome being known in advance. And in the end, it's Fluttershy who has to step up and give Dashie a reality check, kind of turning around the roles for them from for example Hurricane Fluttershy and also showing continuity form the lesson learned in It Ain't Easy Being Breezies. The characterization of them both in this episode is super well done. The "moral" of the episode isn't so much a moral in its own right, or at least it isn't pointed out as such, which makes it stronger in my opinion. The message is that reality won't change to your fancy, and denying the unavoidable is not a long-term solution. The fact that they show that it's okay to be devastated about something and still accept it's necessity, that crying doesn't show you're weak but that you actually give a damn about something, that's a rock solid message, not only for kids. And the ending is a right heart warmer, too.

        That's all good and all, but beyond that, there are some great minor moments that complements the big story line. The return of the Running of the Leaves, for example, and in general the pony-controlled environment which I've been nagging about not seeing again since season 1. That was a requirement for the plot of the episode, and I'm glad they remember to work with it when they can.

        There's a little problem here, though, because it seems like the show is trying to create some kind of chronological continuity, and that just won't work. Going by Rainbow's comment that this is her and Tank's "first winter together", it's been less than a year since she got Tank in S2E7 May the Best Pet Win!. Not only would that imply that there's been a whole hell of a lot going on in Ponyville this last summer, but it also flies in the face of S2E11 Hearth's Warming Eve, just four episodes later, which clearly takes place during winter! Now, the episode needed a reason for Rainbow not to know about a turtle's behaviour during winter, so it might just be that. I just hope they're not trying to create chronological continuity in MLP, because that'd be much more jarring than just not caring about it.

        Wow, got sidetracked there. Anyway, the moments like the pegasi doing their "my name is also a descriptor for something else" routine, Twilight pointing out that "winter is coming", the mutually matching Rainbow and Tank slippers on Tank and Rainbow respectively, and Applejack crying on the inside were, among other things, great ways to enhance the episode.

        This episode was good in the way that I feel has made FiM what it is. Sure, it's great to have big monsters and DBZ combat and other gimmicks at times, but the meat of the show comes from the character-influenced episodes that one by one builds up the image we have of our heroines. I'm very happy with it.

                        Okie dokey.

                        There are Four Factors that make this episode ultimately intolerable, for me anyways. Still, I think it might be more fair to say it hinges on two particular factors, which leads into the third. The fourth one is all me, but we'll get to that(though I imagine it'll become apparent for anyone who reads the other threads quickly). But the combination of the other factors make this the only episode that left me feeling dead, like the episode had taken something from me rather than given something. So, let's go into those factors.

                        1)The Premise of the Episode, or Gutlessness

                        It has already been brought up that the broad theme of this episode is the struggle to cope with someone you care a lot about having to leave, and I can see how people can take and apply it that way, and I don't object to it. That said, the framing of this episode makes it clear that they were going something much more specific.

                        They are very much talking about death.

                        In particular, they are talking about pet death, but again it can be broadened outward, and again I don't fault anyone for that, but I can see the original artistic intention fairly clearly. This is a subject many kids deal with, and pet death is easier to go into rather than the death of a parent, sibling, or friend, and I think the MLP team decided they wanted to tackle that in some way. And you know what, good for them, this is not only an interesting premise, but it's an important one, and I'd normally praise the team for going that route.

                        However, it doesn't take long to see why they didn't go straight for the goal posts there, and I don't blame them for getting gunshy. I described the episode yesterday to a friend as a "Damned if you do, damned if you don't" kind of episode, because the subject matter has no clear answer to succeed without drawing in backlash. It is a heavy subject that can run counter to the mood of the show and upset children. Even if the artists didn't start feeling uncomfortable, executives very definitely would have, and so it has to get pushed off to a close knit metaphor. It's not even a hard transition from a very high perspective. Rainbow Dash, with her anxiety over being left alone, was always going to be the one crushed the most by a separation from their pet, and hibernation is a handy stand in. Shift the details and you're good, right?

                        However, even before we get to the second part of this shit, there's too much too dislike about the shift, and so many problems that comes from this decision.

                        The biggest one, for me, is that refusal to be bold and to take the audience seriously. If you remember any of my hardcore reviews, you know I prioritize risk-taking and bold messages over playing it safe, and even if this episode scored every note right, it would perpetually annoy me that they aimed low.

                        THe second is that by shifting away from a serious subject to a metaphor, in this instance, they weakened the power of the episode, and in fact they damaged the buy-in. This implicitly becomes Rainbow's problem, and it's not necessarily one that everyone can sympathize with, because not everyone has Rainbow's anxiety or has experience with people like her. I know Lesson Zero all about respecting the problems of another person even if we don't understand it, but that episode highlighted the problem here: Tank's coming back, so Rainbow's panicking strikes us as petty(More on that in the second section). On the one hand, we know Rainbow has these issues, on the other hand it seems like a hard thing for the average viewer to take seriously.

                        That conflict of what is happening versus what it stands for creates some serious dissonance throughout the episode, in it's moods, in it's ideas, in it's executions. For once, using a metaphor has not soften the subject, but instead made it a slippy, slidy thing that sometimes runs the risk of not being associated at all. Going back to what I said, the episode can broadly stand in for a lot of things,from death to someone moving away for a while. and that broadness works against it because the thematic and emotional strains don't have anything to grab onto.

                        There's also a question of continuance. I am going to go on in my assumption of this being a metaphor for pet death, at the least, but allowing for broadness makes further episodes with Tank an interesting question. Parents and other concerned parties who use this episode to help a child with the fact that their friend is moving away is going to run into issues if the meaning of the metaphor is taken as legit and Tank never appears again. But if they are taking the episode seriously as a Metaphor, it's fair if Tank never shows up again, and people who use it to help prepare their kids for pet death will be validated that way, where as if Tank shows up again, this creates the false notion that pets come back from the dead in our lifetimes. Kids pick up on these things, and they subconsciously connect things together, and so things can go really badly either way.

                        There's more I can talk about how the gutlessness of their ideas hurt them, but I want to get onto, particularly since this premise will tie into the other sections. Look forward to the words "This is a Dying Animal" ruining things for you.

                        2)The Setup of the Episode, or Contrivance

                        Why hasn't Rainbow Dash dealt with this already?

                        Even if we went with the metaphor, this episode could have worked....back in Season 2 before Hearth's Warming Eve. But this is Season 5, with tons of implied time passed several times throughout the entire series. Are we really supposed to believe that over the course of years, Rainbow Dash has never once noticed the hibernation patterns of her pet? THe episode tries to deal with this in the most ludicrous fashion possible by suggesting that is their first time together for winter. Even if we assume that the episode they met was set in spring, THEY HAD HEARTH'S WARMING EVE ALREADY. There is clearly a winter before this one. And on top of that, are we really supposed to believe that everything from the Changeling Invasion to Tirek's Conquest happened over the course of a god damned year?

                        This is just the first, what, minute and a half? And they have already given me questions that make it impossible for me to invest in this episode. The contrivance in play to make this episode happen is utterly absurd. And it only gets worse.

                        And it all could have been solved by having Tank be dying, just saying. The contrivance of this episode is how I know there's a metaphor at work, because no one bends this far up their ass just to create a semblance of coherence.

                        Contrivance is the only way they can make this episode work. Exaggeration is commonly used, and normally I let that slide, but we're dealing with more mature characters now than we were in Season 2, and there are people who have experience on these things. So, without further ado, some things I just don't buy in. Asides from everything I've already brought up.

                        A) I buy into Rainbow Dash freaking out over Tank hibernating, and if this were Season 2, and I'd even buy into just about everything else she does from here, but Season 5 Rainbow Dash is more mature, a little calmer, a little more open. Yes, she'd freak out for a time, but the excesses she reacts with are nuts for this girl, such as

                        B) Not taking Fluttershy seriously-and further, not taking Spike taking Fluttershy seriously seriously(Not a typo). She knows her friend is the supreme expert on animals, and while denial/anger/bargaining/depression will happen before acceptance, I can't, for a second, believe that her denial takes her as far as it does. I'll allow up to Spike, but at that point, the insanity goes too far

                        C) That the ponies didn't do more to stop her and help her. Look, these guys have lassoed her to the ground and made her listen to reason until she finally came in contact with her emotions. They have done it in various ways throughout the entire series, and I know they are more observant than that. Honestly, Fluttershy probably raised the alarm as soon as Rainbow left her cottage. BUT THAT DIDN'T HAPPEN.

                        D) That Rainbow thought she could have stopped winter. Look, Rainbow is more capable than most ponies, I admit that. There's a reason they have to wrestle her down. Rainbow Dash is a god-damned superhero, and the only thing that stops her from being such is the fact that some of her superhero traits are more commonly shared, but in the end Rainbow is a tough pony to stop. But I think her anger/bargaining state should have died when she confronted the hell out of the weather team with all of her wiles and power and still failed. Nevermind going to the source, you spend all that time going and you fail, you fucking switch to despair.

                        There's a lot more, but these are some of the big movers in the episode, and I think it's easy to see that we're still playing with a Rainbow Dash and other Mane Six we quite frankly don't even have anymore. No one does anything they would, nothing happens the way it should. This episode is shoved out, not carefully weaved together.

                        But, uh ,Kubler-Ross Model at work in this episode, so....not worth anything, no, I'm not giving points here.

                        3)The Execution of the Episode, or Failed Humor

                        Let's create a series of thoughts to repeat.

                        "This is a dying animal."
                        "This wouldn't happen."
                        "Is this supposed to make me laugh?"

                        So, concession-there are images that, completely out of context, are probably fucking hilarious. I know Rainbow made some marvelous faces in this episode, and I imagine I'll enjoy them for a day before the internet overdoes it.

                        But in context? I find myself repeating those three statements a lot. And really, that's not what's supposed to happen.

                        I don't want to say humor doesn't have a place in this sort of topic. I know for a fact it does. In fact, you need humor to make an episode like this work. So, the problem is not that they went for a laugh, but rather that the things they did were not funny in the context of the larger frame. THey might have been fine on their own-in fact, I know a lot of them are fine on their own, but the Premise and the Setup is so damn terrible that it doesn't work.

                        Two stand out points for me? One is a moment where Rainbow is racing off to save Tank from hibernation, and Tank gets knocked off of something. Make that any time this moment happens, because it happens a lot. The other is when Rainbow is yelling at the ponies.

                        The first one is, actually, really funny, because it really fits with Rainbow's personality. Let's be honest about a pony who got a pet out of resentment towards everyone else having one and a disdain for not being involved, she's probably not the greatest owner. I'm not saying she's a bad owner, but her banging Tank against the door on the way out is totally within character, and it's funny within the cartoon realm of violence.

                        But then I suffer the thought "This is a dying animal" and I can't laugh. I just can't. It stops being funny and just emphasizes that Rainbow is a bad owner, which she isn't(not the greatest, but not bad). She is slamming her dying pet around and I'm supposed to laugh. And I can't, because Rainbow, at her most extreme in a situation like this, would be babying that god-damned tortoise worse than out-of-character Applejack ever does Applebloom(AHEM). And you juxtapose this with how much damn work she does to make summer happen, it's jarring. So the entire time, I'm just thinking "This wouldn't happen" and "This is a dying animal" ,and nothing can stand to those thoughts. And it happens a lot.

                        The second one? Am I supposed to laugh? I am supposed to find Rainbow's level of denial and frustration and grief funny, that she has to yell at the ponies about how she's not angry? Out of context, it's hilarious, because contradiction is a great source of humor, but the emotion is too real in context for me to laugh. It's too visceral-hell, it's too real.

                        THose are the ones where it stands out, but the episode is laden with a multitude of these sorts of scenarios that make it just not work for me. By the time I get to the ending which should be getting me all weepy, even through the metaphor, I feel nothing but dead inside.

                        The song also was a fucking mood whiplash. I realize that humor and lightheartedness goes far in this sort of discussion, but given how damn desperate Rainbow is, the song is too happy and cheerful to satisfy, and it's lyrics are perhaps the dullest put in the show yet, which is weird how sharp this show can get with its' music at times. I was probably supposed to get swept up in Rainbows' misguided but noble struggle to stop winter, but that didn't happen because the emotion was utterly false.

                        I'd say that the ending is the best part of this episode, and if I divorced it from the rest of the episode, the little fun touches added to the somber scenes would make for a nice, warm, bittersweet experience, which are my favorite kind of experiences, but as I mentioned, by the time I got there the elements of the show have shorn off my ability to care, because nothing is connected to anything meaningful.

                        0)The Audience of the Episode, or My Personal Issues

                        Despite indulging in some flavor, I have tried to keep this within an appropriate realm of academic criticism, because I think this episode is bad as a piece of artwork. However, I'm fairly sure that if you've read this, you'll notice a certain gravity towards a particular interpretation. A large part of that is because it is the thing that makes the most sense in terms of critical analysis and production speculation, but I would be lying if I said I wasn't also biased towards this particular reading because my experiences as of late is probably filtering my experience.

                        At the end of February of this year, my dog Tyris passed away. It may seem cliche to say, but for those of you who have not had pets be a big part of your life, let me clarify that I have no problems regarding Tyris as an adopted daughter, and I have taken her death about as hard. I am moving on with my life, but I am very mindful of these sorts of subjects and the emotions that go with them because the experience is still fresh with me. And it's not like this is the first death I've dealt with, my father having died in the late 90s and completely changing my life forever-I would not be who I am today if my father was still alive. Despite my considering this to be a predominantly good thing, the reflection on my father's death in the wake of my daughter's death has made me aware of just how much these sort of things affect a person.

                        So when I reached the end of the episode, I did have to ask a question, not just "Would this help a person cope with loss?" but "If I had gotten this episode the Saturday I had to put my daughter down, would it have helped, done nothing for, or hurt what was happening?" There is no clear answer to that question. I don't think there can be for me now. But as it stands now, I felt like the episode spent most of it's time stumbling and inadvertently making light of something that mattered to me, and I have the suspicion that it would have evoked a more violent reaction from me then. Then again, I might have forgiven it's flaws in its' attempt to reach out for me, I can concede that. All I know is that I feel talked down to now.

                        I bring this up as a concession, an admittance of bias. But the point of this allowance is to explain why I personally hate it, not to admit that it might be good. I still, as objectively as I can, think that this episode is at least bad, that it should be on people's bad list. I think it really badly of this episode, but I am conceding that some of it might be because of the experience. This is why it's point zero. It is only a valid point for me, where as the other three, in concert, still make the case.

                        In Conclusion, or This Episode is Really Bad You Guys

                        So despite the concession, I still think the episode is apparently bad. It all feels forced and fabricated, which wouldn't be half the case if the subject matter shifted over to a highly prominent and likely interpretation-so likely it was probably the original intended message. THe entire delivery of the episode on the whole is just incompetent, an oil slick sliding all over the place on top of an ocean of good ideas.

                        The worst part is that this episode could have been so good. This could have been the episode of the season, the one where critics all over talk about it busting ground and delivering a thoroughly satisfying and gutsy message. But they went and played it so safe that it became less than bland and actual becoming uncaring of the audience's emotions. It talked down to the audience.

                        If you think this episode was good, I dunno what to say. I suppose if you turn off a lot and just let it go, it works. But as an academic, it has poor structure and a dead heart, and all that's left is an atrophied husk of a script. Heaven forbid what happens when I think about is as someone who went through the experience.

                        Yeah, no, I don't like it.

                            That was an interesting read, Arcane. I can respect that criticism. I want to make a little point about what you say towards the end in your post, but in doing so, I will also reply to the fullness of it.

                            Point 3, I feel, again comes from the assumption that it's a metaphor for death, so for me, the mood whiplash wasn't as bad. I'm also easy to entertain.

That is because all the differences in our interpretation and evaluation of the episode stems from that point. You say it's an admittance to bias, a concession that you're letting your mind be influenced by unrelated things. I say that the only way to make value judgements about art is to base those judgements in a context. In this case, it's your personal context, and that context is an entirely valid one. Sure, the more closely we tie our interpretations to the material we're critiquing, the more we build up our argument with causal links between events and characters and don't just go "well, I think that...", the easier it will be for people to accept our interpretation. But it will still be a subjective piece, based in a context that some people might not have and which they will therefore challenge with their own interpretation. There can be no objectivity. And that's okay.

                            I'm not saying this to try and undermine your interpretation. I just wanted to say that your experience is a worthwhile foundation for your opinions, and that our differing opinion doesn't inherently invalidate either of our perspectives. In fact, the whole point of criticism could be said to find those differences, so that we can mine the work for more meaning. And I think we have done a good job of that here.

I have had a lot of emotionally volatile reaction to my experience with my daughter's death and a lot of thought has gone into it, and all of that means I could just hate the episode for reasons that actually don't actually reflect on the quality of it. When you're taking a driving test, you should admit to potential tiredness influencing your driving, same difference.

                                        It is part of the experience. It's valuable for analysis, perspective, and interpretation-but it's not so great for quality assessment in a subject that's already subject to, well, subjectivity. I had to acknowledge that.
                                        Well, yes, it's absolutely correct of you to acknowledge your basis of perspective. I also agree that your critique of the "craft" of the episode might not be (fully) based in that same perspective.

                                        However, I disagree that it's therefore objective. When you make a value judgement about anything, you're always already in a subjective realm. If you call the craft of the episode "bad" (or "good", for that matter), then there you go: that is a subjective statement. Why? Because you can only call something good or bad in relation to a context. You say that you think the "craft" of the episode is "bad" because it "does not fulfill the goals and in fact confuses and potentially mocks the ideas that begin the project". So the value judgement that the craft is bad is related to how well it managed to express the ideas behind the episode. And those ideas are... what? Hard to answer, because they are open to interpretation, which is a subjective activity. You fill them in based on your personal experience, and others might fill them in based on theirs, and so the evaluation of the "craft" is based on different foundations and must also therefore have the potential for different outcomes.

                                        And to reiterate, that's okay. We don't need objectivity. It's not about finding a right answer.
