                        Originally posted by PMárk View Post
                        What is '90s rpg design anway? The propensity for lots of floating modifiers and the (relatively, since it varies between games a lot) overall centering around simulating a world, rather than pure narrative? Lots of skills?
                        Those are all pretty common traits in 90s RPGs, certainly. I would personally describe the hallmark of 90s RPG design as a focus on trying to provide detailed rules for everything. A lot of people point to that design era as the age of the rules lawyer. RPGs seemed to be trying to move away from fiat rulings and towards systematizing all aspects of play. That generally lead to very long skill lists and lots of floating modifiers, because under the design paradigm of the era, if there was something a player wanted to do, then there should be a specific rule for how to do it within the game's "engine". This in contrast to 00s design, which treated the rules more like the game's user interface, and modern design, which has trended towards treating the rules as guidelines for a collaborative storytelling exercise.

                        I think the idea that this systematizing of the rules came from a desire to simulate a world over a pure narrative desire was invented later and applied retroactively. The Threefold Model and GNS theory didn't show up until the late 90s, and both have been heavily criticized for being biased in favor of a particular gameplay style, and for conflating modes of play for the underlying goals of play.

                        Originally posted by PMárk View Post
                        If anything, I connect classless, skill-based character creation, with encompassing skill lists and moderate-level of crunc with the '90s as a common theme. Stepping beyond class-based systems, but still being classic-style RPGs. My favorite style, to be honest.
                        There were also a lot of classless systems at the time, as well as systems like 3e that tried to incorporate elements of classless character advancement into a class-based system. Again, I view this more as a result of the design paradigm of the era, rather than a hallmark of it. The goal was to have rules to cover everything a player could ever want to do. That meant, if the player wanted to be a knight, but also wanted to study magic, the rules needed a way to cover that, which previous class-based design didn't really do. Enter 3e-style multiclassing and WoD-style level-less advancement. Suddenly the "engine" allows you to recombine all existing "class" features any way you want.

                                                    Alright, how about this model?

                                                    “Classic” design: The GM is the game’s engine, the rules exist to help the GM determine the outcomes of actions.

                                                    “Turn of the Century” design: The rules are the game’s engine, they determine the outcomes of actions and the GM’s role is to apply the rules fairly and consistently.

                                                    “Modern” design: The conversation is the game’s engine, the rules exist to help the group determine the outcomes of actions, the GM’s role is to mediate the conversation.

                                                    Interestingly, this would peg 4e as distinctly turn of the century, just a very different take on it than 3e’s. And 5e would seem largely classic, with some modern influence.

Making them full casters and giving them a separate "Occult" spell list might help to make this class more coherent than the weird sorcerer/rogue/miscellaneous mix it's always been. Making bardic performances cantrips is a curious choice. Though I'm really not sure why Paizo is ignoring the entire spectrum of possible resources between "daily" and "at-will". And once again, magic-using classes show a level of dedicated design that non-magical ones just... don't.
