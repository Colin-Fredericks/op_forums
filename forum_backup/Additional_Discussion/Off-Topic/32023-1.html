                                                    Seriously, I got started on Werewolf Revised... and most of my experience has been with Whitewolf, I have a few other RPGs but I don't have DnD books... I know some day I need to read at least one but... am I alone in this? It seems virtually everyone else I know who I haven't introduced into roleplaying started with DnD or is most comfortable with DnD?
                                                    I started in Vampire: the Masquerade 2nd edition and didn't get near D&D for several years, so I remember feeling like you do now. It seemed like everyone had started in D&D and knew about it, and I had to hear about things like THAC0 without knowing what the hell they were talking about. I didn't think that I'd enjoy the game based on what I heard and I didn't have the money to experiment with at the time, so I shined them on.

                                                    You can shine them on, too, if it suits you. There is no rule which says that you must read, like, or play any version of D&D in order to consider yourself a gamer. Some folks might argue otherwise and might enjoy the feeling of geek cred, but there's no good reason to divide up an already limited audience of a niche hobby for bragging rights. If the games that you play now do it for you, awesome! Rock them! But many tabletop gamers will expect at least a passing familiarity with some of the basics and you will miss out on aspects of conversations if you don't have it. Given its history and influence, it can be difficult to ignore. That said, not everyone needs or wants to explore the roots of the hobby, and you can simply explain that you have other priorities.

                                                    There are solid reasons to give other systems a try, however, whether or not they're D&D, and if only at a con or a game store. The first is that learning different systems will help you understand game mechanics, system strengths and weaknesses, and your own preferences better. It's kind of like learning a second language and understanding language in general in a more nuanced way, and you've already seen it at work when you were teaching D&D players about Exalted.

                                                    Just reading a game book only goes so far, as well; you really need to see how it plays with a well-functioning group in order to see the array of options and the type of experience that game offers. You might find bits and pieces you can port into your own games and vice versa, and which enhance your experience of all games. For instance: I always played D&D with lots of grit and moral gray area, as well as very dark shadows. That is what I brought with me from cWoD. Then I took the joy of charts and tables and such from D&D back to my WoD games.

                                                    And with exposure and actual play - and most importantly, with a fair and open mind - you might discover something you like and/or something that helps give you a welcome break from the types of games you're used to. I found that after some years I needed a break from the WoD (and I was as shocked to get to that point as anybody) and D&D gave me the experience I was craving. If you're lucky, you'll find something you really love and you'll be able to carry it with you. I've moved between D&D and WoD pretty much ever since, and I love them both.

                                                    In the end, you don't have to play multiple systems, you don't have to play them regularly, and you don't have to do anything you don't want to do with your own leisure time. But if you do find another game you like, that'll mean even more fun for you.
