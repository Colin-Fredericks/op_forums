        So I read the first two issues of Mystery Girl. Basically, Trine, for reasons she's blanked on, has acquired the ability to instantly answer any question she encounters, excepting why she can do this. She makes a living answering questions in her "sidewalk detective" stand, from "where's the most recent copy of my father's will" to "where is the evidence needed to convict this guy for the murder he committed". One day, a woman comes to her asking her to answer the question of the location of a particularly well-preserved woolly mammoth. Out of boredom and a desire to see the mammoth, Trine says she will only show them, not tell them. Meanwhile, there's a serial killer around who has fixated on Trine.

        I'm not sure if I'd recommend this or not. The characters are decent if stereotypical, but Trine's ability - which the whole narrative is built around - has the potential to be story-breaking, or exposition-dumping. There's been a few points where the details are lacking polish already, which doesn't inspire confidence. The art is nice, but every five or so pages there's random nudity, never for any readily apparent reason. It really does come off as "oh, I need to show boobs every few pages. I haven't done that in a few pages. Aaand random shower scene." The pacing could be better in places as well - her travelling to Siberia has gone for several pages already, with none of them having seemingly plot-relevant stuff, but not enough detail to be doing the "Trine going travelling and shaking out of her rut" thing. More than anything else, it almost reads like "hey, these are things I heard Russians do, like stop the train so every man on board can pee in a row, and drink vodka, and boast about how they're strong in broken English!" And yet, there's no sign of anybody finding Trine, a black woman, at all a novelty - I don't want to comment on nowadays, but I vaguely remember black people in Russia were pretty damn unusual sight a couple decades ago? No background characters staring at her or anything.

        It's not bad, by any measure. But I'm not confident in saying it's good, either. The quality will depend entirely on the execution of this rather tricky idea, and the writer has not yet shown that kind of finesse, so my hopes are not high.

            So I finally read the Woods. It was alright, but underwhelming. I think if I hadn't read a similar story dozens of times before it would have more appeal but as it is, I have, so it doesn't. It's not badly written, just kind of predictable, and I don't trust the writers to really pull off the big mystery well. It seems that a lot of writers have caught Lost fever, and decide to just have some crazy mystery with no intention of really resolving it, or at least no idea how to, until they absolutely have to. I'll check on the Woods again when it's finished.

            Oh right, what it's about. A high school gets taken to another world. Like, the entire school building and everyone in it, and the world is shrouded in strange, alien forests full of creepy monsters. The other inhabitants are also Earthlings who have been taken from their world, though so far we've only seen signs of Mayans, Australian Aborigines, an English village and anachronistic Japanese Army. Others were said to have existed but fell, as some of the people seem to think the moon they're on is some sort of testing ground or competition. It seems like each group gets dropped in front of a black obelisk that touches at least one person in the group, granting them control over the flora and fauna and security systems of the planet while also leading them to 'the Black City.' So it's one part Lost in Space, another part Historical Fantasy, and another part Saved By The Bell. There's probably a more modern and relevant teenage high school show but I can't think of one off the top of my head.

            Fortunately I also had Über, which was really good. It's just finished up its first 'chapter,' and the next stage is 'Über: Invasion.' Aranis called it out years ago in this thread. It's basically an alternate history where the question is asked, 'What if Nazi Germany got the atomic bomb first?' But instead of an atomic bomb, they got 'superheroes.' Though I use the term incredibly loosely. They're soldiers with super powers, really. The writers seem to have really thought over the limits of the abilities, and how they work and how they're gained, and they utterly dominate the battlefield. Fortunately for the Allies, there was a deeply entrenched British spy working in the German program, and she managed to escape with enough important information and resources for the Allies to recreate the superbeings. Unfortunately, Germany has a really big headstart, and the guy behind the program had some tricks up his sleeves as well that no one else was aware of. Even though the superhumans weren't revealed until the last possible moments, as Hitler was in his bunker, they've come back with a vengeance. And in the Pacific Theater, it turns out the man with the plan had arranged for another Axis power to gain the benefits of the program, really stifling the American movements there.

            It's a great story, really riveting though I imagine if you're not into history or the military it would get tedious. Surprisingly gory for the writer, but I think it's pretty appropriate, many of the 'battleship' class Übers are unstable and have been driven to extremes by their changes and duties and being used, basically, as nothing but weapons of mass destruction.

                So I read the first few issues of Planetary. I'll get to it in a minute, but first I want to talk about the X-Files.

                The X-Files was, and remains, one of my favourite shows. It had serious issues - most notably the appearance of an overarching plot which totally failed to deliver for a very long time - but the main draw of the show, the atmosphere of mystery and paranoia and the relationship between Mulder and Scully, made for very compelling watching. But the weakest parts of the shows were where it tried to build up the conspiracy, but they hadn't yet worked out what, exactly, was going on. As a result, the allegedly all-knowing and all-powerful conspiracy really came off as conspicuously smoke and mirrors, with no more substance than that, doing whatever was the most mysterious regardless for basic logic. Once they'd worked out what was going on, with the alien colonists and what the conspiracy actually was doing, they tended to be much more cohesive.

                Planetary strikes me as the early conspiracy X-Files episodes. The mystery-of-the-week stuff is cool-ish, but the total lack of answers or payoff is frustrating. And it would be fine if it felt like it was building towards something, but it doesn't - it's just "hey, there's this ghost cop, and suddenly an alien thingy under the pavement! Which the next issue will promptly ignore". And "we have this guy born on this seemingly significant date for no clear reason, and he's immortal! And has ice powers... sometimes!" And "we have this guy without a proper name who has exposition-powers, for no clear reason!" And so on and so on. I want to like the series, I do - mystery alien magic dimensional stuff is compelling. But I can't tell the difference between inconsequential monster-of-the-week, and story-building. And the general reluctance to give any kind of answer to any question is immensely frustrating. I get they're a mystery thing, and they're building mystery. But the every issue I've read so far goes: "cool yet incompletely explored setup, action scene, big reveal (usually of uncertain significance), end of issue". What happened to the reality-computer? Did they destroy it? Did they put it in a warehouse, Indiana Jones style? Are they going to use it? Is it going to be a glorified paper-weight? How did it get a sheet over it if everyone but the cripple was dead after the fight?

                You don't even really learn anything about the characters. So far as I've read, you know no more about them in issue #8 then you knew about them in issue #1. Jakita is your standard Action Girl, The Drummer is and has remained vaguely annoying with exposition powers, and Elijah Snow is gruff and old, with occasional ice powers. We don't know their likes and dislikes, their favourite drink, what they do in their downtime, whether they have downtime, how they were recruited to Planetary, do they have relationships, or anything beyond their initial characterisations. They're... I don't know the technical name, but "Plot Puppets" seems as good as any - they aren't people, they're devices to move the plot - such as it is - along. And I find that very flat.

                I want to like the series, I do. But it asks way too many questions, and its often clumsy handling of individual issues gives me no confidence that there's a worked out overarching plot with actual answers. And I'm reasonably patient, but you can only tease the reader for so long before they stop expecting any kind of payoff. Particularly with characters you seemingly make no effort to make engaging or remotely relatable.

                            So, I've read three comics lately that I want to talk about.

                            The first was Supergirl, specifically the first volume of her New 52 incarnation (issues #1-#7, I believe). Basically, Kara Zor-El/Supergirl arrives on Earth several years later than Superman for reasons not yet explained, and is totally disoriented and has no idea what's going on, a situation that is only exacerbated when her first contact is the henchmen of a powerful, morally-ambiguous-at-best businessman who is not Lex Luthor. The interaction goes about as well as you'd expect when a bunch of henchmen attempt to arrest a disoriented teenage girl who is suddenly manifesting superpowers and has basically no control over them. The volume mostly follows Kara's attempt to orient herself on her new world, and interacting with a bunch of bad guys.

                            It's not bad, but by the end I didn't really know anything more about Kara as a character. She spent most of her time screaming, punching or crying (or all three), and being expositioned at. Maybe further issues flesh that out, but as it stands it was fun, but some more character information would have been nice. I'll keep my eyes out to see if this changes, but in itself I was kind of disappointed. Maybe if I knew the character better it'd have worked better *shrug*.

                            Second is Clean Room. This one is legitimately interesting. Basically a woman's fiance kills himself after being exposed to a self-help group/cult, and she is investigating it to find out why. Along the way she encounters the mythology surrounding the "Clean Room", a mysterious room within the organisations headquarters. Periodically she hallucinates (or they're real, still not sure) demons, and the head of the group is certainly suspicious. I've only read the first two issues, but I'm intrigued enough to keep my eyes on this one.

                            It's hard to explain why this works for me in a way Planetary or other mystery series don't. It's mysterious as hell, gives basically no answers, but it does give the impression that there are answers coming. The art is fine if unremarkable, and the characters are a bit flat, but that could be because we haven't, at this point, spent much time with any of them, and it has so far focussed on setting up the mystery than informing the characters. Chloe (the woman with the dead fiance) is driven but human - she's been broken by her fiance's death, and she's on a crusade largely in a probably futile attempt to put herself back together. The way the group members talk is very much as you'd expect the members of the group to talk - describing her in jargon that is meaningless to outsiders yet clearly meant to indicate some sort of perceived "fault" of hers that the group can "fix". The leader of the group, Astrid Mueller, is powerful, controlled, and (possibly) magical. She clearly has an agenda, and that agenda involves accumulating as much power as possible.

                            Unfollow is another slow-build series. Basically the setup is the creator of a Twitter expy is dying, and sets it up so that upon his death, his billions will be split between 140 people distributed over the world, or however many are alive at the time of his death. It is clearly setting up a "humans are animals who will kill each other for selfish reasons" kind of story, from both the premise and how the characters talk. I can't really talk about the story beyond that because at the end of the second issue we're still gathering the characters. I'm starting to suspect they intend to introduce every single one of the 140 characters (no, I did not come up with that pun. It's explicitly made, several times, in the series).

                            I'm honestly baffled as to whether I like this or not. The art is good, but there's just way too many characters: a special-forces whacko who believes God talks to him, a Japanese author who for some unclear reason cut his own legs off (either in protest of something or for artistic reasons, it's unclear), a female nature documentarian who has a habit of being victimised by men who I am starting to rather gloomily doubt is our "main" protagonist, since she's one of the few who we've seen multiple times, and a black youth from Harlem with gang issues and a sister (no, that's not me being racist: that's literally all we know about him). I suspect the long-term success of this will depend on 1. how long it takes to actually get to telling the story, and 2. how much I end up hating our "main" characters. I'm in for the ride for the moment, but if I don't see something solid in the next couple issues then I'll probably drop it. I want to see it succeed because I like the premise. The art is interesting, doing a king of semi-realistic semi-cartoonish thing which I find nice to look at, and the fauxlosophising is entertaining rather than painful, largely because I can see actual people who would talk like that.
