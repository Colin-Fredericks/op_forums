    Anthem frackas..

    09-13-2016, 04:30 PM
    Over the past 2 weeks or so, there has been a lot of furor over several football (and now even some other games players) either not standing for the national anthem being played, even ON 9/11, but in many cases doing other than just standing, such as 4 members of the Miami dolphins taking a knee like Colin Kaepernick did, or several of the Cleveland browns supposedly standing up and doing the black power/black panther raised fist salute.

    Now, i can agree that there is an issue with blacks and cops. However, there are those saying that "he has the right to free speech and should not be forced to stand up etc, but the NFL said that one team couldn't use THEIR freedom of speech to honor cops via wearing a badge to honor the Dallas victims. So in that case, why is free speech being allowed if it is one way but not the other..

    Also, as someone who served, i take great pride in the Anthem and the flag, and see what is being done especially disrespectful, MORE so for those who did it on a national day of mourning to remember 9/11 victims.

    So what's everyone else's view point on this?

    For me, seeing Caepernick say this was all done to 'raise awareness of oppression (What oppression)' is a little off, especially when seeing him a multi-million dollar worth black man in a sport that is 68% Black, Compared to blacks only making up 13% of the US population (so they are well over represented). And if he was really concerned about struggles and poorness, why is he not using his many millions to HELP his fellow black man up?

                                  People are free to do what they want, but they have realize that there will be blowback. Someone refusing to respect the nation might find himself or herself losing endorsement contracts or attracting bad publicity. In this case, if he's willing to take all that for his beliefs, good on him.

                                  All that said, rejecting America's traditions and symbols is probably not a good way to get Americans to rally around your cause and support you. More likely many will have the attitude of, "If you're rejecting America then why should I (and other Americans) care what happens to you?" That's not necessarily an attitude you want to instill in other people if you're looking to receive their support for long-term social changes. This was one of the problems the Hippies and anti-war protesters in the 60's and 70's ran into. Their overt rejection of America's symbols (including flag burning) only ended with many members of mainstream America rejecting their opinions and stances out of hand.

                                  I suspect that if many other African-Americans begin rejecting the symbol of America like that, you'll see similar attitudes of, "If African-Americans hate America so much then I don't care what happens to them," which is pretty much the absolute opposite of the goal that African-Americans who want change in the social, political and justice systems of America are seeking. They need majority white America on their side.

                                  Honestly I think if you want to affect social change, you're better off draping yourself in the flag and using it to appeal to the sense of brotherhood and solidarity with other Americans than you are with rejecting it and of American symbols. "We're American just like you, we love this country just like you, and as Americans you have a duty to stand with us to prevent the abuse of your fellow citizens at the hands of our shared government." That would probably work better to get Americans on your side, I would think.

                                  In the end, publicly and loudly refuse to stand for the anthem (or otherwise rejecting national symbols) can send a powerful message, but the way many people will interpret and respond to that message might be completely counter the goals that the individual had in mind when he refused to stand for the anthem. I'll agree that it's not right or fair or just that a lot of people will react to that message in that way, but well, the fact is, that's how a lot of people are going to react to it.

                                  I think that there may be better ways of getting white American on board with the idea that African-Americans face serious social and judicial discrimination and that this needs to be changed, than doing things that will make white America pull away out of some twisted sense of patriotic obligation to reject the idea that those changes need to be made.

                                  Well, this largely comes back to arguments among African-Americans during the fights against racial segregation - there was a lot of debate and discussion in the NAACP and other organizations as to how the protests should look. Martin Luther King Jr. and many other leaders felt that the best way to affect change was to bring white Americans on board and create a sense of solidarity by reminding them how injustice towards African-Americans was injustice towards all Americans.
