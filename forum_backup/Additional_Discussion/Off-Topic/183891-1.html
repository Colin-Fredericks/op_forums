    Originally posted by Odd_Canuck View Post
    That limitation is now removed, as for-profit organizations can now directly claim a religious position. Companies now are free to operate employee relations as if those employees hold the same faith, regardless of if they do or not. Besides the issue of granting additional powers to non secular organizations, besides the issue of corporations being granted more power over employees as an abstract thing, this has the double effect of allowing companies to enforce religious practice (if not belief) upon employees, as well as setting up a very messy situations with trust funds going forward.
    Only certain ones. It is actually more narrowly defined than corporations. Closely held private corporations is a special subset. Now, granting that RFRA has not been used in this way before, I want to make sure we're talking apples-to-apples here.

    The first sets up the situation for general abuses of employees in the name of faith. if the founder of a company devoutly believes that medicine is a lie and the only valid treatment is homeopathy, they are welcome to deny all real medical care.
    In fact, the ruling explicitly said this is not the case, and that such trials would have to be separate and likely to fail (Justice Alito's words, citing vaccines, blood transfusions, etc).

    This can also spill further into proscribing activities out-of-work that go against the belief structure of the company... and this can easily expand from Religion to Belief Structure. Work at a store founded by vegans and happen to eat meat? Not anymore, or else.
    No, again... the exact wording applies it to recognized religions, not general belief structures, which have yet to be formally recognized as a thing by the Federal government.

    Further it establishes a very generic grounds for dismissal. This is a Christian company, a worker accidentally stayed to 12:05am one early Sunday morning and failed to stone themselves for their crime, dismissed with cause. Or they ate shrimp, or failed to accept Jesus as their lord and savior for 5 minutes or anything else. A wide range of abuses.
    Maybe. Again, the ruling was quite narrow and prior RFRA cases have already upheld that you can do no such thing that would be contrary to established law, where said law would not create undue burden. For example, stone yourself or be fired would be covered under various State laws, and has been upheld in the Supreme Court even since RFRA was passed. In at-will states, you can be fired for almost any reason... so it's easy enough to pull this off already in the states where this is likely to come up (as at-will states tend to be more right-leaning anyway).

    The second is that with the ruling you can now have Trust Funds with religious ties. Meaning we can have companies that are operating and enforcing a particular set of religious views even long after the direct owners have died.
    ...actually Trust Funds have a different tax status and filing, so it remains unknown whether or not this would still hold sway or if, because of the lack of person directing the trust fund, the same exemption would apply.

    And of course in the modern quest to establish tax havens and control, it would be rather simple to set up a shell religious organization to take all the profit from something pay tax on it, turn around and reinvest it into the same companies that formed the religious organization. You end up with the church having extensive ownership of the company, but since that's just a control feedback loop there's no real problem for the company.
    That would run afoul of IRS code and articles of incorporation currently in the Federal Code for corporation. Could it be done? I honestly don't know... and I'd recommend hiring a really, really, really smart lawyer before trying it.

                http://www.washingtonpost.com/blogs/...trol-decision/ So what exactly is a "closely held corporation?" And how many people will this effect? Some initially thought that this verbiage effectively narrowed the decision to a limited number of businesses, but it's actually the same language Hobby Lobby sought in seeking its exemption from the contraception mandate. It's also a term that covers a lot of businesses and Americans -- like, a lot. Basically, "closely held" is a term that covers as much as 90 percent (or more) of all businesses, according to a 2000 study. But while it covers the vast majority of employers, it doesn't necessarily cover the vast majority of employees. That's because publicly traded companies tend to have many more employees than private ones. Still, according to studies from Columbia University and New York University, closely held corporations employed 52 percent of the American workforce and accounted for slightly more than half -- 51 percent -- of economic output from the private sector. But does that mean the employers of half of all Americans will suddenly nix contraception coverage? Of course not, for several reasons: First, according to a Kaiser Family Foundation poll, 85 percent of large employers had already offered contraception coverage before Obamacare mandated it. And while Hobby Lobby fought that mandate, so far few other large companies have joined them. Second, federal law makes clear that companies seeking religious exemptions from such mandates must demonstrate sincere religious beliefs. In other words, it's not like they can just suddenly decide their religion precludes them from paying for birth control and stop doing so. And lastly, the vast majority of small businesses (96 percent, according to Kaiser) don't have to abide by the contraception mandate because they have fewer than 50 full-time employees. But, again, larger companies employ a hugely disproportionate percentage of the workforce -- which means a significant chunk of Americans work for large closely held corporations. Could other "closely held corporations" who previously complied with the mandate change their tune in the wake of today's ruling? Sure. And the numbers above suggest there's at least the theoretical possibility that more than half of employed Americans could lose their contraception coverage.
