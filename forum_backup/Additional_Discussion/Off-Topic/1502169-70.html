        Stallone's using "can't make X today," as a thought terminating cliche as far as I can tell. He made a Rambo movie in 2019. It was garbage. Without even taking inflation into account First Blood (the 1982 movie) made more at the box office, and cost less to make, than Rambo: Last Blood (the 2019 movie) did.

        Realistically speaking:

        1) The Rambo franchise has been one-upping shitting on its own legacy since the first sequel. Each film has grown more and more detached from the deep and insightful commentary of the first film that turned it into a massive sleeper hit, and further into jingoistic violence-fests where Rambo has accepted that he's nothing more than a killing machine so he'll keep murdering whoever the most convenient "bad people" are until he finally dies. They also keep adding women into Rambo's life only for them to die because the man hadn't been through enough trauma apparently.

        2) Vietnam is history. The war might still be part of living memory, but most people alive today were born after it ended (since that was 50 years ago and all). First Blood was hugely influential in opening up audiences and Hollywood to cinema that was far more critical of how American society treats veterans returning from war. It's not a big genre for understandable reasons, but it stays relevant by today's movies discussing more recent conflicts. People make movies about the US wars in Afghanistan and Iraq now.

        3) One of the best reasons First Blood wouldn't have the same impact now? People listened to the movie! It's no longer taboo to discuss PTSD, veteran homelessness, or suicide rates. We haven't solved these issues, but we're still massively farther along towards making sure vets get the help they need coming back from war than we were 50 years ago. A film who's legacy involves sparking a national conversation in the 80s about the silence suffering of people returning from war doesn't have the same point in the 2020s after that conversation led to most people agreeing that "Yeah, that's bullshit, we should take care of these people properly."

        It's really simple. Compare the Rocky franchise to the Rambo franchise. The Rocky movies are now the Creed movies as Rocky helps Adonis Creed through his rise in the boxing world. New stories, with new characters, but still feeling like part of a continuation of the original thematically; not just because Stallone came back as Rocky. The Rambo movies are still the Rambo movies. John Rambo is 77 years old, and still a one-man army mowing down countless mooks while managing to shrug off injuries that should give healthy 20 year olds permanent disabilities. Rambo III is literally a movie about Rambo helping the Afghanis fight against the Russians, but the 2008 and 2019 films just ignore that going on completely (I don't know about most people, but if you wanted Rambo to be relevant now, the futility of war from his perspective seeing US military causalities from the War on Terror rack up seemed like a pretty obvious narrative to explore).

        -------------

        As an ethical matter rather than an artistic (or corporate financial one), Stallone also just shouldn't be making any movies until he steps down from the position he's accepted from the current administration. As someone that's accepted a political appointment with the stated goal of advocating for the White House's cultural priorities to become the focus on Hollywood output, any film he works on since taking the position should be inherently tainted as propaganda.

                        Blazing Saddles almost always comes up on this, and for some reason people (apparently even Mel Brooks?) forget that the original barely got made in the first place. They had to get the president of Warner Bros. at the time to step in just to let them show the movie in three theaters as a premiere before the studio execs would sign off on a full release. While wildly successful, it was also extremely controversial back in the 70s too. People seem to forget that Mel Brooks was considered to be a provocateur that made transgressive comedy that was meant to be boundary pushing and offensive.

                        Ultimately, comedy, like all art, provides successful artists with two choices: keep doing the same thing that made you successful until you're pandering to a stale shrinking crowd that's paying you for nostalgia instead of being creative, or you need to come up with new material that adapts with the times so you connect to audiences as their tastes and expectations change. A rare few can juggle both for awhile, but most end up chasing one or the other eventually.

                        Also... Mel Brooks is in his late 90s... even if he made something so bad he got cancelled... he finally fully retires in time to see 100? What's there to be scared of when it comes to potentially career ending risks when you've outlived most people anyway.

                                                            The problem is that fictional characters are whatever the authors want them to be. Long running characters are especially prone to seeing big shifts in their narrative roles as stories progress.

                                                            Also, hero and villain are concepts extremely tied to who's perspective you're seeing events from. The people of Latveria are generally presented as seeing Dr. Doom as a hero because, to them, despite some character flaws, he overthrew an utter tyrant and is a reasonable and generous ruler for an absolute monarch. That's to Doom's personal power his tiny poor country has a quality of life (save for personal liberties like privacy) that rival most advanced nations. From the perspective of a Latverian trying to claim the Reed Richards is a hero despite his own extensive character flaws isn't an easy sell. Has Richards used his amazing intellect to do anything but stroke his own ego and play hero when it suits him? Mr. Fantastic isn't exactly proving that the freedoms of liberal democracy are so great compared to the whims of a dictator when Richards can't invent his way into free zero pollution energy for the masses, end homelessness, come up with cheap and easy ways to repair aging infrastructure, etc. And he can team up with Tony Stark, Hank Pym, and a slew of other hero geniuses to solve all those problems but... doesn't.

                                                            Doom is the villain though, because we're on the other side.

                                                            Magneto is even more complicated because the allegory of the X-Men as a stand in for the struggle of minorities to be accepted means it needs to reflect the reality of the lives of minorities. Lots of minorities are looking around and saying, "Magneto was right." Which is to say, people like Malcolm X were right: the ballot or the bullet. If the oppressors in our society refuse to all people as equals in reality not just aspirations ("the ballot"), then then only moral choice for the oppressed is resistance ("the bullet"). It's the oppressors' choice. And Magneto said all along, that the oppressors are never willingly going to share the ballot. Hard to make a case he was wrong right now.

                                                            When you look at real people, real complicated, conflicted people, nobody's really a "hero" or a "villain." There's people doing the best they can, and people doing the worst they can, and a lot of people somewhere in the middle. When George III made it clear he wasn't going to give the ballot to the American colonists, the took him up on the bullet. There was a violent revolution. The colonists won. And the story of history calls them heroes for a number of reasons. You can look across the world to other revolutionary movements fighting their governments who's labels aren't going to be heroes for lots of reasons that has nothing to do with the righteousness of their cause, or even the ethics of how they fought it.

                                                            Ultimately though, "Magneto was right" didn't come from fans. It came from the comics first. No matter how hard Charles and his students fought to prove coexistence was possible, some anti-mutant movement pushed back even harder and with far less effort swayed public opinion their way. It came about because the history of the Marvel universe proved him right; which forces a reexamination of his methods and how justified they were.
