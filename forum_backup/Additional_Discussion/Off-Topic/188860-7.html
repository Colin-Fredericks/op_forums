                                        Well, in the article you cited, there is some truth and some falsehoods. They're right that the underlying issue at stake is the status quo in Gaza is not sustainable. Gaza is the legacy of the refugee camps resulting from several rounds of forced relocations decades ago. The Strip just cannot support the population that lives there. Unfortunately, the politics are a little more complicated. The Palestinian election in 2006 (I think it was) was a disaster in that it established Hamas as the legitimate power in Gaza. Hamas has always been a terrorist organization, and that's how the whole organization thinks. The assertion that Israel instigated the latest round of fighting is true in a sense because of how far they've backed the Palestinians into a corner, but their purpose in the blockade was to force Hamas to relinquish control of Gaza to the Palestinian Authority. The idea that Hamas was somehow innocent in all this is totally false. The existence of all the attack tunnels is proof of that. THAT was the real reason that the Israelis attacked -- to destroy the tunnels, and to a lesser extent, to "mow the grass" with regard to Hamas' other capabilities. What was the central issue that led to the cease-fire collapsing? The Israelis continued to mine the tunnels in the areas that they controlled. Even Hamas admitted that.
                                        As to the huge numbers of civilian casualties, I think Hamas is a little bit to blame (they're profiting politically big-time from the body count), but I think that's really more of a practical issue on both sides. Gaza is so tightly packed that there's no place for the civilian population to evacuate to, and besides Hamas HAS to seek concealment among the civilian population since they'll be slaughtered if they were to separate themselves from the civvies (sort of disreputable, but it's a necessity). On the other hand, the Israelis probably aren't actively trying to cause civilian casualties, but if they know that a rocket attack (as an example) came from a particular location, they're not really required to tiptoe around either.
                                        There's no real good solution here because if Hamas were to simply give up and pursue a diplomatic course, nothing would happen. What really needs to happen is the United States needs to start strong-arming Israel into actually working to make peace. But that effort is going to need to start in the West Bank and start working through with the P.A. a long-term solution to the refugee issue. Hamas needs to either accept the two-state concept at least in principle, or the Palestinians need to kick their butts out of power in Gaza. Neither of those scenarios look even remotely likely.

                                            Australian Jew here, maybe I can provide a perspective, not being from Israel or the States?

                                            Now, I think we can agree that both sides have done some pretty dicey things, for some pretty dicey reasons. And we can also agree that the situation is immensely complex historically, politically, economically, socially and theologically. However, all that said:

                                            While Israel may have done some pretty dicey things (maybe necessary or understandable, but questionable), the national identity of the countries needs to be considered in any opinion on the matter, I think. Let's take Israel for a moment: created as a country so a people, who had historically suffered horrific injustices for literally centuries, from pretty much every major country in the world (the US included), consisting of (but not limited to) being forced to live in slums, being denied access to basic services or forcibly expelled, and outright mass murder (which pretty much nobody blinked an eye at), had somewhere to go, and not only not face being beaten and killed,but actually be accepted. This is, I think, an important point that people tend to gloss over: the Jewish people have not had a country of their own, or a place they could go, pretty much ever, and the establishment of Israel promised an end to this, and in fact represented the fulfilment of some pretty seriously important prophecies. It's roughly equivalent (not exactly, but illustrative) to Jesus coming down to the Western world, and saying "'Sup? Screw this mortal coil, come hang out in Heaven where you don't need to worry about death or pain ever again." And they get their country, and set it up, and then find themselves in a fight for their very existence by a group who want to take that from them? Is it really all that surprising that they may have over-reacted, and are not willing to compromise? And when American people say things like "they wouldn't have a country if it wasn't for us", it doesn't just come off as being yet more American arrogance and entitlement like it does to the rest of the world, it's particularly raw because the American government, on multiple occasions, actively tried to kick out or oppress the Jewish people, and pretty much stood back and did nothing while the Germans conducted one of the more successful and blatant genocide attempts. So, the idea of the Americans giving the Jewish people Israel comes off as pretty bad, because they see it as something they freaking earned, and the US being far from the most innocent of their historical tormentors. Now, you might say that a lot of that was a long time ago, and you'd be right, but it's still very much a major part of how Israel sees itself, and the Jewish people more generally. So, when they're being "disrespectful", maybe think about where that's coming from, and think maybe the US has some disrespect coming.

                                            Now, let's examine Palestine, shall we? Now, granted, this is a country I know less about historically, but I do know that they are kind of similar to the Jews in that they've never really, properly had a country of their own. Not because they didn't have one, but because they kept being invaded and dominated by their various neighbours. Even now, a lot of countries (including the US) refuses to acknowledge the state of Palestine as a country in its own right. And all of a sudden, they have a pretty sizable chunk taken out of their land by the UN (who I believe they still don't get a casting vote with? Not certain about that) and given to a people they have not historically gotten along well with, and told that they, basically, have to suck it. Now, like Israel, they've done some pretty dicey stuff in the past and present, and they're kind of fragmented these days culturally still. However, a lot of the issues are that they have been shown, again and again throughout history, that if they want something (like, say, a place to live and call their own), they have to fight, and fight dirty: god knows their enemies will, and if they aren't every bit as ruthless, they'll lose. Again.

                                            So, essentially, saying one side or the other is 'to blame' for this situation is oversimplifying the situation, in my view. It's entirely too complex, and while I have no idea how a peaceful situation could be arranged, assuming one is even possible at this stage (even if one side did commit, genuinely, to peace, the other side would think of it as a trap and attack, or remember all the times in the past when they did exactly the same thing to the now-aggressors), I do know that pointing fingers is not going to be productive. They've both suffered horrendously, they've both done terrible things, the situation has been exploited, and in some cases encouraged, by a lot of other countries. So any peace cannot begin with the idea of one side being more or less to blame, or even all the blame resting on those two states or groups. Any country that wants to make peace has to own up to their part in it, and try to approach the situation in good faith, and with respect to the experiences of both.
