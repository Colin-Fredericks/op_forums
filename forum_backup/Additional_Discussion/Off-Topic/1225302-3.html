                Originally posted by AnubisXy View Post
                At the end of the day, some material is designed to appeal to certain demographics over others. Content producers do this because they want to make money, and believe that demographic A will get offer more money for the content than demographic B will. As such they may include certain images or ideas that appeal to Demographic A but which Demographic B does not care for. That isn't necessarily a bad thing, in and of itself. In fact, I think it would be problematic to demand that content producers set out from the get-go to produce content that is inoffensive to Demographics A, B, C, D and everyone else.
                ​I'm reminded of a video that I recently watched which mentioned the idea that tribalism in video gaming fandom is not only an arbitrary social construct, but one cultivated by deliberate marketing for the sake of creating a dedicated consumer base.

                ​Even in cases of the statement "artists that want to make money will produce content for an audience" was a completely true and accurate response to the question of art that is sexualized, or otherwise plays into stereotypes, it would still raise the question of why that audience exists in the first place. The answer is probably not "it's just a thing that happened as a result of random, incomprehensible, or otherwise completely natural factors and there's no getting around it".

                ​My initial reference to The Birth of a Nation was probably a bit heavy handed, but I think still makes the point that art doesn't exactly exist in a politically neutral context, and that the capacity of something to be financially successful (as Birth was, to a rather staggering degree) can raise questions of its own, and occasionally disturbing ones.

                So I'm still going to point to that film in particular as a counter-argument to the sentiment of "if you don't like it, just ignore it" (which is alternately highly difficult and irresponsible with it being something that romanticises bigoted terrorists) and "it's neutral and acceptable for artists to produce content for a known paying market" (which would be an issue when the market that is willing to pay quite a bit for it consists of widespread bigots).

                ​Really, whenever something like this comes up around art that disproportionately sexualises women, I think it would be worth considering how the arguments would apply if made about eighty years ago with regards to illustrated and animated representations of African-American people.
