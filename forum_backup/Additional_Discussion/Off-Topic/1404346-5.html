                                Originally posted by Omegaphallic View Post
                                I'll point out that the folks doing these really aren't representative of the respective cultures, they are playing to a white woke liberal audience and often come from simular background.

                                Simular example is the criticism leveled at Ghost of Tsushima, but yet the ACTUAL JAPANESE PUBLIC, ate it up and it sold like crazy.
                                To be fair, Sucker Punch worked pretty closely with a number of Japanese individuals, programmers, historians and cultural experts when they created the game. The only major dissonant note was the inclusion of haiku (which was anachronistic since the haiku form hadn't been invented at the time in Japan). So the important thing to note was that Ghost of Tsushima was very respectful towards Japanese culture and worked closely with Japanese people in order to produce the game. That's one of the reasons why it was pretty popular in Japan because Japanese people could tell that they had approaching the game from a position of being respectful as well as making a really good game.

                                In comparison, many older RPG books (even just from 2 decades ago) were often written by white people whose extent of knowledge of foreign countries came from movies and maybe a few dictionaries or first hand accounts. Rarely did you have RPG supplement writers actually communicating with and working directly with people of the cultures that their work would be exploring. So often times material ended up being kind of awkward in retrospect because the authors were working with second or even third hand information, some of it massaged to appeal to popular American culture.

                                The internet (as well as ease of travel in general) has changed that dramatically, making it far less onerous to collaborate with people from other cultures when producing material that's inspired by or directly modeled on their culture. But older material can definitely be problematic. I'm all up for re-exploring stuff like Al-Qadim, Kura Tur, Kindred of the East, etc. I think they all have excellent ideas and I enjoyed all of them at one time or another. It's just that there's also some embarrassing and backwards material jumbled up with them that, if removed, would make the products more approachable and appealing.

                                                        On a meta-level, when it comes to whether it is "worth it" to improve such products, there's kind of a thing these days where the internet (and the ease of writing negative clickbait) and how corporations milk old products over and over again both feed this cycle of criticising and then iteratively updating products to fix their "problems". And that means you end up with "products by committee" more meant to fix the PR problems of the old product than actually inspired by any strength of creative energy and vision and goal, and with a further problem that the new writers never really own their own creative vision in the product, and more often than not simply disavow it not five minutes later (hardly good for any sense of creating anything interesting or even "fixing problems"!)

                                                        In theory with the internet economy, creativity should be a lot easier, but actually doing anything new and bold seems often crowded out with "fixing" and marketing legacy products (without any really strong creative agenda than to help a corporation to draw on a stock of licences and name recognition to make money).

                                                        I kind of can't help feeling (in a "Yells at cloud" kind of way) that the pre-internet days of things "Just falling out of memory" except in obscure libraries for dedicated researchers, might have been better at actual creative production! (It seems like very much these days is becoming like the bad "universes" of superhero comic books - big, sprawling, don't really make any internal sense, passed from hand-to-hand by creators without any uniting coherent vision, boring, often shallow box-ticking exercises in much of what they are, made by people who are updating something old in the prime creative periods when they should probably be doing something original.)
