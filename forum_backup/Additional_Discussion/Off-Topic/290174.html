    Bitching About ST Thread

    10-22-2014, 09:01 PM
    There was a thread about this in the last forum that seemed pretty popular, so I figured let's revive it! It gives players a chance to bitch about their current or past STs, and STs can read it to see mistakes to avoid.

    I'll open with a story about a guy who used to run reasonably often in my first gaming group. This guy was a real 40k enthusiast: the kind that owns every edition of every codex of every race, drops a significant amount of his paycheck on the game, and has optimised his army to the degree of being unbeatable unless you were spending as much time or money on it as he was. These qualities would normally just be an annoying personality quirk, except they carried over to his game-running style. There was one way to progress in his games, and if you did not take it, you'd spend a lot of time sitting there and scratching your head. It was almost always the least interesting pathway.

    This was exemplified in a 3.5 D&D game he ran. A sort-of sequel to a game he ran years ago with different people, it was essentially Lost World: adventurers are on an island full of dinosaurs. Sounds good, right? Right from the outset, he began to strip away the fun. We can only play Good characters, not even Neutral. We cannot play paladins. We must start with the listed sample gear for our class, regardless of our skills (which meant my ranger with points in Craft(Fletching) had no tools with which to make arrows). One player, new to D&D and thought a barbarian would be fun, wanted to buy a parrot, since we were going on a ship, which made him want to be a pirate. Reasonable enough, he had some points in Animal Handling, so any ST wouldn't mind. After spending twenty minutes going through the PHB and DMG trying to find a standard price for a parrot, he set the price at 50gp, which for a starting character is totally unachievable, even if we all pooled our money together. Being a not-especially-clever barbarian, he drew his axe, intending to go for an intimidating (but not directly harming) strategy. Ok, kind of stupid, granted. The shopkeeper immediately started screaming for the guards, who were all fifth-level fighters. The barbarian only survived by obvious DM enforcement. So we get onto the ship, and spend the next two hours of game time trying to find a place on the ship that's out of the way. Apparently this is impossible on a ship designed to take passengers on longish voyages. Then pirates attacked, seemingly out of nowhere, and our characters somehow didn't notice until it was right on top of us. Again, we nearly died, only living through DM enforcement. Then we get to a town, a seedy place by all accounts. Our Good characters, noticing that the one clean source of water was being controlled by a group of rough halflings who charged exorbitant prices, wanted to stop this. The halflings were extremely aggressive third-level monks with 16 strength, dexterity and constitution. Again, we nearly died.

    I won't bother boring you with more details, but this continued, in fact got worse, all game. At no point did we affect things one iota, and the one time two of our characters tried by trying to nurse an injured Deinonychus back to health (with the intention of it being my rangers animal companion), he threw a T-Rex at us. A T-Rex that apparently killing us, even with us doing a surprising amount of damage to it, decided we were less effort than any of the easier and larger prey around. Then Greater Wyrm dragons got involved, and we were even more pointless.

    Well, how about you? Any STs, current or past, you want to bitch about?

                                              Well, there's a story I've told a couple times on RPG.net that has always been my gold standard for truly horrible storytelling, but I don't know if it ever talked about it on any iteration of these forums. I'll cut it down to the short version: The ST of a VtM game had a good thing going, with several players having by all accounts a great time. One-by-one he compelled the characters to do counter-intuitive, stupid things that made them vanish, collected our sheets, redid them by hand as powered-down members of his homebrew angel/demon/vampire hybrid bloodline, and then physically tore up the old ones in small pieces in front of us and threw them in the trash before we could compare what we had before to what we had now.
                                              The game may have lasted two sessions after, with a meek, apologetic, "You guys can have your old characters back" as too little, too late.

                                              There was also the ST who got skeevy and maybe a little oversexed at a mixed table WtA, where comfort levels had not been previously discussed. Shortly thereafter, the game came to a halt; he's tried to propose getting things going again, but only one of the players is even roughly amenable to the idea; one has dropped off the face of the planet, but two of them are explicitly too uncomfortable with the way he was going, and the other just doesn't enjoy his style of GMing (pat self on the back a lot any time you feel like you've surprised players, and constantly grin and speak loudly because it makes players think what you just said was cool). He did the same things later at a Dragonstar game, informing one character who had been transferred into a wholly mechanical body that his new form was anatomically correct and "wired for pleasure", and let another, turned part-satyr via an ambiguously-worded wish spell, know that she was now constantly turned on and having her constantly propositioned until she was pregnant. He just grinned wildly when asked about it and said "Oh, when I realized what had happened I had to do it!"

                                              There was also the guy who ran a one-shot game of Exalted at a con, who didn't really think much about the mechanical ramifications of the half-dozen rule hacks he put in place for his friends who were oWoD players (I don't think he mentioned he was making any alterations until I asked why 1's were cancelling successes). He insisted that it "made characters feel more heroic," but his adjustment of target numbers and the treatments of ones and tens flattened the bell curve and nudged it to the left. Nor did he put a whole lot of thought into character generation. I took the Dawn--the fighter-type--who only had 6-8 out of 10 Charm selections made, almost all of them out of a single tree, and the others were closely related and redundant (Solar Hero Style up to the form charm along with the first and second Martial Arts Excellency, for those who can make sense of that.)

                                              The lessons here:

                                              1.) What you think is cool is not necessarily cool for everyone. This is really important, and even the good GM's need to know this. Themes, characters, etc. are all good and well, but whether by delivery or by concept, Awesome McCoolpants may not make the table cheer when he shows up, and players may not fall over themselves to fawn over Little Suzie Moedorbs. Repeating yourself and shoving it in our faces DOES NOT MAKE THIS BETTER.

                                              2.) Do not ever. EVER. EVER. damage a character sheet not your own, especially without permission. It's the tangible representation of the character's avatar in the game, and it can represent their involvement and emotional investment like a voodoo doll. Treat it with care

                                              3.) Know your player's comfort zones, especially if you're treading into the gray areas that might make people uncomfortable; if you have to wonder if you're coming close to them, you probably are.

                                              4.) Convention games can suck. As GM, you need to make sure that multiple characters have some sort of rough concept, a full suite of capabilities, etc, and each one can take a lot longer than you'd like. That said, you volunteered, and the onus is on you to do it right.

                                              5.) Rules may not work for you. You may feel inclined to change them. If you're at a convention game, you may attract some new players who aren't familiar with the system--and that's good! Get the word out!--but don't be surprised if at least one of them is going to be confused and/or aggravated by house rules, especially if you aren't 100% clear on them from the get-go.
