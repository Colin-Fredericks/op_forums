                            This scenario is flawed. Despite certain people insisting otherwise, organizations aren't people, and the laws regarding discrimination are different for it. Nothing in the US anti-discrimination laws dictate that you have to violate your beliefs to provide public accommodations to an organization that works towards political ends you don't support outside of specific circumstances (the exceptions are generally around things like political campaigns to prevent a party/candidate from being silenced by lack of access to venues to speak to the public so if your local TV station gives access to the party the station owner favors, they have to give access to the opposing party/ies as well or just stay out of it).

                            A bakery doesn't have to associate with a politically active organization that supports a cause the bakery owner disagrees with. Anti-discrimination laws in public accommodations apply to individuals, which means if there's a risk of discrimination it would only matter if members of the organization wanted a cake and the owner wanted to deny them due to their being members of said organization.

                            Particularly given that baking a wedding cake is a colossal pain for bakers, which is why they charge like wounded buffalos (apart from all the other reasons, of course. Weddings, man), what if the bakers refuse to because they're too busy?
                            They're free too. There's no "affirmative action" provision here. If they're honestly busy and the same-sex couple doesn't order the cake far enough ahead of time? Tough on them. There's only an issue if they lie about how busy they are, such as refusing the same-sex couple on the grounds of being too busy, but then accept the order from a heterosexual couple that was in line next.

                            Where do you draw the line?
                            The line is already drawn in the law. As I've already pointed out, the fight is not where the line should be but in expanding the legal protections to acknowledge minorities that were not covered at the time the law was written. There's plenty of room to debate if the current line the law draws is the right one, but it's not the issue at hand in the real cases regarding anti-LGBTQ discrimination.

                            There isn't a clear, easy line, and given that 99% of the time there is alternatives, and how capitalism works, if a business wants to give up the income, then let the market punish them.
                            It is a very false assumption that "99% of the time there is alternatives," and frankly it doesn't matter if it was true or not. The law isn't just for the majority of the population living in urban areas with large numbers of businesses to choose from. The law is also for small towns in the middle of nowhere without that competition when it comes to niche items.

                            Beliefs are beliefs - you don't get to say "these are OK to violate, these aren't".
                            Actually, as a society, we do this all the time. Again, that's one of the purposes of having laws; to define when society says the norms of society override personal beliefs.

                            There's a degree of wiggle room where people are being physically harmed or harassed or similar, but outside of those scenarios (which are already covered by current laws)?
                            This is not the Constitutional limitation. We have Amendments that cover this. The metric is overriding government interest in violating personal rights (including exercising beliefs), not strictly harm. If the government presents a solid case in the courts that an anti-discrimination law serves and overriding government interest, they get to violate your personal beliefs.

                            And no, these are not solidly covered under the current law; that's why there's a fight over the bakery thing. It's a continuation of a larger fight because the way overriding interests are judged do not currently recognize sexual and gender identities as groups for consideration like race, religion, or biological sex.

                            This is clearly not that case.
                            I don't see how the specifics of this case (or any other) disprove my assertion that laws enforce social norms in a fashion that inherently violates individual beliefs.

                            Nobody is being physically hurt, or really even particularly inconvenienced.
                            There are a lot of LGBTQ people in areas that don't have the large markets you're assuming are a constant that would beg to differ.

                            If this really wasn't an issue for people... why are they getting into expensive legal battles just to get equal treatment in public spaces?

                            No. They chose to go into a line of work that does not accept their beliefs as justifications for their actions in the public sphere.

                            When you operate a business that's a public accommodation, you deal with laws that dictate things you are and are not allowed to do. Just like any other business. There's regulations and laws you have to abide by whether your beliefs conflict with them or not. If you can't handle those restrictions, it is on you as a business owner to find something else to do.

                            This really, really, really, is not about "a difference of opinions." It's about people that either want to use belief to trump the law, or didn't understand the laws that govern the business they went into.
