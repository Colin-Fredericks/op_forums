                        I'm not sure that I'd use "realism" as the defining factor in either game, but they're definitely more distinct.

                        I'd say CoD is closer to our universe insofar as they draw more on mundane experience and wear their metaphors closer to their chest. When you play Requiem, your experience (at least in the early to mid-game) is a fair reflection of your own reality: a nobody living at the mercy of self-interested pricks who have more power than you. Lost is about coping with trauma. Awakening seems much less focused on this eternal global battle and more about the personal thirst for knowledge. All of these games seem to exist in a world that's pretty similar to our own because there's not a massive mythology in place and because the experience tends to be much more grounded. They don't give you a whole lot of expanded mythology to work with, so any ST is going to be drawing more on personal and real world experience to flesh out their settings.

                        WoD always seemed more like a flat-out fantasy setting to me. There's so much mythology built out as to seem overwhelming, which is great for providing an ST with more options and great for a high-flying urban fantasy game. There's so much there and it's all so quantified, from the spelled-out mysteries behind the myths to the power levels that hit the double digits. There's nothing wrong with that by any means, but it creates a less thematically focused game and one that's more withdrawn from our world.

                            I get where you're came from and there is truth in that. However, I'm still thinking that WoD, as a setting is more anchored to our own reality with million threads, because these fleshed-out mythologies and splats have real-world connections to real-world mythologies, subcultures, pop-culture, history, events and so. CofD is more distinct and abstract for me. For example, the kiths in CtD are from actual legends and fairy tales, when the CtL ones are archetypes. I feel I had a lot more connection to Ascension's traditions than to Awakening's paths and Orders, because I can relate to the Hermetic order, or to the druidic Verbenae, or to the Akashayana, much more than to the Obrimos, Acanthus or Adamantine Arrow (I'm pretty much looking forward to how Awakening 2nd edition will handle this 'problem', the open dev excerpst semms promising!). DtF's demons are actual fallen angels with Lucifer an co, when DtD's are the creations of the God-Machine, a wholy in-universe phenomenon with no real-world origin. Don't get me wrong, I like the concept and the story, but it is it's own universe. Aside from this, I agree that CofD's mundane side of the setting is closer to our reality as the supernatural don't have as much power over the mundane world (and the setting as a whole lacks the distinct gothic-punk theme). It's focus is much more the down-to-ground life of the characters and I understand why one could feel more connected to this style and immersed more easily in the setting. So, from a character's viewpoint, yes, it's seems more like our reality on the surface. It could resemble the real world as much as the WoD if the ST takes her time and incorporate real-world elemnts, like local legends, history, gorups etc. but as written, the supernatural side of the game is more distinct, to me, than WoD's, more like it is it's own universe, less the derivative of our own.

                                I think the crux of the issue is, in WoD STs have most of the setting work cut out for them. With dozens of books telling you how stuff is in several settings, and what's transpired on them, it falls squarely into the CofD's ST to bring such elements to life. And it's something that's been remedied largely in second edition with example territories being covered in the corebooks, likely to be expanded upon in future releases. The disadvantage to such an approach was of course that later editions actualized their content without possibility to account for the exploits of PCs in those settings, giving birth to the so characterized metaplot.
