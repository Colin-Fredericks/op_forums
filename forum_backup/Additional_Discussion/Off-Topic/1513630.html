    Questions / reflections about law and moral philosophy

    06-02-2023, 07:30 AM
    A couple things I'm actually debating on Quora too.

    Hypotethical question 1.

    * A man has been convicted for the crime of murder.
    * The sentence is death by lethal injection.
    * He's innocent, so for the spirit of the law his execution is not legit. He did not kill anyone.
    * He manages to escape the prison but in doing so he kills several guards.
    * He had no choice - if he wanted to escape - but to kill those guards. If he instead surrendered, he would have been captured and then executed.

    Is the man morally justified or excusable for his actions?

    - - -

    Hypotethical question 2

    * A man has been convicted for the crime of murder.
    * The sentence is death by lethal injection.
    * He's guilty, so for the spirit of the law his execution is legit. He killed a person.
    * He manages to escape the prison but in doing so he kills several guards.
    * He had no choice - if he wanted to escape - but to kill those guards. If he instead surrendered, he would have been captured and then executed.

    Is the man morally justified or excusable for his actions?

    - - -

    Hypotethical question 3

    * A man has been convicted for the crime of murder.
    * The sentence is life in prison without parole.
    * He's innocent, so for the spirit of the law the conviction is not legit. He did not kill anyone.
    * He manages to escape the prison but in doing so he kills several guards.
    * He had no choice - if he wanted to escape - but to kill those guards. If he instead surrendered, he would have been captured and then imprisoned for life.

    Is the man morally justified or excusable for his actions?    ​
    ​
    - - -

    Hypotetical question 4

    * A woman has been convicted for the crime of refusing an imposed wedding.
    * The sentence is being repeatedly raped, then killed by stoning.
    * She's guilty, so for the spirit of the law ( of her country ) her execution is legit. She truly refused the wedding.
    * She manages to escape the prison but in doing so he kills several guards.
    * She had no choice - if he wanted to escape - but to kill those guards. If she instead surrendered, he would have been captured and then executed.

    Is the woman morally justified or excusable for her actions?

    - - -

    Hypotetical question 5

    * A woman has been convicted for the crime of murder..
    * The sentence is being repeatedly raped, then killed by stoning.
    * She's guilty, so for the spirit of the law ( of her country ) her execution is legit. She killed a person.
    * She manages to escape the prison but in doing so he kills several guards.
    * She had no choice - if he wanted to escape - but to kill those guards. If she instead surrendered, he would have been captured and then executed.

    Is the woman morally justified or excusable for her actions?    ​​

              I think a lot of these questions suffer from compounding morally questions without some sort of baseline being set first.

              As Jim noted, there's a not great assumption that "spirit of the law," has an inherent moral judgement. Especially with these examples, we need to examine the system these punishments are being doled out by. Most people agree there are extreme moral issues with the death penalty in general as a form of punishment, and very few modern philosophers will agree with the idea that state sanctioned torture is morally excusable just because someone broke the law (which definitely applies to 4 and 5, and lots of people would argue 3 as well). What to do about unjust punishments is also extremely contextual. Facing life in prison for a false conviction, but having 15 peaceful avenues of seeking justice is not the same as facing execution after a kangaroo court with no avenue of peaceful redress of your case.

                          It's fascinating to consider the ethics and justification behind the actions of the individuals in each scenario.For the first scenario, where the man is innocent and facing an unjust execution, one could argue that his actions of killing the guards to escape were morally justified. He had no other option if he wanted to avoid an unjust death. However, taking human lives is a serious matter, and it's still a complex and controversial topic. In college I had project on this topic and get some guide from statement writer​ In the second scenario, where the man is guilty and facing a legitimate execution, the situation becomes murkier. While his actions of killing the guards may have been necessary for his escape, it's difficult to argue that they were morally justified. After all, he committed a crime and was rightfully sentenced to death. Taking more lives in his attempt to escape does not erase his initial guilt. The third scenario adds another layer of complexity. If the man is innocent but sentenced to life in prison, his actions of killing the guards to escape could be seen as a desperate act to regain his freedom. However, whether his actions are morally justified or excusable is still a matter of debate. Ultimately, these scenarios raise thought-provoking questions about the nature of justice, morality, and the value of human life. Different perspectives and ethical frameworks may lead to diverse interpretations and conclusions.​
