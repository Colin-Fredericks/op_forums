        You can have real world ideologies in a setting or in individual games without using that setting or game to push an agenda or make certain potential players feel unwelcome.

                All human interaction is nebulous. Where do we draw the line for anything? When it makes people uncomfortable, when it upsets them, when it pushes people away, when it divides us.

                How play games out in the real world is with people who I know, usually friends, and I want to do what I can to make that experience fun for them. If some subject in a game makes one of them uncomfortable then I would stop it. If some behavior around the table makes someone feel alienated, then we should talk about it and change things. It’s not hard or fast rules, it’s just being aware that other people have feeling and accommodating them - even if I don’t personally find something offensive or upsetting that they do.

                For publishing an RPG, which I haven’t done and likely won’t, it will be a little different because I have to think about both the sensibilities of far more people and also must be willing to offend at least some since anything will offend at least someone.

                Werewolf is a game with heavy environmentalist themes. Can you play it without those themes? Absolutely. Are you losing something for doing that? Probably so, but that is subjective. Honestly, not many people oppose environmentalism if the issue is handled right. Can someone run a W:tA that includes environmental issues but still makes people from across the political spectrum feel relatively comfortable? Absolutely!

                It feels like a lot of people have this attitude of “People who believe in x, y, and z are evil trash and we should exclude them and make them feel bad in any way possible.” I don’t think that is a good attitude. We can have games that include contentious political issues without deliberately alienating people or even necessarily pushing some agenda.

                in the case of W:tA, the environment is a pretty central themes and removing that issue would be a big departure that i can’t understand not wanting to do. For other WoD games it’s much more fuzzy (ironically) than W:tA and the games don’t have to take on political issues or can do so in a more nuanced or multifaceted way.

                Can someone run a Vampire, Mage, Changeling, or Wraith Chronicle with a group of highly ideologically diverse people and everybody feels included and has fun? Yes, absolutely! Even with W:tA it can be done, outside of the environmental issue, which most people support to varying degrees.

                So let’s say that there is a W:tA game that somebody wants to run and there is a player who is against environmentalism. Not wanting to destroy the planet or anything, just doesn’t believe in global warming, thinks environmental regulations hurt the economy, that sort of stuff. They want to play because they like werewolves and shaman related ideas. It’s certainly possible to include environmental themes without making that person feel attacked, even if it’s just a matter of how everybody acts in towards the person if not the subject matter in game. Maybe the werewolves can fight against a Pentex subsidiary that is causing pollution but also is sacrificing kids from the surrounding neighborhood and the ST can tell an environmental story while giving the anti-environmentalist player something compelling too.

                That’s just one kind of extreme example, in most cases being ideologically inclusive is way more natural. Just be nice and think of other people’s feelings.

                                                Well, the fact that people think it's true is an interesting study in human cognitive biases anyway.

                                                It is, however, not true in the slightest. To the point where it actually used to be the other way around ("getting older makes you more liberal") during the point where the Silent Generation was the largest group of seniors, and only as the Boomers took over did to flop to the current phrasing. Basically, at best it's a causation vs. correlation error that happens on a generational scale. Older people tend to make up the majority of real power in the government, so their politics tend to dominate an era. Instead of treating each group as a product of their time (and also other demographics), humans tend to then flip causation and correlation and assume that because the old and powerful are of a given political bend, age is the most important factor.

                                                It's far beyond question across multiple fields that humans "calcify" ideologically with age. The older you get, the more strongly you hold to ideological beliefs (and other forms of belief for that matter). The vast majority of people will hold the same broad ideological beliefs in their 30s as they will in their 70s.

                                                What tends to change are:

                                                1) Historical context. Ideology, policy, and methodology are not the same thing. That is, in forty years what your core beliefs are, are unlikely to change, but you will probably adapt how you feel they should be implemented and advocated for. In the US, most older conservatives are more likely to be open to sensible gun regulations as a way to balance the public good with respect to traditional interpretations of the 2nd Amendment. Where as the "2nd Amendment Absolutist" position (I use quotes because very few people are true 100% no laws on any weapons what-so-ever folks) tends to be more young Boomer and Gen X in popularity (because the 90s esp., and not just in the US, so a lot of really stupid weapon control laws that are obviously more driven by parents worried about their kids watching anime* than any real thoughts about public safety, so people felt the government was overreaching and are inclined to push back). And the younger generations are gong to swing way harder back towards weapon control laws because... well... the last 20 years have not been a great case for lax laws on the subject. Nobody who has a conservative stance on gun policies is going to be advocating for (at least in the US) outlawing guns completely or something, but how guns are being used in society is going to modulate how people think about protecting gun rights and protecting lives at the same time. A lot of political assassinations led to conservative gun control bills, a lot of bad nanny state bullshit led to a lot of dismantling even those measures, and then a lot of mass shootings is driving the need to offer conservative gun safety measures because doing nothing is going to be untenable.

                                                2) Trust in the system. This is probably where a lot of the feelings of "you get more conservative as you get older" comes from. Older people tend to trust the system (at least to be the system, even if they don't trust it to be inherently good). Decades of working the system means they see value in institutions, norms, and incremental approaches to advocacy for their policies of choice. Burning the system down because it's flawed doesn't appeal to people that rely on the system to get by (something that is fairly inherent in age). While this plays into the previous point as well, what tends to happen is that older people tend to be more moderate in policy (even if, not in ideology), and protective over gains made rather than pursuing new causes. This is also where there is a stark difference in conservatives and liberals: Conservatives (by nature of being traditionalists in the first place) don't tend to be as big into massive protest movements. Liberals are way more prone to take large collective actions, but the older members of the movement that actually have to make the compromises to further their agenda get labelled as moving to the right instead of being true to the cause.

                                                3) The impact of political identity. One of the obfuscating parts of why there's a perception of ideological shift is that political parties themselves are not stable where human ideologies tend to be. There are conservative Democrats in the US who would have been Republicans in the 80s or 90s (many of them were) They didn't get more or less conservative, but the platforms of the major parities shifted and they had to assess which party was actually the party that supported their ideologies. Your average Eisenhower or Nixon Republican has seen their party shift wildly in policies. Some choose to remain Republicans anyway, and some chose not to, but since we tend to use parties as short hand for ideology, it makes it seem like there's all these old conservatives that are actually on board with lots of things we currently associate with liberal policies (which are really neither conservative or liberal, but what parties focus on).

                                                * - It's actually really funny to look at how the 70s expansion of Hong Kong action movies, and then the 90s spread of anime lead to insane laws that banned items across the Western nations because of the fear that kids would emulate Bruce Lee or ninjas. A Canadian YouTuber I watch occasionally did a great video on how he's more dangerous with a circular saw blade you can legally buy and carry on your person, than he is with a throwing star, but the throwing star is banned to be carried on a person in public. He joked that at least banning nunchucks to spite Bruce Lee prevented a lot of guys hitting themselves in the groin with a questionably practical weapon.
