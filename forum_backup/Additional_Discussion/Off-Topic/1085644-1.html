                            Originally posted by Magicballz View Post
                            Should some High School offer entrance exams to get in? I believe these are called magnet schools?
                            Some magnet schools used entrance exams (though it's not a definition of a magnet school).

                            For the purposes magnet schools generally are put to use, entrance exams make sense. Magnet schools are frequently "opt in tracking" schools with more specialized focus then general public schools.

                            Magnet schools really aren't a solution to most of the larger issues in the US education system though. As long as general public school funding is tied to academic performance in the student body, any program that siphons off the highest performing students is effectively taking quality education (in many different possible forms this thread has already gone over) away from lower performing students.

                            There's also a large debate regarding racial diversity and magnet schools. While magnet schools purposefully built to combat white flight are frequently quite diverse, magnet schools that aren't built with this in mind tend to be much less diverse. This creates a sub-group of magnet schools that serve as a way for publicly funded higher quality schools that are in practice not available to minorities.

                            The district level funding model continues to make this worse. Poor districts don't have the money to build magnet schools. Rich ones do. Unless the state or federal government is stepping in to fund magnet schools in low property value districts, it just creates more funneling of monetary resources to the people that don't need them.

                            There is a similar school in the U.S called Lowell High School in San Francisco, California. The academic achievement of these students are very impressive. One of the reasons why they are doing so well is probably because they are surrounded by intelligent and hard-working peers. The High School is by far the best school in the entire bay area. No school within the proximity can even hold a candle to it. I think we can learn something from this school.
                            I don't really think there's much of a lesson here. Take a school. Build it in a place where it is naturally biased towards students from the two highest scoring ethnic demographic groups, and have exam testing to ensure you're getting the best scoring students from the best scoring demographics... and you get a school that regularly does really well.

                            Lowell's use of arena scheduling is an interesting quirk - and one that's very useful for college prep oriented education - but has no confirmed link to increased academic performance.

                            Chalking performance up to having high performing peers is also very misleading with the amount of selection bias already present in how the school picks its students. Lowell's continual high performance levels is impossible to separate from the fact that they get to pick their students. You can't demonstrate anything about how education should be done in general from this sort of example.

                                                Originally posted by semicasual View Post
                                                Here's an open question for y'all - how do you feel about the idea of free college education? Would it do anything for lower-performing students? Would it help or hurt the entry-level job market? Does the cost outweigh the benefits?
                                                I mean, I'm in favor of it. The more people with college education the better. It might hurt the entry-level job market a bit, but overall I think the benefit to having a more educated populace would absolutely be worth it. As a country we want highly educated citizens, and anything to make it easier for people to get an education can only be a good thing.

                                                At the same time though, I don't think it would help with some of the fundamental problems we're dealing with.In my experience, most people can qualify for student loans and grants. I know people who had bad grades in high school who were still getting 10-12k a year in grants (money they didn't have to pay back), and even people with bad grades could still get loans if they were serious about college. And while taking out tens of thousands of dollars in loans certainly sucks, it's an investment that can pay off. There are, of course, some people who can't get loans or grants at all, but they tend to be in the minority. I think that most people, who actually want to go to college, can already find a way to do so if they're serious about it.

                                                in that that regard, I don't know that free college would necessarily equate to all that many more people attending and graduating from college.

                                                Plus, keep in mind that college isn't for everyone. Even if everyone got to go to college for free, quite a few people wouldn't be able to graduate. Everyone gets to go to high school, but there are plenty of people who hit a wall when they start getting to more advanced classes (such as Calculus and the like). So for those people who can't make it through college, even if you made attending free, that wouldn't really help them at all.

                                                It's also important to consider majors. Getting a bachelors in Accounting or Chemistry is one thing, getting a bachelors in Music or English is something else, and a lot of people graduate college and then find out that their degree isn't worth the paper it's printed on. Then they're forced to compete for lower end jobs against people without college degrees. And that just ends up hurting everyone. The people without college have trouble competing for jobs against people with college, and the people with college are doing a job that they didn't need any college for in the first place.

                                                So I guess I think free college would be a wonderful thing, but I also don't know that it would have a major effect on many of the fundamental issues we're seeing today involving high school drop out rates, or people struggling to get through college, or issues with employment.

                                                Though, New York state recently passed a law allowing New Yorkers to attend college for free, and I am extremely interested to see what happens over the next 4 years, and to see how big of a bump NY colleges get as far as enrollment and graduation go. It could be that my guesses and estimations are completely off base. In fact, I rather of hope they are, and NY's new system proves to function great, is adopted by other states in the country and helps bring the US into a new Renaissance. Well, I'll keep my fingers crossed but I won't hold my breath either.

                                                    While probably not a surprise, I'm very much of the same opinion on AnubisXy on this. It's a good idea, but it isn't a solution to the larger education problem. I agree with the principle, but it can't generate jobs for the graduates with those degrees. It's also, well, the least productive investment in student performance. While this doesn't have to be a zero-sum game, for now the amount of money our country dedicates to education is not enough to cover all the programs that would be awesome to do.

                                                    Free pre-school and increased funding for K-3 education has a drastically better impact on students' long term success than tuition free college would, while there are lots of other measures we can take to reduce the college debt crisis burden. For example, the federal student loan programs could not charge interest, and tie your minimum payments to your income instead of to the amount of the loan. This doesn't eliminate student loan debt, but it does avoid that debt from crippling people economically before they can even find a good job.

                                                    There's a huge number of programs that either need more funding, or need to actually be put in place, that will actually start fixing the US education issues that I would prioritize over free college, while we can address the student debt issues in maybe more complex but less expensive fashions.

                                                            Originally posted by Magicballz View Post
                                                            ^ Well how do you explain why many East Asian Americans are so successful in school then? You can still deal with a not so great school with a strong enough work ethics.

                                                            http://www.pnas.org/content/111/23/8416

                                                            "The authors wrote that family wealth was not a factor in performance."

                                                            "The poverty rates of Chinese and Vietnamese are higher than they are for whites. However the disadvantaged children of Chinese and Vietnamese immigrant families routinely surpass the educational attainment of their native-born, middle-class white peers."
                                                            There are some advantages to 1st Generation migrants and 2nd Generation migrants against locals of similar background, at least in the USA. For Asian migrants as a whole that holds 'til 3rd Generation, then they converge almost exactly to the majority average, while for Hispanic migrants it fades by 2nd Generation - http://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/publ...6/WP-16-10.pdf . There's an advantage for groups which migrate, relative to expected class position, but it fades over the generations.

                                                            That is supplemented by a degree of recent selection for degree holders in the US for certain migrating subgroups. That's quite a new thing in history.

                                                            It's not really a "cultural" effect in the sense of a deep, ethnic or national cultural value placed on education, learning, openness, curiosity, intellectualism, etc. It's more the pattern of selection of migrant groups for people within their groups who are aspirational and view education as "the ladder" in society, them having fewer options other than education in the societies they move to, and in the case of Asian migrants, for people who tend to have higher SES than is typical from where they're from.

                                                            Arguably, the only way to compare the US like-for-like against other countries is to compare the long standing ethnic majority only, because other countries don't have the same history of migration. That does tends to put the US public school system on a fairly high position in the international leaderboards. Anything else is much more complex question.
