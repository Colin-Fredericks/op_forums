    I think that analysis has a distinct oversimplification of a lot of things.

    For one thing, the leadership of the Civil Rights movement was by no means a unified front with a simple avenue to funnel the movement's message to the media. The Civil Rights movement also started just like the groups you're talking about. Leadership arose as part of the movement's growth; not something the movement started with from the start. As well, at the time plenty of Americans critiqued the Civil Rights movement over the same things: they were just noise, the nice ones were just a cover for the violent protests and violent radicals, etc. Trying to reach all of American was always a pointless endeavor.

    The biggest problem for modern grassroots protest organizations has been the decreasing power of the middle ground organizations. On the left the biggest loss is the unions (which is exactly why the right has spent so much energy getting rid of unions). The Civil Rights movement's unspoken political force of reckoning was getting the unions on their side, because that was the political pressure that put things over the top; not the public leaders talking to the media.

        Oh sure, like everything else in life there were a lot of factors.             Only time will tell, but I have a feeling that BLM, like Occupy, is going to burn out and end up in history's dustbin, where it will go down as largely as another failed social movement that managed to get a few high profile cases reviewed or overturned but which didn't have any deep-seated or long lasting effect on the US criminal justice system.

            Because the thing is if you want to effect change in America you need to get the majority of the population on your side, you need to get the political apparatus on your side and you need to get the laws on your side.

            During the 60's and 70's, the leaders of protest movements were able to get political leaders on their side. They would sit down with these people, have discussions, point out very clearly what they wanted and how it would help them. The leaders helped bring the politicians around to their side. With the Tea Party, members ended up running for office to get elected and that's how the Tea Party had such an effect on politics (though, to be fair, the Tea Party was specifically a political movement rather than a social movement).

            With Occupy and (so far) BLM, that hasn't happened. They made a lot of noise, but they haven't really been able to get politicians to do more than just smile and nod, in no small part because there was no coherent, specific message to present to politicians, and there were no leaders to engage those politicians to get their message across.

            Further both Occupy and BLM have done an incredibly poor job of getting your average American behind them. This was the key to the success for the Civil Rights movement - the leaders of the Civil Rights movement managed to convince white America that justice for all was in their interest too. BLM has not succeeded nearly as well and for the most part is viewed as black movement rather than as an American movement.

            I may have mentioned in another thread, I live in a predominantly Hispanic part of the country. Black Lives Matter has had pretty much zero impact here, even though many of the problems that African-Americans face at the hands of the police, the judicial system and society are the exact same problems Hispanics face. The thing is though, most Hispanics here (and most white people, and Asians, and Native-Americans, etc) see Black Lives Matter as a black movement, and since they're not black, it doesn't have anything to do with them.

            Just being loud and trying to be heard isn't enough. Anyone can be loud. But if you don't have a strong message and you can't convince the majority of America to get behind you, and you can't convince the media to get behind you, and you can't convince individual politicians to get behind you because you don't have a coherent agenda, then you won't be seeing any particularly positive changes.

            And worse, every time there's an outbreak of violence and no leaders to disavow it, or the media can't find any leaders to interview so they just set up meetings with whoever says, &quot;Yeah I'm in charge&quot; and that person turns out to be a nut, that's not going to help the movement at all.

            I think there are good reasons to have a leaderless movement, but at a certain point a movement needs leaders and it needs a specific, coherent message. Without those then a movement is just a big, loud, leaderless mob and nobody particularly likes those.

            I'm just saying this as someone who thinks BLM should absolutely succeed, that our criminal justice system absolutely needs to be fixed, and who thinks that seeing BLM ineffectively burn out will be one of the most tragic and unfortunate things I will witness during my lifetime.
