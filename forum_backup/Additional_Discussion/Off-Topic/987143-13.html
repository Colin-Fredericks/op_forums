                            I agree that things like Gerrymandering are a problem that can make things worse, but as Darksider pointed out it doesn't always point in the same direction. Heavily gerrymandered districts are extremely sensitive to the local base of whichever party has been set to control that District. I live in somewhat gerrymandered district (though we don't look it compared to our neighbors) that gives Republicans a fairly reliable 70% of the vote (though if you got rid of the few little odd lines on the map, we'd probably only drop to 65%, so like I said not that gerrymandered). But the Tea Party did very poorly here, because while solidly Republican it's also solidly moderate conservative Republicans that want more pragmatism over purity when it comes to general governance. As an example the idea of shutting down the government instead of actually getting a budget passed was seen in an incredibly poor light here.

                            There's also weird things local politicians have to balance because we have a decent sized population of smaller Christian sects that are generally conservative, but balk at things that get perceived as being too pushy on other people (when you don't put a nativity scene up outside your own Church/meeting house because that's too ostentatious, a lot of the "War on Christmas" rheotric doesn't work on you), and get hesitant about politicians that are too hawkish because they feel it contradicts their beliefs in Jesus' teachings.

                                I'm aware! If you dig into it you'll find most models use an arbitrary chance that all polling information is completely in error. 538 is the most wary in their trust of polling quality this cycle, hedging their bet with a giant factor they've baked into the model itself. If we remove that assumption from 538's model, to use the example I'm familiar with, Clinton's chance of winning the election leaps to 99.8%.

                                We can compare to the Princeton Election Consortium model run by Sam Wang's team which doesn't put their thumb on the scales. There are no simulations where Trump can win outside of catastrophic errors by pollsters with election results far outside the 95% confidence interval of his model. To quote Sam, "It is totally over. If Trump wins more than 240 electoral votes, I will eat a bug." This is fundamentally the same argument playing out again from the GOP primary process. Sam Wang warned that Donald Trump was a lock for the nomination using a data-driven process of poll aggregation while Nate Silver was adding factors to his model under "The Party Decides" trend to insist that Trump would fall to an establishment candidate far past the point where that was plausible.

                                So for this assertion that Trump still has a chance to hold true, we need evidence recent US polling has systemic errors across the board. The most notable has been discussion on the oddities of the tracking polls, where the same panel is polled every day instead of obtaining a new sample. I'm guessing Omega's claim about the polls unreliably is based on the famous LAT tracker story in the NYT about on how one young black man in Illinois who is REALLY excited for Trump has distorted their poll all year. But the tracking polls have favored Trump, so errors there don't help him. Worse, we now have early voting data we could search for evidence that the polls were wrong about a battleground states Trump needs for a path to 270. Republicans normally perform well in early voting only to face a Democratic surge on election day, but their weak ground game is crippling them already in states like Nevada. They were already behind by 44,000 votes in Clark county this morning when their plan to ferry a Trump rally's worth of potential voters to the polls hit a snag; the promised buses became a paltry group of vans instead after a several hour wait.

                                If you still don't believe me, try to build an electoral path for Donald Trump that gains more than 270+ EVs that makes sense with early voting data for each battleground state he supposedly will flip. If Trump has a plausible winning map, it shouldn't be hard to create one with a compelling argument behind it... but I've yet to see one that doesn't rely on Nevada, Pennsylvania, Virginia and North Carolina, none of which he can win any longer.
