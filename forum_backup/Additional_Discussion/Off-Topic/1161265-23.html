        So, I’m curious about this. This was a fairly common grievance folks had with 4e (“everyone’s a spellcaster” and all that.) but I’ve never understood it. What is it about Fighters having pre-packaged, exceptions-based maneuvers that they can perform that diminishes the fun of playing a Wizard? I can’t imagine your fun playing a spellcaster hinges on being the only member of the party with self-contained packets of mechanics they can pick from, so it’s got to be something else. But I’m not seeing what it is.

        Also, to clarify, Wizards in 4e didn’t have more control over their Powers, they were “controllers”, meaning that their Powers were geared towards battlefield control. More AoEs, stuns, forced movement effects, and zone control related abilities, as opposed to a Striker, who’s Powers were more gears towards high single target damage, a Leader, who’s Powers were more geared towards party buffing and healing, or a Defender, who’s Powers were more geared towards drawing aggro and punishing enemies for going after other party members.
        so, first the minor detail- when I've said that wizards had better control over their powers, I was talking about how they manipulate them using Implements. That was their "special thing", which, IMO, wasn't special enough.

        That leads me to the major problem, in my eyes- I had zero problem with Fighters getting their own set of powers. On the contrary- I thought it was a great advancement for the fighters, which were kinda null in 3e. I'm glad that this feature was more or less kept in 5e, in the form of their maneuvers. The problem for me was that all other classes got new and shiny things, while wizards... didn't.

        Let me explain myself- in the older editions, being a wizard was something special. In 2e, you had the slowest power progress, but that encouraged you to collect more and more spells, that was your "secret mojo". When a wizard were to get to the same level as the other classes, they were may have been on the same power level, but with a lot more raw power in their hands. In 3e, the power progressed was unified into a single system, but the point of a wizard was still finding new spellbooks and collect more and more spells. If you decided to play a wizard, you entered the class knowing that your character is going to focus around researching tomes and ancient relics, invent new spells and make new magical items. You knew you had the worst weapon selection and armor, but that if you'll survive for long enough you are going to become one of the strongest characters in the game. School specialization was a representation of that scholar like nature, becoming attuned to a certain aspect of magic and gaining mastery over it.

        4e changed it. Sure, you still had a "spellbook", but it wasn't the same thing. School specialization was gone and replaced with having some magical tool which helps you to control your powers, instead of collecting specific spell reagents and items for each spell (heck, one time I had to search the streets for some red sand for a spell I had to cast!). Sure, some stuff was kept in the form of rituals- but everyone could have learned rituals, Wizards just got the feat for free. You had spells, but everyone had spells or powers of some kind. All you had was some funky old wand which helped you to shoot better, as if you were some Harry Potter wizard. And then, look at the other arcane class! Warlocks with their cool pacts made with demons, fey and stranger beings still. Sorcerers which was a font of wild magic, carried the blood of the dragons, called upon storm and sea or even became one with the universe! Bards which sang their spells and virtues into form! And you? You had some magical staff because you weren't goo enough to control your spells without it, and a spellbook which can't contain more spells than a certain level because the gods said so. Magical libraries? Wizards investing their all life for researching and discovering new and old magic? Spell casters share and trade in arcane secrets? Yeah, a lot of that charm was gone- they could still be around, for rituals, but again, everyone could use rituals. Everyone else where special, but wizards became the "not special" arcane spellcaster, one which weren't born with magical talent or made a pact with a fiend or knew how to sing. You were a controller and controlled the battlefield, great- but being a wizard was so much more in the older editions, turning them into some stereotype or caricature of who they were.

        Again, it is all IMO and personal views, so sorry about the gashing- but it is a bit touchy subject for me. Wizards in D&D was what that got me into writing in the first place, and I grew a bit attached toward them.

        I’d love to see the alternate universe where Essentials had been sold as 4.5, they hadn’t worried as much about trying to preserve back-compatibility with 4.0, and they had really pushed the “we fixed 4e” angle instead of the “this will be easier to learn” angle. Maybe it would have won back enough folks to have kept the 4e train rolling a few more years, or at least salvaged more of 4e’s ideas in the eyes of enough of the community for them to have made it into 5e.
        saying the truth, I've never actually managed to understand what Essentials was meant to be. Was it a new edition? Was it an hack? An expansion? Some books brought subclasses, others treated the classes from 4e as is. It felt to me like the designers couldn't decide what they want Essentials to be. I did liked how it presented some things, but also didn't liked some of the others, mostly because I didn't got what I was meant to do with it. I've never got why Star Pact Warlocks didn't had an Hexblade version, for example.

The basic 4E class list is a rather awkward attempt at squeezing traditional classes into the new power source/role model while keeping most of the sacred cows. Clerics always turn undead, rangers are stuck with dual-wielding or archery, and the wizard remains the generic spell-slinger. Without all the preferential treatment it had always got.

                    I feel like psionic classes and Essentials are examples of how to keep the consistency and balance the power model brings without making every class follow the exact same structure. There was a clear refinement of 4E's concept as the edition went on. Shame it was swept under the rug and forgotten when 5E came.

For a specific illustrative example, the way they made a post about critical hits, made their claim that all the extra rolling they required wasn't good, and laid out the way 4th edition was supposedly going to handle it (rolling a natural 20 = maximum damage, and only in special cases like a particular kind of weapon would any extra dice get involved)... but then when the game was released, more critical hits than not called for extra steps beyond dealing maximum damage because it wasn't just particular kinds of weapons (high crit) it was pretty much any magical weapon too, which the game assumed you'd be getting a steady stream of.

                                                            I’ve never played Shadowrun, but I fully believe it would have attempted the same thing. As you say, it was the zeitgeist of the decade, especially in the tabletop RPG industry. It makes sense. Not only is the turn of the century getting everyone in an out-with-the-old, in-with-the-new kind of mood, but RPG publishing is hot off of a boom, and heading towards a big crash along with traditional publishing in general. And at the same time, geek culture is starting to become mainstream. It makes sense as an RPG publisher to want to ride that wave by appealing to the new generation of geeks. Toss out the bloated, intimidating backlog of baggage accumulated over the past few decades and build a new product out of the best parts of what you had before. It’s like New Game + for your brand.

                                                            The problem was, they counted on brand loyalty alone being enough to carry the existing fans over. I’m sure they expected to lose some portion of the old fan base. but they probably figured they would keep enough, and combined with the massive influx of new fans they would bring in with their Bold New Direction, it would more than make up for those losses. What they didn’t realize was that in RPGs, it’s old players, not new books, that bring in new players. So by leaving the folks who liked the old stuff out in the cold, they bottlenecked their own ability to introduce folks to the new stuff.

                                                            I just so happened to be the exact demographic these moves were targeting - I had just started trying to get into RPGs at the tail end of 3.5, and was turned off by the bloat and baggage so much, I thought they just weren’t for me. Then along came 4th Edition. Clean, sleek, incredibly easy to learn and to play compared to my experiences with 3.5, and with a bunch of cool new ideas I hadn’t seen before in standard Tolkienesque high fantasy. It roped me right in, though I didn’t realize at the time how lucky I was to have found an experienced DM who actually loved the new edition. When I went to college, I found it was nearly impossible to get a group together to play 4e. Everyone who was into D&D wanted to play 3.5 or Pathfinder. So, I turned to LARP. Naturally, being in the think of the D&D Edition Wars, and with a lot of resentment for the “grognards” who I perceived as too blinded by their nostalgia to appreciate how great 4e was, I went right for the “new” World of Darkness. Again, I was lucky enough to find a group of experienced players who actually loved the new stuff (and, like, a big one... college towns, am I right?)

                                                            Anyway, my point is, I happened to be in just the right place at just the right time that the plan behind some of these big moves actually worked exactly as intended on me. So I fully understand where these publishers were coming from. They were trying to get me and people like me playing, and they succeeded. They just over-estimated how big of a demographic we were - folks just a few years older than me were already full grognards by then, and folks just a few years younger didn’t have the same context for those big, bold changes to mean anything to them. And they counted on more people like Michael and Mike (huh, that’s a funny coincidence I never really took note of before) to onboard more people like me.
