    Originally posted by Bluecho View Post
    There's also the argument to be made that a person might worship an Evil deity because they think they HAVE to. Usually out of fear, or because their particular career/lifestyle/home situation/species causes them to fall under the evil god's divine jurisdiction. In that way, if the person wants to avoid the evil god's wrath or gain their favor (or, more properly, to avoid their disfavor), they need to pray to them, perform sacrifices, etc. They may not want to, they just feel they have no choice. That belief may be incorrect, but it's something they still believe, or because breaking out from under that god's influence might be too difficult/perilous.
    That's actually the state in the homebrew setting I run for my games. Due to the very small number of deities in the game (10-11, depends on who you are asking), and with each deity holding power over a specific part of reality, almost every culture worships, to some degree, almost every god. For example, one of my players is a cleric of the god of storms and calamity, which is CE- and his whole worship revolves around both keeping his god satisfied enough to not destroy the world and claiming that through the destruction the god brings, mortals needs to learn to stay strong together and make an ordered society to protect themselves from his wrath. In short, he worships his god in order to object his god's agenda of destruction- because the god needs to be appeased, or else things will start go down to hell.

    That's true for other gods in the setting- the god of war is evil because war is evil, but he has non evil clerics which draw upon him to bless the armies of their country. The goddess of death is evil for death is the end of life- but everyone needs someone to take care of the dead and banish the undead, so they worship her and pray for fair judgement for the souls of loved ones- yet she is still being worshiped by necromancers and those who use the dead for their needs. It also goes the other way around. Orcs worship the good goddess of fertility in order to bless mothers during birth. Goblins worship the good goddess of the sky as the wife of the war god and the one which blessed the founder of the goblin nation to bring peace in a time of chaos. Tieflings share the respect for the infernal princes which birthed them- yet without actually doing anything evil in their name, while the dwarves claim that the chaotic good god of crafts is actually lawful. The gods don't care about the alignments of their faithful- as long as their clerics help to further their agenda and acknowledge their gods, their will be granted with divine powers.

    So yeah, that's far from the rule for most of the D&D settings, but I've thought it would make a fun thought experiment for my game, and for now it turns really well. Just recently the group stumbled upon a cult which unknowingly worships a powerful fiend-goddess of unknown origin (partially based around Pale Night, which is also among her titles). The cult members are not evil, and actually work to help those in need, supported the party once before and fight demonic influence- but they do worship an entity which serves as one of the game's BBEG. They don't really know what they should do with them now. And of course, there are those funny moments when the cleric sees other people which worship his god- and that they have radically different ideas about him, from an elder evil which must be appeased, through the incarnation of apocalypse which they hope would bring the flood soon to a bastard which gives us all a reason to remember to live life ton its fullest. The cleric condemns them all, of course- but he starts to understand that he may not really understand his god as he thought he did.

    Just fun things I thought I should share :P

            Originally posted by Nyrufa View Post

            Isn't that Talos, from D&D? The God of Storms and Destruction, who demands you worship him, or he'll level your city to the ground?
            well, they do share some similarities (of course, as they are both CE gods of ruin), but my version of the deity has a wider role than that- he is the god of storms, sea, misfortune, doom, calamity, elemental fury and natural disasters. It is less "worship me or I'll destroy anything you care about" and more "I'll destroy everything you care about. Be ready for the day after the apocalypse".

            In general gods in my setting care less about faith and more about being personification of what they are- as the God of Calamity, he is a personification of the nature's rage, be it by shooting lightnings from the sky or by shattering the earth itself and spitting magma. As long as mortals fear him, he is happy- and if not for the other gods, he would have probably destroyed the whole world by now. Trying to accept, deal with, appease or prepare for the divine disasters sent by the gods is the foundation for all of the god's many churches and orders. So his followers less try to cause more destruction and more try to deal with the coming ruin- more of apocalyptic cults and less of roving murderers.. usually. There is a reason many of his followers ends up as demon worshipers, or end up serving the god of war (which represents man brought destruction, among others)

            I'd hesitate to say that Death itself is evil, though. Everything mortal dies eventually, and it's often those who seek to avoid death that are classified as evil. I would make the slight alteration that the spreading of unnecessary death is evil. Killing without the need to do so is a waste, and those who habitually take part in it are selfish and impulsive.
            But why everything mortal dies?

            In my setting, the reason that everything dies is because that when the world was created, one goddess has decided to "gift" everything in the world with death, and as such she became the goddess of death. She did it not because of some noble reason, or to attempt and maintain the natural order. She did in order to make sure that no one would grow too powerful, and to regulate the hierarchy of heaven and earth. She brought death as a tool of tyranny, and she doesn't care how death comes to be, just that mortals would remember their place. Free undead anger her for trying and avoid the natural order- but if the dead are controlled properly and serve the greater hierarchy of reminding the finale end of all things- than they do so with the blessing of the goddess.

            At least, that's the widespread theory of theologians about the goddess. And that's of course is specific for this setting- in other settings things die for different reasons, but when you have only one goddess to encompass everything from death by old age to plague, murder, poison, fear, undeath and oblivion, her nature must explain both natural death and "man made" one- for she made them both possible.

                    Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post
                    Looking at what PF2 has to say about alignment, it’s absolutely spot-on with my own assessment of the good/evil spectrum, but falls short of what the lawful/chaotic spectrum means to me, falling instead into the usual wishy-washy “lawful means you prefer follow some set of rules, but it doesn’t have to be the actual laws of your society, and chaotic means you like freedom more than structure but it doesn’t mean you act at random, and neutral means you sometimes follow laws, except when you don’t feel like they suit you” nonsense. And I think I’ve figured out why every game that uses the 9-alignment system falls into this same waffle. True libertarian political thinking (anarcho-capitalist hogwash not withstanding) is so far outside the Overton Window of most of western civilization that the idea of rational actors being actively opposed to state power is just too alien for most of the people making the game to wrap their heads around. So Chaotic Good ends up being this weird “I know it when I see it” phenomenon that they lack the language to accurately describe.

                    This seems like the same old problem with alignment, unchanged for as long as I remember. Good/Evil is intuitively understood and uncertainly arises from degrees - how bad does someone need to be to count as evil, etc. Meanwhile, the Law/Chaos axis doesn't seem to have a definition more than two people can agree on.

                    Predictably enough, the final version of PF2E has the same issues to me that the playtest did. I think I'll spare everyone the griping.

                        Originally posted by Morty View Post
                        This seems like the same old problem with alignment, unchanged for as long as I remember. Good/Evil is intuitively understood and uncertainly arises from degrees - how bad does someone need to be to count as evil, etc. Meanwhile, the Law/Chaos axis doesn't seem to have a definition more than two people can agree on.
                        Indeed. The extremes of the good/evil spectrum map pretty cleanly to altruism/egoism, with neutral being someone who is neither willing to put themselves in harm’s way for others, nor harm others to get what they want. I suppose there is some uncertainty of degree, though for me this is resolved by separating ideals from actions. Most people tend towards altruistic or at least non-egoistic ideals, but in practice, few people really risk or sacrifice more than they can comfortably afford to lose (which makes it not really a sacrifice) for the good of others. So most characters probably believe themselves to be good, and act in accordance with Neutrality. There are some people who earnestly hold egoistic ideals, but they are pretty few and far between. More often, people justify egoistic behavior to themselves under the pretense of utilitarianism, or hedonism, or something else. So, few characters should believe themselves to be evil, but in practice, harming others for one’s own sake is distressingly common, so many characters end up falling easily to evil.

                        It’s worth noting, I run things like alignment restrictions based on ideals, and things like detect alignment spells based on action. Most adventures regularly cause harm to other sapient beings in pursuit of their own goals, so most adventurers would ping Evil on a detect alignment spell. But as long as they hold altruistic ideals and strive to live up to those ideals, I still consider them valid heroes. It is only when one embraces egoism that they cross into villain territory.

                        As for law/chaos, like I said, I think a big part of the problem is that most players’ Overton windows don’t extend far enough towards libertarianism to properly frame the authoritarian/libertarian dichotomy. As well, I expect there are some who do see the connection, but are uncomfortable with the idea of mapping D&D alignment to real-world political theory.

                        To me, it’s pretty intuitive- if you’re for state power, your ideals are Lawful. If you’re against state power, your ideals are Chaotic. If you prefer not to concern yourself with such questions and accept the status quo of your society, you are neutral with regards to Lawful/Chaotic ideology. And as with Good/Evil, your actions may or may not be consistent with your ideals. If you regularly participate in revolutionary action, you detect as Chaotic. If you actively work to maintain state power, you detect as Lawful. If you keep your head down and try not to get involved, you detect as Neutral.

                                        Originally posted by Willowfang View Post
                                        Could have defacto laws that you ALWAYS do or do not follow, like a safety net so you don't technically change alignment as you move around
                                        I agree. Chaos/Law has always been awkward. I appreciate that it exists, but it does tend to be fuzzy though I don't really mind that. Anyway, temporal laws are important when it comes to a characters lawful alignment, but so are religious laws or personal codes of belief. A person could be lawful due to their adherence to religious law, but completely reject the temporal laws of a country and shouldn't undergo an alignment change for doing so.

                                        I think that's basically how D&D always approached it anyway. I remember back in the 2nd edition AD&D Paladin's handbook it talked a bit about that. Basically Paladins are both Lawful and Good but only respect laws that help people and reinforce the greater good. Paladins are free to reject and ignore laws that are designed to hurt or oppress people.

                                        For example, a Paladin who finds herself in a tyrannical land where slavery is legally allowed could absolutely begin freeing the slaves and wouldn't have to worry about losing her Lawful alignment (and therefore status as a Paladin) simply because freeing slaves is against the law in that kingdom. For the Paladin, her Lawful alignment comes form the oaths she swears and her code, and that's more important than following the letter of the law of whatever kingdom she happens to be in at the moment.
