    Originally posted by Luis Carlos View Post
    When I want to enjoy fiction, the last wish is to find more messages about religious fanaticism. Why not talking about revolucionary fanaticism?
    Most Americans (which is where the majority of pop culture is targeted) haven't directly experienced revolutionary fanaticism, but plenty have stories of religious fanatics. It might not be fair, but it is more relatable.

    Don't get me wrong. I think popular media too easily characterizes Christians as either buffoons or crazies. It too often characterizes Muslims as fanatic radicals. But that goes both ways. I remember the anti-Dungeons and Dragons Satanism scare of the 90s. I remember the banning of Harry Potter and the Tipper Gore crusade against "inappropriate" music. I can still hear One Million Moms screaming about our sinful media.

    Censorship isn't appropriate. It wasn't appropriate when the government levied obscenity charges against Jello Biafra or William S. Burroughs. It wasn't appropriate when industry-wide labeling protocol tried to neuter the comic book or record industries. But a company deciding they want to change the content or something to accommodate their audience isn't the same thing.

    I'd love to see a more balanced promotion of faith in popular media. But let's be honest. Sensationalism sells, and there's plenty of sensationalism to go around. "Muslim" terrorists are all over our TVs, even though they don't represent the majority of that faith, because groups like Al Qaeda and ISIS have sensationalized radical Islam. "Christian" fanatics are all over our TVs, even though they don't represent the majority of that faith, because groups like the Westboro Baptist Church and One Million Moms are happy to make fools of themselves. I'm not aware of any atheist groups or factions that provide fodder that juicy on such a regular basis.

    I do think there's a reason why the knee jerk reaction to Christianity is so prevalent in American culture though. I can only speak for myself, but as someone who grew up in the South, Baptism reigned supreme (It still does); and most of the geeky stuff that pop culture geeks loved, from witches and wizards to vampires and werewolves, was seen as deviant at best and outright sinful at worst.
