        I'd say that's broadly accurate, but it's worth remembering that America is big and there's a lot of variations within a country of 330+ million people.

        IME most guides like that oriented around the "newscaster" accents. These are accents largely associated with newscasters/presenters/hosts to maximize clarity and reduce the speakers normal accent; though a lot of people that engage with public speaker or address (like actors) will likely have some coaching in it for the same reason. But they're still somewhat regional, so there's a few that means throwing on the local news means you'll probably have a vague idea of what cost you're on (including the Great Lakes or the Gulf of Mexico).

        A lot of the faster accents make it much harder to hear that vowel shift in "man" much less prominent, while a lot of the accents prone to drawls can make the more direct eh in "men" harder to distinguish because of the tendency to elongate vowels broadly.

        It's also usually a subtle sign that someone grew up in a mixed language family when they do a different vowel shift that makes a bit more sense with their linguistic background.

            Broadly accurate will have to be good enough, it's a starting point and as didactic as it gets. Even countries with far smaller populations or area can have an astounding amount of variation, I would have to say the same for Brazilian Portuguese, or Japanese, and so on. But with an entry point it's easier to notice and understand those variations.

            It was like that when I learned how the TH is pronounced. Before having an entry point, it was just T's and D's switching randomly to me, and without an actual explanation of the phoneme I would still be stuck there decades later. That's what happens when a sound simply doesn't exist in your language. Even the fact that speakers of other languages may hear it as an S sound was wild to me. After learning it in a similar fashion, I could exercise to the point of it becoming very clear to me when it's used.

            People learning Portuguese usually have this problem with the ÃO sound, just to be clear that this isn't an "English" thing, but rather every language will have this kind of "barrier" for every other language, if not always the same barriers for any given language.

            Newscaster accents are usually a good entry point. In fact most language courses focus on this kind of accent or a slightly more didactic version.
