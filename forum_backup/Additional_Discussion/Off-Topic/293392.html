    ST Screw-ups Thread

    10-27-2014, 12:57 AM
    So, we have the Bitching About ST Thread, this one is where we talk about mistakes we have made as STs. We've all made horrible screw-ups of varying length and magnitude, let's discuss them for mutual shame and learning!

    I'll open. I had an idea for a game in which the players (and a bunch of other people) suddenly 'awoke' as psychics. I didn't use the Second Sight rules, mostly because they're kind of terrible IMO, rather opting for creating Merit lines for each power myself. The players (3 of, for this), to their credit, were really on board and worked with me, extending waaay too much benefit of the doubt for me, and were really good about feedback of the powers and their characters. However, there had been one major oversight: I'd forgotten to provide much of a story, expecting the players to more-or-less drive their own plots. Somehow, I'd forgotten that to derive the main reason for the sudden emergence of the psychics (in a different game we'd run previously, actually, but that's another story) required the game to run for at least a year in-game time. And I was going along, covering more-or-less a day per session.

    Like I said, the players were great, but the game was about a half-dozen sessions in before I realised I needed an actual plot otherwise it'd fall apart. So I worked one out that'd be interesting and consistent within what had happened already, and started things going. It ended up developing quite well, but when that ended, it was generally agreed that I needed to do more prep work ahead of time. I managed to adjust, but from then on I always make sure to have a plot going within the story, even if the players end up ignoring it.

    So, how about you? What big stinkers have you done? Bad games or just bad calls, it's all fair game!

                  On topic... I can think o some flubs I made:

                  1. Probably the worst one was when I enabled the party to kill themselves. This was in an Unknown Armies game wherein a player decided to open the Knocking Box. For context, the Knocking Box is an artifact that is filled with thousands of ghosts of people who died from war or plague, some of whom have been trapped inside it for centuries with nothing to do but to take out their misery and isolation on each other. Opening it releases all the ghosts, and they will try to possess anyone nearby en mass. This was after an extended campaign in which the PCs were a group of G-men who were growing increasingly distrustful of their governmental employer, and were being shown more options about who they could work for or follow. The Box was supposed to be their way out - using it as a bribe, they could buy their way into the good graces of any faction they wanted. Alternately, they could hide the box somewhere where no one could reach it, or just keep it. Once the players finally had it, everyone had their own idea about what they should do with it, and instead of trying to debate or wait for consensus, this one guy decides he'll just open the box and see what happens. Predictably, everyone in the party got overwhelmed by the sheer number of ghosts.

                  I thought I gave him and the group enough warnings, but I still feel responsible because after listening to the players argue about what to do with the box for a while, I decided that I would rather take a resolution - ANY resolution - to the issue of what to do with the Box rather than listen to the players debate it for an hour when the game was already running late. What resulted was a cool, dramatic conclusion to the game, but it wasn't very satisfying for most of the players.

                  2. Another flub was when I tried to integrate poker into a later UA game. The party was in Vegas, gambling for cosmic power (of course), and I thought it would be fun if we actually played cards for a bit. I guess I didn't think through the logistics of it very well, though - I planned out what the hands of the NPC players would be in advance, which turned out the be really stupid because the NPC hands were always unrealistically good, and I had to waste a lot of time pulling cards out of the deck before dealing. Fortunately, the players were understanding enough that once I figured out the problem, we just started the section over. For reference, here's some advice on how to play cards in-game and make it fun:

                  * The GM always deals, and the GM can cheat.
                  * On the subject of cheating, the GM can cherry pick out a good (but not great) hand from whatever they deal if they don't get good cards on a natural draw. A high pair, two pairs, three of a kind, or maybe even a flush are good, but more than that defies probability.
                  * The more players are around the table, the more you can cheat. If there is only one other player, don't cheat at all.
                  * Make sure that all the player characters have something they can bet before they start - being unable to continue play because you didn't bring any money to the table is no fun.

                  3. Some minor embarrassments when I just plain forgot to tell players important information, like "Oh, that street performer you were just talking to gave you the keys to the truck you just found," or "Actually, today is the anniversary of the Knocking Box Disaster day, which has great personal and mystical significance for your characters."

                                  This thread could potentially serve to help somebody, somewhere, to avoid my mistakes, so I guess it's worth posting in. Some of my many failures as a GM over the years include:

                                  1. Not checking up on the PC's comfort zones and seeing what they were and were not comfortable with before the start of game. I was young and had never even heard of this idea before. I managed to make at least two of my players deeply uncomfortable and prevent them from having fun...and didn't even hear about it until long after the game was over, because nobody wanted to be That Guy and derail everyone else's fun. I sometimes like games that go to pretty dark places, but knowing that I'm responsible for a couple of friends sitting there miserable and trying to hide it while their friends around them were having a good time and trivializing their feelings without even knowing it...well, it's shitty, and I always do little comfort-zone checklists for my RL players these days. People seem more comfortable talking about these sorts of things one-on-one or in PMs online.

                                  2. Running a D&D game for high-schoolers and letting them play Evil characters. Seriously, I should have known what I was getting into on this one.

                                  3. Not providing a good reason for the party to stick together. One of my earliest attempts to run a campaign almost fell apart because the PCs "met in a tavern" and immediately did NOT hit it off, and I hadn't provided any compelling reason for them to work together. Their backstories and ideologies didn't mesh, and I provided no external opponent for them to unify against, so they quickly cliqued up and faced off against each other. Lesson learned: these days I encourage all of my PCs to have pre-established connections of some kind to the other PCs. Seeing a game sort of disintegrate when the party never clicks is a deeply unsatisfying experience.

                                  4. Running a game before I had the combat system down, and planning a large and tactically involved combat (archers on flying monsters plus a small ground-based ambush party versus the PCs) on the first session. God, that session was horrible! Everyone sitting around bored while I looked shit up in books and got a lengthy on-the-fly lesson from a player who was more experienced with the crunch of the game than I was (he was the reason I didn't just wing it, rule on the fly, and do my studying up LATER when it didn't bore PCs, which is in retrospect what I ought to have done). These days I run a few test-combats in white room and more complicated environments with a new game system with a friend just to get a feel for how combat (or any other mechanically complex system) runs before I run it for a group.

                                  5. GM PCs were never a problem for me...but early on I had a weakness for my antagonists. I loved my villains and wrought elaborate backstories and gave them deep and complex motivations and then the players didn't every find out what they were because they just killed the guys. I responded by making them more over-wrought and more powerful...and the players responded by just killing them. I responded by making the villains MORE powerful in the hopes they'd survive long enough to do something useful...and curbstomped the players. I was trying to find that sweet spot where a villain could show up, be scary/do bad stuff, fight, and either escape the PCs to inconvenience them another day as a recurring enemy or get the PCs to flee. It never worked out that way, and I wasted a ton of time and effort on deeply unsatisfying villain encounters before I improved here. Part of it's system mastery, learning to match the antagonists to the PC's power level. Part of it was getting older and getting older players who were more interested in roleplaying and investigative stuff than fantasy violence...but honestly, most of it was learning to KEEP my beloved little villains OFF THE FUCKING STAGE until the end of the play. That's what minions and messengers and crime scene investigations and stuff are for.

                                  6. Demon babies. Just....just don't make any of your PCs pregnant with demon babies. Especially not without consulting them beforehand? And, um. Don't make it, like, a big prophecy or a central part of your intended campaign arc? It is a thing y'all shouldn't do. This happened to a player of mine in more than one game, to the point where it became something of a running joke, and while the player was fine with it and thought it an interesting story to play out the FIRST time...well. Let's just say I was not the first ST interested in running this plotline. No demon babies.

                                  7. Dear fellow STs: You are not a marriage counselor! Or a relationship counselor! Or a therapist! Unless you are, in which case, you still shouldn't let your players treat you like one unless you're bloody well getting paid for it! I have had three separate games in which I ended up trying to act as a mediator between PCs who had problems with their relationships which threatened the game, and peace-loving old hippie bastard that I am I wasted hours of my time trying to keep everyone happy. Not wanting to kick a player out of the game because their presence makes another player uncomfortable now that they're broken up, not wanting to tell a couple of players who were obviously into each other OOC to tone down the flirtation that was making the other players uncomfortable, not wanting to disappoint a player who wanted to run gigantic one-on-one sessions to play out their complicated relationship with an NPC which was obviously helping them work through some personal issues in a safe space...as an ST you want to bring everyone together to have fun in the world you're making, and it can be really hard to put your foot down and deny someone that fun. The lesson here? Real life has to come first, and a friendship that can't survive a polite but firm kicking-out-of-the-game probably wasn't that strong a friendship to start. And sometimes you've got to admit that a game is no longer fun for you to run, or that a game is delightful but takes too much of your time or energy, and you've gotta end it even though it's deeply important to your players.

                                  8. ...to my shame, I once gave a PC a Rod of Wonder and let him use the d10,000 table online. It, um. It did not end well. I mean, he had a ton of fun, but it absolutely destroyed any suspension of disbelief or sense of verisimilitude the game had.

                                  9. One-shot Pickup games! I hate pickup games! I always come prepared with a pre-planned adventure session I think'll take about three hours...and we wind up running out of time before the PCs are even halfway through the damn thing, and I have to quickly contrive an ending on the spot when we run out of time for game. I've started writing an endpoint in the middle of my intended story arc...and they STILL don't finish the damn things! This one I'm still working on fixing, so I don't have any sage advice here, but I've had several pickups fall flat when I've failed to stick the dismount. The most recent one went a little better...because, thank god, I got some PCs who wanted to play diplomacy games and they avoided violence completely. Since the last pickup was Exalted 2E, if they'd fought something that would have taken up the whole damn session.

                                  10. When a PC asks you if they can play a giant purple owlbear with a pink mohawk who is also a powerful wizard, think carefully on it before you say yes. It turns out when you're trying to run a gritty fantasy-noir game, it kinda ruins the mood.

                                  So I'm making God-Kicking Boot, an Exalted webcomic, now. Updates on Sundays. Full-color, mediocre but slowly improving art. It's a thing.

                                  The absence of a monument can, in its own way, be something of a monument also.                                   -Roger Zelazny

                                                  Another big mistake I made once: running a Mistborn game (three of the people, myself included, were big fans of the book series, and the others were indifferent), the players were a group loosely all tied to one of the PCs who was a noble, and were looking to reduce/remove the influence of a major house in the area. Noble wanted to do it because the house was a rival house, other characters either hated the oppressive system (target house was known for being particularly vicious to the serf class), were being paid to, or were manipulating the situation for their own ends. Lots of fun. Game was going pretty well, we were all learning the system (most of us had read it, but running is a whole different thing)...until the central PC left the state to join the army. Normally, this'd just be one of those annoying accidents, except this guy had a long, long track record of being unreliable and spontaneously disappearing for months at a time. And yet, we let him be the linchpin character, without whom more or less the whole situation fell apart.

                                                  Now, I suppose I could have overtaken the character, except 1. I didn't think of it, and 2. he'd been doing some things with an undefined end goal, which meant to me it was just random stuff (he'd been promising to explain it to me, but see above re: unreliable), so I would have had to do either some quick two-stepping, or change established fact. Besides, him leaving kind of threw the whole dynamic sideways, so the game kind of just unsatisfactorily ended.

                                                  Never doing that again. I like the guy, we remain really good friends, and I'd game with him anytime, but never going to let him be in a central role again.
