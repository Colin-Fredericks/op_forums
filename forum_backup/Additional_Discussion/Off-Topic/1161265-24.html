        Originally posted by PMárk View Post
        As a sidenote, because I'm never being sure about that: did D&D 4e outsold 3e+3.5e, or just 3.5? I'm not even counting the 3rd party stuff and Pathfinder...
        From what I remember about companies and shares of the market at the time (I was paying somewhat close attention to it because I knew several guys who were fairly big in the industry and they would spend a lot of time arguing about sales numbers and market shares), around 2003 D&D 3rd edition was the most successful RPG on the market. White Wolf's world of Darkness however was an extremely close second and there had been a few years where they had recently sold more product than D&D, and then Palladium Books was a distant third, but still a solid force in the RPG publishing business.

        In 2004 White Wolf swapped over to the New World of Darkness and that never really took off. A lot of long-time players enjoyed all of the metaplot elements and decided to just keep playing the old games. The company's market share dropped pretty significantly from the days where they were going nose to nose with TSR for control of the RPG market. Then in 2006 Palladium Books had their "Crisis of Treachery" when an employee embezzled hundreds of thousands of dollars and the company barely avoided going bankrupt but in exchange it had to pretty much halt publishing products for several years. So suddenly D&D found itself the undisputed King of the RPG Hill.

        Unfortunately at this same time the RPG market itself was starting to enter freefall due to two external reasons. The first was because of the massive proliferation of MMOs (especially World of Warcraft). Previously roleplaying games had been a kind of "gateway" for nerds, but now many people were getting into online RPGs instead and bypassing pen and paper RPGs. Lots of older players didn't play as often and fewer newer players were getting into roleplaying. Further, online piracy had also become a lot more common, and many companies were struggling to make the changes necessary to releasing electronically or otherwise dealing with piracy. So the brains at TSR/WotC/Hasbro decided to release a new edition and intentionally included a lot of MMO elements. They were hoping that would make the game more appealing to the younger crowd of people who were introduced to "nerd culture" through MMORPGs.

        However 4th edition was simply nowhere near the kind of success they were hoping it would be. A lot of long-time D&D fans disliked the mechanics which made it feel more like a video game than what they thought Dungeons and Dragons should feel like, or they were unhappy with changes to the settings (or both) and so didn't bother buying the books. And while some new players were attracted, it wasn't anywhere near enough to offset the old players who stopped buying D&D products.

        4th edition was released in 2008 and by 2010 Paizo (who created Pathfinder) had already replaced D&D as the biggest publishing company in the market. Pathfinder was still not as successful as 3/3.5 edition had been during its heyday but it was enough to, for the first time, see a company other than TSR/WotC/Hasbro solidly sit at the top of the RPG market. Games Workshop's Warhammer 40k RPG line took off around this time and I believe Rogue Trader actually outsold 4th edition in 2009 (or maybe it was Deathwatch in 2010, I can't remember which off hand). As an aside Exalted had been HUGE in 2006-08 but was starting to dwindle a little bit by 2009 so I don't believe it ever outsold 4th edition.

        So 4th edition was absolutely less successful than 3/3.5 edition in terms of both product moved and market share because unlike the changeover from 2nd edition to 3rd edition, a lot of 3rd edition players didn't choose to buy 4th edition. But it's also worth keeping in mind the changing demographics in the tabletop RPG industry and that the RPG industry as a whole in 2009 was selling far fewer products than they had been in the early 2000's. Also, 4th edition was an attempt to try and deal with those forces (MMO's) and while it wasn't successful TSR/WotC/Hasbro probably deserves some credit for such forward thinking and willingness to take such a huge risk, even if it didn't pay off in the end.

        At the very least there were players who got into RPGs thanks to 4th edition, and that's always a good thing, company and edition wars aside.
