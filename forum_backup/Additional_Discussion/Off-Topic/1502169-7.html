                My frustrations are slightly different, but only because I live in a very rural area in the US. A lot more of our problems are funding related than urban planning related. For example, all of our buses only go from small towns to the one thing we call a city around here (and it's not that big by city standards). There's no ring routes that connect the outlying towns. Despite the fact that we actually have roads a bus could easily handle, there's no buses that string together a bunch of towns in a row. You can drive, or you can take the bus to the city, and then take a second bus back out to the next town over (if you're lucky that will only take an hour, and they stop real early).

                We have miles of train tracks that basically only get used once a week that services agriculture. It's in use, so it's not like it needs to be fixed up or anything. It can handle a 50 car train full of corn, it can handle some light rail cars between towns.

                And of course, everyone complains about parking (these are small towns, there's no big parking lots or parking garages, street parking is competing with residential parking) and how there's no way to really build more parking because that would require demolishing people's homes. But suggest the idea that we increase transit that doesn't need parking and people act like you're asking them to fund the entire US military budget.

                        Our county is technically growing, but marginally so (about +0.5% last year). It's relatively inexpensive on basic living expenses, and has good access to both local and interstate highways. So as we're losing small farms and other rural staples to megafarms, we're replacing with commuter communities that want quaint small town life that's not too far from major cities. We do have some mass transit, it just doesn't extend well beyond the only "big city" in the county. We also have an Amtrak station that has remarkably good prices to get to Philadelphia (which means depending your budget and schedule, gives you a lot of options as a major transit hub).

                        My town is shrinking (rural very conservative, very poor retention rates of the 18-36 cohorts between jobs and social environment, and the only retirement communities/homes nearby are technically in other municipalities so we're losing on that end even faster too) though there's a serious mismanagement issue since the towns to the east, south, and west of us are all growing (and attracting young families in droves compared to us) despite no drastic economic/etc. differences. A lot of it comes down to our town being convinced to not invest in itself, while the towns around us all are, turning it into a self-fulfilling prophesy. For example, we used to be the only town in our little cluster that had an actual public swimming pool (and it was really nice with lots of space and different swim areas, surrounding by a park with cheap pavilion rentals for events, and it's across the street from the high school). But it hasn't been updated in 20 years because the people in charge of thing don't see it as a reason to get people to move here (and the tax revenue they'd bring with them to offset some red in the ledger for the pool) Less upkeep, less use, less use, less cash flow, less cash, less upkeep, etc. On top of that, in the meantime, the town to the east of us did a public-private partnership rec-center, and a full private rec-center opened in an unincorporated area between the towns to the south and west of us. So we went from drawing in all our neighbors to a nice local public pool (and all the economic impact for local businesses that meant), to everyone paying 3 to 5 times as much, but for equally better facilities that are way more than a pool.

                        Those three neighboring towns are also building pretty good local tourism attractions. They loosened laws so there's actually night life stuff. You can spend a weekend, or even a week, in them and actually having something do to if you're just visiting. If we get better transit here, it's because we'd be leaching off of them.

                                        I just read the Texas' judge's opinion, in which he declared that the FDA could not allow the sale of Mifepristone, and I see where he's going. It's not going to be pretty.

                                        Judge Kacsmaryk repeatedly mentions the Comstock Act throughout his opinion, and notes that the FDA repeatedly violated the Comstock Act, and that this is the primary reason for his ruling. In other words, the Comstock Act is the cornerstone on which his decision rests. Now, the Comstock Act was ruled unconstitutional by the Supreme Court back in 1965 in Griswald v. Connecticut.

                                        If you remember last year when the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade, Justice Thomas specifically called out several other Supreme Court rulings that he believed were "wrongly decided" based on the "erroneous" idea that Americans have a constitutional right to privacy. Among those rulings he listed Griswald.

                                        By basing his entire decision around the Comstock Act, this judge is basically setting the Supreme Court up to overrule Griswald. In sports terms, it's like in volleyball where one player gently tosses the ball so his partner can spike it. That's basically what Judge Kacsmaryk is doing with his legal decision. I fully expect that by next year, the Supreme Court will overrule Griswald.​
