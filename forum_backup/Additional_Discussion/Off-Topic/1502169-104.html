                                    It's not complicated: target demographics are tied to advertising and merch sales.

                                    Popularity only matters if you sell something directly to consumers. A box-office sales don't care about target demographics because it doesn't matter who buys a ticket as long as tickets get sold.

                                    If you make your real money on ads and sales of additional items, then you can't just be popular, you have to be popular with people that are going to spend money based on that. A show that's targeted towards kids is going to attract advertisers shilling kids stuff (mostly so the kids pester parents into buying stuff), and and tie-in products you have in the works have to work for kids. If your show isn't popular with kids, but rather popular with 30 somethings with kids that are enjoying the nostalgia kick but don't have a lot of disposable income, you're going to have to completely swap your ads which is going to be a financial hit for awhile, and you might make a fraction of the merch sales you were expecting to.

                                    That's how popularity doesn't turn a profit because of demographics. It sucks, but it's just something that's gotten worse despite having been the way things have been done for longer than most of the posters here's parents have been alive. Ads have been part of entertainment back when radio was a fancy new technology, and maximizing ad returns by targeting ads has been part of the equation the whole time. It was just back in the early radio days, they could only target on very broad grounds because there were only a few core groups being catered to. Now we've been data-mined to hell and back so we can be microtargeted; which forces creators to play the algorithm game (even if they're on streaming giants like Netflix or whatever) or risk not getting money even with plenty of views.

                                                Originally posted by DarkKing View Post
                                                Did 'collectors' all collectively die out when I wasn't looking?

                                                Why do people even have basement cellars, if they aren't using them as a trophy room for their limited edition action figures?
                                                Collectors aren't that big a demographic, despite growth. For franchises like Star Wars collectors can respond for up to 35% of the market in spending, rarely more.

                                                That's the absolutely very best scenario: a SW show that hits whatever audience will make a lot of money because collectors will buy anything from the show and it's well established among them. Even then, if they get prepared to sell mostly light saber toys for 5-10 yo boys and the show doesn't hit this demographic, that means two thirds of expected revenue is lost.

                                                For a new IP collectors are not an established demographic. They'll be maybe 10%, even less of the viewers. Collector culture around a new show takes time to form. If a new cartoon faces the very same situation, they may see more than 9/10 toys getting dust despite success.

                                                It's not a small thing: merch requires a lot of design, prep, logistics to get to stores. Even just changing production rates with the factory can be a nightmare.

                                                We have a clear contrast of how it works with Disney's Encanto: the movie was a far greater success than expected, but still Disney made the wrong bet. The character Isabela has the iconic Princess appeal despite not being the main one, is a standard pretty girl, and before her development arc is the flowery type. Disney heavily invested in her merch, with huge variety of options and high volumes. The least investment was on Luisa, the muscled strong sister, who got three or four simple toys total.

                                                By the end of 2024, 3 years after release, they hadn't managed to match the merch enough to the actual demand. Isabela's merch tanked hard as even those who wanted her didn't want her flowery design, while Luisa is still today the hardest to find and by then still had considerable waiting lines. For Disney this was not a small hiccup: it was a disaster. Even if for them it didn't mean to "cancel" Encanto, it was a big financial failure.

                                                A series has It even worse with sponsorship and advertising. Those are the things that keep most shows going, and also depend on target audience. Sometimes you can change them with some ease, but there's always a challenge involved as several factors affect who'll benefit from buying the spot, if they want the association, if the time is good for them and if they can match the price you want... Or need.

                                                Sometimes those are all workable problems. But other times the cost of changing course can be genuinely prohibitive, or still so fraught with market uncertainty that ditching the whole thing for a safer bet is cheaper.

                                                        It's also important to remember that contemporary collector culture is directly impacted from C-suite boardrooms down to individual customer choices by the rise of microtransactions and digital "collectibles."

                                                        The cost and barrier of entry to, say, getting a fomo skin colab in Fortnight is an insanely more profitable enterprise for businesses and feels less onerous to consumers in the moment (and is if you're only paying microtransactions very selectively, but that whole environment is designed to get you to be as spend-happy as possible). No factories, no shipping, no storage... just a file that can copied infinitely and given an arbitrary price designed to feel inexpensive but still make tons of money in aggregate.

                                                        This is kinda good for physical collectors because collectibles are actually rare and therefore special again. We're not in the false-scarcity hype bubble that nuked comic books when it burst and made Beanie Babies a thing people were buying as "investments." Physical merch has to actually be kinda special and something desirable to just own and put on display/used instead of being locked away to ensure mint-in-box status. But that also comes with, well, higher prices on top of the economy being shit.
