    Originally posted by MyWifeIsScary View Post
    Perhaps if you're at a professional level, you may be correct, but for a layman, no. I distinctly remember, during the early 2000's, getting taught the narrative of -Classic era good, medieval era bad- at school, among other very silly historical things (Nobody knows why they used straight swords....) . You would have to actually hire an expert for your film to completely ignore if you wanted to pretend your film had some attempt at accuracy. Let's not even go into psychology, where the pulpy quackery of frued is still taught today like it's something anyone should learn, nor should we go into nutrition, where common knowledge has been based on a few unfounded assertions that only a small and unheard-of subset of nutritionists have ever contested. Maybe you're right and contemporary culture wasn't so difficult to get accurate information for, but it'd be the odd one out in a long list of things people were getting consistently wrong. Someone with a low budget, say, someone writing an RPG supplement, probably doesn't have the budget to hire an expert knowlede, so the quality of high-level sources isn't nearly as relevant as the quality and number of easily accessible sources.

    That's still not true, and it is kind of insulting. For professional writers (and people who produce material for RPG supplements like to pride themselves as such), the ability to do research to a reasonably proficient level is, and always was, a basic skill. When people were making mistakes in their writing during the 1990s it was generally due to them writing in a hurry, or being lazy, or lacking the skills which they ought to have. Take al-Qadim, for instance; anyone able to visit a library or reasonably decent bookshop in 1995 could easily pick up a copy of Edward Said's Orientalism, which might suggest that some of the concepts in the game's background might be slightly problematic. That game writers weren't reading Said says more about the people writing games than the ability to carry out competent research.

    If we want a reasonably basic underlying test for accessibility, we would ask if material produced in the recent past is more accurate or sensitive than material produced, say, 25 years ago. The answer is that is probably not. The most recent edition of Vampire: the Dark Ages has worse history than the first edition, despite the notional increased accessibility of resources.

                                                        Originally posted by Kammerer View Post
                                                        I feel like it's very simple actually. Culture isn't homogenous and varies wildly down to the individual perception. Culture also changes overtime, and from the contact with other cultures. "Asian-American culture" is different from the "Japanese culture", and the "Japanese culture" itself is a vague abstraction within which Tokyo culture and Kyoto culture are wildly different and have as many different memes as they have common memes.
                                                        The government of Tsushima said that the game did a great job depicting ancient Japan. Meanwhile some Japanese Americans (and Japanese Canadians and other members of the Japanese diaspora) said it did a poor job of depicting Japan and Japanese people. The developers worked closely with people in Japan to produce the game. They didn't really work much with Japanese Americans for the simple reason that there are no Japanese-Americans in Ghost of Tsushima. The game didn't have anything to do with Japanese expatriots and it's very likely that the Japanese people they worked with also didn't consider how people of Japanese ancestry outside of Japan might feel about things. So the Ghost of Tsushima developers worked closely with people in Japan, but for obvious reasons getting input from people of Japanese origin living outside of Japan was not something they really did.

                                                        This was probably an unintentional blind spot on the part of the developers, and ultimately some members of the Japanese diaspora felt hurt because of the way the game engaged with certain stereotypes or ideas which maybe have been received one way in Japan but in a different way outside of it. Really it just goes to show that making a product depicting a culture is incredibly complicated and there's a lot of to think about, especially in the way that culture is so broad that people who trace their origins to one culture but live in another culture can feel significantly different about the way that original culture is depicted from the people who still live in that culture.

                                                        Ultimately when attempting to determine whether a creator is racist or not racist, more important than simply looking at the results of a product ("was someone hurt or offended by the product?") like was posited earlier in the thread, intent and the sort of work the creator put into the product is very important, because it's possible to simply make a mistake and overlook some aspect of a culture or group of people, which is basically what I think happened with Ghost of Tsushima.
