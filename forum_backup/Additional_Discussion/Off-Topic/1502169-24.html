    I think that analogy really sells short the disparity between physical and digital security.

    Social engineering,which is a decade old practice, doesn't work on getting a physical key to a physical lock, because that's too obvious a security threat. Social engineering works because digital security needs to interface with humans, and humans are not equipped for it. If we use passwords that are easy to remember, they're easier to break, if we use high security passwords we need some way to track them because we can't remember a randomize set of 16+ characters for every login. Which means there's a notebook, or a digital password vault, or something that can unravel the best security on the programming side of things. You can't phish for a physical key, but phishing attacks work great for getting digital keys, because it's a lot easier to make an email that looks legit than it is to sound legit in person.

    Digital security also gets really hard to explain, really fast. We have older generations that didn't grow up with it and just can't contextualize how to prioritize different digital security factors. We also have a younger generation that's so online and so used to their lives being automated, than they don't need to understand how any of it actually works inside the fancy boxes that we poke to do cool things.

    It's like how more and more people just don't know how to do all but the most basic car repairs and maintenance. There is a cultural factor, but there's also the fact that cars have gotten more and more complex making it ever more difficult for DIY folks to fix what used to be simple things without exceedingly expensive equipment that gets to the point where you might as well just ay someone else to do it. A lot of the reasons why are good! Better safety systems, better fuel economy, etc. are all things we want, but part of the cost in jamming all this stuff into a car is making the car too complex for most people to want to learn how to fix unless they plan to do so for a living.

                    That feels like both a mischaracterization of what a lot of people want, and a false dichotomy.

                    The real end result the vast majority of people want is for extremely problematic historical art to be part of a well constructed educational system. It should be preserved, and taught, not locked in some vault, but it should also generally be a guided experience.

                    You can't learn from mistakes if you're only exposed to mistakes by people telling you that they're good things actually. It's hard to learn the right lessons from mistakes by just exposing yourself to things (ask any therapists that works in exposure therapy about the laundry list of horror stories of people that thought they could do that on their own and just made things far worse). While there's an entirely separate rant to be had about how much teachers have been devalued in Western society (even in plenty of countries with far better performing education systems than the US has), well before and well beyond any valid criticism about better pedagogy techniques for a digital age, the whole point of having a teacher instead of just a text book is that we learn more full lessons with a human that knows the subject guiding us.

                    I don't think, and I don't put much stock in the few attempts to make an argument for, the 1915 The Birth of a Nation film to just be out in the wild actually teaches anyone any lessons. I don't think anyone actually learns any good lessons from watching a racist propaganda film just for the sake of watching it. Sure, some people have the preexisting educational background to know enough context around the film to appreciate it for what it is, but most of them probably got exposed to on the way there anyway. The idea that it should be something you learn about in school, or at an educational institution like a museum, really shouldn't be that troubling a concept.

                    If we want to remember our history, especially the bad stuff, for future lessons, I'd also point out that generally speaking historical artifacts are far safer in the hands of professional archivists, rather than in the hands of the highest bidder.

                        I'm not sure it is.

                        Yes, reasonable people keep perspective. But a lot of people aren't reasonable. A lot of people--probably just a vocal minority,and probably mostly on the internet where outrage is cultivated for profit, but still--seem to only want to signal that they're on the right side of history by viciously attacking enemies they perceive for reasons they don't understand with no thought to how it impacts the marginalized communities in question. I've seen it so many times it's hard for me to not be suspicious that any given "ally" might not care about me or my well-being at all rather than the endorphin rush of declaring oneself righteous.

                        That said, I do not think it is inherently bad to cancel harmful art (though for the record, I don't know which art we're talking about or what "canceled" means in this context), or that "it's a product of its time" is a valid excuse. Like, as an example, apparently Mark Twain didn't know to not call people the N-word, but it makes no sense to extrapolate out from there that no one of his era knew it either. I'm pretty sure the people being called the N-word knew it wasn't okay, so to claim that the time didn't know any better is to argue that black people don't count as participants in the culture they were being described as: it's a form of cultural imperialism aimed at history. As you say, there are a lot of legitimate criticisms. I just tend to be skeptical that people who make those criticisms are being sincere rather than shallow.

                            The thing is... people that are just in it to be provocative on the Internet inherently don't care about the actual solutions, or they wouldn't just be spouting off on social media. The US has seen a massive rise in the call to remove certain books from schools (including school libraries), but all those requests are public records. When people went and looked, it was a tiny group of people (like a few hundred across the whole country) spamming the system; not the thousands of people yelling about it online.

                            So it's still a mischaracterization of the less reasonable people to say it's anything about their modern sensibilities being offended, when what they want is social clout with their Internet Tribe and saying "cancel this" and never acting on is what they're doing.

                            Of course "cancelled" is yet another victim of the Internet massively speeding up appropriating language to make tribalism worse. "Cancel" was originally used to mean... cancel. Like cancelling your subscription to a magazine, a newspaper, or your cable company; just applied to social media where you unfollow/block/mute/ignore/whatever. It was a a declaration of personal choice. "I'm not paying attention to So and So on these apps." Sure, making a deal about it wasn't just a matter of personal tastes. You were cancelling something for a reason, and you felt like it was a good reason, and you wanted to bring attention to that reason. George Bush Sr. publicly canceled his NRA membership after the NRA attacked him for a gun safety bill he signed as President during the NRA's transition towards being much more vocal absolutists about no gun-laws instead of being an organization that for a long time (aka what Bush Sr. grew up with) that helped promote positive gun ownership, taught gun safety classes, and generally backed what we'd now call "common sense" laws as a non-partisan issue. He wanted to warn people about how they were losing sight of their founding mission, and he had the platform to do it.

                            Social media of course, gave us all platforms. So that didn't help with these sorts of things. And "cancel" became a warning, "A lot of people are going to cancel you if you don't delete that fast" which is still fine. People say dumb shit, and losing a bunch of friends/followers/etc. because you broadcast your dumb idea to the whole world is something a real world friend should tell you that you just did and you're going to want to undo as much as you can.

                            The problem starts (and this is history repeating itself) when the word because a scary external threat instead of what's just basic human behavior adapting to a new form of interaction. You get people conflating been banned for violating a services ToS with being the victim of "cancel culture" because you got banned because the scary threat of people that disagree without.. not because you posted something bannable even though you totally did. Coopting language and using it to deflect is a very old part of human discourse.

                            That said, I completely agree with not really buying into the "product of the time" thing. It an important part of literary analysis for sure, but to go with your example, people know what they're doing. If you live in a time when a white man calling another white man that word could be seen as such an act of provocation that shooting the man slinging it around would be justifiable homicide under extreme provocation, you know calling people that word is wrong. People in the past were just as capable as us of coming up with mental gymnastics to justify why doing the wrong thing in a given context was actually OK.
