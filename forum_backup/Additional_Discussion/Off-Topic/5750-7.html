    Well, two bits: The bigger problem is that, if the general populace is anything like me on this instance, and for once I think they might, Wonder Woman becomes a very weird thing to take seriously at face value, which means it needs to not just be a good movie, it needs to be very good. When the Justice League animated series was on TV, I remember watching a few of the episodes and being blown away by what an amazing character Wonder Woman was. Before in my life I was never able to take her seriously-she looked like a cheap knock-off of Superman designed to appeal to the female demographic but still had a design to draw in the guys. Learning about the Greek background just made it weirder for me(And I like Greek mythology). I imagine for the ignorant populace, Wonder Woman sits in this same weird place that begs the question "Why should we take her seriously?".

    The problem, then, can't begin with the actor*-the potential for problems exists much higher up the line. You need a production company, writers, and directors who are willing to take the character seriously, understand her extremely well, and work to make her as awesome as she can be, a role-model of sorts**, someone everyone(or a vast majority) can look up to.

    Nolan? Good start. Zack "I dedicated Sucker Punch to my mom" Snyder? And the ball has already been dropped. Particularly if it follows the lead of Man of Steel.

    So....on the one hand, they can cast whoever for Wonder Woman because, as it stands, I have little hope. On the other hand...Man, if any movie deserved to be a good movie, it should have been Wonder Woman. She's someone I think lots of people could use in their personal mythology.

    EDIT: Of course, all of this is naysaying before I've even seen anything about it. If the trailers and reviews are good enough, I will gladly watch it.

    *because actress makes me want to grind my teeth.
    **That's a loaded term filled with nasty expectations, but it stands.

                                                All in the name of feminism.

                                                I hate to make it seem like Sucker Punch has a point, but since we're on it, I seem to remember one person found some interesting things to consider in the imagery. Each girl in the fantasy seems to take on one particular fetish as an image, but likewise also takes on a warrior class from certain cultures. Baby Doll, as an example, is easy-she taps into the otaku culture by being both the sexualized schoolgirl but also drawing on samurai iconography(the Katana being the big one, but the guy made notes of several other small things). Sweet Pea taps into knight imagery, one of them was a viking, etc. On top of that, the settings they worked in on the hyper fantasy psychosis trips were ones where women are often portray sexily for no good reason beyond fan pandering, from high fantasy to steampunk and dystopian cyberpunk(I'm not sure how valid that is, but it was interesting to hear the guy talk about it), so the conquest of those places was also vaguely conquering the sexism. I don't remember all of it, and at this point and time I'm not sure how valid it was, but it was an interesting discussion.

                                                Interesting, but horribly flawed. Even acknowledged by that one guy's ramblings, it just doesn't hold up from the transition of a conceptual design to the execution, particularly when you account for the first two layers of Babydoll's psychosis. The scenes are boring and really don't tie into what's going on and make no real statements beyond some visual metaphor with zero exposition even implied. If the idea was to take the sexualizing objectification of these fetishes and subvert it into being self-reigned icons of female empowerment, then somewhere along the way Zak Snyder got so decidedly distracted by Emily Browning's thigh line that he forgot that idea.

                                                And it's not like the idea can't be executed. Going to my otaku side here, I found it surprising in my later years that Sailor Moon was considered quite a feminist piece, but after having read the manga and rewatched the (Dic/Saban American serialized version of, aka the worst version of) the anime, I was pleasantly surprised to find out they were right. One of the ways it was unexpectedly so was how it subverted the idea behind several traditonally feminine ideas and icons by just flat out making them powerful and respectable, from the sailor fuku to the idea of the more traditionally regarded feminine emotions being the ones that take down the bad guys(and not in a "Oh, I'll redeem it" way most of the time, more in a "Now you burn, now you bleed, now you die" sense, which is weird). I'm not versed enough to talk heavily on the subject, but here's a springboard I picked up from last year: http://henshinjustice.com/2013/08/07...t-sailor-moon/

                                                Sucker Punch never really gets around to the respect part, or even really the powerful aspect. It has violence to feed guys want of action and fetishized women to feed guys want of arousal. THe girls kicking ass never means anything, it's all mindless showcasing, with no significance to the struggle or stakes beyond the basic one to give it impact, and thus it's violence and not power. The fetishization is never given any implication of self-reign or determination(in comparison again to the Sailor Moon fuku), it's mindless titillation, with none of the girls owning it and giving us a reason to respect them regardless of what they're wearing, thus it's arousal, no empowerment.

                                                I'm rambling, and heaven forbid I be the one to turn this into a feminism discussion thread-while I support and try and be mindful of the subject, I am woefully ignorant of the many intricate details beyond the basic idea of "treat people like people, not vaginas and certainly not as dolls"-but it must be said that if all you want is mindless action and titillation, Sucker Punch is...well, not even good there, because the action is boring and the eye candy disturbing, but passable. But for what it was intended to do? Ugh. Man, Zak, I don't know how you can aim so high and end up shooting the ground.
