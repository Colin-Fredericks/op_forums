                                            Public opinion of celebrities (and more really) is also prone to a quasi-uncanny valley type effect.

                                            Rather than a relatively proportional line of how much people dislike a public figure mapping to how much bad stuff you can reasonably know about them, there's a huge dip in popular opinion if you hit a certain level of bad actions that's worse than if you did even worse things. A very common word to describe the emotional reaction is a sense of betrayal (which ties into not putting people on pedestals).

                                            If you already believe someone to be a sexist asshat, you're unlikely to have a strong emotional response to them doing or saying something sexist unless it passes a certain threshold of offense (such as criminality).

                                            If you already believe someone to be staunchly anti-sexist, but then it comes out that they've been doing some very sexist things, that "betrayal" provokes a very strong negative reaction.

                                            And that's what Joss brought on himself. By hyping up his self-identification as a feminist and then failing to act in private according to those ideals, once the public found out, there was a huge backlash because of the added sense of betrayal involved. Compare this to someone like Charlie Sheen, who's public life has been awash in infidelity, intoxication, and minor criminal behaviors is inoculated to a certain amount of public backlash because we expect him to do things many people find repugnant.
