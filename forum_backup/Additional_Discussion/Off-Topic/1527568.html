          Yeesh, there's a difficult one. Part of the problem is that the history of the Doctor as a character is so obvious more than the person on screen. Lots of great actors have had the part, but clearly undermined by behind-the-scenes problems that what we see on screen is just not what other roles have shown them capable of. A lot of Internet lists top with performances that are bolstered by some of the best show-runners and writers the show has ever had.

          If we're just looking at the final result, my rather uncontroversial pick would be Tom Baker. There's a lot besides him there (as the 4th the original run had really hit its stride and voice where the early ones were way more scattershot in what they wanted to do with the character), but his performance is basically what everyone else is trying to do with a balance of whimsy of the Doctor's desire to enjoy experiencing all of space and time, and the gravitas for the serious dramatic moments when that falls aside. There's a reason he had the role for seven years when most actors only last three or less.

          In what's probably a bit more opinionated take, here's my list of the actors that I think elevated what they were given (even if a lot of it was unfixable no matter how good of an actor), so these are ones that are definitely favored actors, even if their Doctors aren't the best:

          1) Peter Capaldi
          2) Jodie Whittaker
          3) Patrick Troughton (though a special mention goes out to being hit the hardest by the lost episodes so a lot of his work never really got its due because there weren't reruns of it like many of the others).

          In a different take, I think David Tennant is the actor I really wish they had cast later. I think he and Matt Smith were cast fairly young in the hopes of being able to take the role for longer than more established actors (and actors that know how to stick up for themselves) like Eccelston and Capaldi, rather then repeat the rapid turn over of 5 through 9, and while I do understand why a lot of fans love their runs as the Doctor, I think it shows too much that they haven't had the range of parts many of the other actors had before taking over. While I appreciate Matt Smith's career since being the Doctor, Tennant is the one who's done a lot of work that I really wish he had under his belt before hand. Just imagine how much better his run would have been if he'd already done Broadchurch, Jessica Jones, Good Omens, and Inside Man before, and was that much better at tapping into the darker side of the Doctor.

          I'm also hoping that Ncuti Gatwa doesn't get hit with the triple no-win of being a minority actor, questionable show running team, and being cast on the young side for a character that needs so much range. He seems like a strong choice, but as I started with, great actors have had meh runs with the character thanks to things beyond their control.

                              I have mixed feelings about the Jodie Whittaker Doctor, but she acted her damned heart out to make three years worth of compelling performances. But there's a reason she's on my list of actors that elevated their material...even if it wasn't always enough to make great material.

                              This actually gets into a hard part about addressing the companions. There are two interconnected shifts between the original Doctor Who run and the revival run. The originals were more "self-contained" even if not to one episode. It wasn't Star Trek with lots of stand alone stories and a few multi-episodes here or there, or an episode that followed up on an older one. But most of the stories were given as many episodes as they needed, and then the show moved on. "This story needs three episodes to work, this needs four, this needs one, boom we have a year's worth of stories lets get to it." This pushed the older shows to be much more from the Doctor's perspective and made the companions more ancillary. This isn't to say the old companions were bad or uncompelling, but they weren't the focus of the plots because the plots jumped around as the Doctor hopped into the Tardis and moved on to the next adventure.

                              The revival shows shifted to having a bigger focus on "building up" to a big arc: A smattering of isolated plots would get sprinkled with hints and foreshadowing of things to come, and then usually a few episodes of climactic stuff. This also pushed the companions much more into the forefront of the stories because the stories moved to be much more how a companion would experience things. So the revival companions got way more focus in the plot, and way more character development, because there would be a more linear continuity to the episodes.

                              Obviously there's some preference on this, and both approaches have their strengths and weaknesses. But it creates a very big gulf of what people think a companion is supposed to be, and therefore what makes for a good one.

                              Personally, I think the most important thing to a companion is that they offer such a radically different perspective from the Doctor. That is even explicitly in the shows, part of why the Doctor does it: the Doctor can't think like a mortal linear-time-frame person and wants to experience and interrogate that perspective vicariously. This... probably unsurprisingly, puts me at odds with a lot of Doctor Who fans because the most popular companions tend to be the revival ones where they're far more relatable thanks to having more character development, and have far deeper ties to the Doctor. I agree that a lot of them a great and fun characters for it, but it gets away from the core dynamic; as well as letting the story play with varied numbers (and even species) of companions by getting too wrapped up in how one or two companions interact with the Doctor over a few years of shows.

                                      Pertwee. I met him at a convention. I was just a little kid and was completely starstruck. I could barely say anything because I was meeting the Dr. Who. He signed an autographed picture for me. I guess I was so stunned I couldn't say anything and just kind of sat there with my mouth hanging open. He laughed and pulled out his wallet and gave me a Pound note. I remember he showed off the naval tattoos he had on his forearms and talked about how he needed to wear long sleeves on the show because, obviously, Dr. Who wouldn't have English naval tattoos.

                                      My favorite companion was always Leela from The 4th Dr's run. She was a warrior, and it was always great to see her pull out her knife and kill people with it, while The Doctor tried to hold her back from her more aggressive instincts. It was a really fun change from the more typical sci-fi dynamic, where female characters were often relegated to being helpless damsels. Instead she was functioning as the Doctor's bodyguard, and on occasion, hitman. I always really enjoyed her, thought she brought a fun change of pace to the show.

                                                      I will bring back my long time complaints about Russel T Davies. The man is magnificent at writing characters and character interaction. He's pretty good at writing spectacle too. He's not good at writing science fiction. That's why he takes heavy inspiration from, and sometimes outright steals, from previous stories. Most of the episodes he's best known for. Dalek is inspired by Jubilee. Family of Blood is an adaptation of Human Nature. Etc. He also tends to be graceless when incorporating political elements into his stories. That's not a complaint about his politics, it's a complaint about his ability.

                                                      Unfortunately, I don't know that he's aware of his problems so I don't have much faith he can avoid them this time around. The four episodes last year were mostly good, but some of his problems were leaking through. The most worrying one is the quick and uncharitable descriptions of how the Moffat era companions left. It came across like RTD was hurt that people didn't like how he handled Donna's departure and was criticizing the fanbase for being hypocritical. It's rarely a good look criticizing the fanbase, and it makes it look like RTD doesn't understand what the complaint even was. Most importantly, though, this was a scene that could have played into his strengths and he didn't use it that way.

                                                      I have hopes, though, that Davies main purpose here it to revitalize interest in the show while training up a new showrunner. He's good with spectacle and in the short term that's what the show needs.

                                                      As far as Doctors go:

                                                      Matt Smith and Sylvester McCoy are my favorite Doctors.
                                                      Tom Baker and David Tennant are the most fun.
                                                      Jodie Whittaker and Colin Baker were the outstanding Doctors brought down by terrible writing. Capaldi almost suffers the same fate.
