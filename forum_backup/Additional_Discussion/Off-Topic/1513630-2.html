    Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
    Ultimately though, are you experiencing an ethical failure, or an emotional failure? We can do lots of things that make us feel like failures that aren't the results of poor moral judgements.     I see it as both. One could argue that I’m somewhat biased, given that I’m basing a moral in part on a deep emotional response, but I don’t think my emotional response takes away from the ethical concerns.

    I believe in redemption and atonement as being something anyone can do. People change, it’s just how the world works. Many, when confronted with their own wrongdoing, will clamp down on whatever they felt justified their actions. Some will never truly believe they were in the wrong, even onto their end. But rare as it may be, some won’t. Some will try to make an amends. Some will go on to do great good. For example, I’ve read of a conquistador who one day realized the scope of the horrors he had inflicted. He became a priest, tried to end slavery wherever he could. He even went back to Spain, and though he didn’t manage change public opinion on Spanish Colonialism or the humanity of those they had conquered, he is considered a founder of Spanish civil rights. If even the worst of us can try to make amends, couldn’t anyone? Killing someone takes away all they could be, all the good they could have done. It inflicts grief and trauma on the loved ones of the killed, and in many way harms those who kill. And of course, very few want to die. Knowing all of that, I just can’t say it’s moral to kill. It may be the most practical solution, maybe even the “best” solution, but it’s just not one I can accept as moral.

    From an emotional standpoint, I think it’s well established that I have a strong aversion to even the idea of killing somebody. But there is also a more self serving reason. I also am prone to obsession, fixation on topics. It’s nice when it’s a video game I like, or something like the life cycles of jellyfish, or talking about morals. It’s less nice when being unable to stop trying to imagine what death and dying is like, or the think about implications of the heat death of the universe. In my experience…it hasn’t mixed well with guilt or grief. I try to make amends, even if I’m the only one who feels like I was at fault. Sometimes I just make things worse. Given how strongly I react just to the thought experiments, and how hard it is to break out of my obsessions, I…don’t know if I would survive the experience. Even if I did, I feel like something key to who I am would break.

        Well, to reemphasize what I was trying to say, I don't think emotional responses shouldn't be part of ethical concerns. Just that they shouldn't be the overriding factor of ethical concerns.

        While the Internet has thoroughly ruined the Trolley Problem, the Trolley Problem assumes the actor is some sort of coldly rationale Vulcan-wannabe. One of the failings of such thought experiments is that it doesn't take into account what the consequences for the person capable of changing the course of the trolley car would be. Part of the transition from classical to modern utilitarianism is ceasing to treat the actor in such thought exercises as immune to the harm, pain, and suffering they endure during and after the event. Adding that variable to the moral math makes what are more cut and dry utilitarian responses in classical formulations much less so.

        My reasoning for not really abiding by the idea of emotions being too potent in ethical evaluations though, is that they run into the same problem as Kant's universalization principles. Going just on emotions runs afoul of the broad range of human emotions. For myself, I know I don't have the moral repulsion to killing you do.         OK, so the phrase "tikkun olam" roughly translates into "fixing the world." It's a phrase that has morphed significantly in scope and cultural meaning over time, but the fundamental core is that the world is flawed, and we have a duty to work to make it better. To quote a more modern phrasing by Edmond Fleg (a poet and playwright that was actually raised in a traditional Jewish family in Switzerland, but moved to France and lived a much more secular life before reestablishing a connection to Judaism later in life) from "Why am I a Jew?"

        "...
        I am a Jew because the promise of Israel is the universal promise.
        I am a Jew because, for Israel, the world is not completed; we are completing it.
        I am a Jew because, for Israel, humanity is not created; we are creating it.
        I am a Jew because Israel places humanity and its unity above the nations and above Israel itself.​
        ..."

        Though to note this was written in 1927, and "Israel" refers to the Jewish people rather than the modern country of Israel.

        The full poem is a bit longer, and rather poignant, but this section gets to the heart of what matters to my own perspectives. In classical utilitarian thought there's a lot of debate around how to value pleasure/good and pain/harm. Bentham was rather binary about things that very much clashed with most people's lived experiences, while Mill tried to incorporate more subjective merits to things. Bentham would say to destroy a priceless cultural artifact if it meant providing the best materials to shelter humans in need, while Mills argued that the balance of good and harm can accept slightly less optimal living conditions to preserve unique things that can cause good events for ages to come.

        The modern Jewish perspective cuts through a lot of that debate by having this preexisting concept of "fixing" the world. Being in a constant state of improving the world, and not just for our personal selves, or our personal communities, but for everyone, there's a much deeper concept of what to weigh in moral judgements when balancing immediate needs vs. future goals. Fleg's last quoted line is the most vital: the unity of humanity overrides the concept of individual nations and peoples within it. Working to a world where all people are one collective unit is more important than many classical utilitarians could really express, because utilitarianism doesn't really come with that sort of built in end goal to be striving for. It doesn't answer what to life for, just how to live until you find it.

        I wonder what overlap a more modern version would have with therapy.
        There's a lot of what could be perceived as precursors to cognitive behavioral therapy and interpersonal therapy in Jewish social theology. A lot of this comes from Judaism not really caring much about the afterlife (which I think a lot of people find odd considering how much Christianity and Islam do care about it). In Jewish theology, the default state of humanity is to get into the "good" version of the afterlife Only extremely specific and egregious actions can get you banned from that beyond any redemption. As such, there's a strong focus on doing what you can with the life you have. We all end up in the Messianic Era together the same. So a lot of these ritualized socialization are very focusing on the here and now: resolving trauma, developing coping mechanisms, providing tools for less personally disruptive reactions to mental health issues, etc. Judaism doesn't propose people are innately damaged and need fixing or dire results await, just that life breaks people enough you need to make sure they can make it through with hope and dignity so we can arrive together at what comes next.
