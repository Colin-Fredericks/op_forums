                    How often do you really know what a director feels? I've seen plenty of accusations fly at various folks that never seem to really be based in much.

                    Like I don't want to see Tim Burton touch any story that centers around Jewish source material, because he's explicitly stated that if he finds a story interesting, he feels it's better to retell the story without its roots to make the story "for everyone." There's interviews of him responding to criticism about his tendency to perform Jewish erasure as a director (notably in The Corpse Bride and Miss Peregrine's Home for Peculiar Children), so I've seen what he's said knowing he's going to be asked about it, and I find it... unpleasant to say the least.

                    But at the same time, I've seen dozens of online people claim that So-and-So doesn't like [genre/franchise/etc.] because of some out of context quote that really amounted to, "I loved the original material, but I wanted to tell something new." A director is a creative position, so most directors want a chance to be creative, not just regurgitate what's been done in the past. The vast majority of times you can find a director seriously hated a film, is either (a) they had some sort of contract and got roped into a film they didn't really want to be doing or (b) they got fed up with executive notes until they started hating something they came in loving.

                    There's also a weird debate going on about just how much of "fans" directors and other main creatives really should be; esp. when working on existing franchises/remakes/etc. Sometimes a bit of an outsider's perspective is good because most movies aren't made just for existing fans. I appreciate that Henry Cavill was frustrated by The Witcher higher ups deviating from the books, but The Witcher TV show is plenty popular, and there's a need to be flexible in adaptations. Meanwhile you have directors like Kenneth Branagh that's not really a comics or superhero kinda guy, but could draw on his love of classical theater and opera to bring out the grandiose nature of Thor to great acclaim, where Alan Taylor coming off of a lot of work on early Game of Thrones put out the least well received Thor movie so far.

During Obama's presidency, the Tea Party (extremist ideologues) started moving to the front of the Republican Party. When Trump won the nomination, many moderate Republicans left the party or were voted out. These days the more radical extremists are the ones in control of the party, and if you don't win their vote, you don't win at all.

                                            Then, keep in mind that the Republican Party doesn't really have a platform these days. In 2016 and 2020 the Republican platform was literally, "We support anything Donald Trump does." So there's no firm, unifying position on a ton of issues. Having "won" the abortion debate with the overturning of Roe v Wade, and other than the Gun Rights argument, the only thing the Republican Party really has left is its culture war issues and its attack on gay/trans/minorities and the so-called "woke" issues. DeSantis is going to have a hard time standing up to Trump on nearly any issue, so what he's doing is, he's decided he wants to be the spearpoint of the Republican Party in attacking the LGBTQ+ community and other groups. His hope is that he will convince Republican voters that if elected, he will hurt those people more than Trump will, and that they will end up being willing to support him over Trump.

That's why he's going all in on "Don't Say Gay", kidnapping transgender children, etc. And ultimately it doesn't matter to him whether he "beats" Disney or not. Just by going after them, he generates press and shows he's willing to stand up to businesses that support gay and transgender Americans. He believes that that will help him on his path to the Oval Office, regardless of how effective (or ineffective) his attempts against Disney end up being.
