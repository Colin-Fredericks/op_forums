            Originally posted by semicasual View Post
            Two questions for y'all:
            1. Are you (legally) white?
            Technically mixed race, white and Hispanic, but you'd never know it from looking at or interacting with me. My grandmother was from Puerto Rico, but married a well-off white dude and assimilated as much as she could. Her children had no real connection to their cultural heritage on her side, and less so her grandchildren. And my dad's side of the family is as Irish as they come in the US, which pretty much washed out any trace of tan I might have got from my mom.

            Since you ask legally though, I guess yes? Legal forms very rarely have an option that accurately represents me, since they tend to have an option for "White (not Hispanic)" and an option for "Hispanic (not white)", and rarely have an option for mixed race.

            Originally posted by semicasual View Post
            2. Have you ever felt like you didn't have an identity of value because you were "white"?
            I never really thought about race as a part of identity growing up. I didn't feel a lack of identity because of it, it just never occurred to me that race played a role in identity for other people. Which is I think part of the identity crisis TYMITC was talking about. It's pretty typical for white folks to be naive to the very concept of racial identity, which is, I think, where you get ideas like treating everyone the same being equivalent to treating everyone equally.

            I genuinely used to think that being "color blind" was the way to be progressive, that all racism (and other predjudice) came from disliking people because they were different, and that it could only be ended by treating everyone as the same. I didn't want to be treated any differently than anyone else because of something as meaningless as the color of my skin, so it made sense to me that other people wouldn't want to be treated any differently because of the color of their skin. I never considered that others' races might have more meaning to them than the color of their skin. It clicked for me that I was looking at it the wrong way, actually related to sexuality first, and race followed. I had a teacher who was an openly gay man, who recounted a story of a coworker telling him (with good intentions), "I don't see you as a gay man, I see you as a human being," to which his response was "I want you to see me as a gay man, because as a gay man I don't have the same rights that you do as a straight man." (this was before gay marriage was legal in most of the country). Suddenly it dawned on me that differences existed between people, whether I acknowledged them or not, because of factors beyond my control, and that by trying to ignore those differences in the name of "equality" I was being complicit in those factors. And from there it wasn't a stretch to realize that this was true of other groups as well.

This is where you start running into issues with with "reverse racism" where groups begin passing things like racial quotas or what not, or colleges adding points to potential recruits based off of race and many white people say, "Wait a second. All the talk about an equal playing field was hypocritical. Your goal was never fairness and equality. It's just petty vindictiveness, trying to put white people on the bottom for a change."

                                                I think progressives have done a good job convincing many white people that treating people differently because of race or ethnicity is wrong, and many white people would absolutely agree with that.                                                 I think it falls to the people who want these kind of progressive policies to "sell" them and convince white people that, sometimes, giving people advantages because of their race or ethnicity is fair. But so far I think they've done a rather bad job of explaining why, and so many white people end up thinking a lot of these calls for equality are actually just hypocritical double-speak for trying to legalize discrimination against white people.
