    Originally posted by TempleBuilder View Post
    So every killing (unless it was accidental) would be murder.
    ...
    It’s justified by practicality,...
    And here we get to a fundamental problem with classical absolutism. Pretty much the big reason anyone studies Immanuel Kant (also ewwww) is because he astutely observed that absolute moral principles can't have exceptions; not even justifications such as accidents or practicality. Either nuance needs to be built into the absolute principle in the first place, or exceptions are self-refutations of one's own moral principles. As he stated, lying is immoral, and thus it is never justified to lie. If you have taken a victim into your home running from a rampaging murderer, and the murderer comes to your door, Kant argues you cannot morally take the practical way out and lie that the victim is not in your home. If the murderer seeking his next victims asks, you should be truthful, and thus regardless of what transpires next, you acted in accordance with the absolute moral principles.

    There's a reason most people default to a much more utilitarian form of absolutism instead.

    I made it, from my understanding of the world.
    ...
    Morality is not grounded in the real, it’s grounded in the ideal.
    And another classical moral paradox. Moral principles are born from the real world around us, translated into ideals, and then translated back into moral philosophies.

    The resolution to is issue is that how well a moral principle can be translated back into the real world is inherently a criteria for refining moral concepts. As our moral principles do not simply appear from nothingness in our minds, but rather stem from our lived experiences, they are subject to revision as we experience more. One of the prime reasons philosophers debate so much is that is the crucible of refinement of moral philosophies. The more holes poked in a moral philosophy, the more it must be either refined or rejected as non-viable.

    Is it more moral to try for the best outcome? Or more moral to try to avert the worst outcome?
    As someone that largely falls into the utilitarian umbrella, my answer cannot be made without taking reality on the ground into account. The morality of the two options is dependent on the reality of executing them. I, personally, morally have to try to evacuate people as I lack the skills to defuse a bomb, wasting time trying to defuse a bomb instead of saving as many people as I can would be morally absurd for me to do. A person with the skills to defuse the bomb in time would have a different moral calculus, as would someone with such a bad reputation as a hoaxer that would likely cause more people to stay inside than flee the bomb.

    "Try for the best, " vs. "avert the worst," are generally meaningless distinctions to me.

                    I have to make a note that I find it odd to assert that morality is not grounded in the real, given how it's actually pretty easy to see how a good number of moral ideas are rooted in evolutionary instincts.

                    Like, keeping it on the subject of murder, part of the reason why murder is pretty universally regarded as a problem is because human beings, as a animal wired towards socialization because it's evolutionarily advantageous for them to work together, are subsequently wired to have murder of strangers like us be really hard-it's a major hurdle to get over and very consequential to deal with the results of, as a general rule. People in our modern society who have murder of other human beings as part of their expected duties basically have to go through some rough deprogramming/reprogramming in order to be actionable in the event society calls for murder to be done (an ex-friend of mine was once working towards becoming a cop, and at one point in the Hunter game we were having at the time, he described the process like "they're trying to make me a slasher"-a process I assumed was successful to some degree, since then he's become a jail guard, laughed really hard at his own idea of supplying inmates with capsaicin soap, and really couldn't grasp why I was so offended at the idea, and the less I talk about what the military has done to the recognition of humanity in former friends, the happier we will all be). While there's a lot of idealism in ideas of morality and ethics, a lot of it is also just trying to take advantage of what humanity, as animals, was already predisposed to do.

                    Morality and ethics are ideas that are, at it's barest, meant to useful short hand for solving problems* in situations where dealing with the complex mess of factors can delay in producing a useful reaction, and it's most is a model we might use to shape our environment into evolving the species into a more "moral" and "ethical" animal, but in addressing that, it has to be acknowledged that nature operates on spectrums** rather than concrete and distinct notions, and as such it's important to remember than any given structure has it's limits of utility, and can ultimately at the end end up as a hinderance. Maybe that wouldn't be a problem if we were ideal creatures, but as creatures of the real, any idea of how we are to evolve ourselves has to have grounding in our reality.

                    To assert a divorce of morality and ethics from the real is a bit like spinning a bike tire in the air. Sure, you're getting a lot of motion there, but it's not moving anything.

                    *Are you surprised that utilitarian humanism is my primary mode of absurdist confrontation?
                    **Even this is inaccruate, but keeping it simple here.

                            I feel like I also have to briefly mention the utility of contradiction(/"contradiction") in practical philosophy.

                            THis example is gonna fall short, but changing subjects briefly for illustration, I am not a person who particularly believes in free will/free agency, which makes a lot of my commentary seem damnedably weird given how centered on choosing and the operational accountability/consequences of choices it is. An example from Chronicles is that I hold beasts, mages, and particularly hunters as the most monstrous of the splats because of their greater agency to choose and commit to that monstrosity*. How do I maintain a standard of demand for accountability at all if I also correspondingly believe that we're all just protein chains being worked through? THe short answer is accepting that reality in practice can be contradictory, and in this particular case that even if free will doesn't exist, the illusion of free will persists, and if we're all going to be busy lying to ourselves involuntarily like that anyways, we might as well make it play into the pursuit of shaping an environment that will evolve humanity into better forms and dispositions.

                            Bringing it back to murder, I think it's actually pretty fair to call murder morally unjust, and more than being fair, it's very valuable-one of the pervasive problems in history is the willingness to rely on murder as a way to solve problems that correspondingly can't, by the nature of it's willingness, provide neccesary counter-activity to handle it. But as I also mentioned, while the murder in of itself, an action that is murder can also yield many great goods that are not invalidated by that one moral injustice (though they absolutely can be, which is why it pays to start from murder as morally unjust), and a refusal to engage with murder in terms of it's utility is pretty well parallel, if not quite entwined, with the "when good [people] do nothing" in the notion of "Evil will triumph when good [people] do nothing."

                            Like, "Murder's basically always wrong" and "If a unjust society that perpetuates great evil rises because you were unwilling to commit neccesary action toward's it's preventation, the evils of that society are, in part, on your hands" can't be mutually exclusive from each other, because strict adherence to either leaves victims in the gutter either way. The contradiction only demands more accountability, it doesn't cause the whole contradiction to self-implode. Two things seemingly in opposition can true if used appropriately in their contexts.**

                                                Something I appreciate in my own journey of building my moral philosophy, is that I find Jewish theology a lot more practical than people think (though I feel like a lot gets lost in translation).

                                                Judaism, for example, has an interesting solution to the conundrum of soldiers: Jewish morals say that those returning from war must spend a period of separation from normal society before fully returning. This specific transition period is supposed to be spent with other soldiers, and largely overseen by female relatives (that is, mothers, aunts, sisters, etc. not wives).

                                                On one hand this is a form of accountability for going to war War taints your soul with acts of violence, and you don't get to just come back without atoning for that. On the other hand, this structured transition from military to civilian life is something a lot of modern solider would likely appreciate having in place instead of being left to figure it out themselves. It helps that Judaism is both extremely obsessed with ritual purity, but makes impurity and cleansing a very normal activity so it's not really a punishment to adults; it's the spiritual equivalent of hygiene since there's far too much to avoid it all you just get used to regular cleansing. It follows a model much closer to the modern concept of restorative justice rather than punishment: the whole point is to easy the return from war, not to diminish the sacrifice made for the greater whole.

                                                            To cherry-pick a bit less, since one can make a case that Kant wasn't a complete hypocrite, Kant never resolved the dilemma of universalization's ability to apply to mutually contradictory ethical stances. His very weak (or at least what he would have lambasted as such in others and only mildly chided himself over) responses were to shrug his shoulders and say, "well, things are the way they are, which had to have happened for reasons, so we should assume the way things are is the better choice." Basically, Kant's philosophy can't - on a fundamental level - differentiate between, "it is moral to treat all humans as equal," and, "it is moral to establish a power hierarchy based on sex/gender, race, and religion." And his response was to effectively say that if said hierarchies exist and have existed for extended periods, then the better default position is that we can't see why hierarchies that just happen to put people like him at the top are just a natural fact of life than assume that we can make an equitable world (without ever saying an equitable world is impossible, again, he couldn't argue either way directly).

                                                            A lot of people I’ve noticed seem to take a stance of moral mathematicians where the net sum must be positive or at least not negative…maybe moral finances is a better word.
                                                            Utilitarianism is the better word; it's what the man who popularized the concept of "moral calculus" in modern philosophy called philosophy that takes this approach. Though utilitarian thought didn't end with Bentham and Mill. That said, even going back to Mill, utilitarianism doesn't reject your own feelings on these things. Mill calls out the moral calculus itself as a source of harm, because it offers the most moral option possible to us, which exacerbates the impact of seeing the suffering of those we couldn't help. Thus the suffering of taking on the pain of choosing to benefit some at the cost of others needs to be built into a fully thought out utilitarian philosophy.

We need to strike a healthy personal balance in our activities of course, but there's a reason why we value looking back at situations that didn't go to expectations (and especially ones that went horribly wrong) to figure out what happened, and what we can do to make sure that doesn't happen again. What soothes the pain of taking a life for the greater good isn't maintaining the current status of the world, but the desire to never experience that pain again leading to productive changes so that such pains can be minimized if not eliminated from the world.                                                             I think I mostly addressed this above, but I feel like the core issue here is the difference between momentary choices, and broad considered goals. I feel like your catastrophizing a bit by focusing on the extremes of lives being on the line. If you're in a moment of imminent choice - regardless of the stakes - you can only act on the information you have at hand. You can't see the Nth order consequences that will spread from your actions. Worrying about things beyond the scope of the moment are morally paralyzing. Regardless of how events ripple away from an action, we can't live our lives effectively is we see each moment of choice as what makes or breaks the whole world. Taking one life to save ten lives doesn't not permanently concede to a world where taking lives to save lives in necessary. That's why I reject the distinction.
