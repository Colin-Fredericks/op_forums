            A free Bonus Action. Also, stronger criticals, which can push its expected damage value a little higher if the character has any features that interact with criticals, like the Half-Orc’s Savage Attacks or the Champion Fighter’s Improved Criticals.

            I get what you’re saying. It boils down to a math problem instead of a meaningful choice. Personally, I’m ok with that being the case at the baseline. If you don’t have any special features that let you do special stuff with your weapons, then all three options should be about equal, simply being three different ways of outputting about the same amount of damage. That way without any investment, it’s a purely aesthetic choice. The meaningful distinctions should come once you start specializing. That’s my opinion, anyway.
            Its being a cosmetic choice before special abilities kick in is something I can get behind, maybe. It's like some systems like Dungeon World and 13th Age utilize class-based damage dice. That's assuming PF2e actually does have proper special abilities for martial styles. I'm not holding my breath for single-handed duelling or hand-and-half weapons receiving any support.

            Sorry to hear you’re not enjoying it. I know what you mean about auto-pilot though, that’s why I worked so hard to get my Barbarian killed in my online game so I could make a warlock. I’ve just accepted that 5e characters need magic to be interesting. That’s fine, that just means it’s a world where everyone has at least a little magic, and I’m ok with that.
            And I wasn't willing to compromise my concept and pay the "you must be this magical to have fun" tax. Everyone having magic is one thing. Everyone having spells is another.

            Originally posted by Caitiff Primogen View Post
            On the topic of historical weapons and their representation in D&D: I've always wished Monk weapons were more rewarding to use. Not "better" per se, but as someone who has trained in and studied Kobudo (mostly sai, tonfa, and hanbo, but also some kama, nunchaku, and eku) there's a richness in those weapons, a uniqueness and character, that really isn't communicated by either their stats or the existing feat trees.

                Originally posted by Caitiff Primogen View Post
                Seconded. I have never understood the preoccupation with Forgotten Realms, charitably the mostly almost third least not alright I guess of the D&D settings.

                I get that they've been cranking out those novels since the earth's crust was still cooling and apparently a lot of people think they were pretty nifty, but I've always looked at FR and felt like it was what would happen if someone said "Lets write the taste of vanilla wafers for three decades. Mmmmm vanilla wafers. Thirty years of vanilla wafers forever..."
                Well, some people like good 'ol faux-medieval heroic high fantasy and gonzoness.

                Also, yes, the novels. Novels are a great way to strengthen fan buy-in to the setting and not constrained solely to the rpg-players.

                That's why a lot of FR fans hated the 4e FR. Not because it was a bad setting, but because killing off the majority of characters and blowing up half of the setting they liked was just atrocious, a spit in the face. I'm saying that as someone who enjoyed the Farideh novels quite a lot.

                I get that for a considerable ammount of roleplayers, FR was uninteresting and done to death. But as a general fan? I wanted to read more about Arylin and Danilo, dammit. That's always the case with detailed worlds and metaplot, it was the same with WoD too. A significant portion of roleplayers, who want to do their own thing hate it, because it's constraining, while a lot of people buys into it, precisely because there is a detailed, "feeling alive" setting and a story (or stories) with cool characters that could catch their eyes.

                Because let's face it, Drizzt and c. made FR the prominent setting of D&D. Drizzt was cool, as a teenager.

                Also, about how "uninteresting" and "vanilla" FR is. FR, IMO mostly feels vanilla, because it defined the whole heroic high fantasy kitchen sink setting style for a long-long time and simultaneously made it the most prominent style of roleplaying. I'm not blaming Lord of the Rings for being vanilla low-magic fantasy. I'm not blaming Ravenloft for being vanilla horror/dark fantasy.

                Besides, FR definitely has a distinct "FR-flavor", in my eyes, the sum of it, the acumulated history. The words "Icewind Dale", or "Menzoberranzan", or "Waterdeep" have weight. Funnily enough, I never got that distinct feel from any of the 5e books. It pains, but they've really made FR into the totally generic indistinct high fantasy background wallflower setting people accused it was for a long time, but I never felt it justified. Now, I do and it pains.

                For f's sake, they aren't even putting the FR logo on anything anymore...

                                                            Originally posted by PMárk View Post
                                                            Yes, there is a large ammount of people, who doesn't like it, precisely because of that. Because It works with tropes they've seen a thousand times and because it's not as open-ended. Those people will always like Eberron a lot more and I understand that.
                                                            Yeah, rather than calling the Forgotten Realms "generic" I'd call it iconic of archetypical. In many respects it was the fantasy RPG setting that other fantasy RPG settings strove to differentiate themselves from - in a lot of ways it was the fantasy RPG setting. Many RPG writers and players were familiar with it. Even people who had never played tabletop RPGs might know about it due to various high profile video games and other forms of mass media where it showed up in.

                                                            As a result, often times new settings would inevitably find themselves compared to the Forgotten Realms, and the creator would feel compelled to come up with a twist in order to make their setting feel distinct.

                                                            I think the Forgotten Realms had unique ideas, but many of them ended up getting pillaged and brought into other games because they were popular (like the Drow), which means those elements don't really feel uniquely Forgotten Realms anymore. And other elements (like Maztica) were literally removed from the setting - which was a shame, since I thought having an area dealing with the horrors of colonialism was pretty interesting. If we're talking about generic fantasy D&D settings there are others like Greyhawk which might be more deserving of that appellation.
