    Okay, Charlaquin, I won't quote and answer specific thoughts of you this time, forgive me, I'm a bit low on energy! However, I'd like to add some further thoughts to the discussion, so:

    About the notion of the design rationale behind some classes are "some nebolous feel, with cobbled toghether mechanics". I don't see it as that. I'd say, the goal is to pick a quite clear thematic concept and back it up with mechanics. Simple as that.

    About the branching class system concept and the meaning of "class". As I gathered from your posts, you'd like to see classes as quasi super-classes, clear and distinct categories of differently focused character options, and delegating the more "niche" concepts into subclasses and feats, etc, yes?

    Now, I'm not saying it couldn't work, it certainly could. However, I think there are some things that should be taken into consideration:

    1. What AnubisXy said, it actually could make the building a more niche concept harder.

    2. There is the question of how determinative you want to make the core class?

    - you could go with the 5e way, where the core class is making up the majority of the character. It's okay, but some people want more depth with a concept, more options within it.

    - or, you could go with the PF archetypes' concept, where a subclass could essentially re-write the whole class. It's good as an option for experienced players, but I wouldn't like it as the only option to give to a new player if they want to play a more niche character concept.

    3. Also, what do you do with the various hybrid classes? Take the ranger. It's one-third fighter, one-third rogue, one-third magic-user. What core class would you put it into? Or the magus. It's literally a half-fighter-half-wizard. The choice seems arbitrary.

    4. If subclasses could modify the core class to the level of distinction we're seeing in the classes now, what is exactly the goal of having core classes? Just to satisfy a desire for categorization?

    For example, let's make a core class, the magic user. We're instantly hitting the problem of having divine/arcane. Do we make the two, very different, very distinct power sources subclasses? If yes, what the core class will provide, which is common in all the primary casters, that could justify the core class mechanically?

    Or, okay, let's make divine/arcane two separate core classes. Take the druid as a concept. As a concept, it's quite distinct and recognizable, but it's essentially a divine caster, so let's make it a subclass of the "divine caster" core class. What the Druid has with the cleric in common? Pretty few things. BAB, the spell progression table, some spells. It has 20 level of unique features and it's own spell list on the other hand. So, the core class would either do very, very little in terms of mechanics, to keep it flexible enough for that wide of disparity between the character concepts, or the subclasses would have to have the ability to almost completely re-write the core class. At this point, again, what is the meaning of having a core super-class, besides arbitrary categorization (not like that couldn't be a reaon enough, mind you)?

    Don't get me wrong, a system, where you're starting from a very, very broad category, like fighter/expert/magic user and then specialize is a totally workable system and yes, I'd agree an elegant one. I'm not against a system like that. I think the Warhammer fantasy rpg done something similar, but I never played it, sadly. However, it begs the question: what if I want to play a more niche concept from the get-go? What if I want to play a ranger, from level 1, not just a fighter aspiring to become a ranger one day? And when I'm becoming a ranger, would I get the option of retraining all my previous choices (skills, feats, etc.), or am I tied to all the stuff I picked up as a fighter to that point? In the later case, we arrived again to the 5e situation, where the more niche concepts don't get as much mechanical depth as the core ones, and/or they're becoming something like the prestige classes.

    In a nutshell, if you want to play a half fighter/half magic user, or a lightly armored, magic-using wodland expert-warrior-hunter, especially from level 1, the most straightforward way of making that possible is having a magus and a ranger class. As subclasses they either wouldn't be deep enough, with lots of options, or they would modify the core class to the point of essentially making it a totally different new thing and at this point, why just don't have different classes, besides categorization (which would be arbitrary in several cases)?


    All that said, I'd concede that some classes are really could be a matter of argument as their justification of existence goes. Case in point, the gunslinger. It's really just a lightly armored fighter with guns. What made it as a standalone class justifiable is the grit mechanic that was introduced with it, that made it distinct. I'm not sure I'd like to introduce that profound of changes into a class with just a subclass. Imagine making the sorcerer into a wizard subclass, or try to make an arcane magic-user core class that could have some mechanical commonality for the wizard and the sorcerer and making them into subclasses of that. What would the core class provide, because those two are working along totally different lines from the start?

    If nothing worked, then let's think!

                                For me a class is a package of specific skills and abilities that allow you to perform a "role" (or to put it another way, allow you to have a specific method of solving problems) within a game.

                                            That's more or less how I see it as well. To me, there are many valid answers to "what is class". The important thing is that there is one, consistent answer, and every class more or less sticks to it. Fighter, ranger and cleric sure as heck don't operate on the same level. And it's clear Paizo isn't interested in even asking the question.

                                            Rangers being a separate class where I think they don't need it or deserve it is just one aspect of it that I thought about recently, after reading a discussion about how 5E rangers are or aren't underpowered. But it appears suggesting that it maybe shouldn't be a full class is enough to get people to jump down my throat. So I think I'm done with it. I'll just cross-post my opinion from a thread that made me come to this realization:

                                            The ranger class has had serious identity issues for a while now. The archetype of a wilderness survivalist and hunter of beasts is, of course, strong... but hardly requires its own class. There's nothing that defines the ranger class that goes beyond the first five levels. It can track, survive in the wilds and scout... which is the kind of thing skills, backgrounds, feats and maybe subclasses can cover. So the core ranger features end up really front-loaded, and then it's just a mish-mash of combat and non-combat abilities.

                                            Now, front-loading the iconic abilities of a class into the first levels and then giving out more generic ones isn't a bad idea... but it's not what 5E went with, since it stuck to the old level progression.

                                            The focus on the wilderness is also pretty problematic if you happen to run a more urban, sedentary, political etc. adventure. A lot of the ranger's iconic abilities will sit unused in such a case. Now, those situations are unavoidable. But it's one thing when you willingly build a character with a very specialized skillset, and another when a whole class is devoted to it. Druid is also nature-themed, but it has shapeshifting and spells, both of which make it far more versatile.

                                            There's a reason that in video games, a ranger class is often built around its animal companion. Because "has a badass pet" is something you can actually work with.
