    Originally posted by Knightingale View Post

    I think it has impacted her creative output as well at this point. She has this crime novel series that she writes under the pseudonym Robert Galbraith and the last two novels are... certainly something. "Troubled Blood" was all about a male serial killer dressing up as a woman to lure in victims. Meanwhile, here latest "Ink Black Heart" was about a creator of a series who was being harrassed online (for being anitsemitic, racist, ableist etc.) - and then killed (and JK Rowling claims this character is not a stand-in for herself, of course... but I mean, come on...). Here where things get really weird, though, because, despite this novel just being a bogstandard whodunnit, it has 1024 pages. And the reason for that is the novel being really obsessed with Twitter. There are entire chapters just being this stylized depictions of arguments on Twitter, people making Twitter threads and then when that isn't happening people are discussing what's said in these Twitter posts. And to make matters more annoying: No character is using their real name for their Twitter account and a central element of the mystery is about who's in control of what Twitter account at what time and who is responsible for posting something. It's boring but it's also weirdly obsessive when it comes to Twitter.
    The Cormoran Strike series started off as thinly veiled commentary posing as a noir-leaning detective series and has slowly been destroying more and more of it's veil as it all goes along, and if you thought you could see dearest JK's bigotry in some of her more out there choices in Potter but aren't sure if it's too much or too far, rest assured you will understand that she hates poor people, fat people, and non-British people in short order by reading Strike.

    One of the hard things to wrap your head around as you get older and particularly as you get into the craft of writing and storytelling is that it's entirely possible to make something that is thoroughly enjoyable and impactful while still having that product be of bad quality, and dearest JK is a very vivid example of that. She's not really that good of a writer, though she grasps the essentials better than others, and she mostly makes up for it by moving a lot of things that make an impression on you, be it a bit of setting detail, a strong character portrait (not quite the same as a strong character, mind), a clever call back, and the like in a rather succinct order-calling it the narrative equivalent of jangling keys is a lttle too dismissive of her skills, but it draws up to it.

    The Cormoran Strike Series is revelatory for her skill because she can't fall back on her imagination for novelty in a story grounded in the real world, and those strong character portraits are realized as characatures because they don't have the benefit of the fantasy setting softening and whimsifying the stereotypes, and while her perchance for setting things up and paying them off remains, her brisk pace makes it a lot more apparent she's trying to skirt past how flimsy any given explanation can be, where by the time you get to the end the only way it works is if you just went with the flow and accepted it.

    And like a lot of people like her, the more she's got to grind in her gears, the more the gears are going to be ground in her books. This also happened with Potter, but again the genre allows for some divide from that, where Cormoran...well, grinding her gears is kind of really all she's got.

                                                            Since there's been a lot of razzing, I do want to say...not a good thing about it, but still get out a disappointment: There's a lot that's frustrating about The Strike Series, but perhaps the most frustrating is that the theory for them being good is there. Dearest JK is a little too forward with her foreshadowing to be as strong as she can be with the mystery genre, but her solid sense of pacing and commitment to setup and payoff(for the most part) is a good cornerstone for the genre, and while she's not so great at hiding the conclusion, she's at least good enough to get people to consider the alternate possibilities. All of that is more than can be said about a fair number of writers in the genre today, and if she could just keep focus on the story as a story to tell, she could be....not one of the greats, but reliably a staple writer for the genre, the sort of person where if you don't know what to listen to on a road trip, you could get one of her books and have a reliably entertaining enough ride.

                                                            And, like, so many writers wish they could be reliably just-above mediocrity in a genre like that. And she is instead blowing it on this instead.

                                                            Honestly, what she needs to do, asides from stop being TERF, a classist, and a nationalist, is to write the YA actual detective version of Harry Dresden.
