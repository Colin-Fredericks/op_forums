                                                            I decided to go and read some things by the defenders of the OSA to even see if there were some points. I actually found an op-ed by someone that work(ed?) for one of the UK agencies that did a lot of the drafting of it, and he did manage to make at least some points that are worth considering. Not enough to change my mind about the OSA (and in many ways his overall arguments made my opinion of it worse), but he at least did make a good case on the balancing act of giving the government flexibility to address problems as the Internet changes (something lots of people have been complaining about for decades is how far behind our governments constantly are regarding digital regulations) with protecting personal liberties.

                                                            For me, the biggest problem with the OSA is that it basically treats everyone on the Internet as an international megacorp with billions sitting around waiting to be paid to executives that could easily absorb the impact of increased regulations. The law has zero considerations for non-profits and small businesses that simply cannot afford to pay third party verification systems; even more so if they want to pay for quality ones that have decent security of people's data unlike the big tech companies that have no motives to use anything but the cheapest vendor that's good enough. What about a 18 year old university student that relies on a school provided computer? That computer is technically the institution's property, which means all the data to verify the student's age is now suddenly also the school's property... or the student is barred from legally protected activities by a law that doesn't provide any outs.

                                                            I really don't think much thought was given into how this is just going to consolidate power over the Internet even more into the hands of the companies that already have too much power over something that is ever increasingly a necessity of life in many countries; not just a perk for those that can afford it. You can make as much of a case as you want about how you're going to make the companies take responsibility for moderating content.... but if your legal schema kills off all of their competitors over time, they can just come back and lobby to deregulate the Internet, or there isn't going to be an Internet for countries that don't comply.

                                                            Data breaches suck... but that's already a constant problem. The influence Jeff Bezos already has over the Internet via AWS is already a problem too, but it's going to get even worse if he can force even more smaller companies to tie themselves into Amazon's ecosystem by making it the only affordable user-verification platform around.
