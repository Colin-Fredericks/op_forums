Please be respectful to your fellow posters opinions and we're all here to help ourselves be understood and understand others better, and because we all deeply care about this subject.

          There were seven in the last week... alone. Which I'd consider more interesting from a (lay person) legal stand point are probably not the biggest decisions in the public's view.

          The PPACA ruling isn't really legally interesting. It's the ruling that should have been reached based on precedent alone. Technical flaws in a law are supposed to be fixed by the legislature passing technical fix bills; not SCOTUS overturning laws over minor drafting errors that do not represent the intention of the law.

          The FHA ruling's not one I see a lot of press about, but it's more interesting from a legal perspective. I'm not sure you can find much overreach in the decision, but it could be a significant ruling for future discrimination cases as it (in a very limited fashion) was a discrimination case based more in institutionalized discrimination rather than direct discrimination.

          The ACCA ruling is probably flying under most radars. 8-1 decisions generally do. Not seeing much of an issue here, as I think SCOTUS is definitely doing its job by ruling that the government shouldn't be increasing criminal penalties through vaguely worded laws. If there's going to be an increase in severity of punishment - especially based on past criminal activity - you need to be specific in what those past activities are in the statues.

          I'm sort of assuming the intent of the thread is the Obergefell ruling on gay marriage. So I'll get back to that.

          The EPA case feels pretty "meh" but that's more on the limitations that have been put on the EPA by the law. The Court's ruling is pretty sound. It's stupid that the EPA can't regulate highly toxic chemicals being pumped into the air because it will cost power plants money. There's no real Constitutional/judicial issue here, there's just a stupid amount of hamstringing of the EPA by corporate interests; even if I'm sympathetic to the dissenting opinion. It's a ridiculous standard that because cost wasn't considered at one point, its consideration later doesn't matter. But cross your T's and dot your I's seems to be the lesson here.

          The ARIC decision was pretty standard (though the fact that it was 5-4 is always a bit worrying). The Court upheld the lower courts. There's not much overreach in, "suck it up, that's what your state law says." Upholding the Arizona legislature's standing to sue is right, because there are going to be potential conflicts between its power and the power of bodies created by ballot initiative that they should be able to seek redress in the courts. They're just wrong on this one.

          The midazolam ruling is fairly worrying to me. The idea that Cruel and Unusual punishment protections have to be weighed against practical alternatives seems a bit dodgy. The Constitution isn't exactly vague on this. Midazolam's big medical selling point is that it usually takes patients less time to come out of its sedative effects than similar drugs, which is not what you want with something that's supposed to make sure you don't wake up and feel the lethal drugs that are going to kill you (and will be very painful if you do). Putting the burden of proof on the people facing punishment that not only is there risk of cruelty (and frankly, they shouldn't have to prove a high risk here), but that they have to show there's a less cruel option available to the state seems very backwards for a Constitutional protection. If the state can't acquire a lethal injection cocktail that solidly meets the set criteria for not being cruel, those being punished shouldn't lose their 8th Amendment rights and be forced to risk a cocktail that has the potential to not live up to the criteria the courts already established.

          ------

          So, yeah, despite what I'd say are the more interesting legal issues with the lethal injection ruling, we're probably talking gay marriage.

          The legal mechanics? I don't really see what the debate on that is. The opinion of the court made a solid case for why this is not judicial overreach. The precedent for the status of marriage as a right was established, as was its protections in the Constitution. The changes in the laws around marriage in the past were established. Is it a bit of judicial activism? Probably. But you could say the same about Loving. There's no real solid counter-argument in the dissents that if the Court can say, "you have to let interracial couples get married even if your state defines marriage as a segregated institution," it can't also say, "you have to let homosexual couples get married even if your state defines marriage as a heterosexual institution."

          Even with disagreement on that, the Court ruling that a valid marriage certificate has to be honored even if you couldn't get it where you are currently located is pretty much impossible to demonstrate a good case against. Anyone's rights as a married couple should not be jeopardy just because they cross a state line.

                  +1. This is the essence of "Full Faith and Credit" where a "final judgment" of one state has to be given "full faith and credit" (that is, fully respected as legitimate law) in every other state. That's one facet of the Obergefell case, but I want to talk about something else about Obergefell.

                  First off, Okami, thanks for starting this thread. Yeah, I think there are alot of people on this forum who want to talk about this, myself included, and passions over this are running high, not just in this forum but all over the country. But that is perfectly proper: this ruling over gay marriage is indeed, to quote Vice President Biden, a big fuckin' deal.

                  A bit of background for me: I am myself a lawyer (albeit a young(ish) and inexperienced one) so I know how to read cases. I imagine that there are a few other lawyers here on this forum and I eagerly await their informed opinions on this topic as well. Also, I haven't finished reading the Obergefell case as I'm doing my own personal research on the Hobby Lobby case (which was the "corporate religious rights" case decided last year). So far I've only read Scalia's dissent and am partly done reading Alito's dissent in the Obergefell case. I still have to finish reading the rest of the case, though I've seen parts of Kennedy's opinion quoted here and there as I've spent the last week (in some shadenfreudian glee, admittedly) reading up on all the political reactions to the gay marriage ruling.

                  As some have noted, the dissents in Obergefell were, shall we say, rather heated. Scalia's dissent in particular...well, it is in no way (IMHO) an exaggeration to say that his dissent reads like a transcript of a temper tantrum performed by a 79-year old. Scalia has been bloviating about "the gay agenda" at least since Lawrence v. Texas (the Texas sodomy ban case) as if the gay agenda was about anything but trying to secure equal rights for American gays and lesbians. Indeed, Scalia even predicted that gay marriage would become inevitable as if it was a bad thing. He can hardly be described as impartial or emotionally detached on the subject.

                  As for this case being "judicial overreach," that is just "jiggery-pokery," to borrow Scalia's words. Kennedy based his reasoning on Due Process and Equal Protection in the 14th Amendment. This requires that all government officials, including those public servants who issue marriage licenses, have to treat everyone who walks into their office equally. Full stop. One reason people are saying that the Obergefell decision is overreach is because when the 14th Am was enacted, no one who was alive then even remotely considered the possibility that equal protection would require protecting same-sex marriage. That is true, of course: I doubt Abraham Lincoln or any of his contemporaries even thought about gay marriage. But, to my mind and to the thinking of many other legal scholars, the intent of the drafters of the 14th Am is irrelevant.

                  (Note to the readers: I use the terms "gay marriage" and "same-sex marriage" interchangeably here. I'm told that there might be a distinction but I am unaware of the specifics. If anyone knows what this distinction might be, assuming there is one, please let us know.)

                  The heart of this dispute in the gay marriage case is between what is known as "originalism," (which says that judges have to consider what the Founding Fathers thought and intended when they drafted the Constitution and that should end the dispute) and the doctrine of the "living Constitution" (which says that present-day understandings of changing social mores have to be taken into account when interpreting the Constitution to make certain that minority rights are not trampled on by "the tyranny of the majority.")

                  For those of you who are not legal scholars, let me tell you about a precedent case that the Supreme Court of the United States (SCOTUS) decided in 1967, namely the Loving v Virginia case. Mr. and Mrs Loving were an interracial couple that married in DC but moved into Virginia for a new job. Virginia (along with a dozen or so other states) had a ban on interracial marriages, thus making the Lovings' marriage null and void and denying them state marriage benefits. The Lovings sued, of course, and SCOTUS decided (9-0, incidentally) that since marriage was a fundamental constitutional right entitled to equal protection, Virginia (as well as all other 49 states) was required to treat interracial married couples equally with single-race marriages. Needless to say, more than a few people were up in arms, declaring the ruling improper because states had an interest to ban interracial marriages to prevent "miscegenation" or the "mixing of the races." It was a crap argument then, just as arguments claiming that gay marriage needs to be banned to promote procreation in straight marriages are crap now.

                  In Loving, people made the argument that the enactors and ratifiers of the 14th Am never intended to promote miscegenation. That was deemed utterly irrelevant by SCOTUS because the (then-in-1967) present understanding of equal protection was that marriage was a right that applied to all couples regardless of their skin color, even though when the 14th Am was enacted in 1868, no one intended to allow the 14th Am to mean that interracial couples could get married.

                  The Obergefell reasoning works the same way as in Loving. A new understanding of equal protection today demands that gay marriages have to be respected equally with straight marriages. A majority of Amercian society (62% in the latest poll) now approve of gay marriage. To deny legitimacy any longer to gay couples who want to get married is now, as a result of society's new understanding and acceptance of gay people, the definition of discrimination and unequal treatment. What Kennedy says in his ruling is that the law has to catch up to society's new understanding of gay people. If a majority of Americans think that not letting gay people get married is unfair, then the law, to preserve fairness in American society, has to treat gay people the same as everyone else.

                  This is the essence of the "living Constitution." As society evolves, so must the law, or else the law isn't just. Needless to say, conservatives who wish to preserve the status quo for whatever reason do not hold to the idea of a living Constitution. They would prefer that changes in society would happen through voting and legislative action. That's all well and good and even desirable in some cases, but whenever there is a fundamental Constitutional right being violated, then SCOTUS is required to act and act immediately. The court's job is to redress violations of rights and simply waiting for the democratic process to move forward simply takes too long to fix those violations. Kennedy and the rest of the Obergefell majority had no choice but to act now. Since marriage is a fundamental Constitutional right (and there is no bigger or more important right in America than a fundamental right) and Mr. Obergefell's right to marriage was being violated by being discriminated against just because he was gay, letting the democratic process settle this just wasn't an option.

                  So yeah, this case makes a big-ass precedent that "originalism" is, if not a dead doctrine, then it's on its deathbed. I think this is what is causing a bunch of the temper tantrums about "judicial overreach" on the right side of the political spectrum.

                  There's more to this case, but this post should be sufficient to sum it up in a big, hairy, Gurahl-sized nutshell.
