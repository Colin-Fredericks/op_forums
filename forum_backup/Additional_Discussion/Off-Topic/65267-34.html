                            Dr. Doom is an egotistical tyrant who believes his way is the only way. He has never asked for help without an ulterior motive that usually leaves those who help him suffering while he comes out ahead. I can't think of a good example of it occurring with Iron Man, I don't read the character's solo stuff, but Dr. Strange and Dr Doom's Triumph and Torment depicted this very nicely, as did the Dr. Doom and the Masters of Evil comic. Stark even refers to a time when they went back in time and Doom abandoned Stark there.

                            Add to that the fact that he's generally considered one of the smartest villains in comics, as well as having technological expertise comparable to Tony Stark, and magical expertise comparable to Dr. Strange, he's very much a threat. I can't even think of any actual appearances of him near others while choosing not to wear his armor, or at least his mask, which is also comparable if not more powerful than Tony Stark's Iron Man armor, so Stark's disbelief that the nicely dressed man asking for help is actually Dr. Doom is understandable.

                            It's probably worth noting that while he's had many defeats and been overcome in many individual comics, writers tend to handwave those and claim, 'It was just a Doombot,' referring to the robots Dr. Doom occasionally sends in his stead, which could never match his level of genius, cunning and power.

                            Odds are there is going to be a flashback or monologue that will explain it all.

                                JLA seems to be taking off.
                                First Batman becomes Bat-God, and now the rest are following. Superman has become the God of Strength, Batman the God of Knowledge, Flash the God of Death, having merged with the Black Racer, and Shazam the God of Gods. Lex Luthor, left to languish on Apokalips, has become the God of Apokalips.
                                I'm not a fan of Johns' writing and his run on Justice League hasn't changed that. Like a lot of Johns' work, there's a lot of big and flashy events but they don't have much of an impact or depth. If you aren't a fan of everything Johns is a fan of, they're pretty dull. He has a tendency to write around the League to get to the characters he really wants to write about. There's an entire issue where the only Leaguer is Cyborg who only appears for a few pages, standing in the darkness waiting for someone else to tell him a story.

                                Gotham Academy is good as usual. I worry it's going to be hijacked by events but considering the characters and who and what they are, that doesn't have to be a bad thing. When some supervillain devastates Gotham, through the teenage students at the Academy and their teachers you can see the impact it has on the normal citizenry. The main protagonist, Olive, is very antagonistic towards the concept of the Dark Knight, and from her perspective it's easy to see why. But the book has really evolved to a more ensemble cast, and her best friend Maps is enamored with all things Bat-related. Even, perhaps, his son. This weeks issue the characters, having formally acknowledged themselves as 'teenage detectives,' of a sort, use the cover of an athletics competition to escape from their chaperones and do some field research, digging into the city hall of records. I've previously mentioned that Batman isn't a major character in this, and it's true, he doesn't actually show up much, but in this issue Red Robin, Tim Drake, does run into them while doing his own research for the Robin War.

                                Batman & Robin Eternal is solid, but it would be nice to have more answers. Almost everyone's together now, with Harper Row, Stephanie Brown and Cassandra Cain all at the Batcave with Dick Grayson, Jason Todd and Tim Drake, trying to dissect the mystery brought up by the previous comics. It's interesting to see their interactions, but they are pretty invested in the strange confession of Batman and their rising suspicions of how it ties into the latest threat to Gotham so they aren't exactly soap opera-ing yet. Considering how Batman Eternal came out, I expect more interesting scenes of bonding and rivalries blooming.

                                                            Oh man, I LOVE Doom. He's just so bombastic and over the top. While I like the Fantastic Four in theory just fine (and Kirby's early work on the series was stellar), the villains clearly outstrip the Four. I can't speak much to the context of his appearances in Iron Man (I've only seen some preview materials and am not a big Bendis fan. He tends to twist characters in inorganic ways to match his very narrow style) or recent events in the character's life (the recent big Marvel event, Secret Wars, got postponed, so there's a mystery of how that ended and how that gap effects the current comics), I can provide some context for his background.

                                                            Doom is like a big dumb Bond villain. He's like Marvel's version of Dracula, sharply dressed and always screaming "FOOLS!" at his opponents. The central conceit is that he's so full of pride that he can't accept his own failures. He blames Reed Richards for his facial scars, and Richards has become sort of his white whale. Doom is a fierce egotist and blames Richards' meddling for most if not all of his failures. There's been strong hints throughout recent runs of the series that he's covetous of Reed's family. For the past few years, the Fantastic Four has largely been under the domain of writer Jonathan Hickman. He was at the center of the last event, where the infinite realities were facing imminent destruction, and Doom saved the few remaining worlds by cobbling them together into a strange patchwork planet where he ruled as god. In this rewritten reality he basically assumed Reed's role, taking his wife and child as his own. I think the implication (not fully realized since the event hasn't ended), is that having a family has changed Doom for the better. He understands the need for compassion outside of his own self interest, and Reed and Susan Richards are missing from this world. There seems to be a thread similar to that in Superman comics where, had Superman never existed, Lex Luthor would have been the savior of mankind.

                                                            As for Latveria, it's a fascist Eastern European state ruled by an army of killer robots designed to look like Doctor Doom. It's exactly as delightfully goofy as it sounds, and it's pretty representative of everything about the character. That said, they seem to be moving away from that direction, which strikes me as a bit of a shame.
