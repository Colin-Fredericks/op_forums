    Majority of U.S High Schools aren't as bad as it might seemed

    04-29-2017, 09:49 PM
    A little background about myself. I am a first generation Chinese American that was born to immigrant parents. We didn't have much so I pretty much grew up in poverty. My parents could barely speak any English which made it a lot tougher to get better paying jobs than the ones they had. Ex: I didn't get a cell phone until my junior year of High School.

    The High School that I went to wasn't the best and I witnessed things that I found was pretty disturbing. There were quite a lot of disciplinary problems with the students. Teachers often have to use delicate class time to discipline students because there were so many students were just messing around instead of paying attention.

    I was kind of quiet. I rarely gave my teachers any trouble and I always tried to do the best I can. Every day after school I would try my best to focus on my studies. I dedicated around 1-2 hours studying everyday after school. Even if there was no homework, I would review the lesson that was taught and lessons that were going to be taught. More time would be spent studying if there was going to be an exam the next day. This means doing my homework and reviewing future chapters that were going to be taught in class. This made me a lot more prepared.

    Many students half-a** their homework and rarely allocated much time for their studies. I maintained a 3.8 GPA throughout High School. I wasn't naturally bright or well-off. I was struggling like many other students yet I accomplish much better academically than those students.

    That's why I would say the education system in the U.S isn't as bad as most people think it is. I really think the entirely of the blame shouldn't shift to the education system but it should be at least 50-50 with the students on the other side.

          Oh boy...

          First off, lets be clear. There's no single problem with the US public school system. There are lots of issues, and most of them are impossible to simply cut out like a tumor. Everything is interconnected into a Gordian knot of issues that takes an obnoxious amount of effort and work to address fully (which is basically why it never happens and things consistently slide to the worst).

          Blaming the kids though, is pretty much the most useless thing to do. Teenagers especially are pretty much in the worst stage of their biological development for traditional education models. Regardless of relative intelligence (however you want to define it) being good at high school is largely a genetic crap shoot of whether or not you luck out with the right order of development and personality that meshes with traditional education. And that's before you even get into socioeconomic issues (both personal to the family, and the community at large).

          Parents... yeah, parents definitely have some blame going on. Of course, part of a parents role is to advocate for their child. Expecting them to do less is silly, if not insulting. Most parents also don't unquestionably side with their kids, the side with their idealized version of who their kids are. That's really the core to most issues with parents. Sometimes you get what AnubisXy was saying. Sometimes you get parents that wonder why the teachers are wasting their time with their "screw up" kid. There's also an issue of engagement. Some parents are really engaged with their kid's education, and some aren't. A lot try to be without learning how to do so effectively, because as a society we just sort of expect parents to know that even though it's not that simple.

          Then there's all the institutional crap. The School Boards vs. the State DoE vs the Federal DoE. A complete lack of unified educational philosophy, sluggish implementation of new research in education, and failure to make proper use of the charter school system to provide for students that need different learning environments (made worse by the wild and random charter standards by state).

          Then there's things like how we fund public schools via real estate taxes, ensuring the poorer areas have less educational resources than wealthier ones, and higher level funding doesn't actually get used to even this out (and focuses on means testing over getting annoyed a schools that use external funding to justify using their normal funding on non-academic spending).

                                      It's not so much that. Rather, remember that funding to public schools is determined by a schools pass/fail rate. And then remember that the administration of a school or district gets compensation for meeting the government mandated pass/fail rate and, conversely, if a school does not meet that standard the district could lose funding or worse, the government could step in and fire every single person working at that school including the janitors and lunch ladies - this actually happens.

                                      This means that there is massive pressure from the administration for teachers to pass students, even if those students need to be held back, because the principal and district leaders want their fat year-end monetary bonus so they can buy a new car or whatever, and they don't want their schools to go below standards. Teachers who choose to fail a student anyway often have to spend a lot of time justifying doing so. And if a teacher fails too many students, then the teacher is held accountable and will often find herself threatened with a loss of job.

                                      How this often plays out is, teachers are given an unofficial limit on how many students they're allowed to fail. If a teacher fails more students than that, then the teacher ends up facing some kind of retribution - poor marks on evaluations with the threat of being fired the next year if they don't "make sure more students pass" is a common one. Teachers who don't learn to play ball (or who refuse to promote students who don't deserve it) don't end up working in the profession for every long.

                                                  Originally posted by Volke View Post

                                                  The education system in America started changing big time after the Supreme Court ruled that "tracking" students was unconstitutional. Prior to that (mainly back during the Boomer generation) schools would track different students into different areas. Those whom the schools felt would be able to go to college were tracked into pre-college classes. Those who weren't, were given vocational studies.

                                                  Which might not seem like a bad idea on the surface, but what was actually happening was that minorities would be primarily be tracked into vocational classes while the college tracks were almost entirely filled with white kids. The goal, of course, was to keep minorities out of college. Which was racist as fuck and why the Supreme Court ruled it was unconstitutional.

                                                  But after they were told they couldn't do that anymore, schools didn't really know what to do, so they just put all students on the "same" track - the college one. Unfortunately at this point we ended up with the "one-size fits all, everyone has to meet the exact same standards" education system. And one size doesn't fit everyone, so you started having more and more students dropping out of school because they couldn't handle the material. In turn, this crisis made the government and the American people feel that something needed to be done. But nobody can really agree on what to do or how to do it. So they started pushing "teacher accountability," with the goal of holding teachers accountable if students couldn't pass.

                                                  But this runs into its own problems of course, more teachers leave the profession, and this means class sizes keep getting larger, and with bigger classes teachers have less time to devote to individual students, so they end up just promoting kids to the next grade level who shouldn't be moving up.



                                                  What America needs to do is collectively decide on what the goal of the public education system should be, but that's simply not going to happen. It's too big of a subject and there's no easy answers. And worst of all, the big thing right now is vouchers, which are a terrible idea.

                                                  Private schools don't have to follow of the same rules that public schools do. If a student misbehaves at a private school, they just throw that student out. If a student can't hack the material, they just throw him out. This means that private schools often have better statistics than public schools, because if anyone can't meet their standards, they're not in school anymore. Public schools meanwhile, have to take everyone and do their best to educate those students.

                                                  So what's going to happen is, the good students who can perform well are going to end up in private schools. The underperforming students who can't won't be accepted into those private schools and will remain in public schools. Which means, with fewer high performing students, the overall statistics of public schools will drop even further. Basically the public schools will be left with the "dregs" (though I hate using that term) and worse, without those hardworking kids to set an example in public schools, you'll see even more students who slack off and don't take education seriously.

                                                  If voucher programs succeed, I think it'll be the deathknell for the public education system in America, and you'll see a generation or two worth of students being left behind, stuck in failing public schools. And let's be honest - who will be going to private schools? Mostly white kids. And who will be stuck in those failing public schools? Minorities.
