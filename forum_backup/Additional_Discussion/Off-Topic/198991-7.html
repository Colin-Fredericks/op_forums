            I think the interesting difference between DC/Marvel when compared to Scandinavian Donald Duck comics are how they have evolved over time. Originally DC/Marvel published pulp paper comics aimed at kids while nowadays they make glossy comics aimed at adults. Meanwhile Donald Duck is still a pulp paper comic aimed at kids which remains remarkably popular among adults since the stories are very short and entertaining which makes them very accessible. The Donald Duck publisher never feels the need to 'shake up' the Donald Duck universe because as Wiemann notes, there is no permanence in the first place, in fact they commonly republish old popular stories since the story is still good and most people will have either never read it or forgotten it.

            Now I bought up Donald Duck mainly to illustrate that DC/Marvel are not that dominant in the comic industry, however when it comes to introducing new characters it's a terrible example as I'm not sure if a single permanent cast member has been added in the past 50 years. It's just the kind of thing where people do seem content reading the same kind of stories about the same kind of characters in perpetuity.

                                        All of this is very much missing my point.

                                        The issue is not how many individual copies are sold. Print is a very low profit margin industry.

                                        The Manga industry his more open to getting away from this cycle in a few ways. One of which is that the industry is a mix of either driven by merchandise that needs constant changes in the source material (ex: Gundam) or largely doesn't drive multimedia empires beyond telling the story (ex: One Piece). When One Piece ends, it ends. The structure of the part of the industry then uses its success to push new projects from the publisher and the creative teams. But this invariably means no single property is actually worth that much in total because the expectation is always that it is limited.

                                                The problem is that Marvel and DC get the attention because of the size of their brands. But a lot of comic book companies are brand based in their market value.

                                                In Europe, comics are mostly a brand based market. The companies in question that are generally putting out way more than comics. Does Scandinavia love the hell out of Donald Duck? Yes, but he's a brand.

                                                The south east Asian comic market avoids being so dominated by this, but it still sets in. When branding is the major driver of an IP, it is generally very consciously done to drive a consumer desire for new stuff. A lot of things are just long comic/animated commercials for something more lucrative, and regularly changing things gives you more of that lucrative new things to sell. The stories in such properties tend to fall into the same repetitive status quo issues as Western comics. Even when you look at the bigger comic-as-primary-value driven manga, it is exceedingly rare for them to reach the kind of sizes that we associate with the "big names" of manga stories. And most of them get there by similar storytelling tactics. The shonen genre is notoriously prone to this.                                                         Originally posted by Heavy Arms View Post
                                                        And most of them get there by similar storytelling tactics. The shonen genre is notoriously prone to this. Sure we have One Piece (though even it isn't actually immune from, "New Island with [dangerous mystery], Luffy idiot heroes his way through the situation while the others put up with it and do their best to help; if [big bad is behind the mystery] the Strawhats will have a major setback before getting stronger and winning."
                                                        I think it's worthwhile to point out that this storytelling tactic is just the standard 3 act play.
                                                        Act 1: Setup
                                                        Act 2: Setback
                                                        Act 3: Victory

                                                        The difference between various comics tends to be the lengths of the acts. In a Donald Duck comic each act might be 2-3 pages long while in a Manga it might be between 1-10 chapters depending on pacing. If you abandon this structure many people will feel like nothing meaningful ever happens or that everything just happens randomly. However the fact that the comic keeps repeating 3-act Arcs does not necessarily mean it is preserving status quo. A good example of this would be the korean webcomic Tower of God that's broken into very clear acts with clear developments between each act.

                                                        I agree that the shonen genre in particular though does strongly favor the status quo, however I think that's forgivable since the genre is centred on fun rather then depth. If you kill off major characters every few chapters it quickly becomes much less casual reading. In series aimed at older readers such as Gantz, Elfen Lied, Kingdom, Zetman etc there are much more permanent consequences in the subplots. They're also much smaller brands which is probably related.
