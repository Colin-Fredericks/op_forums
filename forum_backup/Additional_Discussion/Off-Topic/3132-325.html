                        We are in an era of more-than-usual weaponizing of civility (this is always a thing, since civility is not actually some lofty virtue of its own, and frequently is used to silence things that should be heard using how they're said as an excuse to ignore the truth). Right wing politics (and not just in the US) has shown an continued rise over a while now of a voting base that wants uncivil representatives that will express the voters' anger with the state of the body politic to legislating. Even if performance, right-wing politicians are incentivized to be uncivil when advocating for their side, and demand civility from political opponents, because enough of their voters want that, that if they don't do it they're be replaced by someone that will.

                        This then puts the left-wing of politics in a Popper's Paradox Even if most of the left does value civility as a norm, remaining civil/tolerant of uncivil behavior simply cedes undue power to their political opponents. Abiding by norms the other side has shown they don't care about is a no-win situation, and violating norms that you do care about undermines your perceived moral standing. And all of that just fuels polarization on both sides and makes a return to proper civility all the more difficult.

                        If the center does not hold (and so far that's not looking good even if not impossible), than the return to real civility isn't going to happen until things get a lot worse first.

                                    Something a few politicians have said while being honest: being a politician takes a lot of ego. Of the hundreds, thousands, or millions of potential people that could in theory run for office (so far no country has reached having over a billion people that would be legally allowed to run for any existing office, but we're not that far off), you have to believe you're better suited for it than all of them; and they need to buy it. That is, ultimately, at odds with acting with dignity and civility.

                                    There were more than a few Enlightenment philosophers that tried to actively steer European culture away from the growing roots of liberal democracy because however much they agreed that hereditary monarchs claiming divine authority was hogwash, selecting leaders based on popularity contests isn't actually great either. Descartes was a prominent advocate for technocratic oligarchy (that is, the government should be run by the people that have proven themselves the best at their given fields, with executive power granted by mastery of multiple fields to best balance the interests of each oligarchic domain) because he predicted democracies would get us to... basically where we are right now. Of course, his idea was very flimsy in the same regards to what happened with Communism under Stalin and Mao: powerful authoritarians taking all the power for themselves instead of actual rule by the proletariat. Which his how the sentiment of democracy being horrible, but still being greater than everything else we've come up with and tried set in much earlier than a certain pithy statement I'm alluding to put it.

                                    But at the same time... democracies can do a lot. Democracies are slow, and the larger the populace and the more indirect the democracy is the worse that slowness tends to impact the feelings of the populace, but with the right people elected lots of things can get done.

                                    Remember people in the US are very upset right now because a landmark right is about to get stripped, and under a legal rationale that could justify stripping countless more. But those were all rights that had to be fought for, and established into law, because we didn't always have them. A hell of a lot of work went into making all those rights a reality, because things can get accomplished. Having to refight those fights is stupid, and a giant waste of time, and is going to cause countless tragedies, but moving forward can be done even if other people are going to push things back too. We need politicians to be less civil, less dignified, and more willing to get dirty to really fight this fight though. We didn't make the progress we're losing happen by asking nicely, we're not going to keep it by asking nicely, and we're not getting it back again asking nicely.

                                                    Originally posted by Lysander View Post
                                                    Sorry if I came across as angry in the above post. But I find Cancel Culture and Political Correctness as absurd concepts and a threat to free expression.
                                                    Cancel Culture is free expression. Or rather, an inevitable development of it.

                                                    Yes, people jump to conclusions and make assumptions, then go with gusto after it. But that's old news, happens since always, ask any public figure about how fickle public opinion is.

                                                    And "Cancel Culture" isn't a group, or a movement. It is some people reacting to something with the tools they have at hand. It used to be angry letters to editors and producers, and mass media had a huge amount of power in shaping, directing or defusing such sentiments, usually not used for the benefit of fair doubt. Sometimes Cancel Culture is a bunch of ignorant people going after a half-heard thing. Sometimes it is me, or you.

                                                    What else, after all, should we do? Ignore the things we think are wrong instead of doing something about it? Specially when this something is just using the power of your words and opinion to rally others to... use their words and opinion. And organizing for things like not watching a movie is, again, old as all hell, it's called a boycott.

                                                    Same goes for Political Correctness. It is just people realizing that saying things that hurt other people isn't ok. And it isn't. It isn't always such a terrible thing, it may be unintentional and even unavoidable. People talking in good faith will hurt others sometimes by accident, but they'll also avoid it if they can, because they're talking in good faith, and Political Correctness is just raising attention to how some words may hurt without we noticing.

                                                    Of course, people are people and they'll sometimes miss the mark and overreact. That's not good, but's normal, we correct course. What we should do is just use the same tools and call people out when we think they're exaggerating. Sometimes we are actually wrong, others we're right, but as long as we talk about issues in good faith we can learn and grow from the experience, as people and as society.

                                                    By me, what I don't like is the "anti-political-correctness/cancel-culture" discourse. Not like in not liking you for it, but the source of the discourse, the people that sell the idea of such things as a kind of movement (a fallacy) and blame them in broad terms for cherry-picked instances (another fallacy). This is a disingenuous discourse for the politicians that use it, a bad-faith argument to deviate attention from the real problems and on top of that protect their BS under the false idea that Free Expression means saying what you want without giving the other the right for an argument. Have a mob shouting "censorship" upon any dissenting voice and you won't ever have to actually defend your argument or share Free Expression.

                                                            Clint Eastwood is also a bad example because people don't want to cancel him just because of something from 50 years ago... he has an immensely long history of regressive public statements and a mixed history of political activism under his belt (he consistently picks to favor rich white men for positions of power when he weighs in on things; even ones that directly oppose many of his stated political views... and there are candidates closer to his stated views but not his personal life experience that he could try to elevate but doesn't).

                                                            Of course, cancel culture isn't really a problem... as others have noted it's just the latest spin of two very old things: people pushing norm/etc. breakers out of public discourse, and mainstream culture appropriating minority culture and then losing the original context of the thing (cancel culture starting in Black Twitter and was generally a personal expression of, "I'm not buying that artists work any more," rather than, "nobody should," and ranged widely from silly minor/joking clap backs to major and serious call outs).

                                                            Retribution politics is a real problem, and a real concern. "I, as a private citizen, am canceling Disney because of the litany of horrible things they do as a company," is free expression. "I, an elected official, am introducing laws specifically to economically punish Disney for not agreeing with my politics," is terrifying if you care about free expression. For all that I agree that Disney should have its copyright mangling history stomped flat, it should be on the merits of what good intellectual property laws and public domain interests dictate. Not because some Republicans got their feelings hurt that Disney wants to market to the widest audience possible (which means the policy isn't actually going to fix the US's tremendously bad copyright laws that are largely Disney's fault).

                                                            If politicians can use their positions to make companies they don't like pay more in taxes, or seize their property, or whatever to punish them for agreeing with said politician's efforts, what stops them from doing the same to private citizens? It's the First Amendment for both cases, so if one goes (and not because, again, we fix stupid legal issues like Citizen's United), what's the line on the other? The idea that we might see a serious effort to institute a "woke tax" where "bad"companies get fined for not toeing conservative talking points means free expression is actually being murdered in front of us.
