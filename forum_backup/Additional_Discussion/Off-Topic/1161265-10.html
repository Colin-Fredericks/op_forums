    Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post
    How do you all feel about players initiating their own skill/ability checks? eg “I try to pick that guy’s pocket. 23 on my Sleight of Hand check.” or, “What do I know about the culture of the people who made this statue with a 17 History check?”
    I like players to describe their character's action, or at least say what the actions hope to achieve and by what means (i.e. "I think back on my lessons at school to recall any details I retained about the culture that made this statue" or "I want to get whatever's in that guy's pocket by picking it"), but do not like for them to pick up the die and roll it. Specifically because they might be shooting their own effort, and enthusiasm, in the foot by doing so should their die roll come out low.

    I much prefer being able to determine the odds, even allowing automatic success were it makes sense, or adjudicating automatic failure if for some reason that makes sense. Because then I can communicate those odds, or success or failure, to the player before they've already committed to throwing the die - which gives them the chance to actually buy-in to the odds or try to alter them with refinement or revision of their character's action. The end result, in my experience, always being overall happier players.

    Originally posted by Charlaquin View Post
    On a related note, how do you feel about knowledge skills like History or Arcana or what have you? Personally, because I have my players interact with the game world in the form of narrating what they do, and “I want to know more about this” can take some weird linguistic gymnastics to frame as something you’re actively doing, I generally run Knowledge skills as passive scores instead of checks. I’ll include in my notes any additional information that might be available to players with a high enough passive score in what ever knowledge skill is relevant, and then just bake that additional information into my descriptions. Sometimes I’ll use those skills for active rolls when the PC is actively trying to analyze or identify something, but generally speaking, I don’t like “roll to see how much you know about X”. What do y’all think?
    I also more often than not effectively have all of the "I know stuff" skills (perception included) be passive check only - I just tell the players what their characters need to know, toss in a few not needed but helpful facts should a character be proficient at the skill, and give some esoteric maybe potentially helpful if the player applies it just right detail if a character is not just proficient but also has a really high modifier for the skill.

    We've been playing for a few years now without the players having rolled a single die for certain skills (perception, investigation, arcana, religion, history, insight, and nature), and no one has felt like it wasn't worth it to have a character proficient in those skills. Even had people think it was totally worth it to put expertise in some of those (example: insight - the player wanted to feel certain that his character wasn't going to be buying in to anyone's bullshit without choosing to intentionally go along with known bullshit, and I had that happen without rolling any dice, just compared whatever NPCs Charisma (Deception) modifier against his Wisdom (Insight) modifier, and when his insight was higher gave him clear indicators of things not being as genuine as the speaker intended them to sound)

    Failing to be knowledgeable or adept enough in an area where the character is meant to be knowledgeable or adept is sometimes funny (like a dad joke that occasionally gets a laugh), but is never really "fun and interesting" unless the reason is because you've actually gone up against something beyond your means. So I engineer things to make sure that's the only sort of failure possible.

    Note: all the above answers are specific to D&D (and all not-even-kind-of-D&D-like games, such as Exalted, World of Darkness, Shadowrun, etc.). Other games similar to D&D that I run, I run for different feel and with different goals in mind, so the same aspect of the game is handled differently. For the prime examples, see below:

    Dungeon Crawl Classics: Your character doesn't know shit. No roll needed, and hey, you don't even have skills on your character sheet (unless you are the right class, and then you still don't have this sort of skill on your sheet) so that's very easy to remember. Players pretty naturally try to avoid rolling dice in this system, though, because they know that the odds are not in their favor and the risks are plentiful and severe for most rolls.

    HackMaster: The game has an intentional vibe of sticking to the rules and the rules covering all the important bits of playing through the game, and that attitude is a large part of why we play it (it's kind of like when you sit down to play a video game of a familiar style, but it has a difficulty slider and you decide "what they hell, let's crank it up and see if we can hack it."). So I still prefer the players to tell me what they are doing before throwing dice, but they are initiating checks by describing their actions, they just need my input on whether the check is to be rolled opposed by a check I make as DM (whether it is for an NPC or the environment, because the game doesn't exactly do roll vs. DC), or is a standard check, and if so which difficulty modifier to use (because instead of roll d20, add mods, get equal to or higher than DC for skill checks, the system uses a roll d100, adjust by difficult modifier that ranges from -90 for trivial tasks to +10 for very difficult tasks, get equal to or lower than your skill rating).

    A good example of the way the system works is the Monster Lore skill. First, a character has to have invested character building resources just to have the opportunity to roll. Second, the player has to ask a specific question about a monster (i.e. a monster attacks the party, and the player asks "Is it less affected by fire?" because they want to know if their character should tell the spellcaster to use their go-to fire spell, or to not waste their casting on it), and then a roll is made. Typically the roll has no modifier because it is consider hard difficulty by the rules (with the GM being encouraged to alter the difficulty if the check is being made with resources like a library at hand, or if the character has specific reason to be more familiar with this sort of creature), which means the character isn't likely to know the answer unless a significant portion of character building resources have been dedicated to this skill.

                    You actually can’t fire it like a semi-auto even if you have Extra Attack because of the Loading property (unless you take the Crossbow Expert Feat).

                    Originally posted by Morty View Post
                    Maybe I'll just go straight rogue. But without the archery fighting style, the Sharpshooter feat's ability to sacrifice accuracy for damage is kind of risky. But I can always take another feat.
                    You definitely don’t need the Archery Fighting Stylr for Sharpshooter to be worth taking. That said, I wouldn’t take it as a rogue, for reasons I’ll get into below.

                    Assuming +3 to hit and damage from Dexterity and a Heavy Crossbow, your expected damage is higher when using Sharpshooter’s -5 to hit for +10 damage feature as long as the target’s AC is below 15. This target number will only get higher as you level up and your Proficiency Bonus and Dexterity increase.

                    The formula, if you want to be able to figure out if the extra damage is worth the accuracy trade off with any weapon and any to-hit Bonus is:
                    Add your to-hit Bonus to 32. Subtract your expected damage value on a hit (that’s your damage bonus, plus 2.5 for a d4 damage die, 3.5 for a d6, 4.5 for a d8, 5.5 for a d10, or 6.5 for a d12). Divide the result by 2, and you get your threshold for Sharpshooter. Statistically speaking, it’s better to use it if the target’s AC is below that number, and worse if it’s above that number.

                    This does mean that you should probably just avoid using Sharpshooter if you have an opportunity to land a Sneak Attack, since that significantly increases you expected damage value on a hit, which lowers the AC threshold for when Sharpshooter is worth using.

                    Originally posted by Morty View Post
                    I will say that being able to play a ranged rogue without the system tripping me up at every turn is refreshing. Even if it kind of feels that it's better than a melee one. And even though I do the same thing every round in combat. But I fully expected the latter.
                    It pretty much is better to go ranged with the rogue this edition. The one thing the melee rogue has going for it is dual-wielding, since being able to make another attack with your bonus action gives you much better chances of landing Sneak Attack each turn. Of course, Crossbow Expert with a hand crossbow lets ranged rogues do that too, and eliminates the penalty for shooting while engaged in melee. So yeah, hand crossbows are the way to build rogues, numerically speaking.
