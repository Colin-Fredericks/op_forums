    Can someone explain superhero comics to me?

    09-12-2015, 10:12 PM
    Just right at the outset: my intent is not to bash on comics. Whenever I say something negative about them, my intent is to explain an issue (heh, pun unintended but also unregretted) with what I see as comics as a whole, with the expectation that there is a legitimate reason/explanation which I, in my silliness, have missed.

    But, I really don't understand the appeal? Not of graphic novels generally, I've read a few and thoroughly enjoyed them; they're a perfectly valid, and sometimes the best, way to tell a story. I'm talking specifically about superhero comics. From what extremely little I've read, and what slightly more I've read about, just tracing the continuity can be a pain in the neck, what with the crossovers, reboots, partial reboots, different versions of the same character run simultaneously, jumping around in time (both due to in-universe time travel and odd ordering of stories) and continuity errors. Then we have the simple problems with knowing the order; again, from what very little I've been able to see, they're organised as basically a series of mini-series, but I have yet to find any kind of logical guide to what order those mini-series come in.

    For example, I find the Hulk to be an interesting character, and wanted to get into him a bit more by reading his comic series. So, I understood it's been rebooted several times since the original, when the Hulk was grey and changed at night, which is fine, so I went looking for the 'current' version.

    Two hours, three forums, two wiki-crawls over two seperate wikis and innumeral Googlings later, I gave up. I think I figured out what was the current 'start', but then I read it, and it was referring to a whole bunch of stuff which I had no idea about, and it seemed really, really important (there seemed to be a Justice-League-equivalent involved? I know it wasn't the JL since they're DC, but same thing). I just want to read the Hulk; I don't object to reading others, but it shouldn't be required reading, to my mind. Also, money constraints: each issue/book costs $5-$20, and each other book I need to keep up with multiplies that.

    Again, I'm not looking to bash on comics; a lot of people read them, and they seem to enjoy them. In accordance with the principle of self-skepticism, I assume that there's a logic or fact or something I've missed. Can someone please, please explain it to me, because I just can't figure it out myself?

      The continuity can be difficult to track in a grand scale - which is why most comic fans suggest that any new reader looking to get started either start at an issue #0 (not all series have these, they are usually a prequel, a teaser, or some other type of tale that introduces the character and the general idea of the series in a form similar to a pilot episode for a TV series) or #1, and then just stick to that comic title for a while - when a crossover comes along, it will tell you which issues of which titles are involved in the story so that you don't have to guess, and that might result in you finding that you liked a character that crossed into the title you were following and you can then keep up with issues of that from that point forward.

      Or, at the very least, starting at the first issue involved in a storyline - often times the issue number will be something far from #1, like #78 or even #212, but it will have a title on the cover both for the comic series itself (i.e. Uncanny X-Men) and for the storyline (i.e. Age of Apocalypse - part 1). And, again, from there following that tale just requires picking up issues in the order released or picking up the specifically mentioned issues when the storyline bleeds over to other comic series or has crossover.

      As for the costs, I agree that they can very quickly get quite high... that was even true back when I was buying issues at $2 each as a kid. These days I avoid the costs by waiting for compilation books that collect a whole storyline into a single, often hard-cover volume that has a lower price than buying each issue along the way would have.

      And really, if you stumble across a character that you identify with or that resonates with you (in my case, that's The Punisher, Carol Danvers/Captain Marvel, Doctor Strange, and some but not all author's takes on Wolverine) that is when the whole thing clicks and you realize what it is about comics that digs its hooks in so deep that even people that "grew out of it" can flip open a comic and find a smile on their face and what drives the astronomical sales figures on the recent barrage of super-hero movies.

      Not so noble anymore.

              Some people like the trivia and 'expanded universe.' Just like some folks like to pore over Star Trek or Wars or Game of Thrones, cartoons or anime or even World of Darkness, seeking out details and developing theories from the books and scenes, some of those people turn their minds and interest to comics.

              Others ignore the extra details. For instance if I were you I'd probably find a noteworthy issue of the Hulk from around 2000 or 1990 if I was feeling bold and just start there, reading forward. If you're not interested in the Defenders or Ultimate Hulk, just stick to the 'main' line of them. If you end up liking some other character depicted in a crossover or a team-up, you can then follow them. Joe Casey took over Hulk near 1999, I enjoy him as a writer, which leads to other things.

              Some people just like specific writers more than characters or teams, and will wait until they have a run on an ongoing title or do a mini or limited series.

                      The Hulk probably isn't the best example. But most of the "big name A list" heroes suffer from this; especially ones that have seen rather massive up and down swings in popularity. While you say no media could survive this sort of thing... the big name comics actually thrive on it. Their core customer base - especially in the digital age - are hardcore readers. The more popular the character is, the more you can expect an increase in difficulty tracking their continuity. The hardcore fan base tends to buy into it for greater returns than an potential lost casual reader.

                              Here is my advice: don't worry too much about older continuity. If you want to read a major story arc for a given hero or team, Marvel publishes trades with the banner "Essential" for all their big name characters, that compile important story lines; ask for them at your Local Comic Store(LCS). You can also ask your LCS for Marvel "Visionaries" titles if you want to follow a specific writer's work on a title. I highly recommend Peter David's legendary 10 year run on Hulk.

                              Planet Hulk happens before World War Hulk, and events in PH are the reason for WWH.

                              Titles change or start over at #1 as a marketing ploy, #1s and anniversary issues like #50, #100, or number #300, sell very well. This is also why you see stuff like "Incredible Herc", it's just a gimmick to get people who were already reading Hulk's book to try Herc's book.

                              Oh, and don't get too attached to adjectives, like "Incredible"; most of the time, these words don't really change anything, they're just there to grab attention, or differentiate one volume of a hero's story from another. The one exception in Marvel is "Ultimate", most Marvel "Ultimate" stories happen in a completely different timeline than the main, or "616" universe. The only exception to THAT rule is current issues of "Ultimate Spider-Man", which will be taking place in 616, but feature the Spider-Man from Ultimate, as the Ultimate universe has been destroyed.

                              If you feel you need to know the origin story of a character, there is also a reasonably good wiki-type database at www.comicvine.com.

                              Sadly, recap pages at the beginning, and preview pages at the end of comics died out some time ago, as that page count was sacrificed in the name of advertising space.

                              I hope this helps. As for DC, just start with New52 Justice League Volume 1.
                              DC history is so fluid, you could drink it with a straw.
