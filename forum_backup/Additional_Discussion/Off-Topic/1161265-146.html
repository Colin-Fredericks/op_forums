            Hmm, well, not exactly that^, but like 25 years ago or so, I had a Fighter/Cleric of Loviatar, she was the daughter of a PC of mine, an Avariel Paladin/Magic-User and an NPC, a Drow Anti-Paladin. There's a story there, but basically, the Drow kidnapped and teleported away with her. (I was the DM that did this, and more story there of course, but that's not the story here.)

            So for the sake of some interesting stuff, Grace, the F/C of Loviatar, was an all elf, half Drow/half Avariel with no wings. Started playing her and the party got caught by the mists and wound up in The Plane of Dread (the Ravenloft setting), adventured in some different lands there, and then they faced the Rakshasha(sp) Lord. We were fighting him pretty good until I failed a save versus polymorph and got turned in to a tiger. (I later made lots of saving throws, and the DM gave up and said I kept my mind, but in the body of a tiger.)

            The party quickly wiped without my healing and protection. And that was the end of that campaign. A few months later or so, the DM ran me solo (the Ravenloft stuff was a new DM, not me btw), in that game, I went through the mists and didn't know what land I was in. But a wizard agreed to help me if I helped him in return. But he couldn't quite break the other Dread Lord's curse and it split me in 3. A Tiger, a Drow Fighter, and an Avariel Cleric. Turns out the wizard was Stradh (the vampire) and he wanted us to find Tatyana (look up Strad to learn the story if you don't know it, it's kind long to put down here).

            We found and brought Tatyana to him, but before he could reward us, Tatyana jumped to her death, Strahd then attacked us (we were like 12th level btw) So we fought and we might have won, not that it would kill him for good of course), but he threw the tiger off the balcony and into the water below, killed the Drow fighter, and cursed the Avariel Cleric (so she now looked like a "Disney Gargoyle" more or less) and banished her from Ravenloft and back to Toril. (the DM them moved to New Jersey from California and that was the end of that game)

            So 25 years later, like a year ago, we started an evil campaign, and I ran this character by the DM to play again (this DM lives in the Netherlands btw, I'm still in California, we play by post with some others on Facebook) So she hooks up with their new boss who's trying to depose an evil Duke that killed his parents. Also turns out that when I got banished, so did the Tiger me.

            So they eventually found each other and merged, and now race wise, Grace is a tiger-ish looking Avariel with Dragon like wings. (a little better than the Gargoyle like wings she had after getting curse by Strahd) So cool story and background all and all.

            Okay, so back to the real point here

            She's still a cleric of Loviatar and is now in 5th ed. She says Loviatar is a goddess of Love. That is, love is life, life is pain, therefore pain is love. (still some 'huh? there to be sure) The way I'm playing her, "how can you truly know love is you don't understand pain?" I don't mean whips and chains, but more like if you don't really know the pain of life, you can't really know love either. That's her anyway, not me.

            So the punchline here so to speak, is that she's worshiping Loviatar in a different way then 'pain' pain, somewhat similar when the above says they are worshiping an evil god in a non evil way

            Anyway, just was in a mood to share all that and hope anyone that reads all this finds it interesting.

                                                There's also the argument to be made that a person might worship an Evil deity because they think they HAVE to. Usually out of fear, or because their particular career/lifestyle/home situation/species causes them to fall under the evil god's divine jurisdiction. In that way, if the person wants to avoid the evil god's wrath or gain their favor (or, more properly, to avoid their disfavor), they need to pray to them, perform sacrifices, etc. They may not want to, they just feel they have no choice. That belief may be incorrect, but it's something they still believe, or because breaking out from under that god's influence might be too difficult/perilous.

                                                In that way, the character's alignment is a function of to what degree they are "on board" with the evil god's intent, versus them just doing what they must to appease the one in charge.

                                                See also the Drow, and how their society is under the protection/tyranny of Lolth, Demon Queen of Spiders. It's likely there are many Drow who would prefer not to do evil things. But they live in a society devoted to Lolth, ruled by evil priestesses dedicated to her, and which encourages duplicity and murder. There's only so much a Drow can get away with, vis a vis Not Being Evil, while remaining a part of that society. They certainly couldn't get away with not paying Lolth the bare minimum respect, both socially and privately, lest they get killed or turned into Driders.

                                                There's a reason "good" Drow tend to have to leave their society entirely. Those that do so without dying/becoming Drider, anyway.

                                                            Looking at what PF2 has to say about alignment, it’s absolutely spot-on with my own assessment of the good/evil spectrum, but falls short of what the lawful/chaotic spectrum means to me, falling instead into the usual wishy-washy “lawful means you prefer follow some set of rules, but it doesn’t have to be the actual laws of your society, and chaotic means you like freedom more than structure but it doesn’t mean you act at random, and neutral means you sometimes follow laws, except when you don’t feel like they suit you” nonsense. And I think I’ve figured out why every game that uses the 9-alignment system falls into this same waffle. True libertarian political thinking (anarcho-capitalist hogwash not withstanding) is so far outside the Overton Window of most of western civilization that the idea of rational actors being actively opposed to state power is just too alien for most of the people making the game to wrap their heads around. So Chaotic Good ends up being this weird “I know it when I see it” phenomenon that they lack the language to accurately describe.
