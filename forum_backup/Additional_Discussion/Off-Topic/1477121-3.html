    Rhywbeth
    ​
    Eilistraee is amazing and I admit that I frequently port her to other settings anyway, but otherwise I prefer Greyhawk's deities. Most of the "default" D&D from setting-agnostic books previous to 4th edition isn't actually setting-agnostic, but Greyhawk. Indeed, some rules that many understand as core to the system, if not always liked, are actually Greyhawk things that other settings just decided to not change, like using Vancian magic, Cleric Domains and the role of the Alignment Chart. Famous names found in spells and items lists like Tenser, Mordenkainen, Robilar and many others are Greyhawk NPCs, and the PHB gods are from Greyhawk pantheons. So a lot of players have more contact with Greyhawk than they know, as this goes on from Artifacts to standard monster lore.

    Planescape mostly became absorbed as the default for planar lore and rules whenever it isn't overwritten by setting-specific stuff, so if you ever read Manual of the Planes (any edition), you already brushed Planescape and only a few specifics would be needed to get the setting's lore, mostly about Sigil and its factions (and the use of gifting Mephits as a way to send messages similar to how some people did this with flowers). A similar thing happened to Spelljammer in a lessened way (otherwise it was mostly abandoned).

    In such regards, Dark Sun is interesting for being a very different setting he default, and the birthplace of the standard Psionic rules if those interest you in any way or at least in some settings, along with presenting weird twists to standard presentations, like cannibal Halflings and Elves as marginalized desert runners.

                            It's hard for me to imagine most editions of D&D as having significant barrier for entry. The base d20 mechanic is very simple, you don't have to memorize most of the math behind the scenes as a new player because there's a chart that just tells you what to put on your sheet and then you know what numbers to put together.

                            For a group that's not going optimization happy, and not including a lot of supplements and thus avoiding things like Feat bloat, jumping in to D&D is generally very easy (but 4e and 5e are even more so).

                            What happens to D&D though, is how rapidly the complexity shoot up after you get into it. This is obvious in the mechanics if you just do a simple exercise of create a Level 1 character from scratch vs. create a Level 5 character from scratch. But this is also true of the setting. Level 1 characters don't know much about their own fantasy worlds, so their players don't have to know much either (in most editions of D&D, without a Take 10 even a maxed out knowledge character is still going to fail DC 10+ Know X checks more than not). It's highly unlikely you need to read more than 5 pages worth of material to jump into a D&D game meant to start at level 1, and then the players can learn the setting as their characters explore it. Standard D&D setting tropes are also very well known to a global point; lots of video games (a much larger entertainment industry) draw on things lifted off of D&D so there's a familiarity there even for non-TTRPG folks to start with.

                            The WoD games are somewhat of a flatter curve here. This means there is generally more to digest at start. Even with the real world setting to fall back on, the supernatural societies within it have huge amounts of detail about them in various books. Individual WoD factions frequently have as much text devoted to them as whole continents in D&D settings. A starting level WoD character is generally more work than a starting level D&D character; esp. because of the more free form char-gen process. But things don't ramp up nearly as much. Making a pure starting level WoD character vs. one with 100 XP to spend after creation (again, esp. for non-optimization focused play) isn't going to have the same feeling of a massive leap in character complexity.

                            IME, D&D has the lower bar for entry, but a higher bar for playing for more than a few sessions. D&D 5e has really tried to reduce that as much as possible, but there's limits to possible without running into the 4e "this doesn't feel like D&D!" complaints. The WoD games have generally had a higher bar for entry, but smooth out better once you're past that

                                I take it you don't run your games in such a way as to face palm when players want to take pole arms into a dense forest or sewer tunnels, because none of them have any idea of the size of their own equipment. A bad case of ichigo slashing the hospital ceiling sort of thing. Conversely very few if any people are going to argue the dimensions of a rifle or hand gun. Likewise most urban kids have no idea how big a horse is, nevermind them getting into the "how many hands high" and "how many stones" rating systems. Yes that is specific, but at the same time it is no different than saying a honda civic will fit through a gap but a garbage truck won't.

                                Also after so many decades of playing various RPGs, I have some issues with the standard fantasy genre setup. Adventurers don't really make sense thematically or in setting in most cases. WoD is just our world except a bit darker, a bit more corrupt, a bit less hopeful. It just has random supernatural stuff hidden in the corners.

                                Most D&D is set up in the reverse, it centers around the characters, regardless if the setting, the civilizations, economics, or societies make sense. D&D is the MMORPG design where you interact with the NPCs, hand in quests, and spend your time level and loot grinding. Now can a DM take D&D in a different direction, of course, but it progressively becomes more and more homebrew patching things than what the core game provides.

                                I am not saying that all games have to be "Call of D&D" or "Historical Accuracy Simulator 2023 edition", but it is worth noting exactly how much stuff is getting handwaved which would drastically change the flavor of the games in question.

                                The easiest system I have ever taught people was the Lone Wolf Multiplayer system, based off of the Lone Wolf choose your own adventure books by Joe Dever. A single D10 for all rolls, no XP to track just adventures completed, and an inbuilt inventory management setup to avoid the meme of where did that 10' ladder come from. Sure the characters max out in 15 - 20 games but in truth a lot of groups break up or get board before that point.
