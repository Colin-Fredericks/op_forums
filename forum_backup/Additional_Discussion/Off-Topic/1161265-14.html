        The subclasses are an interesting approach to synthesizing and streamlining several disparate elements of class customization. Overall I'm a fan, but it isn't without its shortfalls. While it doesn't totally lock out meaningful choices after your subclass is chosen it does throttle them down quite a bit. What I would really like is for there to be some method or mechanism of optionally slotting in features from other subclasses within your class, making your progression more of a zig-zag. Obviously this has the potential to create balance issues and awkward interactions between features, but I think it would make for more varied concepts and also make up for not being able to multiclass your own class to access the tree un-climbed.

        The other thing it comes down to is that not all subclasses are created equal. In particular some have more choices than others. Obviously anything that has or grants spellcasting gets an extra suite of potential customization that isn't available to non-casters, but even things like the Battlemaster or the Ranger archetypes create more wiggle room than is afforded to other subclasses. Rogues get it pretty bad, as they just aren't given a whole lot of lists to pick from. Clerics honestly feel to me like the class that was hardest done by 5e though. Making Domain their subclass was a pretty natural choice, but also made Clerics much less personal. Having two Domains really helped you make 3e Clerics into devotees with flavor and style. There's also the fact that, while they've walked it back since 4e, Clerical healing just isn't as essential as it used to be. Having lost their handhold in that age old niche, and not having the sheer versatility they developed through the content glut of earlier editions, 5e Clerics feel a little lost.

                        Originally posted by Morty View Post
                        I've never been to clear on why, honestly. 5-minute short rests worked fine.
                        The short answer is 4e backlash. 5-minute short rests were a 4e thing, so if 5e was going to have short rests at all, they weren’t going to be 5 minutes. The longer answer is that WotC wanted 5e to include healing between encounters. But healing on short rests didn’t poll well on the basis of being too “unrealistic”. What exactly damage and HP actually meant was a huge hot button issue at the time, but anything that implied whatever it was could be recovered by means other than magic, rest, or medical attention were shot down by the “simulationism” crowd. So, healing between encounters got attached to the Healer’s Kit in the playests, and using one took an hour so as not to seem “unrealistically” fast. Then, when the playtests started including abilities with limited uses on non-caster characters, X per day was seen as too arbitrary and X per encounter was seen as too “gameist”. So, the short rest was added back into the playtests and combined with the time it would take to use a Healer’s Kit. By the final release, the healer’s kit was no longer required to spend Hit Dice on a short rest, but Short Rests still took an hour.

                        Changing short rests to 5 minutes as a house rule is very easy, though it’s probabably a good idea to cut the number of uses of any resource that recovers on a short rest in half in that case, because the game’s math assumes there will be about two encounters between each short rest.

                        Originally posted by Morty View Post
                        Subclasses are constrained by character progression being locked into the &quot;one or two features per level, every level&quot; paradigm. One of the underrated 4E concepts was frontloading class features a little. And yes, some classes get it worse. Rogue subclasses, as far as I can tell, get me some nice things, but for the most part the rogue's basic tasks won't change.
                        Sadly, frontloading classes wasn’t really an option because they wanted to bring back 3e style multiclassing. Characters being described as “Fighter 5/Rogue 3” is one of those D&D sacred cows that people attribute to the game “feeling like D&D.” 4e had to do away with that thanks to its front-loaded Classes, because “Taking a level in Monk” would mean a lot more at the first level in a class than at any other level, making the “one level dip” problem even worse. But Feat-based multiclassing was incredibly unpopular. So WotC pretty much decided from the get-go that 5e was going to bring back choosing what Class you want your next level to be, which meant spreading out those Class features over the first couple of levels again.

                        I remember a lot of talk of the first three levels being apprentice levels and to start out at 3rd or 4th if you wanted that 4e style stronger start.

                                        The whole point of a 5 minute short rest is explicitly so you can take one after every single encounter.                                                 In my experience, 3rd edition was all about Prestige Classes. Many of the core classes (wizard, sorcerer, cleric, etc) got absolutely nothing out of staying in their core class at higher levels, other than new spells, and you could get those through Prestige Classes, plus you could get other goodies as well. And for martially focused characters it was hard not to want to jump into a Prestige Class or even just dip into a another martial class - one level of Barbarian or a level of Fighter or Range could give you a big boost. In the changeover to Pathfinder, Paizo tried to get rid of a lot of the "dead levels" (levels where you didn't get anything), tried to make it more attractive to stay in your core class, and tried to make Prestige Classes more of a niche thing than something that everyone was expected to use.

                                                5th edition does some neat stuff, but I agree that leveling often doesn't feel like a big deal. The bonuses you get tend to be fairly minor, and the subclass doesn't do enough to help differentiate characters. I've played three rogues in 5th edition, and frankly I don't really want to play any more because any future rogue I make will feel like, "I've done this before." That was fine back in 1st and 2nd edition D&D, but after playing other RPGs I want a lot of options to make characters feel different, because if I'm going to be playing an RPG for 4 or 5 years I'm eventually going to end up playing multiple characters of the same class, and don't want it to feel primarily like a retread.
