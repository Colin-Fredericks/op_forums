                                    While it's a bit of a small bugbear when it comes to the topic of scientific literacy, I find something very emblematic of the larger cultural problem:

                                    Low stakes science is treated as a joke by mainstream culture.

                                    For most people that aren't part of a scientific field, the entire concept of "low stakes" science is not something they even consider; despite something everyone intuitively understands the moment you bring it up. You don't trust someone that just finished a pre-med Bachelor's degree with heart surgery. Likewise, you don't trust someone with a Bachelor's degree in a medical research field to run a multi-million dollar facility dedicated to working on cancer treatments. Everyone needs low stakes practical experience to hone their skills and prove to the rest of the world that they should be given serious responsibilities. People in trade jobs need apprenticeships. If you want to be a manager in a retail setting, you have to start in sales. Etc.

                                    But most people just do not link that to all the studies that get a passing media snicker and a "what about curing cancer?" punchline. Even people with public perceptions as being very pro science, science literacy, and so on still fall into this. Stephen Colbert is an excellent example of someone that's not a scientist, but loves science, and still can't resist the low hanging fruit on mocking some very niche study that - if nothing else came of it - is still a vital tool for training in the science profession.

                                    A lot of people are also still very caught up in the Great Man Theory of history when it comes to science. Despite science being an inherently collaborative field where anything that actually matters requires lots of and lots of people to do the theoretical work, the practical work, and then the reproduction work, for it to go from an idea to a fact, we think of science in terms of the giants of the field. And even then, a lot of the engineers that turn science into functional technology get a lot of the spotlight as well (not to put them down, but the conflation of the science that shows what we can do, and the people that figure out how to make it work doesn't help people understand what science is really about).

                                    If we talk about evolution... most people can name Darwin. Maybe they might remember Lamarck if reminded. Do they know about Russel (who's graciousness towards letting Darwin publish first led to Russel getting dubbed the "Darwin of the East" despite him figuring out evolution by natural selection first, proving it fast, and proving it far more conclusively)? Do they know how much Thomas Huxley did to earn the title of "Darwin's Bulldog" in his staunch defense of Darwin's work against social critics and stubborn academics? And that's not even getting into the names of folks like the crew of the Beagle that made Darwin's voyage possible. That's not getting into the almost two centuries of evolutionary scientists that have done immensely important work with the field Darwin broke open.

                                    Everyone cares about Edison and Tesla... nobody cares about the crew of people that did the work of figuring out the best light bulb filaments. Nobody considers the material science "sexy" or "ground breaking" even though it lit up our world. The men that sat around testing the applications of electricity to animal hairs are a forgotten footnote, and the scientists doing just as vital work today know they're going to end up just as forgotten... and likely far less likely to be respected than the Menlo Park folks were.

                                                While it's a bit of a small bugbear when it comes to the topic of scientific literacy, I find something very emblematic of the larger cultural problem:

                                                Low stakes science is treated as a joke by mainstream culture.

                                                For most people that aren't part of a scientific field, the entire concept of "low stakes" science is not something they even consider; despite something everyone intuitively understands the moment you bring it up. You don't trust someone that just finished a pre-med Bachelor's degree with heart surgery. Likewise, you don't trust someone with a Bachelor's degree in a medical research field to run a multi-million dollar facility dedicated to working on cancer treatments. Everyone needs low stakes practical experience to hone their skills and prove to the rest of the world that they should be given serious responsibilities. People in trade jobs need apprenticeships. If you want to be a manager in a retail setting, you have to start in sales. Etc.

                                                But most people just do not link that to all the studies that get a passing media snicker and a "what about curing cancer?" punchline. Even people with public perceptions as being very pro science, science literacy, and so on still fall into this. Stephen Colbert is an excellent example of someone that's not a scientist, but loves science, and still can't resist the low hanging fruit on mocking some very niche study that - if nothing else came of it - is still a vital tool for training in the science profession.

                                                A lot of people are also still very caught up in the Great Man Theory of history when it comes to science. Despite science being an inherently collaborative field where anything that actually matters requires lots of and lots of people to do the theoretical work, the practical work, and then the reproduction work, for it to go from an idea to a fact, we think of science in terms of the giants of the field. And even then, a lot of the engineers that turn science into functional technology get a lot of the spotlight as well (not to put them down, but the conflation of the science that shows what we can do, and the people that figure out how to make it work doesn't help people understand what science is really about).

                                                If we talk about evolution... most people can name Darwin. Maybe they might remember Lamarck if reminded. Do they know about Russel (who's graciousness towards letting Darwin publish first led to Russel getting dubbed the "Darwin of the East" despite him figuring out evolution by natural selection first, proving it fast, and proving it far more conclusively)? Do they know how much Thomas Huxley did to earn the title of "Darwin's Bulldog" in his staunch defense of Darwin's work against social critics and stubborn academics? And that's not even getting into the names of folks like the crew of the Beagle that made Darwin's voyage possible. That's not getting into the almost two centuries of evolutionary scientists that have done immensely important work with the field Darwin broke open.

                                                Everyone cares about Edison and Tesla... nobody cares about the crew of people that did the work of figuring out the best light bulb filaments. Nobody considers the material science "sexy" or "ground breaking" even though it lit up our world. The men that sat around testing the applications of electricity to animal hairs are a forgotten footnote, and the scientists doing just as vital work today know they're going to end up just as forgotten... and likely far less likely to be respected than the Menlo Park folks were.
