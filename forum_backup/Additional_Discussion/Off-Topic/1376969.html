                  Elgagusto, you have some point here. Most of historian of previous generation - at least in Poland - say that Middle Ages were 'Dark Ages'. You know, nothing was invented, people were locked in their cities behind walls, religion was over present. And, partially, they are right. General population lived by that. Only the universities became the harbors of those ideas start by Greeks and Romans, that slowly returned to the world. In West, local Universities started to show up in 10th and 11th century, Before them, there were only higher schools build by Byznatines in Italy ( in Venice, Eastern Romans colony, for example ) and those by Muslims ( that started as rebelled provinces in Byzantine Empire that joined new religion - Islam - in 6th and 7th century ). It's all the great chain of learning, streaming from Greece and Rome.

                  Wikipedia on the Medieval University - 'Some scholars such as Syed Farid Alatas have noted some parallels between Madrasahs and early European colleges and have thus inferred that the first universities in Europe were influenced by the Madrasahs in Islamic Spain and the Emirate of Sicily.Other scholars such as George Makdisi, Toby Huff and Norman Daniel, however, have questioned this, citing the lack of evidence for an actual transmission from the Islamic world to Christian Europe and highlighting the differences in the structure, methodologies, procedures, curricula and legal status of the "Islamic college" (madrasa) versus the European university.'

                  Revolution of Renaissance period (15th century) is that is was massive. When Constantinople fell, Universities were already popular in Europe - and then next wave of the teachers from New Rome come to them. In Byzantine Empire period ( up to 11th century ) you had 5 Universities and more or less homogeneous education culture stretch in Greece and Asia Minor. Western Europe, in that time, was divided in various petty kingdoms that needed to 'remember' they need higher education for existence of Empire. Even Charlemagne did not 'know' this, yet.

                              When the Western Roman Empire fell, learning and knowledge in western Europe generally collapsed (compared to the earlier Roman-era). Meanwhile the Eastern Roman Empire basically kept right on trucking and Eastern Europe never really had a "dark age" in quite the same way that Western Europe did. The Italian city states ended up functioning as the trade route between much of the Byzantine Empire and the rest of Europe and when Constantinople was sacked by the Crusaders a lot of that learning and knowledge, old documents, etc, ended up in Italy.

                                      That depends on how you define the Renaissance. Strictly speaking, it is about Western Europe, and that's why it starts at the 15th century. In the east things went a lot different, not only on the Byzantine Empire, but also the Muslim states where Greek philosophers got to be intensely studied.

                                      But while the prevalence of educational institutions dwindled, there are many things that happened and improved in the Middle Ages, even the Early Middle Ages, that just didn't got recognized for a long time. Another feature of the Western Renaissance, and the reason I personally don't like to equate it to what happened in the East, was precisely the creation and spread of rumors and legends about how bad things were in the Middle Ages, because the Humanist philosophers of the time had and wanted to spread as low a vision on the Church as possible.

                                      In truth, the Western Europe Charlemagne found was already more technologically advanced than the Western Romans ever achieved to be. Which makes sense, as their political structure mostly (but not completely) failed, but their knowledge and technology, contrary to Renaissance belief, mostly got through and continued, precisely because the Church of the time invested a lot into keeping knowledge and technology going.

                                      So this belief, more than anything else, is what makes the Renaissance, because it is the reason they even chose the name. They firmly believed the Fall of Rome was kind of the "death of civilization", and they were its "resurrection". This isn't true (and they botched it with the statues).

                                          I think the only real "controversial" part of this is the word "started" in the bold of the first post. History is always a continuum. Breaking up history into periods and eras and whatever is always artificial and arbitrary. "When did X period start?" and "when did Y period end?" are always debated topics because real life doesn't unfurl with such neat and tidy milestones.

                                          As AnubisXy said, it's mainstream historical thought that there is a direct continuity between the Eastern Roman Empire, the Byzantine Empire, and the transfer of thought west in Europe via the Italian city states in the period called the Renaissance. We call it that name for a reason: it was a revival, not an innovation. The period between the First and Fourth Crusade is sometimes called a Byzantine renaissance because the first crusades helped rebuild Byzantium's control over Asia Minor as part of the deal to get western European forces to Jerusalem, which seemed like a resurgence of Byzantine power.

                                          What there isn't is a direct continuity between Byzantine culture and the Italian Renaissance. As much as we can draw lines between history, there's a line to be drawn between them. However much influenced by Byzantine culture, the Italian Renaissance was it's own thing, not Italy becoming Byzantine.
